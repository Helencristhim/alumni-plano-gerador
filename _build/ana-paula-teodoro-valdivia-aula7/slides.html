Chapter 3: The Code

That, Who, and the Two Commas

One tells you which one. The other one teaches you something.

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The clause that worries me is clause fourteen."

"The manager who signed the annex works for the subsidiary."

"Clause fourteen, which worries me, is the indemnity."

"Novation, which replaces one party with a new one, needs everyone's consent."

Two of them tell you which one. Two only add information. What do the commas do?

TypeRuleExample
Defining
(no commas)		It says which one. Take it out and the sentence stops making sense. Use that, who or which.The clause that worries me is fourteen.
Non-defining
(two commas)		We already know which one. It is extra information, and the sentence survives without it. Use who or which — never that.Clause fourteen, which worries me, is the indemnity.
who or whichwho for people, which for things and ideas. A company can take either, but a manager is always who.
The definition
in passing		Put the meaning inside two commas and keep going: term, which means ..., + the rest of your sentence. Nobody has to stop, and nobody has to ask.

In English the commas are not breathing. They are meaning.

The Habit

The One-Breath Definition

The term and its meaning arrive together, or the term arrives alone and the room stops.

Term alone → the room stops

"The severability clause protects us."
He is still on the word. He hears nothing for ten seconds.

Term + clause → the room follows

"Severability, which keeps the rest of the contract alive if one clause fails, protects us."
He learned the word for free and never left the sentence.

Define in passing, never in a footnote.

And the test: if the meaning does not fit in one short clause, the word was too technical for that call.

Chapter 4: The Explanation

One Hour Before the Board

He has to repeat it to eight people who read no contracts

Dialogue

Two Words for the Board

D
Ana, the board meets in an hour. They want to move this contract to our Mexican subsidiary. Can we just do that?
A
Not on our own. What you are describing is an assignment, which means passing our rights to somebody else, and the clause that governs it needs the other side's consent.
D
And if we want the whole contract to move, the obligations included?
A
Then it is a novation, which replaces one party with a new one. Everybody who signed has to agree, including the affiliate that takes over.
D
Let me try it back at you. An assignment is when we hand the contract to the subsidiary, and a novation is when we ask permission first.
A
Almost. The difference is not the permission, it is what moves. An assignment moves the rights, which we can pass on. A novation moves the obligations too, which is why everyone has to sign.
D
Rights travel, obligations need signatures. That I can say to the board.
Comprehension

About David

1. What does David want to do before the board meeting?
Move the contract to the group's Mexican subsidiary.
2. What does he get wrong when he says it back in his own words?
He thinks the difference between an assignment and a novation is asking permission. The difference is what moves: rights, or rights and obligations.
3. How does he sum it up at the end?
"Rights travel, obligations need signatures."

0:000:00

Chapter 5: Practice

Now You Define

Spot the Error

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "The manager which signed the annex is in Mexico."
"The manager who signed the annex is in Mexico." (people take who)
2. "The clause, that worries me, is clause fourteen."
"The clause that worries me is clause fourteen." (it says which one, so no commas)
3. "Clause fourteen, that is the indemnity, is the one to discuss."
"Clause fourteen, which is the indemnity, is the one to discuss." (that never follows a comma)
4. "The clause which I mentioned it is on page nine."
"The clause which I mentioned is on page nine." (which is already the object)
The Real Thing

Where Does the Board Get Lost?

Email to the board
Re: Master agreement — moving the contract to Mexico

Dear all,

Following your request, we have reviewed the master agreement and the annex.

The force majeure clause does not help us here, and a novation is not possible before the end of the quarter.

The clause that governs the transfer, which was drafted by the counterparty, who is also the party that has to give consent, requires a written request that has to be sent thirty days before the date on which the transfer that the board approved would take effect.

Best regards,
A.

1. Which two words leave the board behind, and why?
"Force majeure" and "novation". They arrive alone, with no definition next to them, so a non-lawyer stops on the word and loses the sentence.
2. Rewrite one of them in one breath.
"A novation, which replaces one party with a new one, is not possible before the end of the quarter." The meaning travels with the word.
3. What do you do with the long sentence?
Cut it. Three relative clauses inside one sentence is one too many — two of them are extra information, so they become separate short sentences.
