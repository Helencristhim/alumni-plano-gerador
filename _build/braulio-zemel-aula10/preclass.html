Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

Talking About Time -- The Year After Everything Changed

How to tell the story of thirty-one years so that it stops being a list, and how to talk about the year after it ended. Key expressions: a legacy, a transition, to reflect on, a milestone, to reframe, to accumulate, a sacrifice, a chapter, to look back on, in hindsight, at a crossroads, bittersweet. Structure: past perfect and past perfect continuous -- putting one past behind another.

Vocabulary

A legacy -- what you leave behind after you stop
"The legacy is not the building. It is the people who learned there."
A transition -- the period between one way of living and the next
"It is not a day, it is a transition, and the middle of it is the long part."
To reflect on -- to look at something from the past slowly, and ask what it meant
"I had never had time to reflect on any of it until this year."
A milestone -- a point that marks how far you have come
"Thirty years was a milestone. Nobody at home noticed it."
To reframe -- to look at the same facts a second time and see something else
"I have started to reframe those years. The hours were real, and so was the rest."
To accumulate -- to build up slowly, year after year
"You do not notice it accumulating. You notice it when you stop."
A sacrifice -- something valuable you gave up in order to have something else
"Every Saturday was mine. The other six days were the sacrifice."
A chapter -- a distinct period of a life, with a beginning and an end
"That chapter closed in March, and I am still reading the first page of this one."
To look back on -- to think about the past from where you are standing now
"I look back on those years differently every six months."
In hindsight -- knowing now what you did not know at the time
"In hindsight, the empty middle was never the problem."
At a crossroads -- at a point where you have to choose a direction
"I am at a crossroads, and for once nobody is waiting for the answer."
Bittersweet -- happy and sad at the same time, and neither one cancels the other
"The last day was bittersweet, and I did not have a word for that until recently."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Told as a list, it takes eleven seconds. I worked at the same company, then I stopped, then I started travelling. Every word of that is true and nobody at the table leans forward, including the man saying it.

Told with one layer underneath, it is a different thing. By the time he finished, he had worked in that building for thirty-one years, under four presidents. He had been arriving home at ten at night for most of them, and he had been telling himself the whole time that it was temporary. Nobody had warned him about the Tuesdays. Same facts, and now there is a shape, because the listener can hear which event is the frame and which one is standing behind it.

The words help too, and the useful ones are not the sentimental ones. A transition is not a day, it is the whole period between one way of living and the next. A sacrifice names what the years cost without complaining about them. To reframe is to look at the same facts a second time and see something different, which is what happens to everybody in the first year. And bittersweet holds two things at once, which is more honest than either one alone.

One warning about the grammar. The past perfect is not a more serious past. It is a SECOND past, and it exists only to sit behind another one. I had retired last year is wrong, because there is nothing for it to stand behind; I retired last year is the sentence. Two of these in a two-minute story is plenty. In hindsight, what makes a story is not how many layers it has. It is that it has one at all.

1. "By the time he finished, he had worked there for thirty-one years." Which happened first?
A The thirty-one years -- they were already complete when he finished.
B The finishing, and then the thirty-one years.
C Both at the same moment.
2. Why is "I had retired last year" wrong, according to the text?
A Because retire is never used in the past perfect.
B Because there is only one past moment, so there is nothing for it to stand behind.
C Because last year is too recent.
3. What does the text say happens when the story is told as a list?

B Every word is true and nobody leans forward, including the person telling it.

4. What does bittersweet mean in this text?
A Holding two feelings at once, which is more honest than either one alone.
B Regretting a decision after it is too late.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- A Past Behind a Past

GRAMMAR

Portuguese has exactly this form and you already use it without thinking. What is new is the habit of reaching for it in English, and the trigger by the time, which does half the work for you.

FormWhat it doesExample
had + past participlePuts an event BEHIND another past event. The past simple is the frame; this is the background."By the time I finished, I had worked there for thirty-one years."
had been + verb-ingAn activity that went on and on, up to that past moment. It carries the weight of repetition."I had been arriving home at ten for years."
The triggersby the time, before, already, never ... before, and for with a past anchor."I had never had a free Tuesday before."
When NOT to use itOne past moment on its own takes the past simple. This tense exists to ORDER two of them.NOT "I had retired last year."
"I retired last year."
The economy of itTwo layers in a two-minute story is plenty. More than that and the listener loses which past is which.Depth, not decoration.

A practical note. The single most useful phrase here is by the time. Start a sentence with it and the rest of the grammar almost writes itself, because English will not let you continue in the simple past after it. If you only take one thing from this lesson, take that opening.
