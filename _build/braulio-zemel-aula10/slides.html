Chapter 3: The Code

By the Time I Stopped, I Had Already

The tense that turns a list into a story

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"By the time I finished, I had worked in that building for thirty-one years."

"I had been arriving home at ten for so long that nine felt like an afternoon."

"Nobody had told me that the first month would be the hardest."

"I had never had a free Tuesday before, and I did not know what to do with it."

Each sentence holds two past moments. One of them is the frame and the other is the background behind it. Which is which, and what tells you?

FormWhat it doesExample
had + past participlePuts an event BEHIND another past event. It is the background; the past simple is the frame."By the time I finished, I had worked there for thirty-one years."
had been + verb-ingAn activity that had been going on and on, up to that past moment. It carries the weight of repetition."I had been arriving home at ten for years."
The triggersby the time, before, already, never ... before, for and since with a past anchor."I had never had a free Tuesday before."
When you do NOT need itOne past moment on its own takes the past simple. The past perfect exists to ORDER two of them, not to make a sentence sound serious."I retired last year." (not: I had retired last year)
The economy of itTwo of these in a two-minute story is plenty. Any more and the listener loses which past is which.Depth, not decoration.

Portuguese has exactly this form and you already use it. What is new is the habit of reaching for it in English, and the trigger by the time, which does half the work for you.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"When I retired, I worked in that building for thirty-one years."
"By the time I retired, I had worked in that building for thirty-one years."
"I was arriving home at ten for so long that nine felt strange."
"I had been arriving home at ten for so long that nine felt strange."
"I had retired last year and I had travelled a lot since then."
"I retired last year and I have traveled a lot since then."

The third pair is the one to watch. The past perfect is not a more serious past — it is a SECOND past, and it only exists to sit behind another one. With a single past moment it is simply wrong, and it is the mistake of somebody who knows too much grammar rather than too little. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Comprehension

About Carol

1. How long had Carol been working before she stopped?
Twenty-four years.
2. Which part does she say was the hardest, and how long did it last?
The first six months afterwards -- harder than the last day at work.
3. What does she say nobody had warned her about?
The Tuesdays -- the ordinary empty days, not the last day, which is a party.
4. What changed for her after those six months?
She stopped trying to fill the time and started choosing what to put in it.
Listening

Two Minutes After Dinner

A short farewell speech. Sound first -- no text.

1. How old was he when he started, and what had he already been told?
Twenty-six, and two people had already told him the job would not last.
2. How many years, how many presidents, and how many offices?
Thirty-one years, four presidents, and nine offices without ever changing floor.
3. What had he been telling himself about the ten o'clock nights?
That it was temporary -- and it was not.
Listening 2

A List, or a Story

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does she say people almost always produce, and what is wrong with it?
A list. It is accurate and nobody listens to it, including the person telling it.
2. What is the one grammatical move that changes it?
Putting one thing behind another -- by the time I retired I had been working there for thirty years.
3. How many of these does she say you need, and why?
Two in a two-minute story is plenty -- what you are buying is depth, not decoration.
