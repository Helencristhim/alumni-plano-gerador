300 Pages a Week -- Faster, Smarter, Better

Your master’s program asks for three hundred pages a week. Key words: an abstract, a peer-reviewed article, to skim, to scan, a finding, a sample, a literature review, a hypothesis. Structure: comparatives and superlatives.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An abstract -- the short summary at the start of an academic article
"I always read the abstract first."
A peer-reviewed article -- an article that other experts read and approved before it was published
"My professor only accepts peer-reviewed articles."
To skim -- to read fast to get the general idea
"I skim the article before I read it."
To scan -- to read fast to find one specific thing, like a number or a name
"I scanned the table for the sample size."
A finding -- something that a study discovers
"The main finding is that trust is more important than price."
A sample -- the group of people that a study looks at
"The sample was five hundred online buyers."
A literature review -- the part of a study that explains what other researchers found
"The literature review is the longest part of my chapter."
A hypothesis -- an idea that a study tests to see if it is true
"Our hypothesis is that faster pages bring more sales."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Professor Hill says: do not read every article from the first page to the last. It is the slowest way. Start with the abstract: it is the shortest part, and often the most useful. Then skim the conclusion.

The methods are usually more difficult than the other parts, so read them last. And choose peer-reviewed articles: they are more reliable than blogs.

Anna, a PhD student, says that a translator is faster, but reading in English is better for your vocabulary. Now she reads faster than before.

1. Which part does Professor Hill say to read first?
A The methods.
B The abstract.
C The literature review.
2. Why does Anna not use a translator for everything now?
A Because it is slower.
B Because reading in English is better for her vocabulary.
C Because her professor said no.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A “The abstract is the most short part.”
B “The abstract is the shortest part.”
C “The abstract is more shorter than the rest.”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Use -er than or more than to compare two things. Use the -est or the most to talk about the top of a group. Good becomes better and the best; bad becomes worse and the worst.

JobFormExample
Short adjectives-er than · the -estfast → faster than → the fastest
Ending in -y-ier · the -iesteasy → easier → the easiest
Long adjectivesmore ... than · the most ...more difficult than → the most difficult
Irregulargood · badbetter / the best · worse / the worst
Two things / all thingsthan / theSkimming is faster than reading. The abstract is the shortest part.
The trapnever more + -erNot more faster or the most easy. Say faster, the easiest.

Never: more faster · the most easy · gooder · more difficult the abstract (no than)
