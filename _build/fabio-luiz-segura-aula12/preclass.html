Lesson 12 -- Pre-class

Reading the Industry

Forty pages a week, ten minutes a day, and the two words that carry the meaning. Key words: to skim through, to zoom in on, to point out, to lay out, to break down, to pick up on, to touch on, to come across, to keep up with, to sum up. Structure: phrasal verbs -- separable, inseparable and three-part, and where the object goes.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each verb and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To skim through -- to read fast, without stopping, only to see what is there
"I skim through the summary and the subheadings, and I am not trying to understand anything yet."
To zoom in on -- to stop on one small part and read or look at it properly
"When something snags, I zoom in on that one paragraph and read it twice."
To point out -- to make sure the reader notices a particular fact
"The release points out every number that helps and stays quiet about the one that does not."
To lay out -- to present a plan or an argument in an organized order
"A sector report lays out its whole argument on page two, and defends it for thirty more."
To break down -- to separate something complicated into the two or three parts it is made of
"Real-time settlement across eleven corridors is three claims wearing one coat. Break it down."
To pick up on -- to notice something that nobody said directly
"I picked up on the fact that the timeline disappeared between the second draft and the third."
To touch on -- to mention a subject briefly and then move on
"It touches on regulation in one sentence and spends nine pages on technology."
To come across -- to find something by chance, without looking for it
"Four or five times a year I come across something that changes what my team does that quarter."
To keep up with -- to stay informed about something that keeps changing
"Keeping up with an industry is a habit, not an event. Fifteen minutes, every Monday."
To sum up -- to give the main idea in a few words, at the end
"To sum up: skim, stop once, break it down, and do it on Monday."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Forty pages of sector report, press release and comment are published every week about cross-border payments alone, and nobody with a job reads forty pages. The people who are genuinely well informed do something else, and almost every sentence they use to describe it is built from a verb of two words.

Look at where the object sits. The report breaks the numbers down into three corridors. The report breaks them down into three corridors. I came across the article on Monday. She picked up on it before anybody else did. In the first two, something can sit between the two words. In the last two, nothing ever can. The verb decides, and there is no rule about meaning that predicts it.

There are three families. Separable verbs — break down, point out, lay out, sum up — take the object after the verb or between the two words, and it sounds natural either way. Inseparable verbs — come across, skim through, touch on — keep the two words glued together and the object always follows. And the three-part verbs — zoom in on, pick up on, keep up with — behave like the inseparable family: the object comes after the third word, always.

There is one place where the choice disappears, and it is the mistake a listener notices immediately. When the object is a pronoun — it, them, me — a separable verb is obliged to take it in the middle. Break it down, never break down it. Point it out, never point out it. Long objects are free to go either side; short pronouns are not.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Where the Object Goes

GRAMMAR

Learn the family of the verb, not a rule about meaning.

FamilyWhere the object goesExample
Separable: break down · point out · lay out · sum upafter the verb or between the two wordsIt points out the risk. · It points the risk out.
Separable + pronoun (it, them, me)always between the two wordsIt points it out. · Let me break them down.
Inseparable: come across · skim through · touch onalways after the whole verb, pronoun includedI came across the article. · I came across it.
Three-part: zoom in on · pick up on · keep up withalways after the third wordShe zoomed in on the number. · She zoomed in on it.
No objectsome of them simply close the sentenceTo sum up, the timeline is the real story.
Stressthe second word carries it; the pronoun hides in the middlepoint it OUT · break them DOWN · sum it UP

Never: break down it · point out them · I came the article across · she zoomed the number in on · keep up of the market
