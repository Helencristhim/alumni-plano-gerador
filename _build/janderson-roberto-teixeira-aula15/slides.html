Where You Start

Same Question, Different Temperature

Direct

"Where is the elevator?" -- correct, and a little cold

Polite

"Could you tell me where the elevator is?" -- tonight

The signal

The verb moves to the end, and do, does and did disappear

A direct question is not wrong. But with somebody you do not know — a receptionist, a client's assistant, a stranger at an airport — English adds a small opening in front of it. The question stays the same; the order of the words changes.

Chapter 3: The Code

Could You Tell Me Where It Is

A polite opening, and the verb walks to the end

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Could you tell me where the elevators are?"

"Do you know if they are expecting me?"

"I was wondering if I need to sign in."

"Where are the elevators?"

Three of these are polite and one is direct. Look at where the verb is in each one. What happens to it after could you tell me?

FormWhat it saysExample
opening + question word + subject + verbAfter the polite opening, the words go in the order of a normal sentence."Could you tell me where the elevators are?"
no do, does or didThe helper verb disappears, and the main verb keeps its -s or its past form."Do you know what time the office opens?"
"Do you know if the client called?"
if or whetherWhen the direct question has no question word (a yes/no question), use if."Is the flight on time?" → "Do you know if the flight is on time?"
I was wondering ifThe softest opening. It is not a question, so it ends with a full stop."I was wondering if you could help me."
The opening is the questionOnly the first part inverts: could you, do you. The second part does not."Do you know where she works?"

A polite question is a direct question with an opening in front of it. The opening asks; the rest of the sentence simply states.

Chapter 4: The Front Desk

The Front Desk

A client's office in Miami, at ten in the morning

Dialogue

Which Floor?

M
Good morning. How can I help you today?
J
Good morning. Could you tell me which floor the Prado family office is on?
M
Of course, it is on the twelfth floor. Do you have an appointment with them?
J
Yes, at ten. I was wondering if I need to sign in first.
M
You do. Just your name here, please, and this is your badge for the day.
J
Thank you. Do you know where the elevators are? I cannot see them.
M
Down the hall, on the left. The one on the right is out of order this morning.
J
Perfect. And could you tell me if there is somewhere to get a coffee?
M
There is a complimentary coffee bar on twelve. I will let them know you are here.
Comprehension

About Megan

1. Which floor does Megan say the office is on?
The twelfth floor.
2. What does Megan give him after he signs in?
A badge for the day.
3. Where does she say the elevators are?
Down the hall, on the left -- the one on the right is out of order.
4. What does she tell him about coffee?
There is a complimentary coffee bar on the twelfth floor.
Listening

The Experiment at the Front Desk

A short radio segment. Sound first -- no text.

1. According to the speaker, what is different about the second question?
The information is the same, but it gives the person a moment and shows you see a person, not a machine.
2. What happens to the verb in the second version?
It moves to the end: where the elevator is.
3. Why does she say people notice when you fix this mistake?
Because it is the most common mistake and it is very easy to hear.

The Connection in Miami

Scenario: Your connection in Miami is delayed and you do not know for how long. Go to the information desk and find out everything you need: the new time, the gate, and anything else that matters to you. Finish with a thank-you.

No keywords. The questions are yours.

The Habit

One Polite Question, Out Loud

Before the next class, take one real question from your day — where, when, how much, if — and say it out loud once a day with could you tell me or do you know in front of it. Verb at the end.

Every desk in every airport, hotel and office opens with this sentence. It costs one question a day.
