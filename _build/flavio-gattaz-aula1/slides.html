Lesson 1 · The Opening Question

The Two-Minute Version of You

Somebody across a table asks what you do. You have about two minutes, and the whole conversation depends on them.

Chapter 1: First Words

So, What Do You Do?

Four words, and everybody asks them. Nobody in the room knows what a construction company in São Paulo looks like, so the answer has to build the picture from nothing.

Answer it now, out loud, the way you would answer it at a dinner in Miami. Where did you run out of words?

Today’s Goal

Two Minutes, Three Moves

1. The Words

family-run business, managing partner, headcount, track record...

2. One Structure

the job you hold, and the work you are on right now

3. Your Answer

role, company, current projects — two minutes

You already have the grammar and the reading. What is missing is the frame that carries them: what you do in general and what you are doing at the moment — two different sentences, and English keeps them apart.

One Question

And What Are You Working On Right Now?

Answer it out loud, right now, in one sentence.

Then answer the second one, which is the one that keeps the conversation alive: and how is that different from a normal year for you?

Chapter 3: The Code

The Job You Hold, and the Work You Are On

Two present tenses, doing two different jobs

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I run a construction company in São Paulo."

"At the moment we are building four projects."

"We have twenty-five people on the payroll."

"This year I am applying to business schools."

Two of the red verbs carry an -ing and two do not. Look at the blue part: it only appears on one of the two sides, and that is the clue.

JobFormExample
True in generalI / we + verbI run a construction company.
Happening around nowam / is / are + verb-ingWe are building four projects.
Routinethe plain form, with usually / alwaysI usually go to the site on Tuesdays.
A temporary periodthe -ing form, with this year / at the momentThis year I am applying to business schools.
State verbshave, own, know, need: no -ingWe have twenty-five people.
QuestionDo you...? vs Are you...?What do you do? / What are you working on?
The trapnever in this momentNot in this moment we build. Say at the moment we are building.

In one line: the plain form for the job you hold, the -ing form for the work you are on right now.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Table, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one profile page

Dialogue

A Conference Table, Thursday Morning

K
Karen Whitfield, infrastructure. I do not think we have met. So, what do you do?
F
Flavio Gattaz. I run a construction company in São Paulo. It is a family-run business — my father founded it, and today I am a managing partner with my uncle.
K
Nice. How big is it?
F
Our headcount is around twenty-five, and it goes past a hundred when the subcontractors are on site. At the moment we are building four projects at the same time.
K
Four at once. So you are on site all day?
F
Less than people think. I have a background in civil engineering, but I oversee the projects now. Half my week is business development. In a company this size you wear several hats.
K
That is the part people underestimate. And what brings you here?
F
This year I am applying to executive programs abroad. Thirty years and forty buildings is a decent track record, but I want the management side to catch up. And you — what are you working on at the moment?
Comprehension

About Karen

1. How does Karen open the conversation, and what does she say about the two of them?
She gives her name and her sector, infrastructure, and says she does not think they have met.
2. What does Karen assume about a director with four projects, and how does she phrase it?
She assumes he is on site all day, and she asks it as a question: "So you are on site all day?"
3. Which part of the job does Karen say people underestimate, and what does she ask next?
Wearing several hats, the management side of a small company. She then asks what brings him to the event.
Listening 1 · A Director

The First Round of Introductions

A construction executive introducing himself on day one of a program. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say about the company he runs, and who founded it?
It is a family-run business in Denver with a headcount of about sixty. His grandfather founded it in 1974, and he is the third generation.
2. What is the difference he draws between what he trained in and what he actually does?
He has a background in civil engineering, but he says nobody has asked him to design anything in eleven years. His job today is people, money and clients.
3. Why is he in the room, in his own words?
Because he learned to run a company by doing it, and he wants to find out what he has been getting wrong.
Listening 2 · Admissions

What We Hear in the First Two Minutes

An admissions director on a podcast, saying what she is actually listening for. Sound first — no text.

1. What mistake do most candidates make in the first two minutes?
They recite the resume in order, from the degree to the current job, which is information she can read on the form.
2. What does she say a good opening actually contains?
What you run, how big it is in one number, and what you are working on right now that you did not have to take on.
3. What does she say about candidates whose English is not perfect?
That she does not care about perfect English. She cares whether the person can keep a conversation going, and a slow clear answer beats a fast unclear one.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Second and Third Generation

A business radio report opening a series on family firms. Sound first — no text.

1. What figure does the reporter give about family firms, and what does he say it hides?
That family firms make up around two thirds of all companies in the world. It hides how few of them get past the second generation.
2. According to the report, what is the problem that has nothing to do with money?
That the person who inherits the company usually inherits the technical job as well, and nobody teaches them the management part.
3. What does the reporter say about the owners who go back to study?
That they tend to go back late, in their forties, and they do it because they can already see the ceiling they are about to hit.

Flavio oversees a portfolio of residential projects in São Paulo and shares the direction of the company with a founding partner. His week is split between site work and business development. The firm has a track record of forty completed buildings across three decades, and he is the second generation to run it.
1. One line on this page uses the -ing form and the others do not. Which one, and why does it?
The "Currently" line: running four projects at once. It describes what is happening around now, while the other lines describe what is true in general.
2. The page gives two different numbers for the same company. What are they, and why are both there?
Twenty-five direct employees and up to one hundred and fifty with subcontractors. The first is the headcount on the payroll, the second is the number of people actually on site.
3. According to the paragraph, how is the week divided, and what is the track record?
Between site work and business development. The track record is forty completed buildings across three decades.
