Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

The Two-Minute Version of You -- Saying What You Do

Somebody across a table asks what you do, and the whole conversation depends on the next two minutes. Key words: a family-run business, a managing partner, to oversee, a construction site, a subcontractor, headcount, business development, to run a company, a track record, to wear several hats, hands-on, a background in. Structure: present simple and present continuous.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A family-run business -- a company that is owned and managed by members of the same family, often across generations
"It is a family-run business. My father founded it, and today I run it with my uncle."
A managing partner -- an owner who also runs the daily operation, instead of only holding a share of the company
"I am a managing partner, so I sign the contracts and I am on site on Mondays."
To oversee -- to be responsible for a piece of work and check that it is going well, without doing it yourself
"I oversee four projects. Three engineers report to me on each one."
A construction site -- the place where a building is going up, as opposed to the office
"I spend Tuesday mornings on the construction site and the rest of the week at the office."
A subcontractor -- an outside company hired to carry out one part of a larger job
"The subcontractors do the electrical work. We coordinate them, we do not employ them."
Headcount -- the number of people a company employs, given as a figure
"Our headcount is around twenty-five, and it doubles when the subcontractors are on site."
Business development -- the side of the job that finds and wins new work for the company
"Half my week is business development. The other half is running what we already sold."
To run a company -- to be the person who makes the daily decisions that keep a company working
"I have been running the company with my uncle since my father passed away."
A track record -- everything somebody has actually delivered so far, used as evidence of what they can do
"Our track record is forty buildings in São Paulo over three decades."
To wear several hats -- to do a number of different jobs inside the same organization
"In a company this size you wear several hats. I am finance on Monday and safety on Friday."
Hands-on -- involved in the practical daily work, not only in the decisions taken far from it
"I am a hands-on director. I would rather see the problem than read about it."
A background in -- the training and the experience somebody comes from, before their current job
"I have a background in civil engineering, but my job today is management."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A man sits down at a conference table in Miami and somebody asks him what he does. He runs a construction company in São Paulo. It is a family-run business: his father founded it, and today he is a managing partner alongside his uncle. That sentence is true this year, it was true last year, and it will still be true next year, so it takes the plain form of the verb.

Then the next question comes, and it is a different question: what are you working on? At the moment the company is building four projects at the same time, and this year he is preparing applications for an executive program abroad. Neither of those will be true forever. The headcount is twenty-five people, though, and companies have employees rather than are having them, because have here describes a state, not an activity.

He does not lay bricks. He oversees the work, and the subcontractors do the specialist jobs. Half his week is business development, which is why he says he wears several hats. He has a background in civil engineering, and he is hands-on enough to walk a construction site on a Tuesday, but his track record today is a company, not a drawing.

1. "He runs a construction company." Why the plain form and not "he is running"?
A Because run is a verb that never takes -ing.
B Because it is true in general, not only around now.
C Because the sentence is in the past.
2. Why is it "the company is building four projects" and not "the company builds four projects"?
A Because "at the moment" marks a temporary situation happening around now.
B Because there is more than one project.
C Because building is always continuous in English.
3. The text says companies "have" employees rather than "are having" them. Why?
A Because the number could change tomorrow.
B Because "have" is only used in questions.
C Because "have" here describes a state, and state verbs do not take the -ing form.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

Two present tenses, two jobs: the role you hold, and the work you are on right now.

JobFormExample
True in generalI / we + verbI run a construction company.
Happening around nowam / is / are + verb-ingWe are building four projects.
Routineplain form, with usually / alwaysI usually go to the site on Tuesdays.
A temporary period-ing form, with this year / at the momentThis year I am preparing my applications.
State verbshave, own, know, need: no -ingWe have twenty-five people.
QuestionDo you...? vs Are you...?What do you do? / What are you working on?
Negativedo not + verb / am not + verb-ingI do not design buildings any more.

Never: in this moment we build · we are having twenty-five employees · this year I prepare my applications · what you do in the company? · I am knowing the client.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Somebody at a conference asks what you do. You answer:
A "I am running a construction company since twenty years."
B "I run a construction company in Sao Paulo. It is a family-run business."
C "My work is the construction. I do the buildings."
You want to say the company has four projects open right now. You say:
A "In this moment we build four projects."
B "At the moment we are building four projects."
C "Now we are having four constructions."
You want to say you trained as an engineer but manage today. You say:
A "I have a background in civil engineering, but I oversee the projects now."
B "I am engineer of formation, but now no."
C "I was engineer. Now I am only the boss."
The other person has finished answering. You want the conversation to continue. You say:
A "Very interesting. Thank you."
B "Yes. My company is bigger."
C "And you, what are you working on at the moment?"
