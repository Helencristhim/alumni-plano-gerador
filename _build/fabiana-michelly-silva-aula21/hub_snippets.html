Lesson 21 -- Pre-class

Communicating Under Pressure -- Say Only What You Know

When leadership wants answers now and the facts are not in yet. Key words: holding statement, talking points, preliminary, unconfirmed, speculate, noncommittal, walk back, off the record, buy time, hold the line, composure, level with someone. Structure: distancing language -- it is understood that / is thought to be / is reported to have started / appears to have been.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Holding statement -- a short prepared message you give before all the facts are known
"Let me give you a holding statement now, and a fuller update at four."
Talking points -- the few key messages you plan to repeat in a difficult conversation
"Stick to your talking points: buffer, second supplier, next update."
Preliminary -- early, and not yet final
"These figures are preliminary; the inspection is still running."
Unconfirmed -- not yet checked or officially proven
"There are unconfirmed reports that two pallets were damaged."
Speculate -- to guess about something when you do not have the facts
"I would rather not speculate on the numbers until we have photos."
Noncommittal -- not showing a clear decision, opinion or promise
"They will say your answer sounds noncommittal, so give them a range."
Walk back -- to take back or soften something you said earlier
"Do not walk back what you said about the buffer."
Off the record -- said privately, not to be quoted or used officially
"If someone asks for a number off the record, treat it as on the record."
Buy time -- to delay a decision so that you can get more information
"Promising an update at four buys time without sounding evasive."
Hold the line -- to keep your position when people push you to change it
"When the committee pushes for a date, hold the line."
Composure -- the state of staying calm and in control under stress
"Your composure on the bridge call was noticed."
Level with someone -- to be completely honest and direct with someone
"I will level with you: I do not have a firm date yet."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At seven in the morning, Fabiana gets the call: a fire at the contract manufacturer's plant in Penang. By eight, leadership wants answers. She has half of them. So she opens the bridge call with a holding statement, and every sentence tells her listeners where each fact comes from. It is understood that nobody was hurt. The fire is reported to have started in the packaging area. The assembly line appears to have been untouched. The plant is thought to be closed for three days.

None of these sentences says "I know". None of them says "they told me" either. The grammar does the work: it + passive + that, or subject + passive + to. When the event is happening now, she uses to be. When it already happened, she uses to have + past participle: the fire is reported to have started, not to start.

Then comes the pressure. Someone in sales has heard that twenty pallets were destroyed. That figure is unconfirmed, and she would rather not speculate. The committee wants a date; she holds the line and buys time with an update at four. She does not walk back anything she said. And on the parts she controls, she drops the distancing completely: "We have eleven days of buffer. I'll level with you: I don't have a firm date yet." Careful about the facts, firm about the actions.

1. Why does Fabiana say "the fire is reported to have started" and not "the fire is reported to start"?
A Because "start" is an irregular verb.
B Because the fire started before the report: an earlier event takes "to have" + past participle.
C Because the sentence is in the future.
2. What do all the distancing sentences show?
A That the fact comes from someone else and she has not confirmed it herself.
B That she thinks the information is false.
C That she is refusing to answer the question.
3. Where does she stop using distancing language, and why?
A When she talks about the fire, because it is serious.
B When she talks about the pallets, because sales is sure.
C When she talks about what she controls, like the buffer, because those are her own commitments.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Distancing Language

GRAMMAR

Two shapes, one job: the fact comes from someone else, and the grammar says so.

StructureFormExample
It + passive + thatIt is understood / thought / believed / reported + that + full clauseIt is understood that nobody was hurt.
Subject + passive + to (now)is thought / said / expected / believed + to + base verbThe plant is thought to be closed.
Subject + passive + to have (before)is reported / believed + to have + past participleThe fire is reported to have started in packaging.
seem / appearappears / seems + to (have) · It would appear that + clauseThe line appears to have been untouched.
Negativeis not thought to / is not expected toThe line is not thought to be damaged.
QuestionIs it thought that...? · Is X expected to...?Is the plant expected to reopen on Monday?

Never: It is thought the plant to be closed · The fire is reported to start yesterday · It understands that · The line appears being untouched

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a senior professional would give.

The site manager told you the plant will probably be closed for three days. You tell leadership:
A "It is thought the plant to be closed for three days."
B "The plant is thought to be closed for three days."
C "The plant will be closed for three days, I promise."
You heard the fire started last night, but nobody has confirmed it. You say:
A "The fire is reported to have started last night."
B "The fire is reported to start last night."
C "The fire reports to have started last night."
The CFO insists on an exact recovery date that you do not have. You say:
A "Monday. Probably. Maybe Tuesday."
B "I don't know."
C "I'll level with you: I don't have a firm date yet. You will have an update at four."
A colleague asks for your estimate "off the record". The best answer is:
A "Happy to share it, but please treat it as on the record: it would appear that we have eleven days of buffer."
B "Off the record, we will definitely be fine by Monday."
C "It appears being fine, I think."
