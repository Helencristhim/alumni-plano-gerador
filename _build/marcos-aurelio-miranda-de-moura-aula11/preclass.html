Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

Numbers That Tell a Story -- A Lot of Data, Very Little Time

A board does not need many numbers. It needs a few numbers, compared to something, and one sentence about what they mean. Key words: a figure, a percentage, an average, a breakdown, a trend, a peak, a dip, roughly, to double, to allocate. Structure: quantifiers much, many, a lot of, a few and a little.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A figure -- a number, especially an official one in a report
"I do not have the exact figure for the south yet."
A percentage -- a part of a hundred, written with the sign %
"A percentage on its own is not a story."
An average -- the typical number: you add all of them and divide
"The average visit costs roughly three hundred reais."
A breakdown -- a total divided into its parts, so you can see each part
"Go to the breakdown and look for the small lines."
A trend -- the general direction of a number over many months
"Look at the trend over twelve months, never one month on its own."
A peak -- the highest point a number reaches before it goes down
"Sales reached a peak in March, before the price change."
A dip -- a small, short fall in a number that usually comes back
"There was a dip in July, and it was back to normal in August."
Roughly -- close to a number, but not exact
"Roughly twenty percent sounds more honest than nineteen point seven."
To double -- to become two times bigger
"A small line that doubled in one quarter is a story."
To allocate -- to decide how much money or time goes to each part
"We want to allocate a little more to the south next year."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every board pack has the same problem: a lot of numbers and very little time to read them. So before you talk about any number, ask one question: can you count it? Workshops, regions, visits and reports, yes. You ask how many workshops, and you say there are a few regions ahead of plan. Money, time, budget, data and information, no. You ask how much budget is left, and you say you need a little more time.

In a positive sentence, use a lot of for both: we have a lot of data, we visited a lot of workshops. In a negative sentence or a question, much and many sound more natural: we do not have much time, did we lose many distributors? And be careful with very few and very little: they mean almost none, and they sound like a problem. A little time is good news. Very little time is not.

Now the part that is not grammar. Nobody remembers twelve figures. Look at the breakdown, find the small line that doubled, and say what it means. Never show one month on its own, because it is only a peak or a dip; show the trend. Say roughly three hundred, not three hundred and twelve. Compare the average or the percentage to a target or to last year. And finish with what you want to allocate, and where.

1. Why is it "how much budget" but "how many workshops"?
A Because budget is a more important word than workshops in a board meeting.
B Because you can count workshops, and you cannot count budget.
C Because much is for questions and many is for answers.
2. What is the difference between "a little time" and "very little time"?
A A little time is some time, which is good news. Very little time is almost none.
B There is no difference; very only makes the sentence more formal for the board.
C Very little time is more time than a little time.
3. According to the text, why should you not show one month on its own?
A Because one month of figures takes a lot of time to explain in the meeting.
B Because the board only reads the total.
C Because it is only a peak or a dip, so you should show the trend.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Much, Many, A Lot Of, A Few, A Little

GRAMMAR

Many and a few for things you count. Much and a little for things you do not. A lot of for both.

JobYou can count itYou cannot count it
QuestionHow many workshops did we visit?How much budget is left?
Big quantity, positiveWe visited a lot of workshops.We have a lot of data.
Big quantity, negativeWe did not lose many distributors.We do not have much time.
Small, but someA few regions are ahead.We need a little more money.
Almost noneI read very few reports to the end.We have very little time.
No pluraldata, information, money, advice, budget (the money in it)

Never: a lot of datas · many informations · how many money · we have much budget (use a lot of) · a few time (use a little).

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Diane asks how much of the budget is spent. The exact figure is 69.4 percent. You say:
A "Sixty-nine point four percent of the total budget, according to the September report."
B "Roughly seventy percent, and we are in October, so that is on plan."
C "A lot of."
You want to say the team has too much information and not enough people. You say:
A "We have a lot of data and very few people to analyze it."
B "We have a lot of datas and very little people to analyze them in the market intelligence team."
C "We have many informations and few person."
The digital line doubled, but it is only nine percent of the total. You say:
A "Digital is now our biggest problem, and we have to cut it before the end of the year."
B "Digital doubled. Next question."
C "It doubled, but it started very small."
July was bad, but the twelve months look good. You say:
A "July was a dip, but the trend for the year is up."
B "July was a peak, so the year is finished and there is nothing more we can do about it."
C "We do not talk about July."
