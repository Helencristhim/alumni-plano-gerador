A board does not need many numbers. It needs a few numbers, compared to something, and one sentence about what they mean.

Chapter 3: The Code

How Much, or How Many?

The small word in front of the noun

Grammar Discovery

Can You Count It?

"We have a lot of data, but very little time to analyze it."

"How many workshops did we visit in the third quarter?"

"We do not have much budget left for the trade fair."

"A few regions are ahead of plan, and we need a little more money in the south."

What do the orange words have in common? Look at the noun after each one. Workshops and regions: can you count them? Data, time, budget, money: can you?

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Three Numbers and One Story

One call, three recordings, one breakdown

Dialogue

The Budget Call, Before the Board

D
I have five minutes, Marcos. How much of the marketing budget is already spent?
M
Roughly seventy percent, and we are in October, so that is on plan. There is not much left, but it is enough.
D
The workshop visits look expensive in your report. How many did you do?
M
A lot of them. About four hundred. The average visit costs roughly three hundred reais, which is high, because first visits are the expensive ones.
D
And the digital line? It looks like it doubled.
M
It did double, but it started very small. It went from a little to a little more. I will send you the exact breakdown on Thursday.
D
Fine. So what is the story? One sentence.
M
We have very few problems with money and a lot of problems with time. I would like to allocate a little more to the south, and nothing more to digital.
Comprehension

About Diane

1. How much time does Diane have, and what is her first question?
Five minutes. She wants to know how much of the marketing budget is already spent.
2. Which two lines in the report worry her?
The workshop visits, because they look expensive, and the digital line, because it looks like it doubled.
3. What does she ask for at the end, and how long should it be?
The story behind the numbers, in one sentence.
Listening 1 · A Finance Director

Forty Pages in Four Minutes

A finance director on how she reads a budget report. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she look for on page one?
One figure, the total, and how much of it is already spent.
2. In the breakdown, which lines is she looking for, and why not the big ones?
The small lines that doubled. The big lines never surprise anybody.
3. Why does she never look at one month on its own?
Because one month is a peak or a dip and it does not mean anything yet. She looks at the trend over twelve months.
Listening 2 · A Presentation Coach

Round It, Then Compare It

A coach on the most common mistake with data. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the most common mistake, according to him?
Showing too many numbers: twelve figures on one slide, and nobody remembers any of them.
2. Does he say you should have less data? What is the fix?
No. You should have a lot of data. The fix is to show a few numbers, round them, compare them to something, and say what they mean.
3. What does he say to do when there is not much good news?
Do not hide it in the middle. Put it first, in one sentence, and then show how much budget you need to fix it.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

Three Numbers People Remember

A short report on how people remember numbers. Sound first — no text.

1. What happened when the presenter showed more than five figures?
People remembered almost none of them.
2. What helped people remember three numbers a week later?
Each number was connected to a comparison, like double what we sold last year.
3. Did rounded numbers sound more honest or less honest, and why?
More honest. A rounded number sounded like somebody who understood the data, not somebody reading it from a screen.
