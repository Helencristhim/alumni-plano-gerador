The Text

Paragraphs One and Two

What a Hostile Room Is Actually Testing

A public hearing is not a conversation, and it is a mistake to prepare for one as though it were. The questioner holds the floor, the clock and the last word; the person answering holds none of the three.1 What is being tested is not knowledge.

Most preparation goes into a talking point, which is the least useful instrument in the room. A rehearsed sentence is audible as a rehearsed sentence, and if it does not fit the question it becomes a non-answer, which is the one failure a hearing never forgives.2

Adapted from a comment essay on public hearings · ~600 words · gaps 1 and 2
The Text

Paragraphs Three and Four

What a Hostile Room Is Actually Testing

The moves that work are unglamorous. To sidestep a question in the open, saying what you will not discuss and why, costs far less than pretending. To concede a point early buys the right to hold everything else. To reframe is to accept the subject and change the terms.3 To push back is to disagree on the record, and a room that came for a fight will sometimes respect that more than it respects agreement.

There are two reliable traps. The first is a straw man: answering a weaker version of the question than the one that was asked.4 The second is the unattached caveat, the qualification that eats the sentence it was hung on, until nobody in the room could state what was actually claimed.

Adapted from a comment essay on public hearings · ~600 words · gaps 3 and 4
The Text

Paragraphs Five and Six

What a Hostile Room Is Actually Testing

What separates the people who survive these rooms is preparation of a different kind. They decide in advance where a red line really sits — which numbers, which names — and then they give everything else away freely.5 They look for common ground before they need it, and they steer the discussion without appearing to.

The last skill is temperature. When an exchange heats up the instinct is to match it. The people who do this well rein in the argument rather than the person, slow down when everybody else speeds up, and take the heat out of the room without conceding the point.6 Playing devil’s advocate remains available, but once only, and never twice in the same hearing.

Adapted from a comment essay on public hearings · ~600 words · gaps 5 and 6

The writer ends on what the room EXPOSES, not on what it decides. That is the bridge into the grammar: an inversion does the same job in one sentence -- it puts the thing you want noticed where it cannot be skipped.

What You Both Concede

Four minutes, together

Collaborative task — four minutes. You are both appearing before the same committee next week, on opposite sides of a disputed tariff decision. Together, agree the two things you will both concede in public, so that neither of you is ambushed with the other’s concession. You have to agree on the wording of both.

Long Turn

Two Minutes, Uninterrupted

No notes. No apology at the start.

Long turn — two minutes, uninterrupted. You are before a committee that believes your sector has been over-rewarded for risk it never carried. Answer this: “What have you been paid for?” Name your three red lines out loud at the start, give everything else freely, and use at least two inverted conditionals.

Follow-up

Four Questions, No Restarting

Four examiner questions, straight after the two minutes. No restarting, and no preparation between them.
1You named three red lines. Which of the three would you give up first, and why that one?
2Refuse my next question, out loud, and tell me why you are refusing it.
3I am going to restate your argument in its weakest form. Correct my version without telling me I am wrong.
4One sentence: what would change your mind?
Debate One

Defend the Side You Disagree With

This house believes that in a hostile hearing, saying “I do not know” costs less than any prepared answer.
1Ninety seconds each, alternating. No notes.
2You choose your side, and you open with the strongest argument against yourself.
3At least one inverted conditional per turn. If it sounds forced, it was.
Debate Two

The Side You Were Given

This house believes that a witness who refuses to answer is more trustworthy than one who answers everything.
1The teacher gives you the side. You do not choose it.
2Before you answer, restate the strongest point the other side has made, in your own words, so that they accept your version of it.
3Only then do you respond. A turn that skips the concession does not count.
