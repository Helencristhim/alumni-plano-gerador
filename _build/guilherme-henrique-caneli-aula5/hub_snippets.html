Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To push back -- to disagree with or resist an idea, politely but firmly
"The fund pushed back on the idea that the risk was only political."
A talking point -- a prepared sentence that a speaker repeats to promote a position
"The minister stayed on his talking points for twenty minutes."
To sidestep a question -- to avoid answering a question directly
"He sidestepped the question about the auction date."
To rein in -- to control a speaker who is taking too much time or going off topic
"A good moderator reins in a speaker without embarrassing him."
To steer the discussion -- to guide a conversation in the direction you choose
"She steered the discussion back to the contracts."
A straw man -- a weak version of the other side’s argument, invented so it is easy to attack
"Nobody said foreign funds want zero risk; that is a straw man."
Common ground -- the points on which people who disagree still agree
"The only common ground was that the current model is not working."
A red line -- a limit that one side will not accept crossing
"International arbitration is the fund’s red line."
To concede a point -- to admit that the other person is right about something
"I will concede the point on 2014, but not on today."
A caveat -- a warning or condition added to a statement
"The forecast came with one caveat: no change in the exchange rate."
To reframe -- to present a problem in a different way so that it can be discussed productively
"Let me reframe the question: what would you sign this year?"
To play devil's advocate -- to argue against an idea, even one you may support, in order to test it
"Let me play devil's advocate for a moment."
A non-answer -- a reply that sounds like an answer but gives no real information
"We will look into it carefully is a non-answer."
To take the heat out of -- to make a tense situation or discussion calmer
"A short summary took the heat out of the exchange."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When a panel gets heated, the moderator has two jobs at once: keep the argument alive and keep it from becoming personal. The language that does both is surprisingly calm.

The first move is to restate the attack before answering it: “If I understand you correctly, what you are saying is that the contract, not the country, is the risk.” The second is to concede a point without giving up the argument: “That is a fair point, and yet the numbers tell a different story.” Neither sentence is weak. Both show the room that you heard the pushback and are not afraid of it.

The third move turns blame into a hypothesis, and English has a formal way to do it: the conditional without if. “Had the framework been clearer in 2019, we would have seen more capital” talks about a past that did not happen. “Were the agency to revise the model, the risk would shift” imagines the present or future. “Should the ministry accept international arbitration, the fund would move” describes a possible event.

In all three, if disappears and the auxiliary moves to the front. Never use both at once (“If had the rules been”), and never contract (“Hadn’t the rules been”). Used calmly, the structure does something useful: it moves a red line from an accusation to a proposal, and people can discuss a proposal without losing face.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Inverted Conditionals and Diplomatic Frames

GRAMMAR

Drop if, move the auxiliary to the front, and the hypothesis sounds considered instead of emotional.

JobFormExample
Past, did not happenHad + subject + past participleHad the rules been clearer, capital would have come.
A hypothesisWere + subject + to + verbWere the agency to revise the model, the risk would shift.
A possible futureShould + subject + base verbShould the ministry act, the pipeline would change.
NegativeHad + subject + not + participleHad the auction not been delayed, three funds would have bid.
Restate firstIf I understand you correctly, what you are saying is that... the contract is the risk.
Concede, then push backThat is a fair point, and yet... the numbers disagree.

Never: if had the rules been clearer · hadn’t the rules been clearer · were the agency revise the model · let me do the devil's advocate · a common point

Order

?"That is a strong claim, and I want to give it room."
?"If I understand you correctly, what you are saying is that the contract, not the country, is the risk."
?"I will concede the point on 2014."
?"Had those contracts been honored, we would be having a different conversation."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A panelist attacks your position. Before you answer, you say:
A "You are wrong about that."
B "If I understand you correctly, what you are saying is that the contract is the risk."
C "This is not true, sorry."
You want a calm hypothesis about something that did not happen. You say:
A "Had the rules been clearer, more capital would have come."
B "If had the rules been clearer, more capital came."
C "The rules were clearer, more capital would come."
A speaker has talked for five minutes. You say:
A "Stop, please, you talk too much."
B "OK, next."
C "I am going to stop you there so the room has your main point: the calendar is the risk."
You want to admit the other person is right about one thing. You say:
A "I will concede the point on 2014."
B "I concord with you in parts."
C "You have the reason on 2014."
