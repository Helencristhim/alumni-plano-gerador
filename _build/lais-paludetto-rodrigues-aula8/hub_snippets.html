Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. Do not translate — the example sentence is the meaning.

Hometown -- the town or city where you grew up
"My hometown is Sao Paulo, which is bigger than some countries."
Landmark -- a building or place that everybody in a city knows
"The landmark everybody photographs is not the one people actually use."
Crowded -- full of people, with very little space
"The bus at seven in the morning is the most crowded place I know."
Homemade -- made at home, not bought in a shop
"There is always a homemade cake and nobody remembers who made it."
A public holiday -- a day when schools and offices close for everybody
"A public holiday in Brazil is not the same in every city."
Heritage -- the traditions, food and history that a group passes on
"My grandmother's recipes are the only heritage I actually use."
Stereotype -- a fixed, simple idea about a group of people that is usually too simple
"The stereotype is beaches and football. Half of it is even true."
To describe -- to say what something is like
"Describe one street properly instead of describing a whole country badly."
To compare -- to say how two things are similar or different
"If you compare the two school years, mine ends when theirs starts."
Slang -- very informal words used by a particular group of people
"Half of what my friends say is slang that my mother does not understand."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every exchange student is asked the same question in the first week: so, what is Brazil like? It is a question which is impossible to answer, because Brazil is a country where a four-hour flight still leaves you inside it. So I stopped answering it and I describe one street instead.

The street where I grew up is in a neighbourhood which nobody outside the city has heard of. There is a bakery on the corner that opens at six in the morning, and the people who queue there at that hour are not tourists. It is the most crowded place I know, and my favourite landmark in my hometown is not a monument, it is that queue.

Two things surprise Canadians more than anything else. Our school year ends in December, which means my summer holiday is their winter. And a public holiday in Brazil is not the same everywhere, because a city can have holidays which belong only to that city.

The stereotype is beaches and football. But the thing that is actually typical is smaller: it is a homemade cake that appears whenever more than four people are in a house, and nobody remembers who made it. That is the heritage I would take with me.

Why does the writer say she stopped answering "what is Brazil like?"
A Because she does not like the question.
B Because the country is too big to summarise, so one specific street explains more.
C Because she does not know the answer.
In "the people who queue there", the word who replaces:
A The bakery.
B The people.
C The hour.
"Our school year ends in December, which means my summer holiday is their winter." Here which refers to:
A December.
B The school year only.
C The whole idea in the first half of the sentence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- One Word Joins Two Sentences

GRAMMAR

The relative word replaces a subject, so you never say the subject twice.

FormUseExample
whopeopleThe woman who runs the flower shop.
whichthings, and whole ideasA neighbourhood which nobody has heard of.
thatpeople or things, in the essential kind of sentenceThe bakery that opens at six.
whereplacesThe street where I grew up.
Commas or no commasNo commas: essential, it tells you WHICH one. With commas: extra information you could remove.
The disappearing wordWhen somebody else does the action, you can drop it: "the assignment (that) I submitted". That is what fluent speakers do.

The relative word replaces the subject. Never repeat it: "the bakery that it opens" is the one mistake to avoid.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Somebody has just asked you about Brazil. Choose the best thing to say.

"So what is Brazil like?" The best answer starts with:
A "Brazil is a very beautiful country with happy people."
B "It depends where you are. It's easier if I describe one place."
C "I don't know how to explain."
Which sentence is correct?
A "The bakery that it opens at six is on my corner."
B "The bakery that opens at six is on my corner."
C "The bakery what opens at six is on my corner."
You want to add extra, non-essential information about your sister. You say:
A "My sister, who studies English too, is younger."
B "My sister, which studies English too, is younger."
C "My sister who is she studies English too is younger."
Somebody repeats a stereotype about your country. The most useful reply is:
A "That's wrong."
B Say nothing.
C "That's the version people see on TV. Where I live it's actually..."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is correcting this one.

Imagine you have a table at the culture fair and five minutes to talk. Describe the street where you grew up: what is on the corner, who is there at seven in the morning, what your school looks like, what people eat on the way to class, and the one thing about your life at home that Canadians never believe. Use at least four sentences with who, which, that or where.
