Chapter 3: The Code

Always True, or Happening Now

Two present tenses, and the patient answers a different question with each

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Questions Share?

"Does it itch at night?"

"Is it itching right now?"

"The rash flares up every summer."

"It is spreading this week, faster than before."

Look at the blue words. Two of them name a habit that repeats. Two of them name this exact moment. The red verb follows.

JobFormExample
A habit, a patternpresent simpleIt itches every night.
This exact momentam / is / are + -ingIt is itching right now.
A temporary changeam / is / are + -ingIt is spreading this week.
A question about the habitdo / does + plain verbDoes it hurt when you walk?
A question about nowam / is / are + -ingAre you taking anything for it?
Time words that take the simpleevery day, at night, usually, alwaysShe uses the cream every day.
Verbs that stay simplehave, feel, want, look (= appear)It looks red today. Not it is looking red.

In one line: ask about the pattern with the simple, and about this moment with -ing.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Consultation, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one intake form

Dialogue

Room Four, Tuesday Morning

D
Good morning, Mrs. Parker. I am Doctor Wached. What brings you in today?
P
I have this rash on my hands. It started about three weeks ago, on the back of the right hand, and now it is on both.
D
So it started on one hand and it is spreading. Does it itch?
P
It is very itchy at night. During the day I hardly notice it, and then I wake up at two in the morning scratching.
D
Is it itching right now, while we are talking?
P
No, not at this moment. It is tender if I press it, though. I tried an over-the-counter cream for ten days and it did nothing.
D
Thank you. Does anything like this run in the family?
P
My mother had eczema all her life. Hers used to flare up in winter and then settle down again. Is that what I have?
Comprehension

About Mrs. Parker

1. How long has Mrs. Parker had the rash, and where did it start?
About three weeks. It started on the back of her right hand and it is now on both hands.
2. What has she already tried, and did it work?
An over-the-counter cream, for ten days. It did nothing.
3. What does she say about her family?
Her mother had eczema all her life, and it used to flare up in winter and then settle down again.
Listening 1 · A Dermatologist

The First Ninety Seconds

A dermatologist talking to first-year residents. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say happens when the doctor interrupts too early?
The patient stops telling the story and starts answering questions instead, and the real complaint stays unsaid.
2. What are the two questions she asks before she looks at the skin?
What brings you in today, and what worries you most about it.
3. What does she say about the word itchy?
That patients use it for very different things, so she always asks whether it itches at night and whether it is itching at that moment.
Listening 2 · A Patient

The Same Visit, From the Chair

A patient describing why she finally made the appointment. Sound first — no text.

1. Why did she wait a month before calling the clinic?
Because it came and went, and every time it settled down she told herself it was nothing.
2. What finally made her make the appointment?
Her daughter saw her scratching in her sleep and booked the appointment for her.
3. What does she say she was most afraid of hearing?
That it was something serious, and that she had waited too long to come in.

1. Two lines on this form use different tenses in the note below. Which line becomes a present simple, and which becomes a present continuous?
Usual pattern becomes a present simple, because it is a habit that repeats. Current symptoms becomes a present continuous, because it describes what is happening now.

An over-the-counter cream, for ten days.
3. The form says skin conditions run in the family. What is the evidence it gives?
Her mother had eczema.
