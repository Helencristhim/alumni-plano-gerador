Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

What Brings You In Today? -- Opening the Consultation

A patient sits down and you have ninety seconds to open the consultation, hear the real complaint and ask the first question that matters. Key words: a chief complaint, a rash, itchy, to flare up, tender, over-the-counter, to run in the family, a follow-up, a referral, to settle down, a medical history, to come and go. Structure: present simple and present continuous.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A chief complaint -- the one main reason the patient came to see you today
"Her chief complaint is a rash on both hands."
A rash -- an area of red, marked skin that appears on the body
"The rash started on his neck and moved down."
Itchy -- making you want to scratch your skin
"It is itchy at night, and that is when she scratches it."
To flare up -- to suddenly become much worse for a period of time
"It flares up every summer, and then it settles again."
Tender -- painful when you press it with your finger
"Tell me if this area is tender when I press here."
Over-the-counter -- medicine you can buy without a prescription
"She tried an over-the-counter cream for two weeks."
To run in the family -- to be common among the members of one family
"Psoriasis runs in her family, on her mother’s side."
A follow-up -- a later visit to check how the treatment is working
"I would like to see you again at a follow-up in six weeks."
A referral -- when one doctor sends a patient to another doctor
"Her family doctor made the referral to dermatology."
To settle down -- to get better slowly, without disappearing completely
"The redness settles down a few days after the cream."
A medical history -- the list of illnesses and treatments a patient has had before
"Let me take your medical history before I examine you."
To come and go -- to become worse and then better again, again and again
"The patches come and go, but they never disappear for long."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Mrs. Parker sits down in room four. Her chief complaint is a rash on both hands. It started three weeks ago on one hand, and this week it is spreading to the other. The doctor asks two questions that sound similar and are not: does it itch at night? and is it itching right now? The first one asks about the pattern. The second one asks about this exact moment.

She answers that it is very itchy at night and that it is tender when she presses it. She tried an over-the-counter cream for ten days and it did nothing. Nobody in the clinic is treating her at the moment, so she is not taking anything for it today.

The doctor takes her medical history. Skin problems run in the family: her mother had eczema, and it used to flare up in winter and then settle down again. The rash comes and goes, which is why she waited so long. Before she leaves, the doctor books a follow-up in six weeks and writes a referral in case the treatment does not work.

1. "It is spreading to the other hand this week." Why not "It spreads"?
A Because spreading is a slow action and slow actions always take -ing.
B Because it describes a temporary change happening now, not a habit that repeats.
C Because the rash is on two hands, and two hands take the plural form.
2. "Does it itch at night?" and "Is it itching right now?" What is the difference?
A The first asks about the usual pattern. The second asks about this exact moment.
B The first is polite and the second is direct, but they mean the same thing.
C The first is for a doctor and the second is for a nurse.
3. "Skin problems run in the family." Why is this not "are running in the family"?
A Because run is an irregular verb and irregular verbs never take -ing.
B Because the family is plural and plurals take the simple form.
C Because it is a permanent fact about the family, not something happening this week.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

One decision, made by the time expression: is this the pattern, or is this happening right now?

JobFormExample
A habit, a patternpresent simple (+ s in he/she/it)It itches every night.
This exact momentam / is / are + -ingIt is itching right now.
A temporary changeam / is / are + -ingIt is spreading this week.
Question about the habitdo / does + plain verbDoes it hurt when you walk?
Question about nowam / is / are + -ingAre you taking anything for it?
Negative, habitdo not / does not + plain verbThe cream does not help.
Time words that take the simpleevery day, at night, usually, always, neverShe uses the cream every day.
Verbs that stay simplehave, feel, want, need, look (= appear)It looks red today.

Never: the rash flare up every summer · it is itching every night for three weeks · the skin is looking drier today · it itches when you press it? · she is having a rash on both hands.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A new patient sits down in front of you in an American clinic. You open with:
A "What is your problem?"
B "Good morning. I am Doctor Wached. What brings you in today?"
C "Tell me your disease, please."
You want to know whether the itching is happening at this exact moment. You ask:
A "Does it itch right now?"
B "Is it itching right now?"
C "It is itching now, no?"
The patient has finished a long, disorganised story. Before your next question, you say:
A "So, if I understood correctly, it started three weeks ago and it comes and goes."
B "That was very long. Please be shorter next time."
C "Sorry, my English is not very good."
You want to know what the patient has bought at the pharmacy without a prescription. You ask:
A "Did you buy some medicine of the free type?"
B "Have you tried any over-the-counter cream for it?"
C "Are you using the medicine of the pharmacy?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
