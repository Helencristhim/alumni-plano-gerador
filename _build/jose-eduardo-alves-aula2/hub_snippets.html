My Professional World -- Saying What Other People Do

One week inside a firm, told about somebody else. Key words: to handle, to specialize in, a case, a deal, an associate, a fee, a commodity, to review, a schedule, to sign. Structure: present simple in the third person — the -s, and the does that takes it away.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To handle -- to be responsible for a client or a case, from start to end
"Rafael handles the smaller deals on his own."
To specialize in -- to work in one area of the law only, and to become known for it
"She specializes in commodity contracts, and in nothing else."
A case -- one client problem that the firm works on, from the first call to the end
"He sends me the difficult cases and keeps the simple ones."
A deal -- an agreement between two companies to buy or sell something
"The deal is the agreement. The contract is the paper that holds it."
An associate -- a lawyer who works for the firm but does not own part of it
"An associate prepares the contract. A partner answers for it."
A fee -- the money a client pays the firm for its work
"The client who understands the schedule of fees never argues about the invoice."
A commodity -- a product like corn, sugar or oil that companies buy and sell in very large amounts
"In a commodity market nobody asks what the product looks like."
To review -- to read a document carefully before anybody signs it
"She reviews the contracts her associates prepared the day before."
A schedule -- the list of what happens, and at what time
"He explains the schedule of fees to every new client."
To sign -- to write your name on a document to make it official
"The firm does not sign anything for the client. The client signs."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In the last lesson every sentence was about you. This one is about somebody else, and English marks that with a single letter. Marina specializes in commodity contracts. Her associate handles the smaller deals. She reviews every difficult clause before anybody signs it. Look at the verbs. With he, she or it, the verb takes an -s, and only with those three.

Now the part that costs points. To say no, the -s leaves the verb and goes to does: "She does not go to court", never she does not goes. The same happens in a question: "Does he review every case?", never does he reviews. There is only one -s per sentence, and does takes it whenever does turns up.

Two verbs are irregular and you will use both every day: have becomes has, and do becomes does. "The firm has seven lawyers." Spelling has small rules too: verbs ending in -ch, -sh, -s or -x take -es (watch becomes watches), and a consonant before -y becomes -ies (study becomes studies). Finally, words of frequency go before the verb: "Her schedule rarely changes", "He always reviews the fee first."

1. "She reviews every difficult clause." Why does the verb carry an -s?
A Because the sentence is about the past.
B Because the subject is she, and he, she and it take the -s.
C Because review is an irregular verb.
2. Why is it "she does not go to court" and not "she does not goes to court"?
A Because does already carries the -s, and there is only one per sentence.
B Because go is irregular and never takes anything.
C Because the sentence is negative, and negatives have no verb.
3. Where do words like rarely and always go?
A At the end of the sentence, always.
B Before the verb: her schedule rarely changes.
C Between the verb and its object: she reviews rarely the clause.
