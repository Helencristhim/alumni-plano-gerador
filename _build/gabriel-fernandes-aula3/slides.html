Chapter 3: The Long Way Round

Four Questions, Two Word Orders

The structure English keeps for the formal room

Grammar Discovery

Which Two Would You Say to a Stranger?

"Where did you study?"

"Could you tell me where you studied?"

"Why did you choose that university?"

"I would like to know why you chose that university."

In the orange ones the verb jumps in front of the subject and did appears. In the red ones the question is buried inside a longer sentence, so it goes back to ordinary order and did disappears into the main verb. Longer, slower, and the one you will use in that room.

JobFormExample
Opening politelyCould you tell me + questionCould you tell me where the office is?
Softening it furtherI was wondering + questionI was wondering how many people you interview.
Turning it into a statementI would like to know + questionI would like to know why you chose it.
The word ordersubject before verb, always...where you studied, not where did you study.
The auxiliarydo / does / did disappearsWhy did you choose it? → why you chose it.
Yes or no questionsif or whetherDo you volunteer? → ...whether you volunteer.
The question markonly if the outer sentence asksCould you tell me...? but I would like to know....
The trapnever keep the question orderNot Could you tell me where do you study.

In one line: the polite opening stays a question, and everything after it goes back to being a statement.

Comprehension

About Claire

1. What did she say about his first question?
That it was a better first question than most people ask her, because he asked whether she had studied there herself.
2. What does she say about two years compared with two weeks?
That being a volunteer for two years counts far more than being one for two weeks in the year you apply.
3. What does she write down afterwards, and which of the two does she prefer?
Whether the person was rehearsed or thinking. She would rather read that he hesitated and then said something real than read a perfect paragraph.
Listening 1 · An Interviewer

It Is Not Confidence

Somebody who has interviewed candidates for years. Sound first — no text.

1. What do people expect her to say, and what does she say instead?
They expect her to say confidence. She says the best conversations were with candidates who hesitated, because hesitating usually means somebody is actually thinking.
2. What is the difference between a rehearsed candidate and a genuine one?
A rehearsed one gives the paragraph they prepared and then stops. A genuine one gives two sentences and keeps going, because the thing is real and there is more of it.
3. What does she write down when somebody has no questions at the end?
She writes it down, and she says she does not write it down as a good thing.
Listening 2 · Somebody Who Sat There

I Remember About Four of Them

A student, a year after his interview. Sound first — no text.

1. What had he prepared, and what did he actually talk about?
He had prepared an answer about a science prize and never used it. He talked about the food bank on Sunday mornings, for far longer than he meant to.
2. What did his mother ask him afterwards, and what did he do about it?
She asked whether he had asked the interviewer anything. He had not, so he sent an email that night asking how she had chosen her own subject.
3. What does he say he does not know, and what does he know?
He does not know whether the email changed anything. He knows it cost him ten minutes and that he would do it again.
Chapter 5: The Invitation

One Real Email, Four Real Questions

The message that arrives, and what you write back

Real Document

The Email That Arrives

Office of Undergraduate Admissions — Interview Invitation
To: Gabriel Reis Medeiros Fernandes · Campinas, Brazil · International applicant

Dear Gabriel,

Your application has been read and you have been shortlisted for an interview. The conversation lasts about thirty minutes and is not a test of knowledge.

Please reply with two times that would suit you in the next fortnight. You will be contacted by an alumna of the university who lives in your region.

You will be asked about what you do outside school and about one thing you have kept doing for a long time. You will also be given time at the end to ask questions of your own, and we encourage you to prepare some.

Kind regards,
Claire Adeyemi · Alumni Interview Coordinator

Read it twice. One thing you need to know is not in this email, and finding it is the exercise.
1. What does the email not tell you? Ask for it politely.
It does not say whether the interview is online or in person. "Could you tell me whether the interview will be online or in person?"
2. Turn "Who is going to interview me?" into a question you could actually send.
"I was wondering who is going to interview me." Or: "Could you tell me who will be interviewing me?" The buried question keeps ordinary word order.
3. Which sentence in the email is written in the structure you learned last week, and why?
"Your application has been read", "you have been shortlisted", "you will be asked", "you will be given". The passive, because the procedure matters more than which member of staff did it.

Go Deeper · Optional

Two More Ways to Ask Without Asking

You have the buried question. English has two more moves, and one of them was in the email.

The one that asks for permission. "Would you mind telling me how long you have been interviewing?" Notice the verb after mind: it takes -ing, never to. And notice the trap in the answer, because no means yes here.

The one that checks. "You studied here yourself, didn't you?" The tag flips the main verb: positive sentence, negative tag. You are not really asking, you are inviting the other person to confirm.

Your turn. Take one question you would ask Claire and say it three ways: buried, with would you mind, and as a tag. Then tell me which of the three you would actually use, and why.
