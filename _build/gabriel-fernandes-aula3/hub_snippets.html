Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An interviewer -- the person who asks the questions and writes the report afterwards
“The interviewer is usually somebody who studied there years ago.”
To shortlist -- to cut a long list down to the few people who will be interviewed
“Two thousand files are read, and about ninety are shortlisted.”
Community service -- unpaid work done for people outside your own family
“Community service is counted, and most applicants report almost none.”
A volunteer -- somebody who does work without being paid for it
“He has been a volunteer at the same place every Sunday for two years.”
A commitment -- something you have promised to keep doing, week after week
“Selling cakes every two weeks for a year is a commitment, not a hobby.”
To elaborate -- to say more about something you have just said
“She asked me to elaborate, which meant my first answer had been too short.”
To come across -- to give people a particular impression of yourself
“He knows the subject well, but he comes across as bored.”
Confident -- sure of yourself, without sounding above anybody else
“Confident is not loud. The quietest candidate that day was the most confident.”
To follow up -- to write or ask again after a first contact
“He followed up the next morning with four lines, and she remembered him.”
To hesitate -- to stop for a moment because you are not sure yet
“He hesitated before answering, and that was the best thing he did all morning.”
Rehearsed -- sounding memorised instead of real
“The answer was perfect and completely rehearsed, and she stopped listening.”
Genuine -- real, and not performed for the occasion
“A genuine answer usually has one detail nobody would invent.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of asking the same thing, and the second one is longer on purpose. Where did you study? is the short one, and it is perfectly correct with a friend. Could you tell me where you studied? is the long one, and it is what people use in a room where they do not yet know each other. The extra words are not decoration. They are the distance the language puts between two strangers.

What happens inside is the part worth learning. When a question is buried inside a longer sentence, it stops behaving like a question. The subject goes back in front of the verb, so where did you study becomes where you studied. The little helper verb, do or does or did, disappears completely, and the tense it was carrying moves into the main verb. Why did you choose it becomes why you chose it, with the past now living in chose.

Some questions have no question word at all. Do you volunteer? starts with the helper verb and nothing else. When a question like that is buried, English lends it a word to hold the place: if or whether. Could I ask whether you volunteer? The two are interchangeable in speech, and whether is the one that survives in writing.

The last thing is punctuation, and almost everybody gets it wrong once. The question mark belongs to the OUTER sentence, not to the buried one. Could you tell me where the office is? ends in a question mark because could you tell me is itself a question. I would like to know where the office is. ends in a full stop, because nobody asked anything: that sentence is a statement about what you would like. Read your own sentence back, find the first three words, and let them decide the mark.

1. According to the text, what are the extra words in the long form for?
A They make the question easier to understand.
B They are the distance the language puts between two strangers.
C They show that the speaker is not sure of the answer.
2. What does the text say happens to the tense when did disappears?
A It is lost, and the sentence becomes present.
B It moves into the main verb, so choose becomes chose.
C It moves to the beginning of the sentence.
3. What decides whether the sentence ends in a question mark?
A The buried question, because that is the real question.
B Whether the speaker expects an answer.
C The outer sentence, and the text says to look at its first three words.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions

GRAMMAR

The polite opening stays a question. Everything after it goes back to being a statement.

JobFormExample
Opening politelyCould you tell me + questionCould you tell me where the office is?
Softening it furtherI was wondering + questionI was wondering how many people you interview.
Turning it into a statementI would like to know + questionI would like to know why you chose it.
The word ordersubject before verb, always...where you studied, not where did you study.
The auxiliarydo / does / did disappearsWhy did you choose it? → why you chose it.
No question wordif or whether holds the placeDo you volunteer? → ...whether you volunteer.
The question markbelongs to the OUTER sentenceCould you tell me...? but I would like to know....
The trapnever keep the question orderNot Could you tell me where do you study.

Never: could you tell me where do you live · I would like to know why did you choose it · could I ask do you volunteer · I wonder what time is it?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give. Read all three before you decide.

1. You have never met this person and you want to know where they studied. You say:
A Could you tell me where did you study?
B Could you tell me where you studied?
C Could you tell me where you did study?
2. There is no question word, because the answer is yes or no. You say:
A Could I ask do you interview everybody?
B Could I ask whether you interview everybody?
C Could I ask that you interview everybody?
3. You are writing it down, and the sentence starts with I would like to know. It ends:
A ...why you chose it. With a full stop, because the sentence is a statement.
B ...why you chose it? With a question mark, because it is really a question.
C ...why did you choose it? With both the helper verb and the mark.
4. She gave you a two-word answer and you want more. You say:
A Could you tell me more that you mean?
B Would you mind elaborating on that?
C Would you mind to elaborate on that?

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

The interview is over and you are asked whether you would like to ask anything. Ask five questions out loud. Three of them have to start with a question word, and two of them have to use whether. Before you record, say each one twice and keep the version that sounded right.
