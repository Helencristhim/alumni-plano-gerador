Lesson 08 -- Pre-class

Numbers on a Page -- The Commercial Proposal

The proposal is written and the price is defensible. What is left is the part nobody rehearses: saying the numbers out loud, in English, without hesitating on the one that matters. Key words: a quote, a licence fee, a discount, a term, upfront, to break down, a margin, a benchmark, to waive, a unit price. Structure: comparatives, superlatives and quantifiers.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A quote -- a written price for one specific piece of work
"The quote is valid for thirty days, and after that we redo the numbers."
A licence fee -- the money paid regularly for the right to use software
"The licence fee is annual. The setup is paid once."
A discount -- a reduction in the price
"The discount is not free. It buys us a longer term."
A term -- the length of time an agreement lasts
"A three-year term costs about a third less per year than one year at a time."
Upfront -- paid at the beginning, before the work starts
"Nothing is paid upfront. The first payment is at the first milestone."
To break down -- to show what each part of a total costs
"Let me break it down: licence, setup, and the two days of training."
A margin -- what is left after costs, as a share of the price
"The margin on the licence is good. The margin on the setup is not."
A benchmark -- a number used to compare against
"Their benchmark is what they pay today, not what our competitor charges."
To waive -- to agree not to charge something that is normally charged
"We can waive the setup fee, but not on a one-year term."
A unit price -- the price of one item, before quantity is applied
"The unit price falls above fifty users, and that is where the conversation is."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A price on its own is just a big number. It only means something when it is standing next to another one, which is why a quote is never presented as a figure but as a comparison: cheaper than what, up to how many, about a third less than which alternative.

Take a real page. The licence fee is twenty-eight thousand a year for up to fifty users. The setup is fifty-six thousand, paid once, which means it costs twice as much as a full year of licence — and that is the line every customer attacks, because it is the only number on the page bigger than the annual fee. Option A, one year at a time, is the cheapest to leave and the most expensive to stay in. Option B, a three-year term with the setup waived, is lower in year one and about a third less per year across the three.

Notice the shape of those words. Short adjectives take -er and the -est: cheaper, lower, the cheapest. Long ones take more and the most: more expensive, the most complete. Never both at the same time, which is why more cheaper does not exist. And the word after a comparison is always than, never that — the two are the same word in Portuguese and different in English.

Two habits close the gap. First, break down the total: most of what looks expensive turns out to be three things, and only one of them is being argued about. Second, choose the right benchmark. The customer's benchmark is almost never your competitor. It is what they pay today, people included — and a discount given too quickly says the first price was not real, which puts every other number in the proposal back on the table.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives, Superlatives and Quantifiers

GRAMMAR

A price alone means nothing. Always say cheaper than what, and up to how many.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to compare your annual fee with the competitor's. You say:
A "Our fee is more cheaper than theirs."
B "Our licence fee is lower than theirs, but the setup costs more."
C "Our fee is very cheap, the best in the market."
The customer says the total is too high. Your first move is:
A "Let me break it down: licence, setup, and training. Which of the three are we talking about?"
B "I can give you fifteen per cent."
C "It is the market price, everybody charges this."
You want to say the setup is two times the annual licence. You say:
A "The setup costs twice more than the licence."
B "The setup costs twice as much as one year of licence."
C "The setup is the double of the licence."
You are offering to remove the setup fee, but only for a longer commitment. You say:
A "I will take the setup out, no problem."
B "We can waive the setup, but only on a three-year term."
C "The setup is free if you want."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Take a real proposal of yours. In one minute, out loud: break the price down into its parts, compare the two options, say what each one is cheaper or dearer than, and recommend one. Every price has to arrive with a comparison beside it, and no number may be skipped.
