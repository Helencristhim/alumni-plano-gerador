Lesson 09 — Pre-class

The Lunch

Eight words for a lunch table (small talk, colleague, have in common, catch up, get in touch, keep in touch, brief, reach out) and the two modals that make you sound like a peer: should for advice, might for maybe. Around 30 minutes, in one sitting.

Stage 1.1: Words for a Lunch Table

Vocabulary

Tap Listen, then say each word out loud with your own numbers. Eight words, three minutes.

Small talk — the light conversation before the real one starts
"Small talk is not a waste of time."
Colleague — someone you work with, at your level
"A colleague of mine had the same problem."
Have in common — to share an interest or an experience
"We had more in common than I expected."
Catch up — to talk after a long time and hear the news
"Let us catch up before you fly back."
Get in touch — to contact someone, often for the first time
"Get in touch when you are in São Paulo."
Keep in touch — to stay in contact over months and years
"We said we would keep in touch, and we did."
Brief — short, and not longer than it needs to be
"Keep the email brief and they will answer."
Reach out — to contact someone first, without waiting
"Nobody reached out, so I did."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the text. Then answer the questions.

For three days I avoided the big table, because small talk in English is harder for me than the sessions. On Thursday I sat down anyway. The woman next to me had run the same kind of migration we had, and it had gone badly for her too, so within five minutes we had more in common than I expected. She said I should talk to a colleague of hers who had solved it properly, and that she might be able to introduce us after lunch. She did. Before we left, I did the one thing the programme director had told us to do: I was specific. I did not say let us keep in touch. I said I would send her our post-mortem on Thursday, and I asked for her email. I reached out that same week, kept the message brief, and she answered in an hour. We still catch up twice a year.
Why had he avoided the big table for three days?
A Small talk in English is harder for him than the sessions.
B He did not like the food.
Why is it "she might be able to introduce us" and not "she will introduce us"?
A Because she was not certain — might is maybe.
B Because it was in the future.
What did he do instead of saying "let us keep in touch"?
A He asked for her card.
B He said he would send the post-mortem on Thursday, and asked for her email.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Two words that make you sound like a peer

Should = I recommend it. Might = maybe. Both take the bare verb — no to.

should — advice

You should talk to Marta.
You should sit somewhere new.
We should keep in touch.

might — maybe

It might be worth asking.
She might know someone.
This might sound simple, but…

× "You should to talk to her" — bare verb: should talk
× "It might be worth to ask" — after worth the verb takes -ing: worth asking
× "I might will send it" — one modal at a time: I might send it

Might is also the safest way to disagree with somebody senior: "You might be right — in our market, though, …"

Stage 4: In the room

Quiz
Which sentence is correct?
A "You should to sit somewhere different."
B "You should sit somewhere different."
You want to suggest something without claiming you are right.
A "You must ask her."
B "It might be worth asking her."
Which goodbye still exists next month?
A "Let us keep in touch. Here is my card."
B "I’ll send you that article on Thursday — what is your email?"

Stage 5: Two minutes at a table

Reflection
Record two minutes in English. Imagine you have just sat down at a table of people you have never met. Do three things: open the conversation, find one thing you have in common with somebody, and close so that something actually happens next — something specific, with a day. Use should once and might once. Do not say "let us keep in touch".
