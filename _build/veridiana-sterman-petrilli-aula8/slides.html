Both rates are non-refundable. This week books up fast in October: the same search two days ago showed nine rooms at the inland hotel and four at the ocean one.
1. On how many of these lines does the inland hotel win, and on how many does the ocean hotel win?
The inland hotel wins on rate, room size, noise, shuttle and availability. The ocean hotel wins only on distance to the beach.
2. Which line makes the fifteen-minute walk matter much less, and until what time?
The free shuttle, every thirty minutes until eleven at night.
3. According to the note at the bottom, what is happening to availability, and what does that mean for the decision?
Both hotels are losing rooms fast, so the choice cannot wait. Two days ago there were nine and four; now there are six and two.
Chapter 4: The Code

Cheaper, More Convenient, the Best

The length of the word decides the shape

Grammar Discovery

Why Do These Four Look Different?

"The inland hotel is cheaper than the one on the ocean."

"The ocean hotel is more convenient than the inland one."

"It was the noisiest room we have ever had."

"The location is better, but the sleep is worse."

Count the syllables of the red adjectives, and then of the blue ones. The last sentence breaks the pattern completely — there are only a handful of those.

JobFormExample
Short adjective, comparing twoadjective + -er + thancheaper than, quieter than, bigger than
Long adjective, comparing twomore + adjective + thanmore convenient than, more expensive than
Short adjective, out of allthe + adjective + -estthe cheapest, the noisiest
Long adjective, out of allthe most + adjectivethe most expensive hotel on the street
Saying two things are equalas + adjective + as · not as + adjective + asThe rooms are as clean as each other.
The irregular onesgood → better → the best · bad → worse → the worst · far → furtherThe location is better, the sleep is worse.
The trapnever both at once, and never a comparison without thanNot more cheaper. Say cheaper than.

In one line: short words take -er and -est, long words take more and the most — and never the two together.

Listening 1 · A Travel Agent

Everybody Compares the Wrong Thing

Eleven years of booking travel for other people, in three rules. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does she say comparing the price is the wrong comparison?
Because the price is only one line of the bill. The cheaper hotel is usually further from everything, and two taxis a day for nine days pays the difference three times over.
2. Which reviews does she say to read, and why?
The ones in the middle, because they are written by people who are not angry and not in love, and they say what the place is like on a normal Tuesday.
3. What is her third rule, and how does she justify it?
Choose the quieter one. Nobody in the history of travel has ever come home and said the room was too quiet.
Listening 2 · Three Streets Inland

The Block on the Ocean

What surprises visitors about American beach cities. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say about the block right on the ocean?
It is the loudest, the most expensive and the most crowded, and it is where almost every visitor books.
2. What changes three streets inland, and by how much?
The same room is thirty per cent cheaper, the street is quieter at night, and the beach is still a five-minute walk away.
3. What does he say about the shuttle, and where do you have to look for it?
A lot of the bigger hotels run a free one, and it is almost never on the booking page — only on the hotel's own website. Two of the three hotels he stayed in last year had one.

Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Sixty Seconds, No Notes

Scenario: Compare two real things from your own life — two gyms, two schools, two clinics, two ways of getting to work. Give four comparisons, name the trade-off, and finish by saying which one wins and why.

No keywords, no notes, sixty seconds. It only counts if it ends in a decision.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I use -er with short adjectives and more with long ones, and never both.
I never leave out than in a comparison, or the in a superlative.
I can say two things are equal with as clean as, and unequal with not as close as.
I finish a comparison with a decision and a reason, not with a list.
I know the words: a rate, a neighborhood, a review, within walking distance, a trade-off, convenient, noisy, value for money, a shuttle, to book up.
