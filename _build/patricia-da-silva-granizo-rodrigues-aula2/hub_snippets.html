Running the Room

The ten expressions that steer a meeting, the four modal verbs that make you direct without sounding like an order, and the written rules of the monthly people call. Key words: agenda, to chair, action item, takeaway, to weigh in, to loop in, to circle back, to park, follow-up, bandwidth. Structure: modal verbs for chairing and softening.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read it inside a real sentence. All ten do the same job: they move a meeting without ordering anybody around.

Agenda -- the written list of what a meeting will cover, in order
"She sent the agenda the night before, with a time next to each item."
To chair -- to lead a meeting and decide who speaks and when
"Greg chairs the people call, so he opens it and he closes it."
Action item -- a task that comes out of a meeting, with an owner and a date
"Every action item has a name next to it before we hang up."
Takeaway -- the one thing people should remember when the meeting ends
"If there is one takeaway today, it is that attrition is not a pay problem."
To weigh in -- to give your opinion on something the group is discussing
"I would like HR to weigh in before we commit to a date."
To loop in -- to bring somebody into a conversation or an email thread
"Let me loop in legal before we answer that one."
To circle back -- to return to a point later, when there is time or information
"We can circle back to the bonus question at the end."
To park -- to leave a question aside on purpose so the meeting can move on
"I would rather park that until we have the legal answer."
Follow-up -- the message or the task that comes after a meeting
"The follow-up goes out the same afternoon, never two days later."
Bandwidth -- the time and capacity a person or a team has for more work
"My team does not have the bandwidth for a second rollout this quarter."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Shall we start with retention and come back to headcount at the end? Could you walk us through the numbers before we decide anything? If the answer is not in the room, I would rather park the question and put it in the follow-up than guess at it now.

That last point might be one for finance rather than for us. Would you mind taking the action item and looping in Marcus? And if we run out of time, we can circle back on Thursday -- I may have the legal answer by then.

A It makes the sentence more formal.
B It proposes the next move and asks the room at the same time.
C It shows that the speaker is not sure what the agenda is.
2. Why does the writer use I would rather park the question instead of we must park the question?
A Because it states a preference without overruling anybody in the room.
B Because must is grammatically wrong after we.
C Because would rather is more polite in writing than in speech.
3. That might be one for finance. What does might protect?
A It protects the speaker from having to name a number.
B It turns a judgement into a possibility, so nobody has to defend a position.
C It signals that the speaker will decide later.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Modals That Steer a Meeting

GRAMMAR

Four small words carry most of the steering in an American meeting. They do not make you sound weak: they make you sound like somebody who expects to be answered rather than obeyed.

FormWhat it doesExample
Shall we ... ?proposes the next move and asks the room at the same timeShall we start with retention?
Could you ... ?a request that sounds like a choice, even when it is notCould you walk us through the numbers?
I would rather ...states your preference without overruling anybodyI would rather park that until Monday.
might / mayturns a judgement into a possibility, so nobody has to defend a positionThat might be a question for finance.
the trapCan you... and We must... are correct English and land as orders. Use could and would when you are not the most senior person in the room.

Can you... and We must... are perfectly correct English and land as orders. Keep them for your own team, not for a room of peers.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

One right answer each. Read the situation before you look at the options.

You are chairing and somebody raises a legal question nobody in the room can answer. What do you say?
A We cannot discuss that here.
B I would rather park that until we have the legal answer, and I will put it in the follow-up.
C That is not my department, sorry.
D Can you send me an email about it?
You want the finance director to give his opinion before the group decides. What do you say?
A Marcus, you must weigh in now.
B Marcus, could you weigh in before we decide?
C Marcus, do you want to say something or not?
D I need your opinion, Marcus.
The call is running over and one item is left. What is the professional move?
A Rush through the last item so that nothing is left out.
B Park the last item, name an owner and put it in the follow-up.
C Stay on the call until everything is covered.
D Ask everybody to read about it later.
Greg is mid-sentence and you have one important fact he does not have. What do you say?
A Excuse me, I am very sorry to interrupt you.
B Greg, can I add one thing there? Two minutes and I will stop.
C Wait for him to finish and raise it in the follow-up email.
D Sorry, sorry, can I speak please?
