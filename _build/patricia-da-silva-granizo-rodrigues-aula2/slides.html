Chapter 4: The Code

Four Words That Change the Temperature

How to be direct in an American meeting without sounding like an order

Grammar Discovery

Direct, but Not Blunt

"Shall we start with retention and come back to headcount?"

"Could you walk us through the numbers before we decide?"

"I would rather park that until we have the legal answer."

"That might be a question for finance, not for us."

All four sentences say something quite direct. What are the coloured words doing to the directness?

FormWhat it doesExample
Shall we ... ?proposes the next move and asks the room at the same timeShall we start with retention?
Could you ... ?a request that sounds like a choice, even when it is notCould you walk us through the numbers?
I would rather ...states your preference without overruling anybodyI would rather park that until Monday.
might / mayturns a judgement into a possibility, so nobody has to defend a positionThat might be a question for finance.
the trapCan you... and We must... are correct English and land as orders. Use could and would when you are not the most senior person in the room.

None of these makes you sound weak. They make you sound like somebody who expects to be answered rather than obeyed, which is exactly the register of a director talking to peers.

Chapter 5: The Ear

Two Rooms, Full Speed

One meeting opening and one colleague explaining how to cut in

Listening 1

The First Ninety Seconds

A chair opens the monthly people call. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What is on the agenda, and who takes which part?
Retention for the quarter, the onboarding redesign and the hiring plan for the two new sites. Patricia takes the first two and the chair takes the third.
2. What are the two ground rules she gives, and why does she give them today?
Say the number and not the adjective, and say at the start if something needs a decision today. She gives them because there are new people on the call.
3. What happens to anything that does not need a decision today?
It is parked and it goes into the follow-up.
Listening 2

How to Cut In Without Being Rude

A colleague explains what he learned in his first year on American calls.

0:000:00
1. What did he do in his first year, and what happened as a result?
He waited for a gap, and at the end of the call somebody would say that they had not heard from him.
2. What are the two things he does now to get into a conversation?
He says the person name first, which buys him half a second, and he says how long he will take.
3. What does he say you should do if somebody cuts you off?
Not apologise. Say let me finish this point, and finish it.

Chapter 6: Your Turn

The Part Where You Talk

You open, you steer and you close -- three times, with less help each time

Guided

Open the Meeting

Task: Open the monthly people call as if you were chairing it. Say what is on the agenda, how long you have, who takes what, and one ground rule. Use the frames below. Then do it again without looking at them.

Support:

Let us get started...Three things on the agenda...I am going to ask...Anything that does not need a decision today...Over to you.
Semi-free

Steer It When It Goes Off the Rails

Task: Your teacher plays a participant who talks for too long, raises a legal question nobody can answer, and asks you something that belongs to finance. Handle all three without saying no and without letting the meeting run over.

Support:

Let me park that...Could you...I would rather...That might be one for...Let me loop in...
Free

Close It in Sixty Seconds

Task: Close the meeting: name every action item with an owner and a date, give one takeaway, and say what goes into the follow-up. Sixty seconds, no notes. Your teacher will have written down what was actually said -- you have to recover it from memory.

No support. Target: three action items with owners, and one takeaway in one sentence.
