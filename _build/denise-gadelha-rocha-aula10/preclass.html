Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

Speaking Under Pressure -- Fluency, Recovery and Executive Presence in High-Stakes Conversations

What to say in the four seconds while the word is still on its way. Key words: draw a blank, buy time, trail off, lose your thread, filler, placeholder phrase, rephrase, backtrack, stall, delivery, poise, gather your thoughts. Structure: used to, be used to and get used to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. Most of these name something that already happens to you every week.

To draw a blank -- to find that a word you know is simply not there when you need it
"I drew a blank on a word I use every week."
To buy time -- to keep speaking so that you have a few seconds to find what you want to say
"I am not asking you to speak faster. I am asking you to buy time out loud."
To trail off -- to let a sentence fade out without ever finishing it
"The sentence trailed off, and somebody else took the floor."
To lose your thread -- to forget, in the middle of a sentence, where your own argument was going
"He interrupted twice and I lost my thread completely."
Filler -- a small sound or word that buys you half a second while you think
"A filler buys you half a second. A whole sentence buys you five."
Placeholder phrase -- a ready-made sentence you keep for the moment you have nothing
"Keep one placeholder phrase ready for the question you cannot answer yet."
To rephrase -- to say the same idea again in different words
"Nobody hears a rephrase as a rescue. They hear it as care."
To backtrack -- to go back and correct something you have just said
"Let me backtrack: the figure I gave you was the gross one."
To stall -- to delay on purpose until you are ready to answer
"Twice in the second hour she stalled on purpose and held the floor."
Delivery -- how you say it: pace, pauses and volume, rather than the words themselves
"They will not remember your vocabulary. They will remember your delivery."
Poise -- visible calm under pressure, whether or not you feel it
"A deliberate pause reads as poise. The same pause with an apology does not."
To gather your thoughts -- to take a deliberate pause before you answer
"That is a fair question. Give me a second to gather my thoughts."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Watch every "used to" and decide what it is doing.

Denise used to prepare for the difficult questions by learning more vocabulary. It never worked, and now she knows why: the words were never the problem. In ninety minutes of a regional review she drew a blank exactly three times, and all three were words she uses every week. They arrived late. They did not fail to arrive.

What she is not used to is speaking while she searches. In Portuguese she has never had to: the sentence and the word arrive together. In English there is a gap of three or four seconds, and until this month she filled it with an apology. "Sorry, my English", four times a call. She took that for modesty. The room took it for a discount, and priced her accordingly.

She is getting used to the alternative now. A rephrase, a short answer at low resolution, or an open stall — "that is a fair question, give me a second on it" — and the floor stays exactly where it was. Her delivery did not get faster. It got harder to interrupt, which is a different thing, and the only one the room was ever measuring.

1. "Denise used to prepare for the difficult questions by learning more vocabulary." What does this tell you?
A She prepared that way in the past and does not any more.
B She is accustomed to preparing that way now.
C She is slowly becoming accustomed to preparing that way.
2. "What she is not used to is speaking while she searches." Why "speaking" and not "speak"?
A Because the action is happening at this exact moment.
B Because in "be used to" the "to" is a preposition, and a verb after a preposition takes -ing.
C Because "speak" cannot follow the word "used".
3. "She is getting used to the alternative now." What does "getting" add?
A That the change is in progress: she is becoming accustomed and is not there yet.
B That she has completely finished adapting.
C That she did it in the past and stopped.
4. Why does the text say the room took the apology "for a discount"?
A Because it made the meeting shorter for everybody.
B Because apologising for your English asks the room to expect less of you, and it does.
C Because the apology did not sound sincere to anyone.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Used To, Be Used To, Get Used To

GRAMMAR

Three words, three meanings. One test separates them, and it takes two seconds.

FormWhat it meansExample
used to + verbA habit or state in the past that is over now. Past only — there is no present form of this structure.I used to translate everything in my head.
be used to + -ingYou are accustomed to it. It is normal for you. Works in any tense: I am, I was, I will be.I am used to presenting in English.
get used to + -ingYou are becoming accustomed. The process, not the result.I am getting used to the pace.
The two-second testCan you say "I am used to it"? Then the "to" is a PREPOSITION and the verb after it takes -ing. Cannot? Then it is past habit, and the verb stays bare.
Negative and questionThe "d" moves to the auxiliary: I did not use to prepare them · Did you use to dread that call? Never did not used to.
The one to watchI am used to work means nothing at all. Either I used to work (past habit) or I am used to working (it is normal for me). Two different sentences, one letter apart.
Why it matters this weekThis is how a senior person describes their own history with a language without sounding defensive. "I used to translate everything in my head" is the sentence of somebody who has moved. "My English is not good" is not a sentence about progress at all.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

A word will not come and you are in the middle of a sentence. You:
A Keep the floor: "Let me put that a different way."
B Stop and apologise for your English, then start again.
C Use the nearest Spanish word and hope it exists in English.
Which sentence is correct?
A "I am used to work in three languages."
B "I am used to working in three languages."
C "I am used working in three languages."
You want to say that five years ago you translated everything in your head, and that you no longer do. You say:
A "I used to translate everything in my head."
B "I am used to translating everything in my head."
C "I am getting used to translate everything in my head."
Someone asks for a figure you do not have with you. The strongest answer is:
A "I do not have that number in front of me. I will send it today."
B An approximate figure, said with confidence.
C "Sorry, my English. Can you repeat the question?"
Six months into the role, the pace of the English calls still feels fast, but less than before. You say:
A "I used to the pace now."
B "I am getting used to the pace."
C "I used to be the pace."
In a heated call you need four seconds. The move that costs you the least authority is:
A Apologising for your English while you think.
B Letting the sentence trail off and waiting for somebody to help.
C Stalling openly: "That is a fair question. Give me a second on it."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

Think of the hardest question anyone has asked you in a meeting this quarter — the one you would rather not be asked again. Answer it now, out loud, in English, in real time. When a word does not arrive, use one of the four moves instead of stopping, and do not restart the recording. The point is not a clean take: the point is hearing exactly what you do in the two seconds after a word goes missing.
