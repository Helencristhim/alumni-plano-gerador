Chapter 3: The Code

Present Perfect vs Past Simple

for · since · already · yet · just · ever · never

Grammar Discovery

Read and Notice

"I have worked in tax law for over ten years."

"I worked at PwC from 2008 to 2012."

"I have already reviewed the impact projections."

"The reform has not been signed yet."

Two sentences say when. Two do not. What changes?

FormUseExample
Present Perfect
have / has + past participle		The time is open: it started in the past and still counts now.I have advised banks since 2016.
Past Simple
verb + -ed / irregular		The time is closed: it finished, and we usually say when.I joined the firm in 2016.
for / sincefor + a length of time (for ten years) · since + a starting point (since 2016)
already / yet / justalready = sooner than expected · yet = not up to now, in negatives and questions · just = a moment ago
ever / neverExperience in your whole life, with no date: Have you ever worked with foreign banks? I have never worked in London.

The test is simple: if you say when, use the past simple. A finished date closes the door on the present perfect.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I work in tax law since 2010."
"I have worked in tax law since 2010."
"I have joined the firm in 2016."
"I joined the firm in 2016."
"I am working here for six years."
"I have worked here for six years."

A period that started in the past and reaches today takes the present perfect, never the present. A closed date takes the past simple, never the present perfect.
