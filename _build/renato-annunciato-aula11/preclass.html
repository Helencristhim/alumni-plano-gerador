Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

Presenting Data -- Charts, Trends and What They Mean

Narrate the chart instead of reading it. Key words: bar chart, line graph, axis, trend, to peak, to level off, to dip, steady, sharp, breakdown, outlier, percentage point. Structures: quantifiers (most of, a few, very little, fewer), and the three steps of a chart narration.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. Four are about the chart, five about how a number moves, three about the detail.

Bar chart -- a chart that compares amounts with bars of different lengths
"The bar chart compares the six cities in one look."
Line graph -- a chart that shows how a number changes over time, with one line
"The line graph shows units per month for the last twelve months."
Axis -- the line along the side or the bottom of a chart that shows the scale
"The months are on the bottom axis, and the units are on the side."
Trend -- the general direction a number is moving in over time
"The trend is up, even with the dip in July."
To peak -- to reach the highest point, before going down again
"Sales peaked in March and then started to fall."
To level off -- to stop going up or down, and stay at about the same level
"After July, the numbers leveled off for three months."
To dip -- to go down a little, usually for a short time
"Units dip every July, when the clinics are quieter."
Steady -- changing slowly and regularly, with no sudden jumps
"The South shows steady growth, month after month."
Sharp -- very sudden and large, when a number goes up or down
"There was a sharp rise in November, before the summer."
Breakdown -- the same total, divided into its parts
"The breakdown by city is on the next slide."
Outlier -- one result that is very different from all the others
"One clinic in Curitiba is an outlier, so I show it separately."
Percentage point -- the difference between two percentages: from 18% to 21% is three of these
"Our market share grew by three percentage points."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the chart notes and answer the questions. Notice the small words in bold in front of each noun.

Renato is preparing one slide for the meeting in Boston. Marta, the analyst, built a line graph of units per month. The trend is up. Sales peaked in March, dipped in July, and then leveled off. In November there was a sharp rise, and most of the growth came from a few clinics in Sao Paulo and Curitiba.

One of those clinics is an outlier: it ordered almost as much as the whole city of Porto Alegre. Renato will show it in the breakdown, not on the main chart, because very few clinics in the region look like it. There was very little growth in the interior of Sao Paulo, and none of the interior accounts ordered in July.

Renato has fewer prescribers in the interior than in the capital, and he has less time to visit them. But the opportunity is real: market share grew by three percentage points this year, and it took a great deal of work to get there.

1. Why is it "a few clinics" but "very little growth"?
A Because "a few" is for things you can count, like clinics, and "little" is for things you cannot count, like growth.
B Because "a few" is used for good news and "very little" is used only when the news is bad for the company.
C Because clinics are in the capital and growth is in the interior.
2. Why is it "fewer prescribers" but "less time"?
A Because "fewer" is more formal and is used mostly in written reports to directors.
B Because prescribers are countable and time is uncountable.
C Because the sentence is about the interior.
3. "Most of the growth came from a few clinics." Why is "the" there?
A Because "most of" always needs "the", "our" or "these" before the noun.
B Because "growth" is a countable noun and countable nouns always need an article.
C Because it is the past tense.
4. Which sentence would you say in Boston?
A "The most part of the growth came from Sao Paulo and from Curitiba."
B "Most of the growth came from Sao Paulo."
C "Most of growth came from Sao Paulo."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Quantifiers

GRAMMAR

One question decides the quantifier: can you count the noun?

Type of nounQuantifiersExample
Countable (clinics, units, accounts)many, a few, few, fewer, several, a large number ofA few clinics ordered more.
Uncountable (growth, demand, work, time)much, a little, little, less, a great deal ofThere was very little growth.
Bothsome, any, a lot of, plenty of, most, hardly any, noMost clinics · most growth
A specific groupquantifier + of the / of our: most of the growth, none of our clinics
A few vs fewa few / a little = some (positive) · few / little = almost none (negative): Few clinics ordered in July is bad news.
Negative and questionmany / much sound natural here: We did not open many accounts. How much growth was there?In positive sentences, prefer a lot of.
Two trapsNever the most part of — just most of the. And never less clinics — countable nouns take fewer.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

The chart is on the screen. Your first sentence is:
A "So, January, four thousand units. February, four thousand two hundred. March..."
B "This line graph shows units per month for the last twelve months."
C "Sorry, the chart is a little complicated, but I will try to explain it."
Market share went from 18% to 21%. You say:
A "Our market share grew by three percent this year, which is a very good result."
B "Our market share grew by twenty-one percent."
C "Our market share grew by three percentage points."
Sales stopped growing and stayed the same for three months. You say:
A "Sales leveled off for three months."
B "Sales peaked for three months, and then they started to go up again in November."
C "Sales dipped for three months."
Only two clinics in the interior ordered this quarter. You say:
A "A lot of clinics in the interior ordered this quarter, so the region is doing well."
B "Very few clinics in the interior ordered this quarter."
C "Very little clinics in the interior ordered this quarter."
One clinic ordered as much as a whole city. You decide to:
A Show it in the breakdown, as an outlier, and keep the main chart clean.
B Put it in the middle of the main chart, because it is the most impressive number you have.
C Remove it from the data so that nobody asks about it.
A director asks for the numbers of each city. You say:
A "I do not have that now, sorry, maybe I can send it to you later by email."
B "The breakdown by city is on the next slide. Let me show you."
C "All the cities are more or less the same."

Reflection
