Chapter 3: The Coffee Before the Session

Ten Minutes and a Stranger

Half work, half coffee, line by line

Dialogue

Before the Session Starts

T
Is this seat taken? I am Tomas, from a fund that buys credits. I saw your badge: legal, is that right?
A
Please. I am Amanda, and yes, I am the lawyer who looks after contracts for an agribusiness group.
T
Agribusiness. So this whole carbon world is new for you, or have you been in it a while?
A
Very new. I have never worked on a carbon credit project, but I have read a lot in the last month.
T
Everybody in this room read a lot in the last month. What is on your desk right now?
A
A thirty-year concession agreement for a reforestation project. We have been reading the first draft all week, and honestly, the land part worries me more than the carbon part.
T
It should. That is where these projects die. Can I ask the obvious question? Why did you move into this?
A
Because if I stayed only in supply contracts, I would be doing the same work in ten years. And the regulatory framework here is being written right now.
T
That is the right reason. They are calling us in. Find me at the break, I want to hear about the land part.
A
I will. And if you have a template you can share, I will buy the second coffee.
Comprehension

About the Conversation

1. Who is Tomas, and how does he start the conversation?
He works for a fund that buys credits, and he starts by asking if the seat is taken and reading her badge.
2. What does he say about the land part of these projects?
That it should worry her, because that is where these projects die.
3. How does the conversation end, and why is that ending useful?
They agree to meet at the break and she offers to buy a coffee for a template. It turns ten minutes of small talk into a contact.
Chapter 4: Putting It Together

Four Structures, One Answer

Lessons 1 to 4, in a single paragraph

Review

What Each Sentence Is For

"I am the lawyer who looks after contracts for an agribusiness group."

"I have never worked on a carbon credit project."

"We have been reading the first draft all week."

"If I stayed only in supply contracts, I would be doing the same work in ten years."

Each sentence does a different job. What is the job of each one?

LessonThe job it doesThe signal
1 · who, which, thatdescribes: two short sentences become one long onea noun, then who or that, then a verb with no new subject
2 · present perfectexperience and open periods: no finished time in the sentencenever, ever, already, yet, for, since
3 · have been + ingwork in progress: it started earlier and it is still goingall week, since Monday, for three weeks
4 · if + past, wouldshows a consequence you do not want, so you can refuse the ideaa past form after if, and would in the other half
The real testnot saying them one at a time, but letting them live in the same answer. That is what this lesson is for.

Sound Check

The Four Points, Together

Word stress · the -ed ending · weak have · weak would

Why these four, and no others, for now

Stress decides whether a long word is understood at all. The -ed decides whether a sentence sounds past or present.

The weak forms of have and would do two jobs at once: they make you sound natural, and they train the ear for the speed at which other people will say them to you.

Chapter 5: Real Speed

The Call and the Room

What lands on your desk, and how the market explains itself

Listening 1

The Call That Changes Your Month

Karin, from risk, has a new file for you. Sound first, no text.

1. What does Karin say about the supply contract, and who noticed?
The final version came back with the annual cap and one and a half per cent, exactly as asked, and the finance director noticed.
2. What has the group bought, and what kind of contract is behind it?
A stake in a reforestation project in the north, sitting on a thirty-year concession agreement with the state.
3. What exactly does she ask Amanda to do by Monday?
Read the first ten pages of the framework document and say what worries her most.
Listening 2

How the Market Explains Itself

The opening five minutes of a technical session. Sound first, no text.

1. What is one carbon credit, in the speaker's words?
One tonne of carbon dioxide that was not released into the atmosphere, or that was taken out of it.
2. Who has to certify that the tonne is real, and why is that a separate job?
An independent verification body, so that the company does not certify itself.
3. Why does the speaker say the legal part matters more than people expect?
Because most projects sit on a concession agreement, so two parties have to keep a promise about the same piece of land for thirty years.

Sample data for this lesson. Nothing here is a real project record.
1. Two lines on this card are not decided yet. Which ones, and why does that matter?
The certification body is not appointed and the environmental permit is still pending. Without them there is no revenue and no start.
2. Say, in one second-conditional sentence, what would happen if the permit were refused.
Example: "If the permit were refused, the project would have no activity to certify, and the credits would never exist."
3. The last line is the open legal question. Why is thirty years the reason it is difficult?
Because the credits are produced slowly over decades, so ending the concession early raises the question of who owns what was already grown.
