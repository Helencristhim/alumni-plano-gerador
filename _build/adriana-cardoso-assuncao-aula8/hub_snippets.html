Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. You will hear all eight in any business bulletin this month.

Chokepoint -- a narrow passage that a large part of world trade has to go through
"The strait is a chokepoint that carries a fifth of the world's oil."
Supply chain -- the whole path a product takes, from raw material to customer
"Every company in that supply chain is reporting delays."
Disruption -- an interruption that stops something from working normally
"The disruption lasted eleven days and the effects will last months."
Headline -- the title of a news story, and the only part most people read
"That is the part of the story that reaches the headlines."
Knock-on effect -- a consequence that reaches something apparently unrelated
"The knock-on effect is overtime in a plant nobody was watching."
Outlook -- what is expected to happen in the near future
"The outlook for the next quarter is volatile."
Volatile -- changing quickly and without warning
"Volatile is the word people use when nobody knows."
To brace for -- to get ready for something difficult that is coming
"Companies that are bracing for a difficult quarter freeze hiring."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The strait, which carries about a fifth of the world's oil, has been partly closed for eleven days. Shipping insurance, which was the first number to move, has more than doubled. Those are the parts that reach the headlines. The part that reaches companies is slower: the factories whose parts come from Asia are reporting delays, and the plants where the overtime is highest are the ones under most pressure. The teams whose managers have been pushing them since January are the teams a company loses first. And our chief executive, who started his career in operations, is the one person in the room who reads the shipping news every morning.
Why does "which carries about a fifth of the world's oil" have commas?
A Because it is extra information — we already know which strait
B Because the clause is long
C Because it comes after a noun
"The factories whose parts come from Asia are reporting delays." What happens if you remove the clause?
A Nothing — the sentence means the same
B You no longer know which factories, so the clause is defining and takes no commas
C The sentence becomes a question
Could you write "our chief executive, that started his career in operations"?
A Yes, that works everywhere
B No — after a comma you use which or who, never that
C Only in spoken English

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

A relative clause adds information to a noun without starting a new sentence. There are two kinds, and the commas are the difference.

definingno commas · that / which / whoThe factories that depend on Asia are reporting delays. (Which factories? These ones.)
non-definingcommas · which / who, never thatThe strait, which carries a fifth of the world's oil, has been closed.
peoplewhoThe manager who owns the plan will present it.
possessionwhoseThe teams whose managers have been pushing them.
placeswhereThe plant where the overtime is highest.

Two traps: never repeat the subject (the plant that it is behind), and never use that after a comma. The test for the commas: remove the clause — if you still know which thing you mean, it takes commas.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz
You want to add, as extra information, that the strait carries a fifth of the world's oil. Which sentence?
A "The strait that carries a fifth of the world's oil has been closed."
B "The strait, which carries a fifth of the world's oil, has been closed."
C "The strait, that carries a fifth of the world's oil, has been closed."
You need to identify which teams will lose people first. Which sentence?
A "The teams whose managers have been pushing them will lose people first."
B "The teams, whose managers have been pushing them, will lose people first."
C "The teams which managers have been pushing them will lose people first."
The chief financial officer asks about the story at breakfast. What is the right length of answer?
A Five minutes with all the details you read
B Two sentences: what happened, and what it means for the company
C "I have not read it yet."
You are asked for the outlook and you genuinely do not know. Which answer sounds senior?
A "I have no idea."
B "Volatile. What I would watch is the overtime line."
C "It will be fine."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Choose one real business story from this week. Record ninety seconds: one sentence saying what happened, one sentence saying what it means for a company like yours, and one question back. Use at least two relative clauses, and do not read.
