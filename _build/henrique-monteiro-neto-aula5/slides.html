Chapter 3: The Code

A Past That Did Not Happen

Joined to a present that did

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If they had signed that treaty, we would not be arguing about it now."

"If the archive had been destroyed, historians would still be repeating the old story."

"If I did not read three papers a day, I would have missed it entirely."

"If he were a more cautious man, he would never have made that call."

Two halves in every sentence, and they are not in the same time. Which half is about the past, and which about now — and does the order stay the same?

FormWhat it doesExample
If + past perfect,
would + base form		A past that did not happen, joined to a present that did. This is the counterfactual of history and the most useful of the two."If they had signed it, we would not be arguing now."
If + past perfect,
would still be + -ing		The same shape, but the present result is a continuing state. Very common when the subject is a debate, a habit or a belief."If the archive had been destroyed, we would still be repeating it."
If + past simple,
would have + participle		The reverse: a permanent fact about now explaining something that did or did not happen then."If I did not read three papers, I would have missed it."
If + were,
would never have + participle		Character explaining a past action. Were for every person — the same form you use in if I were you."If he were more cautious, he would never have made that call."
might / could
instead of would		Lowers the certainty of the result without touching the condition. In a serious argument this is often the honest choice."If it had gone the other way, we might not be here."

The mixed conditional exists because history is mixed: causes sit in one time and consequences in another. Getting it right is not decoration — it is the difference between saying that something changed the world and saying that it changed a moment.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If they would have signed that treaty, we would not be arguing now."
"If they had signed that treaty, we would not be arguing now."
"If the archive had been destroyed, historians would still repeat the old story."
"If the archive had been destroyed, historians would still be repeating the old story."
"If I had not read three papers a day, I would miss it entirely."
"If I did not read three papers a day, I would have missed it entirely."

The first is the classic: Portuguese allows the conditional in both halves, English forbids it in the if half — never if … would have. The second and third are subtler and matter more here: decide which half is the PAST and which is the PRESENT before you open your mouth, because once the sentence is running you cannot go back and fix it.

Chapter 4: The Night It Nearly Happened

The Night It Nearly Happened

A political scientist on the four minutes nobody knew about for ten years

Dialogue

Four Minutes in a Bunker

V
My students always want the story to have a hero. And it does, more or less. What they do not like is that it also has a broken satellite and a man who was not supposed to be on duty.
H
So if the other officer had been there, we might be having a very different conversation now.
V
That is the honest answer, and it makes people uncomfortable. Procedure said report it. He waited. Everything hung in the balance for about four minutes.
H
Was it judgement or was it a fluke? Because those are very different lessons to draw.
V
Both, and he said so himself. He thought five missiles were too few for a real attack. That reasoning was sound. That he happened to be the one in the chair was not.
H
It is easy to second-guess him now. If I were in that chair with four minutes, I am not sure I would have waited.
V
Nobody is. And if he had reported it, the system above him would probably have paused too. Probably. That word is doing an enormous amount of work.
H
Which is why the story keeps being told. If it had a clean answer, it would have stopped being interesting years ago.
Comprehension

About Vera

1. What does Vera say her students do not like about the story?
That it also has a broken satellite and a man who was not supposed to be on duty.
2. What does she say procedure required?
That he report it — and he waited instead.
3. What reasoning does she say was sound?
That five missiles were too few for a real attack.
4. What does she say about the word "probably"?
That it is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence.
Listening 1

Five Missiles on a Screen

A lecture, told from the chair. Sound first — no text.

1. How many missiles did the system report, and why did that matter?
Five — too few to be a real first strike, which is what made him doubt it.
2. How long did he have to decide?
About four minutes.
3. What does the speaker say the world only learned ten years later?
That the incident had happened at all.
Listening 2

The Trouble With What If

A historian arguing against her own field. Sound first — no text.

1. What is her objection to counterfactual history?
That you can never test it, so the argument is decided by whoever tells the better story.
2. What does she accept it is good for?
Showing that outcomes were contingent and not inevitable.
3. What rule does she propose?
Change one thing only, and change it as late as possible.
