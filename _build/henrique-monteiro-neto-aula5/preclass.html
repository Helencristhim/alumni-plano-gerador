Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

The Roads Not Taken — Counterfactual History

A different past joined to this present, and the sentence shape that can hold both. Key expressions: a near miss, contingent on, to avert, on the brink of, to hang in the balance, to tip the scales, by a whisker, to backfire, a knock-on effect, to be doomed to fail, against all odds, a fluke, to hold sway, to run its course, down to, a twist of fate, to second-guess, to dodge a bullet. Structure: mixed conditionals — if + past perfect with would + base form, and the reverse.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

A near miss -- something serious that very nearly happened and did not
"The Cold War is a list of near misses we only learned about later."
Contingent on -- depending entirely on something that is itself uncertain
"The whole outcome was contingent on one man's judgement."
To avert -- to prevent something bad just before it happens
"He averted a catastrophe by doing nothing for four minutes."
On the brink of -- extremely close to a dangerous or decisive situation
"Twice that year the two countries were on the brink of war."
To hang in the balance -- to be undecided, with something serious depending on the result
"For twenty minutes the answer hung in the balance."
To tip the scales -- to be the small thing that finally decides the outcome
"A single missing signature tipped the scales."
By a whisker -- by an extremely small margin
"The motion passed by a whisker, and nobody expected it to."
To backfire -- to produce exactly the opposite of the intended result
"The sanctions backfired within eighteen months."
A knock-on effect -- a consequence that goes on to cause a further consequence
"The knock-on effects are still being argued about today."
To be doomed to fail -- certain to fail from the very beginning
"In hindsight the plan was doomed to fail, though nobody said so."
Against all odds -- despite being extremely unlikely to succeed
"Against all odds, the negotiation held."
A fluke -- a lucky accident, not the result of skill or planning
"He is honest about it: the timing was a fluke."
To hold sway -- to have dominant influence over people or events
"That theory held sway in Washington for a decade."
To run its course -- to continue naturally until it comes to its own end
"They decided to let the crisis run its course."
Down to -- caused by, and attributable to, one particular thing
"Whether it was down to luck or to judgement is still argued."
A twist of fate -- an unexpected turn of events that changes everything
"By a twist of fate the other officer was off duty that night."
To second-guess -- to question a decision afterwards, knowing how it turned out
"It is easy to second-guess a man who had four minutes."
To dodge a bullet -- to escape narrowly from something that would have been very bad
"The world dodged a bullet and did not hear about it for ten years."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

It is now the most famous near miss of the Cold War, and for ten years nobody outside two rooms knew it had happened. Just after midnight the new warning system reported five launches. Procedure was unambiguous: report it upwards. The officer on duty had roughly four minutes, and everything hung in the balance while he thought about the number.

His reasoning was simple. Five is too few. If a country had been opening a war of that kind, it would not have sent five missiles. So he decided the system was wrong and did not report it, and he was right: a satellite had misread sunlight on high cloud. The whole outcome was contingent on one judgement, taken alone, at midnight.

What makes historians uncomfortable is the rest of it. By a twist of fate he was covering somebody else's shift. If the other officer had been in that chair, we might be living in a different world now — or we might not, because the system above him would probably have paused as well. Probably. It is easy to second-guess a man who had four minutes, and it is easy to call the outcome a fluke when it was partly judgement and partly luck.

The wider lesson has knock-on effects that are still argued about. One school holds that the alert systems of that decade were doomed to fail sooner or later, and that the world dodged a bullet more than once. Another argues that the caution which held sway in both capitals is exactly why nothing happened. If we did not now have the archives, we would still be guessing at both.

1. "If the other officer had been in that chair, we might be living in a different world now." Which half is about the past?
A Both halves.
B Only the second half.
C Only the first half — the result is about today.
2. Why might be rather than would be?
A Because it lowers the certainty of the result without changing the condition.
B Because the sentence is a question.
C Because would is impossible after if.
3. "If we did not now have the archives, we would still be guessing." What is being said?
A That the archives were destroyed.
B That a permanent fact about now explains a continuing present state.
C That historians will guess in the future.
4. What does the text mean by calling the outcome a fluke?
A That it was carefully planned.
B That it was inevitable.
C That luck played a real part, alongside judgement.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Mixed Conditionals — Two Times in One Sentence

GRAMMAR

A conditional is called mixed when the condition and the result sit in different times. History is full of them, because causes and consequences almost never share a decade.

FormWhat it doesExample
If + past perfect,
would + base form		A past that did not happen, joined to a present that did. The workhorse of counterfactual argument."If they had signed it, we would not be arguing now."
If + past perfect,
would still be + -ing		Same condition, but the present result is a continuing state rather than a fact. Very common with beliefs, debates and habits."If the archive had been destroyed, we would still be repeating it."
If + past simple,
would have + participle		The reverse direction: a permanent fact about now explains something that did or did not happen then."If I did not read three papers, I would have missed it."
If + were,
would never have + participle		Character or disposition explaining a past act. Were for every person, exactly as in if I were you."If he were more cautious, he would never have made that call."
might / could
in place of would		Reduces the certainty of the RESULT only. In a serious argument this is usually the honest form, and it is what separates analysis from assertion."If it had gone the other way, we might not be here."

One absolute rule and one habit. The rule: never if … would have — Portuguese allows the conditional in both halves and English does not, and this is the single most audible error at C1. The habit: decide which half is past and which is present before you start the sentence. Once it is running, there is no way back.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are arguing about how close something came to going the other way. Choose the sentence that works.

A past condition with a present result:
A "If they would have signed it, we would not be arguing now."
B "If they had signed it, we would not be arguing now."
C "If they signed it, we would not have been arguing now."
A permanent fact about now, explaining something that did not happen then:
A "If I did not read three papers a day, I would have missed it."
B "If I had not read three papers a day, I would miss it."
C "If I would not read three papers a day, I had missed it."
You want to lower your confidence in the result:
A "If it had gone the other way, we might not be here."
B "If it would have gone the other way, we are not here."
C "If it went the other way, we might not have been here now."
You want to say the outcome depended entirely on one thing:
A "The outcome was contingent on one judgement."
B "The outcome was contingent of one judgement."
C "The outcome was down of one judgement."
Somebody says the crisis was always going to end well. You disagree:
A "Yes, it was doomed to fail from the start."
B "Against all odds it held — and calling that inevitable is second-guessing with the answer in front of you."
C "It ran its course by a whisker of a fluke."
