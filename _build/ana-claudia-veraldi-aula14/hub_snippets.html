Asking Nicely -- and Who You Are Asking

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Blunt -- saying exactly what you think, without trying to be gentle
"He is not rude, he is just blunt. He says what he thinks."
Tactful -- careful not to upset people when you say something difficult
"She found a tactful way to say that the soup was too salty."
Straightforward -- honest and clear, with no hidden meaning
"Norwegians are very straightforward. A question is just a question."
Courteous -- polite and respectful, especially to people you do not know
"The owner was courteous, even when we arrived two hours late."
Pushy -- trying too hard to make somebody do what you want
"I did not want to sound pushy, so I asked only once."
Awkward -- uncomfortable, and not sure what to say or do
"There was an awkward silence after my question."
To come across as -- to give other people a certain impression
"Without please, a short question can come across as rude."
To impose on somebody -- to ask somebody for something that gives them extra work or trouble
"I do not want to impose on you, but could I stay one more night?"
To hedge -- to avoid a clear, direct answer or request, often to be polite
"The British often hedge: I was just wondering if maybe..."
To beat around the bush -- to talk about other things and avoid the main point
"Stop beating around the bush and tell me what you need."
To get to the point -- to start talking about the most important thing
"In Oslo, people get to the point in the first sentence."
To walk on eggshells -- to be extremely careful about what you say, so you do not upset somebody
"I felt I was walking on eggshells with my new landlady."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When Ana stayed in a guesthouse in Norway, her host Ingrid sent her a message: Kitchen closes at 10. Thanks. At first it seemed blunt, but Ingrid was only being straightforward. Norwegians like to get to the point.

In England it was different. Her landlady, Margaret, never said anything directly. She liked to hedge: I was wondering if you could close the window, or would you mind turning the music down? Ana sometimes felt she was walking on eggshells, because she did not want to come across as pushy.

In the United States, her host Josh was friendly and quick. When Ana asked, Could you tell me where the supermarket is?, he laughed and said she did not need to beat around the bush. For Ana, the lesson was clear: the most courteous question is the one that fits the person you are asking.

1. Why did Ingrid write such a short message?
A Because she was angry with Ana.
B Because Norwegians are straightforward and like to get to the point.
C Because she did not speak English well.
2. "Could you tell me where the supermarket is?" Why is not where is the supermarket?
A Because in an indirect question the second half has statement order.
B Because is must always go at the end of a sentence.
C Because the question is in the past.
3. What is the main idea of the text?
A English speakers are always very polite.
B Ana did not like her hosts.
C The right level of politeness depends on who you are asking.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect and Polite Questions

GRAMMAR

The polite part goes to the front. The question itself goes back to statement order.

FormWhat it doesExample
direct questionVerb before the subjectWhere is the station?
Could you tell me + wh-wordStatement order at the endCould you tell me where the station is?
Do you know + if / whetherFor yes/no questions, no doDo you know if it opens on Sundays?
Would you mind + -ingA polite requestWould you mind closing the door?
I was wondering if + couldVery polite, typical in the UKI was wondering if you could help me.

Never: Could you tell me where is the station · do you know what time does it open · I was wondering if could you · would you mind to open. The second half of an indirect question is never a question.

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

Ana arrives at Josh’s cabin in Vermont. Put the conversation in order.

?"Hi, Ana! Welcome to Vermont. Did you find the cabin okay?"
?"Yes, thank you. Could you tell me where the nearest supermarket is?"
?"Sure! It is about ten minutes down the road."
?"Great. And do you know if it is open on Sundays?"
?"It opens at eight on Sundays. Anything else? Just ask."
?"Would you mind lending me a heater? The cabin is cold at night."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

You are in London and you need directions. The most natural question is:
A "Sorry, could you tell me where the museum is?"
B "Sorry, could you tell me where is the museum?"
C "Tell me where the museum is."
You want to know the opening time of a shop. You say:
A "Do you know what time does the shop open?"
B "Do you know what time the shop opens?"
C "Do you know what time opens the shop?"
A Norwegian host sends you: "Breakfast at 8. No shoes inside." What does it mean?
A She is angry with you.
B She is being straightforward, which is normal in Norway.
C She does not want you in the house.
You need your British neighbor to move his car. The best choice is:
A "Move your car."
B "I was wondering if you could move your car a little."
C "I was wondering if could you move your car."
