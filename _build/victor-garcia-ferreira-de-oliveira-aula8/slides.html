How this card is used: the numbers are not a score and they are not compared with anybody else. They are compared with one person, seven weeks ago. The last line is the only one that changes what happens next, and it holds exactly one item on purpose.
1. Three lines on this card are numbers and three are yes or no. Which kind is harder to argue with, and why?
The numbers, because they are measurable and two people can agree about them. The yes or no lines depend on a judgement, so they need an example to stand up.
2. Why does the card compare you only with yourself seven weeks ago?
Because a benchmark only means something next to the same measurement of the same person. Comparing with somebody else measures two different starting points, not progress.
3. The last line holds exactly one gap. What would be lost if it held four?
The next eight lessons would be spread across four things and move slowly on all of them. One named gap is what makes the second half decidable.
Listening 1 · The Assessor

The Curve Nobody Warns You About

A language assessor on what improvement actually looks like. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say the first six weeks feel like, and why?
They feel fast, because the first things you learn are the ones you use twenty times a day, so the change is visible immediately.
2. What does she say is actually happening during a plateau?
The learning has moved to things that appear rarely, so the same amount of progress is spread across far fewer moments. Nothing has stopped; it has become harder to see.
3. What is her advice about measuring, and what does she say memory does?
Keep track of something small and countable, and never trust memory, because memory keeps the two bad days and quietly deletes the twenty ordinary good ones.
Listening 2 · Twelve Recordings

What a Year of Recordings Actually Showed

Somebody who recorded himself once a month for a year. Sound first — no text.

1. What did he expect the recordings to show, and what did they actually show?
He expected his vocabulary to grow. What actually changed was the pauses: the words were roughly the same, and the gaps between them almost disappeared.
2. What happened in the two months he did not record, and what does he conclude from it?
He fell behind and did not notice until the next recording. He concludes that the recording was not measuring the progress, it was causing part of it.
3. What does he say about listening back to your own voice?
That it is unpleasant for about three recordings and then it stops being about you at all. He says by the fourth one you are listening to the sentences, not to yourself.
