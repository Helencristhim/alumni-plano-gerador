Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

To bring up -- to start talking about a subject or a concern
"I need to bring up a concern about this dosage."
To put off -- to delay something until later
"We cannot put off the biopsy any longer."
To come across -- to find or meet something by chance
"I came across a rare case of pemphigus last week."
To look into -- to investigate a problem to find out more
"The team is looking into a possible drug reaction."
To point out -- to make somebody notice something
"The attending pointed out a finding I had missed."
To look up -- to search for information in a book or online
"If you do not know a term, look it up."
To take on -- to accept a responsibility or a challenge
"I decided to take on Step 3 during residency."
To fall behind -- to fail to keep the pace that is expected
"It is easy to fall behind on notes in the first month."
To catch up -- to reach the expected level after falling behind
"I will catch up on my notes tonight."
To get by -- to manage with what you have, but only just
"I can get by with basic phrases for now."
To deal with -- to take action to solve a problem
"The paperwork is the hardest thing to deal with."
To show up -- to arrive or appear, often for something planned
"Please show up at the office at seven on Monday."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word -- up, out, into, off -- and together they mean something new. Look is look; look up is search for information; look into is investigate. American hospitals use them all the time: a chief resident does not say we will investigate, she says we will look into it.

The grammar is about the object, and especially about pronouns. Some phrasal verbs are separable: the object can go in the middle or at the end. Look up the term and look the term up are both correct. But with a pronoun, the middle is the only place: look it up, never look up it. Point out, put off, bring up and take on work the same way.

Other phrasal verbs are inseparable: the object always goes at the end, pronoun or not. I came across it, could you look into it?, I will deal with it. And some need a third word before the object: you fall behind on your notes and you catch up on them.

Finally, some have no object at all. You show up at seven, you get by with the English you have, you fall behind and then you catch up.

1. Where does the pronoun go with a separable phrasal verb such as look up?
A At the end, as in look up it, the same as with any other verb.
B Before the verb: it look up.
C In the middle: look it up.
2. Which sentence is correct with an inseparable phrasal verb?
A Could you look it into?
B Could you look into it?
C Could you into look it?
3. What does the text say a chief resident says instead of we will investigate?
A We will look into it.
B We will look it up in the manual and then report back to you.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Phrasal Verbs: Separable and Inseparable

GRAMMAR

With a separable phrasal verb, a pronoun must go in the middle. With an inseparable one, it always goes at the end.

JobFormExample
Separablelook up, point out, put off, bring up, take onLook up the term / look the term up.
Separable + pronounpronoun in the middleLook it up. Point it out.
Inseparablecome across, look into, deal withI came across it. I will deal with it.
Three wordsfall behind on, catch up onI fell behind on my notes.
No objectshow up, get by, fall behind, catch upShe showed up at seven.

Never: look up it · point out it · look it into · come it across · I fell behind my notes
