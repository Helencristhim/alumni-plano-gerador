Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

How Often Do You...? -- My Professional Habits

In lesson 6 you learned to say when something happens. In this lesson you say how often — and that is the question every interviewer asks. Six words from your inbox (email, check, send, reply, update, late) and five little words that describe the way you work: always, usually, often, sometimes, never. The difficulty is not the meaning — you already know what “always” means. It is the place: in English these words have one address only. This Pre-class is only a first contact: come to class with the sound in your ear, not on the tip of your tongue.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Six words — and your whole inbox fits inside them. Two of them you already use every day (email and update). Listen to each one and repeat it out loud, alone. Three sound traps: check ends on a dry K (never “checky”), reply is stressed on the second syllable (re-PLY, rhyming with my) and update, as a noun, is stressed on the first (UP-date).

Email -- a message you send from your computer
"I always check my email at eight." Notice the verb that goes with email in English: it is check.
Check -- to look at something to see if it is right
"I check my email in the morning." Never “see my email” — in English you check your email.
Send -- to make a message go to someone
"I send the report on Friday."
Reply -- to answer a message
"I usually reply in the morning." Say re-PLY, with the stress on the second syllable.
Update -- short news about how something is going
"I send an update on Friday." It is not the whole report: it is three lines — where the account stands and what the next step is.
Late -- after the time it should be
"I am never late." In English being late goes with the verb to be: "I am late" — never "I have late".

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions. The words in bold are the grammar of this lesson — look at which side of the verb each one falls on.

Monday, eight in the morning. David sends an email explaining how the team works.

"Good morning, Paula. Welcome to the team. Let me tell you how we work.
We always start the day with email. I check my inbox at eight — every day.
Sofia usually sends her update on Friday, at five. She is never late.
We sometimes visit brokers on Thursday — not every week.
And I often write in English. My English is not perfect: I always make mistakes, and I send the email anyway."

Paula reads it twice and notices something. The five little words — always, usually, often, sometimes, never — are always glued to the verb. But not always on the same side: in "I always check" the word comes before the verb; in "She is never late" it comes after is. (That single difference is the whole lesson. And notice what David does when he makes a mistake: he sends it anyway.)

1. Why does "I always check" put the adverb BEFORE the verb, and "She is never late" put it AFTER?
A Because one sentence is positive and the other is negative.
B Because with an ordinary verb (check) the adverb comes before; with the verb to be (is), it comes after.
C Because it makes no difference — in English the adverb can go in both places.
2. How often does David check his email?
A Sometimes, when his schedule allows it.
B Every day, at eight — the team always starts the day with email.
C Once a week, on Friday.
3. Is Sofia ever late with the update?
A No. She usually sends it on Friday, at five — and she is never late.
B Yes, sometimes she sends it on Monday.
C The text does not say.
4. What does David do when he makes an English mistake in an email?
A He asks a colleague to check it first.
B He rewrites the email until it is perfect.
C He sends it anyway — he says he always makes mistakes and always sends.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Adverbs of frequency: always, usually, often, sometimes, never

GRAMMAR

Good news: you already know what these five words mean. Today's work is not the meaning. It is where they go in the sentence.

Where the adverb goesInglêsWhen you use it
Ordinary verb → BEFORE the verbI always check my email.The adverb sits in front of the verb.
Verb to be (am/is/are) → AFTERI am never late. · She is always busy.Here the adverb follows am / is / are.
Third person (lesson 4)She usually sends the update.The S stays on the verb, as always.
NegativeI don't usually reply at night.In the negative, it comes before don't.
QuestionHow often do you send the update?To ask about frequency.
Short answerEvery day. · Twice a month. · Not very often.Every day. · Twice a month. · Not very often.

The scale, from 100% to zero. always = 100% · usually = 90% · often = 70% · sometimes = 40% · never = 0%. Five words cover everything — you need no numbers and no arithmetic.

The most common mistake here: saying “I check always my email”. It is a stubborn one, because in your first language the word for “always” can move anywhere in the sentence and still be correct. In English it has one address only. It is not carelessness: it is your first language pushing a sentence that, in that language, is perfect.

The trick that solves both cases: before you open your mouth, look for am / is / are in the sentence. Is it there? The adverb comes after it (“I am never late”). Is it not? It comes before the verb (“I always check”). There is no third option — which is why this grammar, annoying as it is, comes cheap: one question settles the whole sentence.

Why this matters: “I visit brokers” says nothing about you. “I usually visit brokers every week” says that you have a method. In an interview the question is always How often do you...? — and whoever answers only “yes” sounds afraid; whoever answers with the full sentence sounds like someone who knows. Today's grammar is small; what it buys is not.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen to each sentence, then record your voice. Nobody is grading you — it is only you and your microphone. Careful with two of them: often is said OFF-en (the T is silent in American English) and usually is YOO-zhu-li. And listen to the rhythm: the adverb comes out weak, almost swallowed — the stress falls on the verb and on what follows it.

I always check my email in the morning.
How often do you send the report?
I am never late.
She usually replies in the afternoon.
I sometimes travel for work. Not very often.
I often send an update to the broker.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each real situation — the Zurich interview, the curious colleague, the broker who writes at eight in the evening.

The interviewer asks: "How often do you visit brokers?" You visit them every week. You answer:
A "Yes, I visit brokers." (this does not answer the frequency — and that is what he asked)
B "I usually visit brokers every week."
C "I visit always brokers."
You want to say that you are never late. The right sentence is:
A "I never am late."
B "I am never late."
C "I am late never."
You want to ask the broker how often he visits the office. You say:
A "How often you visit our office?"
B "How often do you visit our office?"
C "How much often do you visit our office?"
A broker emails you at eight in the evening. You never reply at night. You say:
A "I never reply at night. I usually reply in the morning."
B "I reply never at night."
C "Sorry, sorry, I answer now." (replying when you did not want to)
The interviewer asks: "Do you usually work with brokers directly?" Yes — it is your job, every day. You answer:
A "Yes. I usually work with brokers directly."
B "Yes. I work usually with brokers directly."
C "Yes, brokers."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record your answer. Speak for 60 seconds, with no script. Do not redo it more than twice. This is exactly the exercise you will do in class — and the homework audio afterwards.

A Zurich interviewer asks: "What do you usually do at work?" Answer in five sentences about your REAL routine today — and use a different adverb in each one: always, usually, often, sometimes, never. For example: "I always check my email at eight. I usually reply in the morning. I often talk to brokers. I sometimes travel for work. I never reply at night." It does not have to be perfect — if you put the adverb in the wrong place, keep going and carry on speaking. Remember David: he makes mistakes every day and sends it anyway.
