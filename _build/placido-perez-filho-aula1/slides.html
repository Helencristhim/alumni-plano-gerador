Questions After the Presentation

You know your business, and the presentation is the easy part. Then the audience starts asking: what if we ordered more, what if the price went up? Today you train the ear for the question and the grammar for the answer.

Chapter 3: The Code

If I Were You

Imagined situations, real answers

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Questions Have in Common?

"If we ordered twice the volume, would you lower the price?"

"If I were you, I would ask for the spec sheet first."

"If the lead time were shorter, we could buy more from you."

"What would happen if the price of aluminum went up?"

None of these situations is real now: they are imagined. Look at the red verbs: they look like the past, but they talk about the present or the future. Look at the blue words: would or could in the other half. And look at I were and the lead time were: not was.

JobFormExample
Imagine a situationIf + past simple, would + verbIf we ordered more, would you lower the price?
Be, for every personif I / he / she / it wereIf the lead time were shorter...
Give adviceIf I were you, I would / I’d...If I were you, I’d ask for a quote.
A possibilitycould or might instead of wouldWe could buy more from you.
The order can changewould-part first, no commaWhat would happen if the price went up?
The trapno would in the if-partNot if we would order. Say if we ordered.

In one line: imagined now or future = if + past, would + verb; with be, were for everybody.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Questions, at Real Speed

One Q&A, three recordings, one quote

Dialogue

The First Three Questions

M
Thank you, Plácido, that was very clear. Can I start the Q&A session with a hypothetical question?
P
Of course, Megan. Go ahead.
M
If we offered you a two-year contract, would you buy more from us than from your other suppliers?
P
Probably, yes. But it would depend on the lead time. If the lead time were shorter than ten weeks, I would move more of my orders to you.
M
Interesting. Could you elaborate a little? How much more?
P
Off the top of my head, about thirty percent more. I can give you a ballpark figure now and the exact numbers next week.
M
That works. And if the price of aluminum went up next year, would you still sign?
P
If I were you, I would put that in the contract. It is always a trade-off: a fixed price or a reliable delivery. Let me get back to you on that by Friday.
Comprehension

About Megan

1. What kind of contract does Megan imagine?
A two-year contract.
2. What does Megan ask him to do after his first answer?
To elaborate: to say how much more he would buy.
3. What is Megan’s last hypothetical question about?
The price of aluminum going up next year.
Listening 1 · A Buyer in London

Three Questions From the Floor

A British buyer asks questions after a presentation at a trade fair in London. Sound first — no text.

1. What would the buyer need before he placed an order?
The spec sheet, with the alloy and the thickness.
2. What does he ask about the volume?
If they doubled the volume, could the company scale up in time.
3. When does he want an answer about the price?
By the end of the week; a ballpark figure is fine for now.
Listening 2 · The Supplier’s Answer

Answering What If

A sales manager from an aluminum supplier in India answers a hypothetical question from a client. Sound first — no text.

1. What would happen to the lead time if the client ordered every month?
It would go down from twelve weeks to about eight.
2. Why can she not give a fixed price for two years?
Because the price of aluminum changes every day on the market.
3. What does she offer instead?
A fixed price for six months, and then a review.
Listening 3 · A Presentation Coach

The Questions You Cannot Rehearse

An Australian presentation coach talks about the Q&A session in a podcast. Sound first — no text.

1. According to him, what do most people prepare, and what do they forget?
They prepare the slides and forget the questions.
2. What should you do if you do not know the answer?
Say you will check and get back to the person, with a date.
3. Why does he like hypothetical questions?
Because they show that the audience is already imagining the deal.
