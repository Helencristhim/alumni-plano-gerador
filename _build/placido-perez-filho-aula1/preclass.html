Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Questions After the Presentation -- What If?

After the presentation, the questions start, and many of them begin with what if. Key words: a Q&A session, to elaborate, a ballpark figure, off the top of my head, to get back to someone, to back up, a quote, a spec sheet, lead time, to scale up, reliable, a trade-off. Structure: the second conditional.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A Q&A session -- the part of a presentation when the audience asks questions
"The Q&A session was longer than the presentation."
To elaborate -- to give more details about something you said
"Could you elaborate on the delivery times?"
A ballpark figure -- a number that is approximately right, not exact
"I can give you a ballpark figure today and the exact price tomorrow."
Off the top of my head -- from memory, without checking any notes
"Off the top of my head, we import about forty percent."
To get back to someone -- to contact someone later with an answer
"Let me check the numbers and get back to you by Friday."
To back up -- to support an idea or a claim with facts or numbers
"Our clients back up what I said about quality."
A quote -- a written price for a specific order
"We asked three suppliers for a quote."
A spec sheet -- a document with the technical details of a product
"The spec sheet shows the thickness and the alloy."
Lead time -- the time between the order and the delivery
"The lead time from Asia is about ten weeks."
To scale up -- to make something bigger, or to produce more of it
"If demand grew, could you scale up production?"
Reliable -- that you can trust to work well every time
"Our clients need a reliable supplier more than a cheap one."
A trade-off -- a balance between two good things that you cannot have at the same time
"There is always a trade-off between price and lead time."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At the end of his presentation, a buyer asks Plácido a hypothetical question: “If we ordered twice the volume, would you lower the price?” He answers with care: “It would depend on the lead time. If it were shorter, we could offer a better price.”

The buyer asks him to elaborate. Plácido does not have the numbers with him, so he gives a ballpark figure, off the top of his head, and promises to get back to the buyer with the exact quote.

At the end, the buyer says: “If I were you, I would send the spec sheet with the quote. Good numbers back up a good presentation.”

1. Why does the buyer say “if we ordered” and not “if we order”?
A Because the order happened last year.
B Because it is an imagined situation, not a real plan yet.
C Because the buyer is British.
2. Why does Plácido give a ballpark figure?
A Because he does not have the exact numbers with him.
B Because the buyer asked for a fixed price.
C Because the price is a secret.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A “If I was you, I would send the spec sheet.”
B “If I were you, I would send the spec sheet.”
C “If I would be you, I send the spec sheet.”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Second Conditional -- If + Past, Would + Verb

GRAMMAR

Use it for situations that are imagined, not real: a hypothetical order, a price that could change, advice. The verb after if looks like the past, but the meaning is present or future.

JobFormExample
Imagine a situationIf + past simple, would + verbIf we ordered more, would you lower the price?
Be, for every personif I / he / she / it wereIf the lead time were shorter...
Give adviceIf I were you, I would / I’d...If I were you, I’d ask for a quote.
A possibilitycould or might instead of wouldWe could buy more from you.
The order can changewould-part first, no commaWhat would happen if the price went up?
The trapno would in the if-partNot if we would order. Say if we ordered.

Never: If I was you · If we would order more · If the price were lower, we will buy · I’ll get back you
