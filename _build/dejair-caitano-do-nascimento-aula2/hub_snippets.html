Your Research Story -- Saying What You Investigate

Describing your own research out loud, and asking other people about theirs. Key words: to investigate, to publish, to collaborate, to involve, treatment, side effect, nerve damage, clinical trial. Structure: present simple questions and negatives with do and does.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. You have read all eight of these in papers for years. This week you start saying them.

To investigate -- to study something carefully to find an answer
"I investigate why nerve damage causes pain years later."
To publish -- to put your work in a scientific journal
"We published two papers on leprosy pain last year."
To collaborate -- to work together with another person or group
"We collaborate with a hospital in the north of Brazil."
To involve -- to have something as a necessary part
"My study involves ninety patients and four hospitals."
Treatment -- what a doctor gives a patient to make them better
"The treatment works, but it takes about six months."
Side effect -- something a drug does that you did not want
"Patients stop the treatment because of the side effects."
Nerve damage -- injury to a nerve, so the signal does not pass
"Leprosy causes nerve damage, and the pain comes after."
Clinical trial -- a study that tests a treatment on real patients
"Our clinical trial involves six centres and two hundred people."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Yelena Sokolova investigates chronic pain after surgery. Her group does not develop new drugs — they test treatments that already exist, and they measure the side effects that patients report at home. Her work involves about four hundred patients every year, and she collaborates with two hospitals in her city. At the coffee break a researcher asks her: "Do you work with children too?" She answers: "No, I do not. And does your group publish in English?"
Why does the question use "Do you work" and not "Do you works"?
A Because the question is about the future
B Because the helper "do" carries the tense, so the main verb stays in its plain form
C Because "work" is an irregular verb
Why is it "does your group publish" and not "do your group publish"?
A Because "your group" is one thing — third person singular, so the helper is "does"
B Because a group has many people in it
C Because the verb "publish" ends in -sh

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Questions and Negatives

GRAMMAR

In the present simple, a question or a negative needs a helper: do or does. The helper carries the tense and the -s, so the main verb goes back to its plain form.

question — I / you / we / theydo + subject + verbDo you collaborate with hospitals?
question — he / she / itdoes + subject + verbDoes your group publish in English?
negative — I / you / we / theydo not + verbWe do not develop new drugs.
negative — he / she / itdoes not + verbShe does not work with children.
wh- questionwh- + do / does + subject + verbWhere does she collect the data?

One -s per sentence. "Does she publishes?" is wrong — the -s is already inside "does".

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz
A researcher asks about your group's work with new drugs. You want to say your group does not do that. What do you say?
A "We not develop new drugs."
B "We do not develop new drugs. We test treatments that already exist."
C "No drugs here."
You want to know whether her institute publishes in English. Which question is correct?
A "Does your institute publishes in English?"
B "Does your institute publish in English?"
C "Your institute publishes in English?"
Someone asks how big your study is. Which answer is clearest?
A "It involves about ninety patients, in three centres."
B "Ninety. Three."
C "It is a big study, very big."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
A researcher from another country sits down next to you and asks what you investigate. Record ninety seconds: what you investigate, what your study involves, who you collaborate with, one thing your group does NOT do, and one question you want to ask her about her own work. No script -- just speak.
