Chapter 3: Which One Do You Mean

Two Commas, Two Meanings

One identifies. The other just adds a fact.

Grammar Discovery

Which Two Tell You Which One?

"The colleague who handles the filings is based in São Paulo."

"Marcelo, who handles the filings, is based in São Paulo."

"The report that we sent on Friday has been approved."

"The third-quarter report, which took three weeks, was well received."

Cover the blue parts with your hand and the sentence still tells you who or what. Cover the red ones and it does not.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

One Email, and No Names

One conversation, two recordings, one org chart

Dialogue

Who Should I Write To?

G
I have to send something to your team tomorrow and I do not know a single name. Who should I write to?
J
Depends what it is about. The colleague who handles the monthly filings is Marcelo, and he is based in São Paulo like me.
G
It is about the file we reopened in March. The one that came back from the auditors.
J
Then it is Ana, who is covering for Marcelo until the fifteenth. She liaises with the auditors anyway, so she knows it better than he does.
G
Good. And is that in her remit, or am I creating a problem for somebody?
J
It is in mine, technically. But she is the point of contact for anything that comes from the auditors, so write to her and loop me in.
G
That is the clearest answer I have had all week. Most people give me four names and no explanation.
J
Four names is not an answer, it is a list. And who is the person in Chicago I should write to, if the same thing happens the other way round?
Comprehension

About Greg

1. What is Greg's problem at the start of the conversation?
He has to send something to her team tomorrow and does not know a single name.
2. Which file is he writing about?
The one they reopened in March — the one that came back from the auditors.
3. What does he say about the answers he usually gets?
That most people give him four names and no explanation, and that hers was the clearest answer he had had all week.
Listening 1 · An Onboarding Lead

Why a Name Alone Is Useless

On the difference between a list of people and an answer. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say happens when you give a new person a list of names?
They write to the wrong one, or to all of them, and both outcomes cost somebody an afternoon.
2. What does he say the useful answer always contains?
What the person does, before the name — and a reason, so the new person can work out the next case themselves.
3. What does he say about the second week?
If you explained the rule rather than the name, by the second week they stop asking, because they can work it out.
Listening 2 · Business Radio

The Comma That Cost Five Million

A short piece on punctuation that ended up in court. Sound first — no text.

1. What were the two possible readings of the clause?
Either the exemption covered one combined activity, or it covered two separate ones — and the punctuation was the only thing that decided.
2. What does the piece say the lawyers argued about?
Not the facts, which nobody disputed, but which of the two readings the sentence actually supported.
3. What is the lesson the piece draws for anybody who writes rules?
That a sentence which can be read two ways will eventually be read both ways, by people with opposite interests — so the writer should remove the ambiguity, not rely on the reader.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I join two pieces of information into one sentence instead of saying them separately.
I leave the commas out when the information says which one, and put them in when it only adds a fact.
I use who for people, which or that for things, where for places and whose for owners.
I never repeat the pronoun: "the person I spoke to", not "the person who I spoke to her".
I know the words: to narrow something down, a counterpart, to liaise with, to report to somebody, cross-functional, a remit, to be based in, to cover for somebody, a point of contact, to loop somebody in.
