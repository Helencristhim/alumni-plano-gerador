Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

What the Requirements Say -- Reading the Document They Send

They liked the call, so they sent you a document. It is eleven pages long, it never says who does anything, and everything in your proposal depends on reading it correctly. Key words: a requirement, scope, to comply with, a constraint, an assumption, to validate, a milestone, to sign off, a dependency, an exception. Structure: the present passive.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A requirement -- something the customer says the solution has to do
"There are forty requirements, and six of them decide the price."
Scope -- everything that is included in the work, and nothing else
"Training is not in the scope. That is a separate conversation."
To comply with -- to do what a rule or a standard demands
"The platform complies with their data rules, and we can prove it."
A constraint -- a limit you cannot change and have to work inside
"The constraint is the maintenance window: two hours, once a month."
An assumption -- something taken as true without proof, so that a plan can be made
"The plan has one assumption: that their data is clean. It never is."
To validate -- to check that something really works as it should
"Nothing goes live until the finance team has validated a full month."
A milestone -- a date on which one clear part of the work is finished
"There are three milestones, and payment is attached to two of them."
To sign off -- to approve something formally so that the next step can start
"Their security team has to sign off before anybody writes a line of code."
A dependency -- something that has to happen before another thing can start
"The only dependency is their firewall change, and it is not on anybody's list."
An exception -- a case that the rule does not cover
"Ninety per cent is standard. It is the exceptions that take the six weeks."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A requirements document is written in one voice from the first page to the last, and it is not the voice anybody speaks in. "The data is sent to the ERP every night." "Access is granted only after the security review." "Nothing is deleted without a written request." Read those three again and ask the only question that matters commercially: who does it?

The answer is not there, and that is deliberate. The form is is or are plus the past participle, and it exists precisely because the action matters and the person does not. Sometimes the person is added at the end, after by — "all the changes are approved by the finance team" — but only when somebody decided it was worth naming.

The commercial risk is simple. A sentence with no person in it will find one at signature, and it is usually the vendor. So the reading has three passes. First, what is outside the scope. Second, every line with no person in it. Third, the dates: the milestones, who has to sign off, and every dependency that belongs to the customer.

Two words deserve special care. A constraint is a limit you accept and work inside — a two-hour maintenance window, once a month. An assumption is something taken as true so that the plan can exist, and it is the sentence that decides whether the estimate holds. If supplier files arrive in three formats and your estimate assumed one, the estimate was not wrong: the assumption was. And an exception is worth naming too, because ninety per cent of any integration is standard and it is the exceptions that take the six weeks.

1. "The data is sent to the ERP every night." Who sends it?
A The customer, because it is their document.
B The ERP, because it is the last word in the sentence.
C The sentence does not say. That is the point of the structure, and it is why you have to ask.
2. Why does the text say a sentence with no person in it is a commercial risk?
A Because at signature somebody will be assigned to it, and it is usually the vendor.
B Because passive sentences are always badly written.
C Because lawyers cannot read the passive voice.
3. "The estimate was not wrong: the assumption was." What is the difference the text is making?
A There is no real difference, it is just a polite way of saying the same thing.
B A wrong estimate is a mistake; a wrong assumption is something both sides wrote down, so it becomes a conversation instead of blame.
C That estimates should never be written down at all.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Present Passive

GRAMMAR

Is or are plus the participle, and the person appears only if somebody decided to name them.

JobFormExample
The action matters, the person does notis / are + past participleThe data is sent every night.
Say who, when it matters+ by + personapproved by the finance team
Agreementis with one thing, are with severalAccess is granted · Changes are approved
Regular participleverb + -edapprove → approved · delete → deleted
Irregular participlelearn it as a pairsend → sent · give → given · keep → kept
Negativeis / are not + participleTraining is not included in the scope.

Never: The data is send · The changes are approve · Access are granted · The logs are kept for the security team (when you mean by) · It is depend on their team

Stage 2: Put the Response in Order

Order

Six lines of a response to a requirements section, out of order. Put them the way you would send them.

?"Thank you for section 4. Here is one line per requirement."
?"The nightly transfer is supported as standard."
?"The two-hour maintenance window is accepted as a constraint."
?"Historic data migration is not included in the scope and is priced separately."
?"The firewall rule change is a dependency on your team."
?"Our estimate is built on one assumption: supplier files arrive in a single format."
