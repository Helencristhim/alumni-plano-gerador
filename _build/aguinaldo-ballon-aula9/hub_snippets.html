The Panel, Again -- Disagreeing Politely

Another speaker says something you disagree with, on a public stage. Key words: a keynote, to interrupt, a rebuttal, a point of view, to concede, a counterargument, to hold the floor, to wrap up, to elaborate, an audience. Structure: would, could and might for polite disagreement.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A keynote -- the main speech at a conference, usually at the beginning
"The minister gave the keynote on the first morning."
To interrupt -- to start speaking while another person is still speaking
"Sorry to interrupt, but I would like to add a point."
A rebuttal -- an answer that tries to show that another argument is wrong
"Her rebuttal was short, polite and very clear."
A point of view -- a particular way of thinking about a topic
"From a regulator's point of view, stability comes first."
To concede -- to admit that part of the other person's argument is true
"I concede that the rules have improved."
A counterargument -- an argument against another argument
"That is a strong counterargument, but the data is old."
To hold the floor -- expression: to continue speaking and keep the attention of the room
"Speak slowly and you will hold the floor."
To wrap up -- to finish a speech, a meeting or a discussion
"Let me wrap up with one last point."
To elaborate -- to explain something in more detail
"Could you elaborate on that idea?"
An audience -- the people who listen to a speech or watch a performance
"The audience was mostly investors and lawyers."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

After the keynote, the panel starts. A banker says that investors do not care about regulation. Aguinaldo raises his hand: "Could I add something here?"

He does not attack. First he concedes a point: "That might be true for short-term funds." Then he gives his rebuttal: "But I would say that infrastructure is different." The banker asks him to elaborate, and he gives one example with numbers.

At the end, he asks: "Would you agree that stable rules are cheaper for everybody?" The audience applauds. Notice how the modal verbs make the form polite, while the argument stays firm.

1. What does Aguinaldo do before he disagrees?
A He concedes a point.
B He leaves the panel.
C He agrees with everything.
2. Why does he say "I would say" and not "The rules are the problem"?
A Because he is not sure about the argument.
B Because the modal makes the form more polite, but the argument is the same.
C Because "would" is the future.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A "Could I to add something here?"
B "Could I add something here?"
C "Could I adding something here?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Would, Could and Might for Polite Disagreement

GRAMMAR

Modal verbs soften the form, not the argument. After a modal, always the base form of the verb.

JobFormExample
Give a strong opinion politelyI would say (that) ...I would say the rules are the real problem.
Ask to speak or interruptCould I + verb ...?Could I add something here?
Concede, then disagreeThat might be true, but ...That might be true, but the data shows the opposite.
Invite agreementWould you agree that ...?Would you agree that stability matters?
Suggest another viewIt could / might be argued that ...It could be argued that investors care about rules.
The trapthe modal is followed by the base form, never toNot I would to say. Say I would say.

Never: I would to say · Can I to add something? · That might is true · You would agree that...? (as a question, say Would you agree...?)

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to join the debate politely. You say:
A "Stop, I want to speak."
B "Could I add something here?"
C "Can I to add something here?"
You want to disagree, but concede a point first. You say:
A "That might be true, but the data shows the opposite."
B "You are completely wrong."
C "That might is true, but the data shows the opposite."
You want to give a strong opinion politely. You say:
A "I would to say that the rules are the problem."
B "I would say that the rules are the problem."
C "I will saying that the rules are the problem."
Another speaker interrupts you. The best answer is:
A "If I could just finish my example, I will answer your point."
B "Be quiet, please."
C "Sorry, sorry, you can speak."
