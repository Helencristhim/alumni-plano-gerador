The Email After the Call

Eight words from the inbox — attached, regarding, as per, clarification, approval, pending, reference number, acknowledge — and the passive voice: the shape that states the fact without pointing a finger. Around fifteen minutes.

Stage 1.1: Words from the inbox

Vocabulary

You read all eight of these every week. Today you start writing them. Tap Listen and say each one out loud.

Attached — sent together with the email, as a file
"Please find the revised proposal attached."
Regarding — about, on the subject of — the formal one
"Regarding the onboarding timeline, two things changed."
As per — exactly as agreed before — it points back to something
"As per our call, the parallel run starts in October."
Clarification — making something clearer, when it was not clear enough
"One clarification on the scope, before you sign."
Approval — the formal yes that lets the work start
"We are waiting for approval from head office."
Pending — waiting, not decided yet, still open
"The contract is pending approval, not pending signature."
Reference number — the code that lets both sides find the same document
"Please quote the reference number on the invoice."
Acknowledge — to tell someone their message arrived, before you can answer it
"Just acknowledging receipt — a full answer follows on Monday."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

A follow-up email sent the same afternoon. Read it once, then answer.

Dear David,

Thank you for your time this morning. As per our call, here is what was agreed.

On our side. The revised proposal will be sent by Wednesday, including the four-week timeline and one clarification on late data.

On your side. The employee file needs to be sent to us by Friday.

Still open. The budget has not been approved yet. I understand London meets on the twentieth.

One more thing: the last onboarding of this size, we completed two days early.

Please quote reference number AUR-2291 in any reply.

Kind regards,
Fábia
Why "the budget has not been approved yet" and not "you have not approved the budget"?
A Because it states the same fact without pointing a finger at him.
B Because she does not know who approves it.
Why is the onboarding line the only one in the active voice?
A Because it happened in the past.
B Because the credit is hers — and the active voice is what makes that land.
What does each of the three headings give the reader?
A Who does what, and by when.
B A summary of the whole commercial proposal.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

The fact stays. The person steps back.

That is the whole job of the passive in a business email.

Active

I sent the proposal.
You have not approved it.
We will issue the invoice.

Passive — be + past participle

The proposal was sent.
It has not been approved yet.
The invoice will be issued.

Name the doer only when it helps you: "Every process is audited by an external firm." There, by whom is the whole point.

Passive is not always better. When the credit is yours, the active voice sells: "We delivered it two days early", never "it was delivered". Passive for the problem, active for the achievement.

Stage 4: Writing the follow-up

Quiz
The client has not approved the budget. Which line keeps the relationship intact?
A "You still have not approved the budget."
B "The budget has not been approved yet."
Your team finished two days early. How do you write it?
A "We completed the onboarding two days early."
B "The onboarding was completed two days early."
A long email arrives on a day when you cannot answer it properly.
A Leave it and answer fully on Monday.
B "Just acknowledging receipt — a full answer follows on Monday."

Stage 5: Dictate the email

Reflection
Record ninety seconds in English. A call has just ended. Dictate the follow-up email out loud, in three blocks: what was agreed, what you will do and by when, and what you need from them and by when. Use the passive for anything that is late or open, and the active voice for anything your firm did well. Do not write it first — speak it.
