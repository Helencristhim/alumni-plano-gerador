What You Knew When You Decided

Telling the story of a decision instead of the outcome, and putting the events in the right order. Key words: rationale, a trade-off, to weigh up, buy-in, a sticking point, to escalate, a fallback, at stake, a contingency, to sign off, the upside, to bite the bullet. Structure: past perfect and past perfect continuous.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These twelve describe the inside of a decision — the part nobody sees from the outside, and the part a board actually wants.

Rationale -- the reasoning that makes a decision defensible to somebody else
"The rationale fitted on one page, which is why the board approved it in ten minutes."
A trade-off -- what you gave up in order to get something else
"The trade-off was goodwill with two clients for four months of visibility."
To weigh up -- to compare options carefully before deciding
"We weighed up cutting before the renewal against cutting after it."
Buy-in -- agreement from the people who have to make it work
"Approval is a signature. Buy-in is the controllers not going around you."
A sticking point -- the one issue that is stopping an agreement
"The sticking point was never the money. It was who would sign the reconciliation."
To escalate -- to take a decision to somebody more senior
"Escalating is not failing. It is deciding who should own the decision."
A fallback -- the plan you use if the first one fails
"We had a fallback, and saying so out loud is what got the board comfortable."
At stake -- what you lose if it goes wrong
"What was at stake was not the margin. It was whether anybody believed the forecast again."
A contingency -- money or time set aside for what you cannot predict
"A contingency you have to explain twice is usually the right size."
To sign off -- to give formal approval, and to take responsibility for it
"I signed it off, which means it is mine regardless of who built the model."
The upside -- the best realistic outcome, not the best imaginable one
"The upside was four months of visibility, and that was all it ever was."
To bite the bullet -- to finally do the unpleasant thing you have been postponing
"We bit the bullet in the third quarter, about two quarters later than we should have."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

By the time the board met in November, three things had already happened. We had lost the account in February, we had rebuilt the forecast twice, and we had been running the old reporting system alongside the new one for about eighteen months — which nobody had ever formally decided to do.

So the decision in front of us was not whether to cut. It was whether to cut before or after the renewal date. The rationale either way fitted on one page. What tipped it was the trade-off on cash: cutting early cost us goodwill with two clients and bought four months of visibility. We had agreed a fallback with the audit committee the week before, and the contingency covered two months of the gap, which is why I was willing to sign it off.

The sticking point was never the money. It was who would sign the reconciliation, and I got buy-in by taking the rationale to the two controllers before the board saw it. What I did not know at that moment, and I want to be clear about it, was that the market would soften in the second quarter. That made the decision look better than it was. What was at stake was not the margin; it was whether anybody would believe the forecast again.

1. "By the time the board met, we had rebuilt the forecast twice." Which happened first?
A The rebuilding. The past perfect marks the earlier of the two moments.
B The board meeting, because it is the main clause.
C They happened at the same time.
2. Why "had been running" rather than "had run"?
A Because the action finished before the board met.
B Because it was still going on at that moment, and the eighteen months is the point.
C Because "run" is irregular and cannot follow "had".
3. What does the text say the decision actually was?
A Whether to cut the cost base at all.
B Whether to cut before or after the renewal date.
C Whether to replace the reporting system.
4. Why does the text point out what he did not know?
A Because the outcome was partly luck, and saying so separates judgement from luck.
B To explain that the decision was somebody else's responsibility.
C To apologize for a mistake in the forecast.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Which Thing Had Already Happened

GRAMMAR

A decision story has at least three moments: what was already true, what happened, and what you did. One tense keeps them in order.

FormWhat it saysExample
Past simpleThe main moment of the storyThe board met in November.
Past perfect
had + participle		Already finished before that momentWe had modelled three scenarios.
Past perfect continuous
had been + -ing		Still going on at that moment, and for how longWe had been running two systems for eighteen months.
Negative and questionAt that point we had not agreed a fallback. — Had you already decided before the meeting?
Time markersby the time, before, already, at that point, by then all signal that the earlier event needs the past perfect.
You do not always need itWhen before or after already makes the order obvious, the past simple is fine. The past perfect earns its place when the order would otherwise be ambiguous.
Why it matters in an interviewWithout it the listener reconstructs the sequence themselves, and they get it wrong — usually in a way that makes you look slower than you actually were.

In speech had contracts to 'd and the participle is the only clue left: we'd modelled, we'd been running. That is why this is a listening point as much as a speaking one.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A board member wants the decision, not the outcome. Choose the version that takes them into the room.

"Walk me through the hardest decision you have signed off." The strongest opening is:
A "By the time the board met, we had lost the account and rebuilt the forecast twice."
B "We chose option B and fortunately it worked out very well."
C "It was a very difficult period for everybody in the group."
Which sentence is correct?
A "By the time the board met, we modelled three scenarios."
B "We had ran two systems for eighteen months."
C "At that point we had not agreed any fallback."
You are asked what the trade-off was. The best answer:
A Names both sides: what you gave up and what you got.
B Explains why there was really no downside at all.
C Lists everything the decision improved.
"When do you escalate rather than decide?" The most senior answer is:
A "When I am not sure enough to take responsibility for it."
B "When what is at stake belongs to somebody else. Escalating is deciding who owns the decision."
C "Rarely. I prefer to solve things at my own level whenever possible."
"What did you not know at that moment?" The right instinct is:
A Name one thing that was genuinely not knowable, and say what effect it had.
B Say that you had all the information you needed at the time.
C Use it to explain that the result was not really your responsibility.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is correcting this one, and there is no score.

"Walk me through the decision-making process behind the most significant structural change you led." Speak for about three minutes, one take, no notes, in four moves: what had already happened before the decision point, what the real question was, the trade-off, and what you did not know at that moment. Use at least two past perfect sentences. Do not soften the fourth move — naming what you could not see is a fact about the information you had, not a judgement about you.

Past simple, past continuous and past perfect working together in one story. Connection to Lesson 11: a decision story has at least three moments, and without the past perfect the listener rebuilds the order themselves — usually in a way that makes you look slower than you were.

Talk with transcript
"How to make hard choices" -- Ruth Chang, TED (15 min)

A philosopher on why the hardest decisions are hard: not because we lack information, but because the options are not comparable on a single scale. Connection to Lesson 11: that is precisely what a trade-off is, and being able to say it out loud is what separates a decision story from a result.

Tip: watch once, then write the trade-off of one real decision on a single line, with both sides of it. If you cannot name what you gave up, you have not described a decision — you have described an outcome.
