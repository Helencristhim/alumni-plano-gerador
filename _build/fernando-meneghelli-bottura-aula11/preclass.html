Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

Under Fire -- Three Hard Questions in a Row

Conceding a fact without losing your position, naming the premise of a question, and giving the same answer again when they press. Key words: to press, to bristle, poise, to reframe, a premise, relentless, to rebut, terse, to stay on message, to take it on the chin, to temper, unflappable. Structure: contrast and concession (although, whereas, having said that, even so).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These twelve are for disagreeing with somebody more senior than you, in a language that is not yours — firmly, and never defensively.

To press -- to keep pushing on the same point after the first answer
“She pressed on the same number three times before she moved on.”
To bristle -- to react visibly, as if the question were unfair
“He bristled at the word "decline", and the panel saw it.”
Poise -- calm control while you are being judged
“What they remember is the poise between the questions, not the answers.”
To reframe -- to describe the same fact in a more accurate way
“Let me reframe that: it was a decline in one client, not in the business.”
A premise -- the assumption a question is built on
“The premise of that question is that we lost the account. We did not.”
Relentless -- continuing without any pause or softening
“The last ten minutes were relentless, and that was the point.”
To rebut -- to answer a claim with evidence, not with feeling
“I rebutted the number with the audited figure, and then I stopped.”
Terse -- very short, to the point of sounding cold
“A terse answer is fine under pressure, as long as it is complete.”
To stay on message -- to keep returning to the point you came to make
“Whatever they ask, I stay on message: cost first, then growth.”
To take it on the chin -- to accept criticism without arguing
“I took that one on the chin, because the criticism was fair.”
To temper -- to make a strong statement less absolute
“I would temper that slightly: it worked in two of the three units.”
Unflappable -- never visibly shaken, whatever happens
“The CFO they hired was unflappable, and that is a skill, not a personality.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The three questions came together, with no pause: the margin, the eight months without a role, and the fact that I have never worked outside Brazil. Although the margin fell in those two years, the cash position improved every quarter, and that is the sentence I used. Not because it sounds good, but because both halves are true.

What I have learned is that a panel is not waiting for a defence. They are waiting to see whether I bristle. The first year of that decline is mine: I moved late on the overhead. Having said that, two competitors in the same market held their margin, so the market is context, not an explanation. One fact, one contrast, and then I stop.

The third question is the one I reframe, because its premise is not quite right. I have never lived abroad, whereas I have reported to New York and to a French group for nine years. When somebody presses the same point a third time, I give the same figure and the same reason, almost in the same words, and I offer the audited page. Saying it again calmly is stronger than finding a new way to say it.

1. "Although the margin fell, the cash improved." What is the speaker doing with the first half?
A Denying that the margin fell.
B Accepting it, and keeping a second fact standing next to it.
C Explaining why the market was difficult.
2. Why does he say the market is "context, not an explanation"?
A Because two competitors in the same market held their margin.
B Because the market was not difficult that year.
C Because the panel asked him not to mention it.
3. What does he do with the third question?
A He accepts the premise and apologises for it.
B He names the part of the premise that is not right, and reframes it.
C He answers a different question instead.
4. What does he do when somebody presses the same point a third time?
A He finds a new way to explain it.
B He gives the same figure and reason again, calmly, and offers the evidence.
C He asks the panel to move on.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Two Facts, Both True

GRAMMAR

Concession says: this is true, and so is the thing I am about to say. It is the only structure that lets you agree with a hard question and keep your position in the same sentence.

FormMeaningExample
although / even though + sentenceConcede a fact without losing your ownAlthough the margin fell, the cash improved every quarter.
whereas / while + sentencePut two different facts side by sideI have never lived abroad, whereas I have reported to New York for nine years.
Having said that / That saidA bridge: what I just said is true, and so is thisWe moved late on the overhead. Having said that, the decision itself was right.
Even soKeep your position after conceding
The trapNever use but in the same clause as although, and never despite of — it is despite or in spite of.
Why it matters in an interview"I used to find that difficult, and I am used to it now" answers half the hostile questions in any process. It shows change without claiming to have been perfect — which is precisely what a panel is listening for.

The three together make a complete arc in one breath: what you used to do, what you are used to now, and how long it took to get used to it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The question has an accusation inside it. Choose the answer that is not defensive.

An interviewer asks three hard questions together. The first thing you say is:
A "There are three parts to that. I will take them in your order."
B "Could you repeat the second one, please? I was not ready."
C "Let me start with the easiest one."
"Your margin fell in the last two years." The strongest opening is:
A "The market was very difficult for everybody that year."
B "Although the margin fell, the cash position improved every quarter."
C "It fell, but that was not really my responsibility."
The same number is questioned for the third time. You:
A Find a new way to explain it, so it sounds better.
B Give the same figure and the same reason, and offer the evidence.
C Say you would rather move on to another point.
A question is built on something that is not true. The move is:
A Answer it anyway, to avoid an argument.
B Name the premise, then give the accurate version.
C Say the question is unfair.
You have conceded and given one piece of evidence. Now you:
A Add a third sentence to be safe.
B Stop, and let the silence sit there.
C Ask whether the answer was good enough.
