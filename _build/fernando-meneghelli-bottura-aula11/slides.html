Chapter 3: The Code

Which Thing Had Already Happened

Two pasts, and the order between them

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"By the time the board met, we had modelled three scenarios."

"We had been running two systems for eighteen months before anyone called it a problem."

"The client left in February. We had not rebuilt the forecast at that point."

"I signed it off because we had agreed a fallback the week before."

Every one has two moments in the past. Which came first, and what tells you?

FormWhat it saysExample
Past simpleThe main moment of the storyThe board met in November.
Past perfect
had + participle		Something already finished before that momentWe had modelled three scenarios.
Past perfect continuous
had been + -ing		Something still going on at that moment, and how long forWe had been running two systems for eighteen months.
The markersby the time, before, already, at that point, by then all signal that the earlier event needs the past perfect.
You do not always need itWhen the order is already obvious from before or after, the past simple is fine. The past perfect earns its place when the order would otherwise be ambiguous — which, in a decision story, is most of the time.
Why it matters in an interviewA decision story has at least three moments: what was already true, what happened, and what you did. Without the past perfect the listener has to reconstruct the order, and they will get it wrong — usually in a way that makes you look slower than you were.

In speech had contracts to 'd, and the participle is the only clue left. We'd modelled, we'd been running.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"By the time the board met, we modelled three scenarios."
"By the time the board met, we had modelled three scenarios."
"We had ran two systems for eighteen months."
"We had been running two systems for eighteen months."
"I signed it off because we agreed a fallback the week before."
"I signed it off because we had agreed a fallback the week before."

After had comes the past participle: modelled, agreed, run. And the continuous keeps its been.

Chapter 4: In the Room

A Non-Executive Director Asks

She has read the outcome. She wants the decision

Dialogue

Not the Outcome. The Decision

M
I have read what happened. What I would like is the decision itself. Take me to the room.
F
By the time the board met in November, we had lost the account, we had rebuilt the forecast twice, and we had been running two systems for about eighteen months.
M
So what was actually on the table?
F
Not whether to cut. Whether to cut before or after the renewal date. That was the real question, and the rationale either way fitted on one page.
M
And what tipped it?
F
The trade-off on cash. Cutting early cost us goodwill with two clients and bought four months of visibility. We also had agreed a fallback the week before, which is why I was willing to sign it off.
M
Good. Now the question I actually came for. What did you not know at that moment?
F
Which way the renewal would go, and I want to be clear about the other one: I could not see that the market would soften in the second quarter. That made the decision look better than it was.

Listening

Outcomes Are Half Luck

A board member on what she is actually listening for. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does she say the outcome does not interest her?
Because outcomes are luck as often as judgement, and she can read the outcome anyway.
2. What is the difference between the two candidates she describes?
One says they chose option B and it worked, which tells her nothing. The other says what had already happened, what had been modelled, and what they had no visibility on — which shows how they think.
3. Why does she say naming what you did not know is rare?
Because it feels like admitting you were not in control — and, as she puts it, you were not, and nobody is.

The Paper

What the Board Actually Saw

Board Paper 04 — November
Cost Base — Timing Decision
Author: CFO
Decision required

The question

Not whether to reduce the cost base, but whether to do it before or after the renewal date in March.

The trade-off

Early: four months of cash visibility, at the cost of goodwill with two clients. Late: goodwill preserved, no visibility until April.

Fallback

If the renewal is lost, the contingency covers two months of the gap. Agreed with the audit committee on the 12th.

What we do not know: which way the renewal will go, and whether the market softens in Q2. Neither is knowable before the decision date.

1. How does the paper frame the question, and why does that framing matter?
Not whether to cut, but when — which turns a judgement about courage into a judgement about timing.
2. What does the fallback section do for the reader?
It shows the downside is already covered and already agreed with the audit committee, which is what makes approval easy.
3. Why is there a formal section for what is not known?
Because it is a normal part of a board paper — naming the unknowable is a professional standard, not an admission of weakness.
