Two at Once -- The Twins, College, and What It Costs

The conversation every neighbor starts, and the words on every letter a university sends. Key words: tuition, a scholarship, in-state, to apply to, a major, a senior, financial aid, a dorm, to take out a loan, sticker shock, to be on track, a gap year. Structure: the three ways English talks about the future -- will, going to and the present continuous.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Tuition -- the money a college charges you for the classes themselves
"Tuition at a private university is close to sixty thousand dollars a year."
A scholarship -- money a school gives a student for grades, for a sport or for a talent
"She got a small scholarship, and it took nine thousand off the price."
In-state -- at the lower price, because your family lives and pays taxes in that state
"The in-state price is about a third of what everybody else pays."
To apply to -- to send a school your file and ask for a place
"They are applying to six schools next year."
A major -- the main subject a student chooses to study at college
"She has not chosen a major yet, and that is completely normal."
A senior -- a student in the last year of high school, or of college
"Both girls will be seniors next year."
Financial aid -- money the college takes off the price after it looks at what your family earns
"We filed the financial aid form in October, and that saved us."
A dorm -- the building on campus where students live during the year
"A dorm costs about fourteen thousand dollars a year, food included."
To take out a loan -- to borrow money from a bank and pay it back over many years
"Half the families here take out a loan for the first year."
Sticker shock -- the feeling when you see a price far higher than you expected
"Every parent here gets sticker shock the first time."
To be on track -- doing what you need to do to arrive on time
"She is on track to graduate in June."
A gap year -- a year between high school and college, usually working or traveling
"One of them is thinking about a gap year before she starts."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The twins are juniors this year, so next year they are seniors and everything starts. They are applying to six schools in the fall — the list is finished, the deadlines are on the calendar, and nothing about that is going to change.

In October we are going to visit two campuses, one in the state and one three hours away. That is a plan we already have. And after the visit, honestly, I think one of them is going to choose the closer one, because I saw her face when we drove past it in June.

Nobody knows the final number yet. It will be expensive — everyone here says so — but there is financial aid, and both girls are on track. If the letter is bad in April, I will call the aid office the same afternoon. And I will not sign anything until I read every line of it.

1. "They are applying to six schools in the fall." Why the present continuous and not "will apply"?
A Because the action is happening at this exact moment.
B Because it is an arrangement that is already fixed: the list is closed and the dates are set.
C Because "apply" is a verb that cannot be used with will.
2. "I think one of them is going to choose the closer one." Why "going to" here?
A Because it is a prediction with evidence: she saw her daughter's face in June.
B Because "going to" is the polite form of will.
C Because the sentence is about a place, and places take "going to".
3. "I will not sign anything until I read every line of it." Why is there no "will" after "until"?
A Because the reading already happened in the past.
B Because two verbs in the future are never allowed in one sentence.
C Because after until, when, if and before, English uses the present, even for the future.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Three Ways to Say Next Year

GRAMMAR

The more fixed the future is, the more the sentence sounds like the present.

FormWhen you use itExample
Present continuousAn arrangement already fixed: a date, a booking, a form sentThey are applying next year.
going to + verbA plan you already have in your headWe are going to visit two campuses.
going to + verbA prediction with the evidence in front of youThat is going to be a problem.
will + verbA prediction, an opinion about the futureIt will be expensive.
will + verbA decision you make while you are speakingI will call the office tonight.
Negative and questionwon't / are not going to · Will you...? / Are you going to...?We won't take out a loan. Are you going to visit?

Never: I will to call the office · She is going apply to six schools · We are visit two campuses in October · I will send the form when I will get her tax number.
