Four Sizes of "Better"

"Better" Never Says How Much

Question: Northwind or Deep Harbor?

"Northwind is better."

True, and empty. The listener still has no idea whether the race was close.

"Northwind is marginally better."

A small gap. Somebody could reasonably pick the other one.

"Northwind is far better."

A big gap, and now the sentence carries an opinion worth arguing with.

"Deep Harbor is nowhere near as good."

The same gap, measured from the other end. Same fact, different speaker.

A ranking is only interesting when somebody tells you how big the gaps are. That is what today adds to everything you already have.

Chapter 3: The Measure

How Big Is the Gap?

The words English puts in front of a comparison

Grammar Discovery

Four Gaps, Four Sizes

Northwind is by far the most ambitious game on the list.

Deep Harbor is nowhere near as generous with your time as Northwind.

Deep Harbor is every bit as beautiful as the trailer promised.

Paper Lanterns is marginally shorter than both of them.

Put the four sentences in order, from the widest gap to no gap at all. The word that decides the order is never the adjective.

StructureThe gap it measuresExample
by far / easily + superlativefirst place, with daylight behind it"by far the best soundtrack of the year"
far / much / considerably + comparativea big gap, in either direction"far more ambitious than last year's"
marginally / slightly / a little + comparativea small gap, close enough to argue about"marginally shorter than both of them"
nowhere near as + adjective + asa big gap, measured downwards"nowhere near as polished as Northwind"
not quite as + adjective + asjust below, and only just"not quite as sharp as the first one"
every bit as + adjective + asno gap at all, said with force"every bit as clever as the expensive ones"
the + comparative, the + comparativeTwo things moving together: "The longer you play it, the thinner the story gets." Each half starts with the and the comparative comes first in both.

The Tool

Three Places It Goes Wrong

Trap 1 · never mark it twice

A comparative is already comparative. "More better" and "most easiest" do not exist. The size word goes in front instead: far better, much better, by far the easiest.

Trap 2 · as ... as, never as ... than

Once a sentence opens with as, it has to close with as: "nowhere near as polished as Northwind". Than belongs to the other family, the one with -er and more. Mixing the two is the single most common error here.

Trap 3 · "very better" is Portuguese in an English coat

Very never stands in front of a comparative. English takes much, far, a lot or considerably: "much better", never "very better". Very is only allowed in front of the plain adjective: "very good".

Get the size word right and a ranking becomes an argument. Get it wrong and the listener hears the mistake instead of the opinion.

Arguing With the List

N
You read the list before me, so you go first. Was Northwind really the best thing all year?
M
By far. Nothing else on that page is even a serious contender.
N
Deep Harbor is every bit as good as Northwind, and it looks better. It got edged out for no reason I can see.
M
It does look better. It is nowhere near as generous with your time, though, and that was the benchmark they chose.
N
Then the benchmark is the problem. They measured one single thing and called the winner game of the year.
M
Maybe. But they said it out loud in the first paragraph, which is more than most lists do.
N
Fine. What about Paper Lanterns? I had not even heard of it until Tuesday.
M
Neither had I. It flew under the radar all year, and it is the least derivative thing on the list.
N
Then make a case for it next month and put it first. I will read that, and I will still disagree with you.
Comprehension

About Nina

1. Which game does she think was treated unfairly, and why?
Deep Harbor. She says it is every bit as good as Northwind and that it looks better, so being edged out makes no sense to her.
2. What is her objection to the way the list was decided?
That the writers measured one single thing and then called that winner the game of the year. For her the benchmark itself is the problem.
3. What does she tell Mark to do about Paper Lanterns?
Make a case for it next month and put it in first place. She says she will read it and still disagree with him.
Listening

Saying How Much Better

Your teacher on the size words, and on the three traps. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she say the word "better" fails to do?
It never tells the listener how big the gap is, so the sentence is true and still says almost nothing.
2. Which words does she give for a big gap, and which for a small one?
Far, much and considerably for a big one; marginally, slightly and a little for a small one. All of them go in front of the comparative.
3. What has to close a sentence that opens with "as"?
Another "as", never "than". "Nowhere near as polished as Northwind", never "as polished than Northwind".
4. Which mistake does she say comes straight from Portuguese?
Putting "very" in front of a comparative. English takes much, far or a lot: "much better", never "very better".

Somebody Else's Showcase

A student ranking his three favorite things of the year, out loud, in ninety seconds. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he name before he names anything on his list?
His benchmark: whether he went back to the thing a second time without being asked to.
2. Why does he put the series in first place instead of the game?
Because he has watched it three times and it holds up, while the game was thrilling once and lackluster the second time.
3. What does he say about the thing in third place?

Sizing the Gaps

Fix the Comparison

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "Northwind is more better than everything else this year."
"Northwind is far better than everything else this year." (a comparative is never marked twice — the size word goes in front)
2. "Deep Harbor is not as polished than Northwind."
"Deep Harbor is not as polished as Northwind." (a sentence that opens with as has to close with as)
3. "Paper Lanterns is very better than I expected."
"Paper Lanterns is much better than I expected." (very never stands in front of a comparative)
4. "It is every bit as clever than the games that cost ten times more."
"It is every bit as clever as the games that cost ten times more." (the same trap, inside an equative that means equal)
5. "The more you play it, the story gets thinner."
"The longer you play it, the thinner the story gets." (both halves take the plus a comparative, and the comparative comes first in each half)

The Game of the Year Ballot

1st — NorthwindBy far the most ambitious game here, and head and shoulders above the rest at the one thing we measured.

Mark — your ballotOne title, one position, and one structure that says how big the gap is.
1. In the second line, why is it "nowhere near as generous as" and not "nowhere near as generous than"?
Because the sentence opens with "as", and the second half of an equative is always "as". "Than" belongs to the family with "-er" and "more".
2. The ballot names its benchmark at the top. Why does that change how you read the order?
Because the order only makes sense against that one question. With a different benchmark the best-looking game would be first, and the writers would not be wrong — they would be measuring something else.
