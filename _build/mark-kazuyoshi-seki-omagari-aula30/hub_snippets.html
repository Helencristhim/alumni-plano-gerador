Lesson 30 -- Pre-class

Phase 3 Showcase -- Ranking and Comparing

"Better" is true and empty: it never tells anybody how big the gap is. This lesson gives you the words that do -- by far, far, marginally, nowhere near as, every bit as -- and then asks for the Phase 3 showcase: three titles, one order, three minutes, defended out loud. Key words: benchmark, contender, runner-up, edge out, surpass, hold up, derivative, lackluster, pale in comparison, head and shoulders above, fly under the radar, make a case for.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. These are the words you need to put things in order and then defend the order you chose.

Benchmark -- the standard that everything on the list gets measured against
"That question is the benchmark, and it moved two titles down the list."
Contender -- something with a real chance of finishing first
"Nothing else on that page is even a serious contender."
Runner-up -- the one that finishes second, just behind the winner
"Deep Harbor is the runner-up, and it lost by a single vote."
Edge out -- to beat something by a very small margin
"It edged out Deep Harbor in the final week, and only just."
Surpass -- to become better than something that was already good
"In pure beauty it surpasses everything else we played this year."
Hold up -- to still be good when you come back to it much later
"A game that is forgettable by March is not a game that holds up."
Derivative -- built out of ideas that other people had first
"It is not a bad game. It is a derivative one, which at this price is worse."
Lackluster -- done without energy, so nothing about it stays with you
"It delivered a lackluster version of a game we had already played twice."
Pale in comparison -- to look weak once you put it next to something better
"Beside that coastline, every other world on the list pales in comparison."
Head and shoulders above -- better than the rest by a distance nobody argues about
"It is head and shoulders above the rest at the one thing we were measuring."
Fly under the radar -- to be good and go almost unnoticed
"Paper Lanterns flew under the radar all year, and it was made by four people."
Make a case for -- to give the reasons why something deserves to win
"Make a case for your own order and we will print the best one next month."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the end-of-year list and answer the questions. Notice what each structure in bold tells you about the SIZE of the gap, not only about who won.

Three of us spent November arguing about this list, and before the ranking, one word about the method. We did not ask which game we enjoyed most in the week it came out. We asked which one we would still want to play in five years, because a game that is thrilling for a weekend and forgettable by March is not a game that holds up. That question is the benchmark, and it moved two titles down the list.

First place goes to Northwind, and it is not close. It is by far the most ambitious thing here, and it is every bit as careful with the small details as it is with the enormous ones. It is head and shoulders above the rest at the one thing we said we were measuring.

Deep Harbor is the runner-up, and it was edged out by a single vote. In pure beauty it surpasses everything else we played, and beside that coastline every other world on the list pales in comparison. But it is nowhere near as generous with your time as Northwind is, and the longer you play it, the thinner the story gets.

Third place is the surprise. Paper Lanterns flew under the radar all year, it was made by four people, and it is marginally shorter than the other two. And the one nobody will defend: Ash and Iron promised more than any game this year and delivered a lackluster version of something we had already played. It is not a bad game. It is a derivative one, which at this price is worse.

1. "It is by far the most ambitious thing here." What does "by far" add that the superlative alone does not?
A It says the writer is not completely sure about the choice.
B It says the gap to second place is enormous, not that the game merely came first.
C It says the decision was taken a long time ago.
2. Why is it "every bit as careful ... as", and never "every bit as careful than"?
A Because "than" can only be used in questions.
B Because "careful" has two syllables.
C Because a sentence that opens with "as" has to close with "as" -- "than" belongs to the family with "-er" and "more".
3. "Nowhere near as generous with your time as Northwind is." How big is that gap, and in which direction?
A A big gap, measured downwards: Deep Harbor is much less generous than Northwind.
B A small gap: the two are almost the same.
C No gap at all: they are equal on this point.
4. Why does the writer name the benchmark in the first paragraph instead of at the end?
A To make the article longer before the ranking arrives.
B Because the order only means something against that one question, and a reader who disagrees with the benchmark can argue with the whole list.
C Because the rules of a school paper require it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Measuring the Gap

GRAMMAR

"Better" is true and empty: it never says how much. The word that carries the size of the gap goes in front of the comparison, and it is never the adjective itself.

StructureThe gap it measuresExample
by far / easily + superlativefirst place, with daylight behind it"It is by far the best game of the year."
far / much / considerably + comparativea big gap, in either direction"It is far more ambitious than last year's."
marginally / slightly / a little + comparativea small gap, close enough to argue about"It is marginally shorter than the other two."
nowhere near as + adjective + asa big gap, measured downwards"It is nowhere near as polished as the winner."
not quite as + adjective + asjust below, and only just"It is not quite as sharp as the first one."
every bit as + adjective + asno gap at all, said with force"It is every bit as clever as the expensive ones."
the + comparative, the + comparativeTwo things moving together: "The longer you play it, the thinner the story gets." Each half opens with the, and the comparative comes first in both halves.
Three trapsNever mark a comparative twice ("more better", "most easiest"). Never close an as sentence with than ("as good than"). Never put very in front of a comparative -- English takes much, far or a lot.

Order

Listen first, then put the five moves of a spoken showcase in order.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen to each sentence, then record yourself saying it. The stress falls on the size word, not on the adjective: BY FAR the best, NOWHERE NEAR as polished. That stress is what tells the listener how big the gap is.

It is by far the best thing I played all year.
The runner-up was edged out by a single vote.
It is nowhere near as polished as the winner.
It flew under the radar, and it is every bit as clever.
Let me make a case for the one that came third.

Quiz

A friend asks why your third choice deserves a place at all. The strongest answer is to:
A name your benchmark and show what the order becomes under it.
B say that you like it more than the other two and leave it there.
C list everything wrong with the winner and give no reason of your own.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

This is the Phase 3 showcase, and you are rehearsing it. Take the three best things you played, watched or read this year and rank them. Start with your benchmark -- the one question you are using to decide -- and only then give the order. Defend every position with a structure that says how big the gap is, using a different one each time: by far, far, marginally, nowhere near as, every bit as. Concede one thing your winner does worse than the others. Finish by saying which of the three will still hold up in five years, and why the other two will not.
