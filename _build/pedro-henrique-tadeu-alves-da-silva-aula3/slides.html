Chapter 3: The Code

I Have Heard It, and I Heard It Yesterday

The same event, and two completely different sentences

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I have listened to it three times."

"I listened to it last night."

"Have you ever worked with a live band?"

"We finished the mix in March."

Look at the blue part. It is only there twice — and exactly where it appears, the red verb changes shape.

JobFormExample
It happened, and the time does not matterhave / has + past participleI have listened to it three times. She has rewritten the second verse.
It happened at a time that is finishedthe past formI listened to it last night. We finished the mix in March.
Life experience, up to nowHave you ever ... ? · I have never ...Have you ever worked with a live band? I have never mixed an orchestra.
The typical pair in a conversationpresent perfect opens, past simple explains— Have you heard the new version? — Yes, I heard it yesterday.
Irregular participles you will need todaywrite / wrote / written · hear / heard / heard · send / sent / sentShe has written three drafts. I have sent you the reference.
The trapnever a finished time with the present perfectNot I have heard it yesterday. Say I heard it yesterday, or I have heard it.

In one line: the moment a finished time appears in the sentence, the verb goes to the past form.

Listening 1 · The One Rule

Say What You Heard Before You Say What You Think

The opening minute of a songwriting workshop, before anybody plays anything. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the one rule, and what three examples does she give of following it?
Say what you heard before you say what you think. Her examples: the drums came in late; the second verse was shorter than the first; I lost the words at the end of the chorus.
2. She says the reason for the rule is not politeness. What is it?
Accuracy. She says ninety percent of what people call feedback is a guess about the intention, and half the time the guess is wrong.
3. Why does she say the writer cannot hear their own song any more, and what does she allow at the end?
Because they have listened to it four hundred times. The opinion is welcome, but it goes second, and it goes in one sentence.
Listening 2 · The Best and Worst Note

I Stopped Listening at One Minute Forty

A producer on two notes he has never forgotten, for opposite reasons. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the worst note he has ever been given, and what did it cost him?
Three words: make it pop. Nobody could say what pop meant, so he spent eleven days guessing, and in the end they accepted the first version with the vocal two decibels louder.
2. What was the best note, who gave it, and what did she not say?
"I stopped listening at one minute forty", from a drummer who does not produce anything. She did not say why and she did not say fix it. She said where.
3. What was actually wrong at that point, and what does he do now when he listens to somebody else’s work?
The second chorus repeated without changing anything; he cut eight bars and the problem disappeared. Now he writes down the timecode where his attention left the room.

Note for the group: the Heard line is not optional. If you have not written anything there, you do not speak first.
1. Two lines on this form do completely different jobs. What is the difference between Heard and Thought?
Heard is what physically happened in the audio, and anybody in the room would agree with it. Thought is the opinion, it comes second, and the form allows only one sentence of it.
2. Why would a workshop ask for a timecode instead of a general comment?
Because a timecode can be acted on. "I stopped listening at 1:40" sends the writer to one exact place; "it loses energy" sends them nowhere.
3. According to the note, what happens if the Heard line is empty?
You do not speak first.
