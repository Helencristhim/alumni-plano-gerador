Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Saying What You Think

Somebody plays you their work and then goes quiet. This lesson fills that silence with something useful. Key words: a draft, to stand out, subtle, to overdo, a hook, to land, clunky, to tighten, a reference, blunt, to hold up, a nitpick. Structure: present perfect when the time does not matter, past simple the moment a finished time appears.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A draft -- an early version that is not finished yet
"This is a draft, so tell me about the structure and not about the mix."
To stand out -- to be noticeably better or different from everything around it
"The bass line stands out, and everything else is doing the same thing."
Subtle -- small and quiet, and easy to miss, but it changes everything
"It is subtle: the drums come in half a bar late, and that is why it breathes."
To overdo -- to use so much of it that it stops working
"The strings are overdone in the last chorus, and the voice disappears."
A hook -- the short part of a song people remember without trying
"The hook is four notes long and I have not stopped hearing it since yesterday."
To land -- to have the effect you wanted, on the person listening
"The first chorus lands. The second one repeats it and nothing happens."
Clunky -- heavy and awkward, so the listener notices the machinery
"The transition into the bridge is clunky, and you can hear the edit."
To tighten -- to make something shorter and more exact, with nothing loose
"If you tighten the bridge by four bars, the whole thing holds up."
A reference -- another track you point at to explain the sound you want
"Send me a reference, because warm means six different things."
Blunt -- saying the true thing directly, with no soft edges
"Can I be blunt? The second verse is where I stopped listening."
To hold up -- to still work well after you hear it many times
"I have played it twenty times this week and it still holds up."
A nitpick -- a very small criticism that does not change the whole thing
"One nitpick, and then I will stop: the fade at the end is two seconds long."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody plays you two minutes of their own work and then goes quiet. Every sentence you are about to say sits on one decision, and the decision is not about politeness. It is about whether the sentence names a finished time. "I have listened to it three times" carries no date, and that is the point: the fact matters, the moment does not. "I listened to it last night" names a time that is over, and the verb goes straight to the past form. Same event, two different sentences, and the only thing that changed is last night.

In a feedback round the two forms usually arrive together, in this order. The question opens with the present perfect: "Have you heard the new version?" The answer explains with the past simple: "Yes, I heard it yesterday." The same pattern covers experience. "Have you ever worked with a live band?" asks about a whole life up to now, so there is no date. "I have never mixed an orchestra" says the same thing from the other side. Three irregular participles do most of the work in this room: write becomes written, hear becomes heard, send becomes sent.

Then there is the content of the note itself. The hook lands or it does not. The bass line stands out. The transition is clunky. The strings are overdone and the voice disappears under them. One change is subtle and already works; one problem would go away if you tighten the middle; one thing is only a nitpick and you say so out loud, so the writer knows how much it weighs. And if you cannot describe the sound you want, send a reference instead of an adjective. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: never put a finished time next to the present perfect. Not I have heard it yesterday.

1. Why is it "I listened to it last night" and not "I have listened to it last night"?
A Because "listen" is an irregular verb.
B Because the sentence names a finished time, and a finished time sends the verb to the past form.
C Because the present perfect cannot be used with the verb "listen".
2. According to the text, what order do the two forms usually arrive in during a feedback round?
A The question opens with the present perfect, and the answer explains with the past simple.
B The past simple always comes first, because it is shorter.
C They never appear in the same conversation.
3. The text says to send a reference instead of an adjective. Why?
A Because adjectives are informal in a feedback round.
B Because a reference is faster to send than a sentence.
C Because an adjective like "warm" means six different things, and another track shows exactly what you mean.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect vs Past Simple

GRAMMAR

One decision: does the sentence name a finished time?

JobFormExample
It happened, and the time does not matterhave / has + past participleI have listened to it three times.
It happened at a time that is finishedthe past formI listened to it last night. We finished the mix in March.
Life experience, up to nowHave you ever ... ? · I have never ...Have you ever worked with a live band?
The typical pair in a conversationpresent perfect opens, past simple explains— Have you heard the new version? — Yes, I heard it yesterday.
Irregular participles you need todaywrite / wrote / written · hear / heard / heard · send / sent / sentShe has written three drafts. I have sent you the reference.
Negativehave not / has not + participle · did not + base verbI have not heard the new version. I did not hear it last night.
The trapnever a finished time with the present perfectNot I have heard it yesterday. Say I heard it yesterday.

Never: I have heard it yesterday · Did you ever work with a live band? · She has wrote three drafts · I have finished the mix last night · I have sended you the reference

Stage 2: Put Your Feedback in Order

Order

Somebody has just played you a draft. Put your response in a useful order.

?"I have listened to it three times, and the chorus is the part I remember."
?"The hook lands, and the second verse is where it stops working for me."
?"I think the strings are a bit overdone -- it is a nitpick, not a problem."
?"One subtle thing: the drums come in half a bar late, and it works."
?"If you tighten the bridge by four bars, the whole thing holds up."
?"And you, what did you think of mine?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Somebody asks whether you have heard the new version. You heard it the night before. You say:
A "Yes, I have heard it yesterday."
B "Yes, I heard it last night."
C "Yes, I am hearing it yesterday night."
You want to say exactly where the track stopped working, without being cruel. You say:
A "Can I be blunt? I stopped listening at one minute forty, in the second verse."
B "Sorry, I did not like it very much, it is my opinion only."
C "It is nice, but something is missing, I do not know what."
The problem is tiny and you want the writer to know how much it weighs. You say:
A "There is a big problem with the strings in the last chorus."
B "One nitpick: the strings are a bit overdone in the last chorus."
C "The strings are terrible, but it is only my nitpick opinion."
Somebody gives you a note you cannot use: "make it bigger". You say:
A "Ok, no problem, I will do it bigger."
B "Bigger is very subjective, I disagree with this."
C "What do you mean by bigger, and where exactly? Can you send me a reference?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Choose something you have heard or watched recently and did not entirely like. In ninety seconds: say what you heard, say exactly where it stopped working, give your opinion in one sentence, and finish with one thing that does hold up. The word "nice" is not available.
