Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

Cross-Cultural Navigation

The ten expressions you will hear in your first hour in Atlanta, and the question shapes that let you ask a stranger anything without interrogating them. Key words: small talk, icebreaker, to hit it off, common ground, to catch up, ballpark, heads-up, to touch base, no-brainer, to swing by. Structure: indirect questions and question tags.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read it inside a real sentence. None of these is taught in a course and all ten are used constantly.

Small talk -- light conversation about nothing important, before or around the business
"The first four minutes of that call were small talk, and they were not wasted."
Icebreaker -- something you say or do to start a conversation comfortably
"The flight is the easiest icebreaker there is, and everybody uses it."
To hit it off -- to get on well with somebody immediately
"We hit it off because we had both worked in a French company."
Common ground -- an interest or an experience that two people share
"Find common ground in the first minute or the conversation stays polite and empty."
To catch up -- to exchange news with somebody you have not spoken to for a while
"Let us catch up properly when I am in Atlanta."
Ballpark -- an approximate figure, close enough to work with
"Give me a ballpark and we can refine it later."
Heads-up -- a warning given in advance so that somebody is not taken by surprise
"Thanks for the heads-up about the reorganisation."
To touch base -- to make brief contact to check how something is going
"I will touch base with you on Thursday before the call."
No-brainer -- a decision so obvious that it needs no thought
"Moving the training budget forward was a no-brainer."
To swing by -- to visit somewhere briefly, without arranging it in advance
"Swing by my desk when you land and I will introduce you to the team."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

The first four minutes of the reception were small talk, and they were not wasted. Have you ever been to São Paulo? Do you know whether the Marietta team is on the monthly call? You are the one who wrote the note about the first ninety days, aren't you?

By the fourth exchange it had become something else. I was wondering if you would have twenty minutes while I am here. Would Thursday work, or is Friday easier? None of that is an interrogation, and all of it is a question. That is the whole trick.

1. Do you know whether the Marietta team is on the call? What has happened to the word order after whether?
A Nothing -- it keeps the question order.
B The inversion has gone: the inner clause is written as a statement.
C The verb has moved to the beginning of the clause.
2. Why does the writer end with aren't you? instead of a full stop?
A To check a fact she is unsure about.
B To hand the turn back to the other person and keep the conversation alive.
C To make the sentence more formal.
3. I was wondering if you would have twenty minutes. Why not can you give me twenty minutes?
A Because the second is grammatically incorrect.
B Because the first leaves the other person a way to say no, which is what makes it easy to say yes.
C Because the past tense is more polite in every request.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions and Tags

GRAMMAR

Two shapes do most of the social work in English. The indirect question lets you ask a stranger anything; the tag hands the turn back so that the conversation does not die after your question.

FormWhat it doesExample
Do you know if / whether + statement ordersoftens a direct question, and the word order inside stays a STATEMENTDo you know whether the Texas team joins the call?
Could you tell me + wh- + statement orderthe most useful shape for asking a stranger anythingCould you tell me how long you have been here?
I was wondering if ...the softest way to ask for somebody time or a favourI was wondering if you would have twenty minutes.
question taginvites agreement and hands the turn over -- the engine of small talkYou have been to Brazil before, haven't you?
the trapkeeping question order INSIDE: do you know where is the room? The inversion belongs to the outer question only. Say where the room is.

The one mistake that marks a non-native speaker instantly: keeping the question order inside. Do you know where is the room? The inversion belongs to the outer question only.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

One right answer each. Read the situation before you look at the options.

You join a call four minutes early and one American is already there. What do you say?
A Nothing -- wait for the others to arrive.
B Morning. How was your weekend?
C Shall we start early, as we are both here?
D Sorry, I am early. I can leave and come back.
You want to know whether somebody is on the monthly call. Which sounds natural?
A Do you know is the Marietta team on the call?
B Do you know whether the Marietta team is on the call?
C You know if is the Marietta team on the call?
D Is on the call the Marietta team, do you know?
An American says we should grab lunch sometime. What is the useful reply?
A That would be lovely, thank you.
B Would Thursday work, or is Friday easier?
C Yes, let us do that sometime.
D I will send you an email about it.
Somebody asks how your project is going. How long is the answer?
A Four minutes, because they asked.
B About twenty seconds, with a door for them to walk through.
C One word, so as not to take their time.
D As long as it takes to explain it properly.
