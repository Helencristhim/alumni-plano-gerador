Making the Case

You said on the first day that you want to be able to give a talk about your company in English. This is the half of that nobody prepares: the part where somebody says no.

Chapter 1: First Words

From the Handover to the Argument

Last time the subject was work that other people do for you. Today it is the opposite: something you want, that nobody will do unless you convince them first.

Think of one thing you want to change in the group this year. Say what it is in a single sentence, and then say the strongest objection somebody would raise.

Today’s Goal

Three Missions

1

Twelve words for a proposal, an objection and an approval.

2

Put the important part of a sentence where the ear expects it.

3

Take a real objection and answer it without giving ground.

The structure of the day is the one English uses when a plain sentence is not enough: we need a second supplier is information, and what we need is a second supplier is an argument. The words are almost the same. The effect is not.

One Question

What Would They Say Against It?

Two sentences, out loud, right now.

One that states the change you want. One that states the objection, in the words the other person would actually use. If the second sentence is easier for you than the first, that is worth knowing before we start.

Chapter 3: The Code

Where the Important Part Goes

Two ways to move the weight of a sentence

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"What we need is a second supplier, not a cheaper one."

"It was the timing that worried them, not the cost."

"What the pilot showed us was that the bottleneck moved."

"Not only do we make it in-house, but we also design it."

Say the plain version of each one first: we need a second supplier, the timing worried them. Nothing new has been added. Something has simply been moved, and the sentence now has a place where the weight falls.

JobFormExample
Emphasise a thing you need or wantWhat + subject + verb + is/are + the thingWhat we need is a second supplier.
Emphasise which one it wasIt + was/is + the thing + that + the restIt was the timing that worried them.
Emphasise a whole ideaWhat + clause + was + that ...What the pilot showed us was that the bottleneck moved.
Emphasise the action itselfWhat + subject + did + was + verbWhat we did was run it as a pilot first.
Add a second point with forceNot only + auxiliary + subject ..., but ... alsoNot only do we make it, but we also design it.
The pair that carries the argument... not X, but Y / ... rather thana second supplier, not a cheaper one.
The trapafter Not only the sentence invertsNot Not only we make it. Say Not only do we make it.

In one line: move the important part to the front with What or It, and the ear knows where to listen.

Comprehension

About Claire

1. What does Claire say about her own position at the start, and how does she phrase it?
That she is not going to pretend she is there yet, and she asks him to talk her through it.
2. Which number does Claire keep coming back to, and what is her concern about it?
The capital. Her concern is that three years is a long time to wait for it to come back.
3. What does Claire say moves her, and what does she say most people do instead?
His naming the downside himself. She says most people bury that part.
Listening 1 · The Executive

The Room I Lost in Four Minutes

A director on the presentation that went wrong, and why. Sound first — no text.

1. How did he open the presentation, and what had he not done?
He opened with the solution, on one clean slide with the cost, timeline and return. He had not spent a single sentence on the problem.
2. What question did the chair ask, and why was he not ready for it?
What happens if we do nothing. He had no answer ready because in his head the question had been settled months earlier -- settled for him, not for them.
3. What was different about the version that got approved?
The same plan and the same numbers, but it started with two minutes on what the current line costs every quarter.
Listening 2 · The Investor

What Makes a Case Believable

An investor on what she listens for in a business case. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say separates a strong business case from a weak one?
Not the quality of the plan, but whether the presenter tells her the downside before she finds it herself.
2. Why does she say a number with a condition attached is stronger?
Because a caveat makes the claim credible. She says it sounds backwards until you have seen a few forecasts fall over.
3. What third thing does she say almost nobody does?
Ruling options out loud: naming the three options considered and rejected, and why, so she does not have to wonder.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Pilot That Never Became a Rollout

A business radio report on why pilots succeed and rollouts do not. Sound first — no text.

1. What figure does the report give about pilots, and what happens to most of them?
Roughly two thirds are judged a success by the people who ran them, and a much smaller share ever reach a full rollout.
2. What does a pilot have that a rollout does not?
Attention: the best people are on it, problems are escalated the same afternoon, and everybody knows they are being watched.
3. What is the unpopular recommendation, and what is the trade-off?
Run the pilot badly on purpose -- ordinary people, ordinary hours, no special access. Companies that do this report worse pilots and considerably better rollouts.

The page names the problem before the proposal on purpose: what the second line solves is a ceiling in high season, and not the unit cost, which is the objection this page expects. Three options were considered and rejected, and they are listed so that nobody has to ask. What the pilot buys is a quarter of real data before the capital is committed in full.
1. Two lines on this page do something most proposals avoid. Which are they, and why are they there?
"Downside" and "Caveat". They are there because naming your own weakest number unprompted is what makes everything else on the page credible.
2. Why does the page list the options that were ruled out?
So that nobody has to wonder whether they were considered. It answers an objection before it is raised.
3. What exactly is being asked for at the bottom, and how is that different from the proposal at the top?
Approval as a pilot for one quarter, not a full rollout. The proposal is the second line; the ask is a quarter of real data before committing the capital.
