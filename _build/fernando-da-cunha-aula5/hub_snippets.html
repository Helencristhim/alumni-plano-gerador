Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

Making the Case -- Defending a Proposal

Somebody in the room is not convinced, and a plain sentence will not move them. Key words: a business case, a payback period, a benchmark, a stakeholder, pushback, to rule something out, the downside, a caveat, a pilot, a rollout, to make headway, to bring somebody round. Structure: cleft sentences for emphasis.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A business case -- the written argument for why something is worth doing, with the numbers in it
"The business case for the second line was four pages. Two of them were the risks."
A payback period -- the time it takes for an investment to earn back what it cost
"The payback period is under three years, which is what got it approved."
A benchmark -- a number from outside your company that you measure yourself against
"Our lead time is half the industry benchmark, and that is the whole argument."
A stakeholder -- anybody whose interests are affected by a decision, inside or outside the company
"Every stakeholder in that room had a different reason to say no."
Pushback -- resistance or objection from the people you are trying to convince
"I expected pushback on the cost. The pushback came on the timing."
To rule something out -- to decide that an option is not possible, and remove it from the discussion
"We ruled out a third plant early, and saying so bought us credibility."
The downside -- the part of a plan that can go wrong, or what it costs you if it does
"If you do not name the downside yourself, somebody else will name it for you."
A caveat -- a warning attached to a claim, which limits how far the claim goes
"The number is solid, with one caveat: it assumes the tariff stays where it is."
A pilot -- a small first version of a plan, run to test it before committing to it
"We ran it as a pilot in one plant for a quarter."
A rollout -- the process of putting something into use across the whole operation
"The pilot was the easy part. The rollout took eighteen months."
To make headway -- expression: to make visible progress against something that is resisting you
"We are finally making headway with the drugstore chains."
To bring somebody round -- expression: to change somebody’s mind until they agree with you
"It took two meetings and a plant visit to bring him round."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A director walks into a room with a good plan and loses it in four minutes. The numbers are right, the payback period is under three years, the benchmark is on the page. He says we need a second production line, which is true and which nobody remembers ten seconds later, because it is a sentence with no shape. Every word in it weighs the same.

English has two ways to fix that, and both of them move something to the front. What we need is a second production line. Nothing has been added: the plain sentence is still in there, and the only change is that the ear now knows where to land. Or: It is the timing that worries them, not the cost. Same trick, different tool — It is plus the part you want heard, then that and the rest. Use that here, never what.

The same shape carries a whole idea: What the pilot showed us was that the bottleneck moved. The little that before the clause is not optional in careful speech. And when you want to stack a second point with force, English inverts: Not only do we make it in-house, but we also design it. The auxiliary jumps in front of the subject, which does not happen in Portuguese and is where the mistake comes from. None of this makes a weak argument strong. What it does is make sure the strong part is the part they hear — which matters most when you are also naming the downside and the caveat yourself, and asking for a pilot rather than a rollout.

1. Why is "What we need is a second production line" stronger than "We need a second production line"?
A Because it adds information the plain sentence does not have.
B Because it moves the important part so the listener knows where to land.
C Because it is more formal and formal is always better.
2. In "It is the timing that worries them", which word joins the two halves?
A what
B that
C which
3. What happens to the sentence after "Not only"?
A Nothing changes; the word order stays normal.
B The verb is dropped.
C The auxiliary moves in front of the subject: Not only do we make it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences and Emphasis

GRAMMAR

Nothing new is added. The important part simply moves to where the ear expects it.

JobFormExample
Emphasise a thing you need or wantWhat + subject + verb + is/are + the thingWhat we need is a second supplier.
Emphasise which one it wasIt + was/is + the thing + that + the restIt was the timing that worried them.
Emphasise a whole ideaWhat + clause + was + that ...What the pilot showed us was that the bottleneck moved.
Emphasise the action itselfWhat + subject + did + was + verbWhat we did was run it as a pilot first.
Add a second point with forceNot only + auxiliary + subject ..., but ... alsoNot only do we make it, but we also design it.
The pair that carries the argument... not X, but Y / ... rather thana second supplier, not a cheaper one.
The trapafter Not only the sentence invertsNot Not only we make it. Say Not only do we make it.

Never: Not only we make it in-house · It was the timing what worried them · What we need is a second supplier, is not a cheaper one · What the pilot showed us was the bottleneck moved · What we did was ran it as a pilot.

Stage 2: Put the Case in Order

Order

You have two minutes in front of people who have not read anything. Put your case in a logical order.

?"Every high season we turn away volume we could have made."
?"What we need is capacity in high season, not a lower unit cost."
?"The payback period is under three years, against an industry benchmark of four and a half."
?"We ruled out a third plant and outsourcing the peak, and here is why."
?"The downside is a line at half load if the volume does not follow."
?"So what I am asking for today is one quarter as a pilot, not a rollout."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want the room to remember that the need is capacity, not price. You say:
A "We need capacity and not price, this is the point."
B "What we need is capacity in high season, not a lower unit cost."
C "The capacity is what we are needing, not the price."
You want to stack a second strong point. You say:
A "Not only we make it in-house, but we design it also."
B "Not only do we make it in-house, but we also design it."
C "We not only make it in-house, but also we design it."
An investor asks about the risk. The strongest answer is:
A "There is no real risk in this plan."
B "The downside is a line at half load if the volume does not follow, which is why the ask is a pilot."
C "Every plan has risks, as you know."
You want to emphasise which figure is the problem. You say:
A "It is the payback period that looks long, not the capital."
B "It is the payback period what looks long."
C "The payback period is that looks long."

TED -- Chris Anderson, "TED’s secret to great public speaking" (8 min)

The head of TED on the single thing every talk that works has in common: it builds one idea in the listener’s head, in order, instead of listing what the speaker knows. It is the lesson of the first recording, said from the other side of the stage.

Tip: eight minutes and very clear. Watch it once, then say out loud what the one idea in your own proposal is, in a single sentence.
