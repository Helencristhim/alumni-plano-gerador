Lesson 18 -- Pre-class

Midpoint Review -- Integrating the Full Grammar and Vocabulary Arc

The review where you are the subject: what changed, what proves it, and what has not moved. Key words: milestone, take stock, trajectory, inflection point, plateau, consolidate, internalise, second nature, step change, track record, move the needle, demonstrable. Structure: nothing new -- the six structures of Lessons 1 to 17 in a single read-out.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. You already use all twelve about the directors who report to you.

Milestone -- a fixed point in a plan where progress is measured, not celebrated
"The midpoint is a milestone, not a finish line."
To take stock -- to stop and assess honestly where something stands
"Before we set the next target, let us take stock of what actually changed."
Trajectory -- the direction and speed of change over time, not the position today
"Her trajectory matters more to me than her level today."
Inflection point -- the moment a trend changes direction
"The inflection point was the March renewal call, not the coaching."
Plateau -- a stretch where the effort continues and the improvement stops
"Everyone hits a plateau at this stage. What matters is what you do inside it."
To consolidate -- to make a gain stable enough to survive pressure
"We are adding nothing new this quarter. We are consolidating."
To internalise -- to know something so well that it no longer costs you attention
"You have internalised the structure. You are not choosing it any more."
Second nature -- automatic, done without deciding to do it
"Signposting used to be a decision. It is second nature now."
Step change -- a large, visible jump rather than gradual improvement
"That was not progress. That was a step change."
Track record -- the accumulated evidence of what someone has actually done
"I do not promote on interviews. I promote on track record."
To move the needle -- to produce a change large enough to be measured
"The reading moved nothing. Chairing the call moved the needle."
Demonstrable -- able to be shown with evidence, rather than claimed
"I need something demonstrable by December, not a feeling."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the read-out below. Nothing in it is new. As you read, ask one question of every bold structure: what job is it doing here?

"We closed the quarter at ninety-four per cent of plan. Since April we have been carrying a heavier weather load than we modelled, and that load has not peaked yet. Two of the largest losses were reported after the cut-off, so they sit in this quarter and not in the last one."

"What changed the number was not frequency. It was severity. The three accounts that drive most of the variance are all in the same region, and they are all renewals we wrote before the new limits. We should have flagged the concentration in February, and I take that."

"On the evidence we have, the trend would appear to hold into Q4, though I would not commit to a number today. What I will commit to is the reserving page by the eleventh."

Six structures, in forty seconds, from six different lessons. None of them is new. The only decision the speaker made was which one, and when.

1. "We closed the quarter" and "Since April we have been carrying". Why are these two different tenses in consecutive sentences?
A The quarter is a finished period with a name on it; the weather load started in April and is still running now.
B The second one is longer, so it needs the longer tense.
C The first is a fact and the second is an opinion.
2. "Two of the largest losses were reported after the cut-off." Why passive here, and active in "We should have flagged the concentration"?
A Because the first sentence is about the past and the second one is not.
B Passive for the problem, where the author of the event adds nothing; active with your own name on it for your own decision, where hiding behind the passive reads as evasion.
C Because losses are always reported in the passive in insurance.
3. What is "What changed the number was not frequency" doing that "Frequency did not change the number" would not do?
A It moves the emphasis onto the cause and sets up the correction, so the room stops looking at the wrong figure.
B It is more polite.
C It is shorter.
4. "On the evidence we have, the trend would appear to hold." Why hedge this line and not the line about the reserving page?
A Because a forecast is always hedged, as a matter of form.
B Because hedging everything makes the whole read-out sound more careful.
C Because the trend is genuinely uncertain and the date is not. Hedging what you actually know is the fastest way to sound junior.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Map, Not the Forms

GRAMMAR

Nothing here is new, and there is nothing to memorise. This is a map of moments: which structure does which job, and where you first met it.

The momentWhat does the jobSounds likeFrom
Opening on a closed periodPast simple — the period ended and it has a name"We closed the quarter at ninety-four per cent."L1
Something that started and is still runningPresent perfect — the line from then to now is the point"Since April we have been carrying a heavier load."L1
Reporting the problemPassive, no agent — the event matters, the author does not"Two of the largest losses were reported after the cut-off."L4
The room is looking at the wrong numberCleft — moves the emphasis without raising your voice"What changed the number was severity."L6
Being precise about which oneRelative clause — narrows the noun instead of adding a second sentence"The three accounts that drive most of the variance..."L7
Owning what should have happenedModal perfect — retrospection with your name on it, no excuse attached"We should have flagged the concentration in February."L11
An outlook you will not guaranteeHedging — the distance is deliberate and it is visible"On the evidence we have, the trend would appear to hold."L14
The rule behind the mapPassive for the problem, your own name for your own decisions. Cleft when the room is looking at the wrong thing. Hedge only what is genuinely uncertain — hedging what you know is the fastest way to sound junior.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

Group Talent asks how the first half went. Your first sentence is:
A "Rather than tell you it is going well, let me take stock: here is what changed, and here is what proves it."
B "Very well, thank you. I am much more comfortable now."
C "I have completed seventeen of the thirty-six sessions."
Which of these counts as evidence in a midpoint review?
A "I feel far more confident in English calls than I did."
B "In June I gave the read-out live and took eleven minutes of questions."
C "I have worked on it consistently since January."
One area of your work has not improved in four months. In the review you:
A Name it as the plateau yourself, before anybody else has to.
B Leave it out, since the review is about progress.
C Mention it only if you are asked directly.
You are reporting a loss that arrived after the cut-off, and separately a call you should have made in February. The right pairing is:
A Passive for both, so the tone stays consistent.
B Passive for the loss, and your own name on the call you should have made.
C Active for both, so nothing sounds evasive.
You are certain about a date and uncertain about a trend. You:
A Hedge both, to stay safe.
B Hedge the trend and commit flatly to the date.
C Commit to both, and correct it later if necessary.
Group Talent asks what the second half should do. The strongest answer is:
A "Cover more ground — there is a lot I still have not seen."
B "Keep going as we are. It is working."
C "Consolidate rather than expand: one demonstrable milestone for December, with the evidence defined in advance."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

Take stock of your own first half, in four moves and about three minutes. Where you were in January. What changed, with the month and the meeting where it happened. Where the trajectory is flat, named by you. And one milestone for December that somebody else could verify without asking you anything. No adjectives in the third move.

Series
The Crown -- Season 1, Episode 7 ("Scientia Potentia Est")

The most senior person in the room takes stock of her own capability and finds a gap nobody had told her about. What makes the episode useful is not the outcome but the method: she treats her own preparation as a work object, names the weakness in one sentence, and does something demonstrable about it. That is the conversation of this lesson, in a room where the stakes are absurd.

Tip: watch for the moment she stops describing how she feels and starts describing what she cannot do. Write that sentence down and rewrite it about yourself.

Podcast
WorkLife with Adam Grant -- the episodes on feedback and on rethinking

Look for the episodes on how senior people take criticism and on why experience makes us slower to change our minds. His argument sits underneath the whole lesson: the higher you go, the more people describe your performance to you in adjectives, and the harder it becomes to find out what has actually not moved.

Tip: while you listen, decide who in your own organisation would tell you the uncomfortable version. If the answer is nobody, that is the finding, and it is worth more than the episode.
