Chapter 3: The Code

Everything, in One Read-Out

Six structures. Nothing new. One speech.

Grammar Discovery

One Speech, Six Decisions

Read it straight through, out loud, before you look at the colours. It is one person opening one quarterly read-out.

"We closed the quarter at ninety-four per cent of plan."

"Since April we have been carrying a heavier weather load than we modelled."

"Two of the largest losses were reported after the cut-off, so they sit in this quarter and not in the last one."

"What changed the number was not frequency. It was severity."

"The three accounts that drive most of the variance are all in the same region."

"We should have flagged the concentration in February, and I take that."

"On the evidence we have, the trend would appear to hold into Q4, though I would not commit to a number today."

Six different structures went past in forty seconds, and you have used every one of them since Lesson 1. The question is not what they are. It is which job each one did.

Opening on a closed periodPast simple for the period that ended and has a name; present perfect for what is still running nowLesson 1
Reporting the problemPassive, with no agent — the event matters, the author does notLesson 4

The rule behind the mapPassive for the problem, your own name for your own decisions. Cleft when the room is looking at the wrong thing. Hedge only what is genuinely uncertain — hedging what you know is the fastest way to sound junior.

The Structure

A Stock-Take in Four Moves

The same four you already use on the directors who report to you.

1 · Where it started, honestly

"In January the read-out was drafted for me and I delivered it as written."

2 · What changed, with a date on it

"In June I gave it live and took eleven minutes of questions. That moved the needle."

3 · Where the trajectory is flat

"Written work has not moved since March. That is the plateau, and I would rather name it myself."

4 · One milestone, and how anyone would verify it

"By December, one demonstrable milestone rather than four soft ones — and here is what would prove it."

Move 3 is the one people skip. It is also the only reason anyone believes moves 2 and 4.

O
Send me the one, in writing, by Friday. And Denise — the reason I take these seriously is that most people at your level describe a feeling. You have just given me a trajectory.
Comprehension

About Otto

1. How does he describe this conversation at the start?
Not an appraisal — one he holds at the midpoint of every senior development plan, and mostly he is listening.
2. What does he tell her that she did not know?
That the written work had already been flagged — by Milan, in April, and not by her. He says it is not a criticism, and that it is why he asked for the meeting.
3. What does he ask for at the end, and why does he say he takes these seriously?
One milestone, in writing, by Friday. He takes them seriously because most people at her level describe a feeling, and she gave him a trajectory.
Listening

A Feeling Is Not Evidence

A group talent director on what a midpoint review actually measures. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he say most people bring to a midpoint review?
A feeling and a list — the feeling that it is going well, and a list of how much they have done. Neither is evidence, and he has stopped asking for them.
2. What is the only thing he counts?
A situation you could not handle at the start, when you handled it, and who was in the room.
3. What does he say about being on a plateau at the halfway mark?
Almost everybody is on one, and it is not a failure — it is what learning looks like once the easy gains are gone.
4. What does he say the second half of any plan is for?
Not for learning more, but for making what already exists hold under pressure.
Listening 2

Four Months of Nothing

A regional director on her own plateau, and what ended it. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What was happening to her effort during those four months?
She was working as hard as ever, and the work was going into the parts that were already automatic. Effort landing in the wrong place, not laziness.
2. What had she assumed the problem was, and what was it?
She assumed she needed more words. The words were there — she was not using them anywhere uncomfortable.
3. What was the inflection point?
Being asked to chair a call she had attended silently for two years. She was worse at it than she wanted to be, and the following month she was measurably better.
4. What is her one practical piece of advice?
Keep a short record of what you handled, with dates — because you forget very quickly what you could not do six months ago, and a feeling will not help when somebody asks for evidence.
Chapter 5: Practice

Your Own Record

Spot the Error

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "The decision was taken by us in October, after the reserving page."
"We took the decision in October, after the reserving page." (passive for the problem, your own name for your own decisions)
2. "We should have to flag the concentration in February."
"We should have flagged the concentration in February." (should have + past participle, never "have to")
3. "I comment you that the number has changed."
"Let me tell you that the number has changed." (from "te comento que" — in English you cannot comment somebody)
4. "In this moment we are revising the limits."
"At the moment we are reviewing the limits." (two at once: "neste momento" is "at the moment", and "revisar" is "review" — "revise" is what you do before an exam)
