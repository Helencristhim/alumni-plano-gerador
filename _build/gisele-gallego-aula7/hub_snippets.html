At the Doctor’s Office -- Booking, Symptoms and What the Plan Covers

The front desk, the phone menu and the fine print of an American clinic. Key words: a co-pay, a referral, a primary care physician, to run tests, a prescription, to refill, a symptom, to flare up, sore, swollen, a follow-up, coverage. Structure: modals of advice and obligation -- should, ought to, had better, have to, must, do not have to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A co-pay -- the fixed amount you pay yourself at each visit, on top of what your plan pays
"Your co-pay today is forty dollars."
A referral -- a note from your own doctor that lets you see a specialist
"I cannot book the specialist until I have a referral."
A primary care physician -- the main doctor who sees you first and sends you on to specialists
"You have to choose a primary care physician before anything else."
To run tests -- to do a set of medical checks in order to find out what is going on
"She wants to run tests before she says anything."
A prescription -- the written order a doctor gives you for a medicine
"The prescription goes straight to the pharmacy on the corner."
To refill -- to get more of a medicine you already take, without seeing the doctor again
"Call two days early if you need to refill it."
A symptom -- a sign in your body that tells you something is wrong
"Write every symptom down before the visit, so you do not forget one."
To flare up -- to become painful or bad again after a quiet period
"It was fine all winter, and then it flared up again in March."
Sore -- painful when you touch it or move it
"My shoulder has been sore for about three weeks."
Swollen -- bigger than normal because of an injury or an illness
"My ankle is still swollen this morning."
A follow-up -- a second, shorter visit to check how you are after the first one
"They put a follow-up on the calendar for two weeks from Thursday."
Coverage -- everything your health plan pays for, and nothing else
"Check your coverage before the lab sends you a bill."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The first thing anybody in Washington will tell you is that you should choose a primary care physician before you need one. You have to start there anyway: if you end up needing a specialist, that doctor has to write you a referral first, and without it most plans will not pay for the visit at all.

At the desk you pay a co-pay before anybody sees you, and forty dollars is normal. You do not have to pay for the lab that day, because the lab bills your plan separately — but you ought to check your coverage before the doctor decides to run tests, not three weeks later, when the bill arrives.

And when you leave, take the summary with you. It lists every symptom you reported, the prescription and how many times you can refill it, and the date of the follow-up. You had better not lose that piece of paper. You must not take two of those pills on the same day, and if the pain flares up before the follow-up, you should call the office instead of waiting.

1. "You do not have to pay for the lab that day." What does this mean?
A It is forbidden to pay the lab that day.
B It is not necessary. The lab bills the plan later.
C The lab work is always free.
2. "You must not take two of those pills on the same day." Why must not and not do not have to?
A Because it is a prohibition: it is dangerous and it is not allowed.
B Because must not is simply a more polite form of do not have to.
C Because the sentence is about the future.
3. Why "you have to start there" and not "you should start there"?
A Because have to is stronger only in writing.
B Because should is never used with doctors.
C Because it is a rule of the system, not a friendly suggestion.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Advice and Obligation

GRAMMAR

Six small words in front of the verb. The verb after them never changes.

MeaningFormExample
Adviceshould / ought to + base verbYou should call the office in the morning.
Strong warninghad better (not) + base verbYou had better not skip the follow-up.
Obligationhave to / must + base verbYou have to get a referral first.
No obligationdo not have to + base verbYou do not have to pay the lab today.
Prohibitionmust not + base verbYou must not take two on the same day.
QuestionDo I have to ... ? / Should I ... ?Do I have to bring my insurance card?

The pair that matters: do not have to = you may skip it. must not = you may not do it. They are opposites, not synonyms.

Never: You must to get a referral · I should to call the office · You not have to pay · Do I must bring my card? · She should calls the office.
