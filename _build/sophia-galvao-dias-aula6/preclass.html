Lesson 06 — Pre-class

Two Schools

Two schools, and one question that is harder than it looks: by how much? Before the lesson: eight words for what a school actually does to you — timetable, workload, assignment, strict, demanding, keep up, fall behind, get used to — and the words that put a size on a comparison: far, considerably, slightly, and the more … the more.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Eight words for the school you are in. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Timetable -- the sheet that tells you which class you have and when
"My new timetable is only slightly longer than the old one."
Workload -- the amount of work you are given to do
"The workload here is considerably heavier."
Assignment -- a piece of work a teacher gives you to finish on your own
"We get one big assignment a week instead of three small ones."
Strict -- quick to make people follow the rules exactly
"Everybody told me the new place would be much stricter."
Demanding -- asking a lot of you, and hard to satisfy
"A demanding school is a gift, even when it does not feel like one."
Keep up -- to stay at the same speed as everybody else
"The more subjects I have, the harder it is to keep up."
Fall behind -- to end up slower than the rest of the group
"Students who fall behind early tend to stay behind."
Get used to -- to stop finding something strange after a while
"I got used to the place faster than my sister did."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the message. The words in bold are the ones that put a size on a comparison.

People keep asking me the same question about the new school, and I never manage to answer it in one word. The building is far bigger — three times the size, easily. My timetable is only slightly longer, ten minutes, and nobody would even notice. But the workload is considerably heavier: one long assignment a week instead of three small ones.

The teachers are not really stricter, only marginally. What is genuinely different is that they are more demanding. In the first month I was certain I would fall behind in everything, and it took me about four weeks to get used to the way the classes run here.

The strange part is English. It is no harder than it was — it is easier, which sounds like good news and is not. The more I relax in class, the less I actually speak. So now I do thirty minutes of my own every evening, and the more I do at home, the easier it is to keep up with everybody else.

"My timetable is only slightly longer." What is slightly doing there?
A It says that the timetable is longer — which is what longer already says.
B It says how big the difference is: real, but small enough that nobody notices.
C It says that the writer is not sure about it.
"The more I relax in class, the less I actually speak." Why is there a the in front of both halves?
A Because the two thes are what makes it one structure — take either one out and it stops working.
B Because the sentence has two verbs in it.
C Because more and less always take the in English.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Comparatives that say how much

Bigger tells you the direction. It does not tell you the size. In English you almost never leave a comparative on its own — you put a word in front of it that measures the gap.

A big difference

  • far / much / a lot / a good deal / considerably + comparative
  • "The building is far bigger." · "The workload is considerably heavier."

A small difference

  • a bit / slightly / marginally + comparative
  • "My timetable is only slightly longer." · "The rules are marginally tighter."

No difference at all

  • no / not any + comparative: "It is no harder than it was."

Two things moving together

  • The more …, the more / the less / the easier …
  • "The more I read, the easier it gets." — both thes are obligatory.

Two traps. Very never goes with a comparative: not "very bigger", but much bigger, far bigger, a lot bigger. And one comparative is enough: not "more heavier", just heavier — or far heavier if you want the size.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz
Your new school is three times the size of your old one. Which sentence is correct English?
A "It's very bigger than my old school."
B "It's far bigger than my old school."
C "It's more bigger than my old school."
Your day got ten minutes longer — nothing else changed. Which sentence says that honestly?
A "The timetable is considerably longer."
B "The timetable is only slightly longer."
C "The timetable is much more longer."
You want to say that two things go up together. Which one is the structure?
A "More I study, more I learn."
B "The more I study, more I learn."
C "The more I study, the more I learn."
You need the English word for the paper with all your classes on it, and it will not come. What do you do?
A Say the word in Portuguese and hope they understand.
B Say "it's the paper that tells you which class you have and when".
C Stop the sentence and change the subject.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Somebody your age is about to change schools and asks you what it is really like. In about ninety seconds, compare the two schools you know best — and you are not allowed to say better or worse once. Name one thing that changed enormously, one that changed only slightly, and one that did not change at all. Use at least four different size words (far, considerably, a bit, slightly, marginally, a good deal) and finish with a sentence that starts The more…
