The Code

Bigger is not enough

The new building is far bigger.

My timetable is only slightly longer.

The workload here is considerably heavier.

The more I do outside school, the less it matters what happens inside it.

You put in frontAnd the difference is
far / much / a lot / a good deal / considerablybig — anybody would notice it
a bit / slightly / marginallysmall — real, but you have to look
no / not any + comparativenone at all: it is no better than before
the more X, the less / the more Ytwo things moving together, one because of the other

Take the word away and the sentence still works — it just stops saying anything. Bigger is a direction; far bigger is a measurement. Two traps: very never goes with a comparative (very bigger does not exist — use much, far or a lot), and one comparative is enough (more heavier is heavier twice). In the more, the more, both thes are obligatory.

The Code

Say how much

"The new building is three times the size. It's ___far bigger."

"I have twice the homework I had last year. The workload is ___considerably heavier."
"The more homework they give me, ___the less time I have."

Listening 1

Ruby changed schools too

1. What did everybody ask her, and why is it the wrong question?
Whether the new school is harder. She says it is not harder, it is differently hard — a good deal easier in math, far more reading in history.
2. What happened to her English hours, and what did she notice afterwards?
They went from four hours a week to fifty minutes. She noticed her own accent getting worse.
3. What is her advice to anybody who is moving?
Hold on to the subject you were already good at. Nobody takes it away from you — you put it down yourself.
Listening 2

The school's own message

1. What two things does he say about the school day and the timetable?
The day runs from seven fifty to one thirty, slightly earlier than most families are used to. The timetable comes out in the first week and does not change after that.
2. When is the written workload heaviest, and what does he tell you to do about it?
In the first term, considerably heavier than in the second — and on purpose. Speak to your grade coordinator in the first two weeks, because students who fall behind early tend to stay behind.
3. What does he ask families for at the end, and how does he put it?
To tell the school things early: "the more you tell us in September, the less we have to guess in March."
