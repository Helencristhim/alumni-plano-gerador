Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Eight of these are already yours -- they come back from earlier lessons because the board will hear them. The last six are new frames for talking about your own progress. Tap Listen to hear each one.

Scenario -- one of several possible futures that you model and price before a decision is taken
"We modelled three scenarios, and the board only needs to understand the difference between them."
Regulatory framework -- the whole set of rules that governs an activity, taken together
"The regulatory framework was published in December and has not been amended since."
Withholding tax -- tax that is taken out of a payment before the money reaches the person being paid
"The withholding tax falls on the fee the subsidiary pays to the parent, not on the parent."
Provision -- a single clause or section of a law or a contract
"Only one provision of the reform actually creates the problem, and it is not the one the market talks about."
Exposure -- the amount a company could lose if the risk actually happens
"The exposure is calculable today, which is exactly why the decision can be taken today."
Restructuring -- changing how the companies in a group are organized, usually to reduce cost or risk
"The restructuring would still be worth doing even if the phase were postponed to 2028."
Hedge -- to soften a statement so that you are not promising more than you can defend
"He pushed on the number and I hedged: in our view there might be a narrower window."
Advisory mandate -- the formal instruction from a client that authorizes a firm to advise on a matter
"The board wants the full advisory mandate, and the paperwork is coming on Monday."
Over the past fifteen sessions -- a frame that opens a statement about what you have built across a whole period
"Over the past fifteen sessions, I have developed the vocabulary to run a technical meeting in English."
I can now confidently -- a frame for naming something you can do that you could not do before
"I can now confidently lead a meeting in which the client challenges my numbers."
The area I want to keep working on -- a frame for naming your own weak point without apologizing for it
"The area I want to keep working on is my pace when I am under pressure."
I notice I still hesitate when -- a frame for describing your own performance while it is happening
"I notice I still hesitate when I have to report what a colleague said in an earlier meeting."
I appreciate the opportunity -- a formal thank-you that opens or closes a professional presentation
"I appreciate the opportunity to present our position, and I will keep it to twelve minutes."
I look forward to continuing -- a professional closing that leaves the relationship open
"I look forward to continuing this conversation once you have had time with the numbers."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The board gives Leonardo twelve minutes, and Vera Lindqvist, who signs, tells him to start with what he wants them to do. So he does. He recommends restructuring into a single Brazilian entity before the second phase, and he gives the reason in one sentence: the regulatory framework was published in December and has not been amended since, which is why the exposure is calculable today. At minute four she interrupts him and asks where the eleven percent comes from. He does not answer it. He parks it: "Let me come back to that in one minute, because the second scenario changes the answer." Then he does exactly what he said he would do. If the phase is implemented as published, the burden rises by roughly eleven percent, and it falls on the withholding tax on the intercompany fee. If it were postponed to 2028, ALMA would still recommend the same restructuring, because doing it later costs more than doing it now. Only then does he come back to her question, and he hedges it honestly: eleven is the firm's modelling, not the authority's, and in his view there might be two points either side. She calls that a wide door. He does not narrow it to please her. Instead he puts the earlier advice on the record: the team advised her finance team in March to avoid signing any new agreement before the decision was taken, and the provision that creates the problem is not the one the market talks about. When she asks what happens if the board waits, he refuses to promise anything he cannot defend: he cannot tell her the guidance will not come, only that it was promised in June and has not arrived. Twelve minutes after he started, she asks for the advisory mandate. He was not convincing because his range was narrow. He was convincing because it was honest.

1. Why does Leonardo park the question about the eleven percent instead of answering it immediately?
A Because he does not know the number and needs time to find it.
B Because the second scenario changes the answer, so the number would be heard out of context.
C Because it is impolite to answer a finance director directly in a board meeting.
2. "If the phase were postponed to 2028, ALMA would still recommend the restructuring." Why the past form after if?
A Because the postponement already happened in the past.
B Because the sentence is a report of something somebody said earlier.
C Because he is modelling that outcome, not predicting it. The past form marks the distance.
3. What does hedging the eleven percent actually buy him with this board?
A Credibility. A range he can defend is worth more to the person who signs than a number he cannot.
B Time, because a vague answer delays the difficult part of the meeting.
C Nothing. Boards prefer a single confident number and he took a risk.
4. Why does he report the March advice instead of quoting what anyone said?
A Because he cannot remember the exact words that were used.
B Because reported speech puts the firm's earlier position on the record without it sounding like gossip about a colleague.
C Because direct quotation is not allowed in a board meeting.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Eight Structures in One Turn

GRAMMAR

This lesson adds no new structure and no new technical word. Everything below has been yours for weeks, taught one at a time across fifteen sessions. What is new is that a real presentation never asks for one at a time: it asks for all eight, in nine sentences, while somebody interrupts you.

The formWhat it does in this roomWhat you say
Present perfect vs past simpleSeparates the relationship that is still running from the event that closed."I have advised this group since 2019" · "the reform changed the question"
Passive voicePuts the rule at the centre instead of the person. This is how a framework is described."The framework was published... and has not been amended since."
First conditionalThe scenario the board treats as likely, so both halves stay in real time."If the phase is implemented, the exposure rises by eleven percent."
Second conditionalThe scenario you are modelling but not predicting. The past form is what marks the distance."If it were postponed, we would still recommend it."
Relative clauseNarrows one item out of many, which is the whole job of a precise adviser."The provision that creates the problem..."
Reported speechPuts your team's earlier advice on the record without quoting anybody."We advised your finance director in March to avoid signing..."
Gerund vs infinitiveThe verb decides the shape. The wrong shape is the error a board actually hears."recommend restructuring" · "decided to model" · "advise you to decide"
Hedging modalsSays how certain you are. This is the one that makes a board trust the other seven."There might be..." · "you may want to..." · "we would recommend..."
Talking about yourselfThe only new language today, and it is not technical: "Over the past fifteen sessions, I have developed...", "I can now confidently...", "The area I want to keep working on is...", "I notice I still hesitate when...". Present perfect for what you have built, present simple for what is still true.
DeliveryNot grammar, and it decides as much as grammar does. Slow down before a number. Pause where you would put a full stop instead of running three sentences together. And keep the intonation flat on a hedge: "there might be" said with a rising tone sounds unsure, and said flat it sounds precise.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each moment in the board meeting.

The chair gives you the floor and says you have twelve minutes. How do you open?
A "Thank you. Let me start with some background on the group's Brazilian structure since 2019."
B "I appreciate the opportunity to present our position. Our recommendation is that the group should restructure before the second phase, and I'll give you the three scenarios behind that."
C "Thank you. Before I begin, could somebody tell me how much detail the board would like?"
The finance director interrupts you in the middle of a sentence with a question about a number. What do you do?
A Stop, answer it in full, and then try to find your place again.
B "Let me come back to that in one minute, because the second scenario changes the answer." Then finish the point and come back to it yourself.
C Apologize for not being clear and start the section again from the beginning.
She asks how confident you are in the eleven percent. Your modelling gives a range. You say:
A "It will be eleven percent, and the authority will accept our position."
B "It is difficult to say. There are many factors involved."
C "Eleven is our modelling, not the authority's. In our view there might be two points either side, and I'd rather give you the range than a number I can't defend."
Your teacher asks what you want to keep working on after the program. The strongest answer is:
A "The area I want to keep working on is my pace under pressure. I notice I still speed up when I am nervous."
B "Everything, really. My English is still very bad and I have a lot to improve."
C "Nothing in particular. I think the program covered what I needed."
