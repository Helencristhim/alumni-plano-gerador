Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

Rules and Deadlines -- What Is Obligatory and What Only Looks Obligatory

How to say what is required, what is forbidden and what is simply not necessary, and why the two negatives are opposites. Key expressions: a deadline, to approve, a policy, to be allowed to, to fill in, a form, to hand in, due, overtime, a reminder. Structure: have to, must and do not have to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These ten stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

A deadline -- the day or time by which something has to be finished
"The deadline is Friday at six and nobody moves it."
To approve -- to say officially that something can happen
"Two people have to approve it before it goes out."
A policy -- an official rule of a company or a country
"It is not my decision, it is the policy."
To be allowed to -- to have permission to do something
"You are not allowed to take the documents home."
To fill in -- to write the information in the spaces on a document
"You have to fill in the form before the meeting."
A form -- a document with spaces where you write information
"There is one form for everything, and it is two pages long."
To hand in -- to give a document to the person who has to receive it
"Hand it in to reception, not to me."
Due -- expected or required by a particular time
"The report is due on Thursday morning."
Overtime -- extra hours you work beyond your normal hours
"Nobody has to do overtime here, but people do."
A reminder -- a message that makes you remember something
"I will send you a reminder on Wednesday."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every office has three kinds of rule and most people only notice two of them. There are the things you have to do: fill in the form, hand it in to reception, get two signatures before anything reaches a client. There are the things you must not do: take documents home, send something that has not been approved, promise a date that is not in the policy.

And then there is the third kind, which is invisible: the things that are not obligatory at all and that everybody does anyway. Coming in on a Friday. Doing overtime that nobody asked for. Rewriting a summary that was fine last quarter. Nobody has ever put those in a document, and they cost more hours than the real rules do.

The deadline is the one thing that does not negotiate. The report is due on Thursday at midday, two people have to approve it before it goes out, and one of those two people is away until Wednesday. That arithmetic is the whole job, and no amount of good intention changes it. Somebody always sends a reminder on Wednesday morning, and somebody always needs it.

There is one grammatical detail buried in all of this, and it is not decoration. You must not send it and you do not have to send it are opposites. One forbids, the other frees. Say the wrong one to a colleague who is not allowed to ask twice, and they will do exactly the wrong thing, politely and on time.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Have To, Must and Do Not Have To

GRAMMAR

Three ideas, two verbs, and one pair of negatives that Portuguese speakers confuse for years. The pair is the whole lesson.

FormWhat it saysExample
have to + plain verbObligation from outside: a rule, a boss, a policy, a law."You have to sign it before Friday."
"He has to approve it first."
must + plain verbObligation that feels internal, or a written rule on a document or a sign."I must call him today."
"Visitors must report to reception."
must not = forbiddenThere is a rule against it. Strong, and correct when it is true."You must not send it before it is approved."
do not have to = not necessaryYou may do it if you want. The opposite of must not."You do not have to come in on Friday."
Questions and the pastHave to behaves like an ordinary verb; must has no question form and no past."Do I have to fill it in?"
"I had to sign it twice."
NOT "I musted"
The safer choice at workHave to sounds like a fact about the rules. Must, from a person, can sound like an order."We have to wait for the second signature."

One phrase worth stealing for client conversations: we are not allowed to. It says the same thing as we must not and it moves the refusal from you to the rule, which is where it belongs.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Somebody has just joined your team and is asking how things work. Choose the best thing to say.

Coming in on Friday is optional:
A You must not come in on Friday.
B You do not have to come in on Friday.
C You have not to come in on Friday.
Sending it before approval is against the rules:
A You do not have to send it before approval.
B You must not send it before it is approved.
C You have not to send it before approval.
You want to ask about an obligation:
A Do you must sign this today?
B Do you have to sign this today?
C Must you to sign this today?
You are talking about yesterday:
A Yesterday I must sign it twice.
B Yesterday I had to sign it twice.
C Yesterday I musted sign it twice.
You want to explain a refusal to a client without blaming yourself:
A I do not want to do that.
B We are not allowed to send it before it is approved.
C It is impossible for reasons of internal procedure.
