Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Who Is Bruna? -- A Homebody Who Travels

Somebody at an RV park in Alaska asks what your story is, and every answer is either the week that repeats or the trip that ended. Key words: a homebody, a couch potato, to be into something, to wind down, a career change, an RV, a road trip, scenery, to grab something, worth it. Structure: present simple versus past simple — positive, negative and question.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A homebody -- a person who is happiest at home and does not go out much
"I am a homebody, and I am fine with that."
A couch potato -- a person who spends a lot of free time sitting and watching television
"On a cold Sunday I am a complete couch potato."
To be into something -- to like something a lot and give it your free time
"I am really into books and true crime series."
To wind down -- to slow down and relax at the end of a busy day
"I wind down with a book, never with my phone."
A career change -- the moment you leave one profession and start a completely different one
"I made a career change four years ago."
An RV -- a big vehicle with a bed and a kitchen inside, so you sleep where you stop
"We rented an RV and we lived in it for seventeen days."
A road trip -- a long journey by car, where the driving is part of the trip and not only the way there
"Alaska was the best road trip of my life."
Scenery -- everything you see around you in a place: mountains, water, trees, sky
"The scenery up there does not look real."
To grab something -- to get something quickly, without making a plan of it
"Let me grab a coffee before we drive out."
Worth it -- good enough to pay for the time, the money or the effort it took
"Seventeen days in a small RV, and every mile of it was worth it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A stranger at an RV park asks who you are, and everything you answer falls into one of two boxes. The first box is the week that repeats. "I work at the office three days a week and I work from home two days. Every morning I wake up at eight and I take the dogs out. In the evening I wind down with a book, because I am a homebody and I am fine with that." Look at those verbs. They are bare. Nothing was added to them, because the time around them is still open: that week happened last week, it is happening this week, and it will happen again.

The second box is one block of time that started, ended, and will not come back. "Last month we flew to Anchorage. We picked up an RV the same day and we drove it for seventeen days." Here every verb changed shape. Some of them change in a regular way, by adding two letters, and some of them are old enough to change completely: go becomes went, fly becomes flew, drive becomes drove. There is no rule for those. You learn them the way you learned the words themselves — by hearing them.

The negatives and the questions use a helper, and the helper carries the time so the verb does not have to. For the open week: "I do not go out much." / "Do you travel a lot?" For the closed trip: "We did not stay in hotels." / "Did you like the scenery?" And here is the trap that costs most speakers their credibility in the first thirty seconds: after do or did, the verb goes straight back to its bare form. Never did you went. Never we did not stayed. The helper already said everything about the time.

1. Why is the verb bare in "I work from home two days a week"?
A Because the sentence is about work.
B Because the time around it is still open: that week repeats.
C Because "work" is an irregular verb.
2. The text says "fly becomes flew" and "drive becomes drove". What does it say about verbs like these?
A They follow a rule that you can work out from the spelling.
B They only change after the word "last".
C There is no rule for them. You learn them by hearing them.
3. Why is "Did you went to Alaska?" wrong?
A Because "did" already carries the time, so the verb goes back to its bare form: "Did you go".
B Because questions about travel never use "did".
C Because "Alaska" needs "to the" in front of it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple versus Past Simple

GRAMMAR

If the time is still around you, the verb stays bare. If the time closed, the verb changes.

JobFormExample
A week that repeatsI / you / we + bare verbI work from home two days a week.
One finished block of timethe past form of the verbLast month we drove to Alaska.
Negative, repeatingdo not + bare verbI do not go out much.
Negative, finisheddid not + bare verbWe did not stay in hotels.
Question, repeatingDo + subject + bare verbDo you travel a lot?
Question, finishedDid + subject + bare verbDid you like the scenery?
Irregular past formsno rule — learn them by eargo → went · fly → flew · drive → drove · take → took
In speechdo not becomes don’t, did not becomes didn’tWe didn’t stay in hotels.

Never: every day I woke up at eight · I worked from home two days a week (when you mean every week) · last month we go to Alaska · did you liked the scenery? · we did not stayed in hotels

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Somebody asks you to describe a normal day of yours. You answer:
A "I woke up at eight, I took a shower and I worked from home."
B "I wake up at eight, I take the dogs out, and I start work at nine."
C "I am wake up at eight and I am work from home."
You want to tell them about the trip you finished last month. You say:
A "Last month we go to Alaska and we drive an RV."
B "Last month we did go to Alaska and we did drive an RV."
C "Last month we went to Alaska and we drove an RV for seventeen days."
You want to ask them about that same finished trip of theirs. You say:
A "Did you like the scenery up there?"
B "Did you liked the scenery up there?"
C "You liked the scenery up there, no?"
The other person has finished answering. You want the conversation to keep going. You say:
A "Very nice. Thank you."
B "And you, do you travel a lot?"
C "Yes. My trip was longer."
