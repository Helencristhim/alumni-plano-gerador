Lesson 14 -- Pre-class

What a Committee Looks For -- Six Criteria and a Threshold Under Each

Nobody is looking for the best candidate. Two readers score your file against a sheet with six lines on it, and almost every file in the pool clears every line. Key words: a criterion, a rubric, an applicant pool, a threshold, to weigh something against something, well-rounded, a deal-breaker, to check a box, to clear the bar, a caveat, to offset something, across the board. Structure: quantifiers and generalisation -- most vs most of the, few vs a few, hardly any, a handful of, virtually all, none of.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A criterion -- one of the separate things a decision is measured against (plural: criteria)
"There are six criteria on the sheet, and not one of them is about your job title."
A rubric -- the scoring sheet a reader fills in, so that two readers judge the same way
"Nobody improvises. There is a rubric, and both readers are marking against it."
An applicant pool -- everybody who applied in the same year, and who you are compared with
"You are not measured against the standard. You are measured against this year's applicant pool."
A threshold -- the lowest score that is still acceptable on one criterion
"Six is the threshold. A seven is not a compliment, it is one point above the floor."
To weigh something against something -- to judge one thing by comparing it with another, instead of adding them up
"I weigh the goals answer against the responsibility line, because one explains the other."
Well-rounded -- good at many different things, with no weak side and nothing unusual either
"Every file in the pool is well-rounded. That is the problem, not the standard."
A deal-breaker -- the one thing that ends the discussion, however good the rest is
"A weak number is not a deal-breaker. A claim nobody can check is."
To check a box -- to satisfy a requirement on paper without the reader learning anything about you
"He checked every box on the sheet and gave me no reason to remember his name."
To clear the bar -- to reach the level that is being asked for
"Most applicants clear the bar on paper. Very few clear it on one criterion so clearly that you remember them."
A caveat -- a short warning added to something you have just agreed with
"Recommended for interview, with one caveat: ask him about the goals answer first."
To offset something -- to balance out something weak with something strong somewhere else
"The low number is still there. It is offset by something nobody else in the pool has."
Across the board -- the same in every part, with no item higher or lower than the rest
"Good across the board is the easiest file to score and the hardest one to remember."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every answer you give in an admissions interview puts you somewhere in a group, and English marks that group with one small word. When you talk about the kind of person in general, the quantifier takes the plain plural noun and nothing else: most candidates, few applicants, many engineers. When you talk about a group that is already on the table — this year's pool, the six criteria, them — the same quantifier pulls in of plus a determiner: most of the files, few of my decisions, hardly any of them. Portuguese uses the article in both cases, which is why the most of candidates sounds natural to a Brazilian ear and lands badly in an interview.

The second thing to get right is the pair that Portuguese collapses into one word. Few means almost none, and it is a complaint: few candidates name a cost says that the absence is a problem. A few means some, and it is neutral or positive: a few of them name two. The same split works with uncountable nouns as little and a little, and the trap is that few only goes with countable nouns. Little candidates is not a smaller quantity of candidates; it is a mistake.

Three more are worth having ready. Hardly any is the spoken way of saying almost none, and it is already negative, so it never appears with no or nobody: say hardly anybody, never hardly nobody. A handful of is a small countable number, and it sounds more precise than some without committing you to a figure. And none of is the formal zero, followed by the group: none of the six criteria is about your title, with a singular verb in writing, although most speakers use the plural out loud. Put together, these are the words that let you say where you sit without saying the word only.

1. According to the text, when does a quantifier take "of the"?
A Whenever the noun is plural.
B When the group is already on the table, such as this year's pool, the six criteria, or them.
C Only after "most", and never after "few" or "hardly any".
2. What does the text say is the difference between "few" and "a few"?
A "Few" means almost none and is a complaint; "a few" means some and is neutral or positive.
B They mean the same thing, and the article is only a matter of style.
C "Few" is for people and "a few" is for things.
3. What does the text say about "hardly any"?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Quantifiers and Generalisation

GRAMMAR

One question decides the form: the kind in general, or a group we have already named?

JobFormExample
The kind in generalmost / many / few / all + plain plural nounMost candidates prepare the wrong thing.
A group already namedmost of / many of / few of + the, this, your, themMost of the files in your pool come from banking.
Almost none, and it is a problemfew (countable) · little (uncountable)Few candidates name a cost.
Some, and that is gooda few · a littleA few of them name two.
Almost none, in speechhardly any + noun · hardly anybodyHardly any file gets an argument.
A small countable numbera handful of + plural nounA handful of files go to a third reader.
Nearly everythingvirtually all / almost all (+ of the, only if the group is named)Virtually all applicants clear the bar on paper.
Zero, formallynone of + the group (singular verb in writing)None of the six criteria is about my title.

Never: the most of candidates · almost all of applicants · hardly nobody · little candidates · most of people.

Stage 2: Put the Answer in Order

Order

This is how you place yourself against the six criteria: strongest line first, the low one named before anybody asks.

?"Hardly any applicant in my year signs off on the money, the schedule and the safety of the same site."
?"Most of the files in that pool come from consulting and banking, and mine does not."
?"On five of the six criteria I am comfortably above the threshold."
?"The sixth one is clarity of goals, and few of my sentences about the next five years are specific yet."
?"That is offset by a track record none of the other files in the pool has."
?"Without me the class loses the only person who has run a family company through a downturn."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Place yourself against the six criteria in ninety seconds. Start with the line you clear most clearly, then say what most of the pool has that you do not, then where you are below the threshold and what offsets it, and finish with one sentence about what the class would lose without you. Use at least four different quantifiers, and say "of the" only when you mean this year's pool.
