Chapter 3: If I Got In

Three Imaginary Sentences and One Real One

The structure English keeps for what has not happened

Grammar Discovery

Which One Could Happen This Year?

"If I got a place there, I would major in law."

"If I had a scholarship, I could go next year."

"If I were you, I would read the essay out loud."

"If I send it late, they will turn me down."

The three red sentences step one tense backwards after if, and the result half takes would or could. Nothing there has happened. The orange one stays in the present and takes will, because the deadline is real and the date is on a calendar.

JobFormExample
Imagining a situationif + past simpleIf I got a place there...
Saying what followswould + base verb...I would major in law.
Softening the resultcould / mightIf I had a scholarship, I could go next year.
Giving adviceif I were you, I would...If I were you, I would read it out loud.
Either order worksno comma when if comes secondI would major in law if I got in.
The real versionif + present, will + base verbIf I send it late, they will turn me down.
The trapnever would after ifNot if I would get in. Say if I got in.
The written formwere after I, he, sheIf he were older, he would apply now.

In one line: step back in the if half, would in the other half, and never both in the same half.

Chapter 4: Real Voices

Two People Who Read Applications

One conversation and two recordings

Dialogue

The Question She Asks Everybody

N
So you are eleven, and you already know where you want to go. That is earlier than most people who write to me.
G
I know. My neighbour David wants the same university, so we talk about it a lot.
N
Good. Tell me something first: if you got a place there, what would you actually do in the first year?
G
I would major in law. After that I would train as a pilot in France.
N
That is a real answer, and most people your age give me a feeling instead. Now the harder question. What do you do that nobody makes you do?
G
I make cakes and I sell them at the market in our condominium, every two weeks. I keep the money in my own account.
N
Then that is extracurricular, and it is worth more than you think. Half the applicants I read have never run anything.
G
I thought admissions only looked at grades and at the essay.
N
Grades get your file opened. After that we are reading for a person. If everybody in the pile has perfect grades, grades cannot make anybody stand out.
G
And if I could only fix one thing before I apply, what would you tell me to fix?
N
Your writing, and not your English. Slow down and read your own sentences out loud before you send them. If I were you, I would start that habit now and not at seventeen.
Comprehension

About Naomi

1. What does she say grades can and cannot do?
Grades get your file opened, but if everybody in the pile has perfect grades, grades cannot make anybody stand out.
2. What does she say about half of the applicants she reads?
That half of them have never run anything themselves, which is why the cake stand is worth more than he thinks.
3. What is the one thing she would fix, and what is it not?
His writing — and she says it is not his English. She would slow down and read her own sentences out loud before sending them.
Listening 1 · An Admissions Officer

What They Are Really Reading

Somebody who reads applications for a living. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say grades actually do?
They only get your file opened. After that, admissions are reading for something else.
2. What example does she give, and what was in it?
A boy who ran a small business at a street market with his neighbour, and who wrote about the week he lost money.
3. What does she tell applicants not to send, and what does she want instead?
Not a list of everything they have ever won. She wants one thing they actually built, and what it cost them.
Listening 2 · A Second-Year Student

Nobody Here Is Impressed by Fast

A student from Sao Paulo, two years in. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say was the hardest part, and what was it not?
Learning to write slowly. He says it was not the English.
2. Why did he answer fast at home, and what happens where he is now?
Because answering fast was how you looked clever. There, nobody is impressed by fast — they read what you wrote and ask why you chose that word.
3. What one habit would he go back and change?
He would read his own sentences out loud before sending them, and he says that single thing would have saved him a whole term.

Your Application, Item by Item

Office of Undergraduate Admissions — Application Checklist
Applicant: Gabriel Reis Medeiros Fernandes · Campinas, Brazil · International applicant

1. Personal essay — 650 words. Received: no.

2. Two recommendation letters — one from a teacher who has taught you this year. Received: no.

The extracurricular record — swimming at competition level and the cake stand at the market, with the hours for each.

They would not read it. The deadline is the first, and if you send it late they will turn you down.

Go Deeper · Optional

The Two Sentences Nobody Taught You Yet

You have the second conditional. There are two more, and one of them is in the recording you just heard.

The one about the past. "If somebody had told me at eleven that I would be sitting in this library, I would have laughed." Nobody told him, and he did not laugh. The whole sentence is about a past that did not happen.

The mixed one. "If I had started reading out loud at eleven, my writing would be better now." Past cause, present result. The two halves are in different times, and that is allowed.

Your turn. Make one sentence of each about your own life. One about something that did not happen, and one about something that did not happen and still changes today.
