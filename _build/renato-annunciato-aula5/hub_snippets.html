A Day in the Field -- Coaching on a Joint Visit

Two hours in the car, one sales call, and the four minutes at the end that turn it into coaching. Key words: joint visit, to shadow, call plan, sales call, gatekeeper, rapport, pitch, objection, lead, to close, follow-up, debrief. Structures: must, have to, should, must not and do not have to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. This is the vocabulary of a day you already spend every week.

Joint visit -- a visit where the manager goes to the client together with the consultant
"I do two joint visits a week with my team."
To shadow -- to go with someone and watch them work, without taking over
"Today I am only shadowing you, so the room is yours."
Call plan -- the written plan of who you will see and what you want from each visit
"Send me your call plan on Friday for the following week."
Sales call -- one meeting with a client, face to face, with a commercial purpose
"He makes around eight sales calls a day in the interior."
Gatekeeper -- the person who decides whether you get to see the client at all
"The receptionist is the gatekeeper, and she remembers everybody."
Rapport -- the comfortable, trusting relationship that lets a real conversation happen
"He has excellent rapport with the clinics in the south."
Pitch -- the short, prepared part where you explain what you are offering and why
"Her pitch is clear, but it starts too early in the conversation."
Objection -- the reason a client gives for not buying, or not buying yet
"The objection was price, but the real problem was the schedule."
Lead -- a person or clinic that might become a client, but is not one yet
"The congress gave us eleven leads in two days."
To close -- to get the client to agree to the next step, out loud
"He explained everything well, but he did not close."
Follow-up -- what you send or do after the visit, so the visit does not die there
"The follow-up should go out on the same day, not on Friday."
Debrief -- the short conversation after the visit, where you look at what happened
"The best debrief I ever had lasted four minutes."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the coaching note and answer the questions. Notice which sentences give a rule and which give advice.

Before a joint visit, the manager and the consultant must agree on one objective. Without it, the day is an inspection. They also have to leave early, because the clinic gave them only one slot at nine. The call plan is sent the evening before, so nobody arrives with questions that could have been answered in the car.

Inside the room the rule is simple: the manager is there to shadow, and he must not rescue the conversation. If he answers for the consultant, the doctor learns to speak to him instead. He does not have to stay completely silent, but he should stay out of the pitch and out of every objection. Building rapport with the gatekeeper is part of the visit too, and it is usually the consultant who does it.

The debrief happens straight after the sales call, in the car. It should take four minutes, not forty. One thing that worked, with the reason. One thing to change: today, the consultant did not close, so he should ask for the next step before he stands up. Then they agree who does what. The consultant sends the follow-up the same day, and the new lead from the congress goes into next week's plan.

1. "They must agree on one objective" and "they have to leave early". What is the difference?
A "Must" is the rule they set for themselves; "have to" comes from outside — the clinic only gave them one slot.
B "Must" is for the future and "have to" is for the past.
C There is no difference; the two are always interchangeable.
2. "He must not rescue the conversation." What does this mean?
A It is not necessary, but he can if he wants to.
B It is forbidden — doing it breaks the whole point of the visit.
C He forgot to do it.
3. "He does not have to stay completely silent." What does the text tell us here?
A Total silence is not required. It is optional, and either choice is acceptable.
B Speaking is forbidden.
C He is not able to stay silent.
4. "He should ask for the next step before he stands up." Why "should" and not "must"?
A Because "should" is the past form of "must".
B Because it is advice in a debrief, not an order — and advice is easier to accept and act on.
C Because "must" cannot be used with the verb "ask".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Must, Have To, Should

GRAMMAR

Two questions decide the form: is it an obligation or advice? And if it is negative, is the thing forbidden or simply not necessary?

FormWhat it meansExample
must + verbStrong obligation, usually coming from the speaker.You must agree one objective first.
have to + verbObligation from outside: a rule, the company, the situation.We have to be there by nine.
should + verbAdvice. The right thing to do, but nobody is forcing it.You should send it the same day.
must not + verbProhibition. It is forbidden.You must not answer for the consultant.
do not have to + verbNo obligation. It is optional.You do not have to know every answer.
should not + verbAdvice against something. Not forbidden, just a bad idea.You should not give five points of feedback at once.
Softer optionsYou could... · It might be worth... · Have you thought about... — better with a senior consultant.
Three trapsNever must to or should to. Never do not must — the negative of must is must not. And must not is not the same as do not have to: one forbids, the other frees.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

You are in the car, ten minutes before the clinic. You say:
A "Before we go in, let us agree on one objective for this call."
B "Today I am going to watch everything you do."
C "You must to be careful with this doctor."
The doctor asks a question and your consultant hesitates. The right move is:
A Answer quickly, so the meeting does not lose momentum.
B Stay quiet. If you rescue him, the doctor starts talking to you instead of to him.
C Repeat the question in a louder voice.
Bringing the printed price list is optional. You tell your consultant:
A "You must not bring the price list."
B "You do not have to bring the price list."
C "You do not must bring the price list."
You want to give one piece of advice about the ending of the visit. You say:
A "You should to close before you leave the room."
B "You should close before you leave the room."
C "You have to closing before you leave the room."
You noticed eleven things during the visit. In the debrief you:
A Give one thing that worked and one thing to change — a person carries one change.
B Give all eleven, so nothing is lost.
C Give none, and send an email on Friday instead.
The clinic gave you only one slot, at nine in the morning. You say:
A "We must to be there at nine."
B "We have to be there at nine, because that is the only slot."
C "We should be there at nine, it is obligatory."
