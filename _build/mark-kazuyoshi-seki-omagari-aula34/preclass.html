Lesson 34 -- Pre-class

Hardly Any, a Great Deal -- Advanced Quantifiers

"A lot of" and "not many" are correct and vague: they never say how much, or whether that is good news. This lesson gives you the words that say exactly how much there is -- hardly any, a great deal of, few and a few, plenty of, fewer and less, both and neither -- and you use them to compare Tokyo and Kyoto before the trip. Key words: hidden gem, tourist trap, bustling, low-key, landmark, day trip, off-peak, picturesque, nightlife, scenery, overcrowded, get a feel for.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. These are the words people use when they compare two places to visit.

Hidden gem -- a great place that very few people know about
"The best ramen we had was a hidden gem behind the station."
Tourist trap -- a place full of tourists where things cost too much and are not very good
"The shopping street next to the temple is a bit of a tourist trap."
Bustling -- busy and full of people, in a lively way
"Shibuya is bustling at every hour of the day."
Low-key -- relaxed and quiet, without much noise or excitement
"Kyoto feels far more low-key than Tokyo."
Landmark -- a famous building or place that is easy to recognize
"Tokyo Tower is the landmark everybody photographs."
Day trip -- a visit to a place and back again on the same day
"Nara is an easy day trip from Kyoto."
Off-peak -- at a time when fewer people travel or visit
"Go to the temple off-peak, before eight in the morning."
Picturesque -- very pretty, like a picture or a painting
"The old streets of Gion are picturesque in the rain."
Nightlife -- the places to go and the things to do in the evening
"Tokyo has plenty of nightlife; Kyoto has very little."
Scenery -- the natural things you see in a place, like hills, rivers and trees
"The scenery from the train is worth staying awake for."
Overcrowded -- with too many people in it
"By ten in the morning, the bamboo forest was overcrowded."
Get a feel for -- to start to understand what a place or a situation is like
"Walk around for a day to get a feel for the city."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the travel page and answer the questions. Notice how each quantifier in bold says exactly HOW MUCH, and whether that is good or bad news.

Every student who goes to Japan asks the same question: Tokyo or Kyoto? Our writer spent four days in each, and her first conclusion was simple. Neither city is better; both are good at different things.

Tokyo is bustling from morning to midnight. There is plenty of nightlife and a great many landmarks, but very little silence. Still, a few hidden gems are waiting in the side streets, where hardly any tourists go.

Kyoto is low-key and has a great deal of history. The famous temples are overcrowded by ten, so go off-peak, and you will see fewer people and hear less noise.

Her advice? Keep both, and use one day for a day trip to Nara, which few visitors regret.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Saying Exactly How Much

GRAMMAR

"A lot of" and "not many" are correct but vague. These quantifiers say exactly how much there is, and one little "a" decides whether the amount is good news or bad news.

QuantifierWhat it meansExample
hardly any / hardly anyone / hardly anythingalmost none -- already negative"There were hardly any tourists."
a great deal of + uncountablea large amount"a great deal of history"
a great many / a large number of + plurala large number of things you can count"a large number of temples"
few + plural / little + uncountablenot enough -- disappointing"Few people speak English there."
a few + plural / a little + uncountablesome -- good news"A few of the temples were free."
plenty of / fewer / lessPlenty of = more than enough. Fewer + plural ("fewer tourists"), less + uncountable ("less noise").
both / neither / eitherFor exactly two things. "Neither city was cheap, but both were worth it." Never "hardly no", never "a great deal of people", never "less people".
