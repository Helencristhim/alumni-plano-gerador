Four Ways to Say How Many

Same Morning, Four Amounts

"There were not many people at the temple."

Correct, and vague. How many is "not many"? Ten? Two hundred?

"There were hardly any people at the temple."

Almost nobody. Now the listener can see the empty courtyard.

"Few people were there, so it felt a bit empty."

Small, and a little disappointing.

"A few people were there, so it did not feel empty."

Small, and good news. One word changed the whole message.

Anyone can say "a lot" and "not many". Saying exactly how much, and whether that is good or bad, is what makes a comparison useful.

Chapter 3: How Much, How Many

How Much, How Many, How Good

The words English uses to say exactly how much there is

Grammar Discovery

Four Sentences, Four Amounts

There were hardly any tourists at the shrine at seven.

Kyoto has a great deal of history, but very little nightlife.

A few of the temples were free, so we saved a little money.

Neither city was cheap, but both were worth it.

Which words make the amount sound small and disappointing, and which make it sound small but good? Which pair only works when there are exactly two things?

QuantifierWhat it meansExample
hardly any / hardly anyone / hardly anythingalmost none — a strong negative"There were hardly any tourists."
a great deal of + uncountablea large amount, and a little formal"a great deal of history"
a great many / a large number of + plurala large number of things you can count"a large number of temples"
few + plural / little + uncountablenot much, not enough — disappointing"Few people speak English there."
a few + plural / a little + uncountablesome, enough — good news"A few of the temples were free."
plenty of + bothmore than enough"plenty of nightlife"
fewer + plural / less + uncountablea smaller number / a smaller amount"fewer tourists, less noise"
both / neither / eitherFor exactly two things. Neither + singular noun: "Neither city was cheap." Neither of the + plural: "Neither of the cities was cheap." Either = one or the other, it does not matter which.

The Tool

Three Places It Goes Wrong

Trap 1 · "hardly" is already negative

It means "almost not", so it takes any: "There were hardly any tourists." Never "hardly no tourists", and never "there weren't hardly any" — one negative is enough.

Trap 2 · a great deal of, only with what you cannot count

"A great deal of time, history, noise." With people, temples or trains, use a great many or a large number of. And the same split for fewer (people) and less (noise).

Trap 3 · one little "a" changes the message

"Few people came" is disappointing: you hoped for more. "A few people came" is good news: somebody came. Same number of people, opposite feeling. The same goes for little and a little.

Choose the right quantifier and the listener sees the place. Choose the wrong one and they hear the opposite of what you meant.

Listening

Saying Exactly How Much

Your teacher on the quantifiers and the three traps. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she say "hardly any" means, and what must never come after "hardly"?
It means almost none, and it is already negative, so you never say "hardly no".
2. When do you use "a great deal of", and what do you use for temples or trains?
"A great deal of" is for things you cannot count, like time or history. For countable things, "a great many" or "a large number of".
3. What is the difference between "few" and "a few"?
"Few" is small and disappointing; "a few" is small and good. The number is the same, the feeling is the opposite.
4. Which words does she say to use when there are exactly two cities?
Both, neither and either.

Chapter 5: Practice

Getting the Amount Right

Spot the Error

Fix the Amount

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "There were hardly no tourists at the shrine."
"There were hardly any tourists at the shrine." (hardly is already negative, so it takes any)
2. "Tokyo has a great deal of temples."
"Tokyo has a great many temples." or "a large number of temples" (a great deal of is only for what you cannot count)
3. "There were less people off-peak."
"There were fewer people off-peak." (people are countable: fewer, not less)
4. "Neither of the city was cheap."
"Neither city was cheap." or "Neither of the cities was cheap." (neither + singular, neither of the + plural)
5. "We had a few free time, so we took a day trip."
"We had a little free time, so we took a day trip." (time is uncountable: a little, not a few)
