What Television Gets Wrong About Law

A series everybody has seen, a profession that looks nothing like it, and the one thing the show gets right. Key expressions: a courtroom drama, a cross-examination, a closing argument, a verdict, to object, a billable hour, a paralegal, a deposition, a plea bargain, far-fetched, poetic license, a trope, behind the scenes, to cut corners. Structure: participle clauses and reduced relative clauses -- filmed in, having read, lawyers working at, not knowing.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to the courtroom on screen, or to the office behind it that television never films.

A courtroom drama -- a film or series built around lawyers, trials and courtrooms
"Every courtroom drama needs a trial, even when the real case would never reach one."
A cross-examination -- the questioning of a witness by the lawyer for the other side
"On television, a cross-examination ends in a confession; in real life, it ends in a short answer."
A closing argument -- the final speech a lawyer makes to the jury before it decides
"The closing argument took the hero four minutes and the writers four weeks."
A verdict -- the decision a jury reaches at the end of a trial
"Most of our cases end in a settlement, not a verdict."
To object -- to protest formally in court that a question or a piece of evidence should not be allowed
"A good lawyer will object when it matters, not every thirty seconds."
A billable hour -- an hour of a lawyer's work that is charged to the client
"Nobody on the show ever seems to record a billable hour."
A paralegal -- a trained assistant who does legal work but is not a lawyer
"The paralegal found the email that won the case, and the partner took the credit."
A deposition -- a witness's statement taken under oath outside court, before the trial
"The deposition lasted seven hours and produced one useful sentence."
A plea bargain -- an agreement in which the accused admits a smaller crime in exchange for a lighter sentence
"Most criminal cases in the United States end in a plea bargain."
Far-fetched -- so unlikely that it is hard to believe
"A lawyer with no law degree at a top firm? That is far-fetched, even for television."
Poetic license -- the freedom a writer takes to change the facts to make a story better
"The writers took some poetic license with the timeline."
A trope -- an idea or situation used so often in stories that everybody expects it
"The surprise witness is the oldest trope in legal fiction."
Behind the scenes -- out of public view, where the real work happens
"Behind the scenes, the case was won in the document review."
To cut corners -- to do something badly or break a rule to save time or money
"In real life, lawyers who cut corners do not keep their licenses for long."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Spoken English joins ideas with full clauses: the series, which is set in New York, follows a young man who has no law degree. Written English often cuts those clauses down: Set in New York, the series follows a young man with no law degree. Nothing was lost. The words which is and who has simply disappeared.

These shorter pieces are called participle clauses, and they come in three forms. The -ing form has an active meaning: lawyers working at big firms are lawyers who work there. The -ed form has a passive meaning: the documents requested by the court are documents that were requested. And having + past participle shows that one action came first: Having read the file, Mike walked into the meeting.

To make one negative, put not in front: Not knowing the rules, the witness kept talking. And remember the one rule that catches everybody: a participle clause has no subject of its own. It borrows the subject of the main clause. Walking into the courtroom, the judge looked at me means that the judge was the one walking.

Articles, judgments and prepared speeches use these clauses all the time, because they carry the same facts in fewer words. In conversation the long version is perfectly natural. The skill is to recognize both, and to choose.

1. Why does written English often use participle clauses instead of full clauses?
A Because full clauses with who and which are not allowed in formal writing.
B Because they carry the same facts in fewer words.
C Because they make the sentence sound more personal and closer to conversation.
2. Which sentence has a passive meaning?
A Lawyers working at big firms rarely see a jury.
B Having read the file, Mike walked into the meeting.
C The documents requested by the court arrived late.
3. What does having + past participle show?
A That one action happened before the other.
B That the action is still happening now.
C That the sentence is in the passive voice and the doer of the action is not important.
4. According to the text, what does Walking into the courtroom, the judge looked at me really mean?
A That I walked into the courtroom.
B That the judge was the one walking.
C That both of us walked in together, and then the judge looked at me from the bench.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Participle Clauses

GRAMMAR

Your first language reduces clauses too, especially in writing. What is new is the choice between -ing and -ed, and the rule that the participle borrows the subject of the main clause.

PatternWhen it happensExample
-ing participleAn ACTIVE meaning. It replaces who / which + verb, or because / while + subject + verb."lawyers working at big firms" = lawyers who work at big firms
past participleA PASSIVE meaning. It replaces which is / was + participle, or because it was."Filmed in New York, the series..." = the series is filmed in New York
having + past participleThe action happened BEFORE the main one."Having read the file, he walked in."
not + participleThe negative goes BEFORE the participle."Not knowing the rules, the witness kept talking."
the same subjectThe participle borrows the subject of the main clause. Different subjects = a sentence that says something you did not mean."Walking in, I saw the judge." not "Walking in, the judge saw me."

One habit for this week: when you read an article or a judgment in English, underline three participle clauses and rebuild the long version of each in your head. When the long version comes easily, the short one starts to appear in your own writing.

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to how a real lawsuit unfolds, far from the cameras, then put the steps in order.

?The depositions begin: witnesses answer questions under oath in a conference room, with no judge and no jury.
?A week before the trial, the case settles, and the courtroom scene never happens.
?The client's complaint is filed in court, and the other side has a few weeks to respond.

Quiz
