Chapter 3: The Code

Shorter Sentences, Same Facts

Participle clauses and reduced relative clauses

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Filmed in New York, the series follows a lawyer with no law degree."

"Having read the file once, Mike remembers every word of it."

"Lawyers working at big firms rarely see a jury."

"The documents requested by the other side took six months to review."

Each orange part replaces a longer piece of a sentence. Rebuild the long version of each one. What disappeared, and why do some orange words end in -ing and others in -ed?

FormWhat it doesExample
-ing participleAn ACTIVE meaning. It replaces who / which + verb, or because / while + subject + verb."lawyers working at big firms" = lawyers who work at big firms
past participleA PASSIVE meaning. It replaces which is / was + participle, or because it was."Filmed in New York, the series..." = the series is filmed in New York
having + past participleThe action happened BEFORE the main one, and the main clause is its result."Having read the file, he walked in." = after he had read it
not + participleThe negative goes BEFORE the participle, never after it."Not knowing the rules, the witness kept talking."
the same subjectThe participle has no subject of its own: it borrows the subject of the main clause. If the two subjects are different, the sentence says something you did not mean."Walking in, I saw the judge." not "Walking in, the judge saw me."

Your first language does this too, especially in writing. The trap is the subject: an English participle always belongs to the subject of the main clause.

Register

Long Version, Short Version

The full clause first, then the reduced one

"The series, which is set in a fictional firm, follows a young man who has no law degree."

The long version. Correct, but heavy: two clauses, two extra verbs.

"Set in a fictional firm, the series follows a young man with no law degree."

The short version. Same facts, eleven words instead of nineteen.

"After he had reviewed the documents, the associate who was working on the case called the client."

The long version. Natural in speech, slow on the page.

"Having reviewed the documents, the associate working on the case called the client."

The short version. This is how the article you are about to read is written.

Both versions are correct. The short one is what you find in articles, judgments and prepared speeches; the long one is what you use in conversation.

Listening 1

The Duel That Never Happens

A trial lawyer on what a real cross-examination sounds like. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the one rule she follows in a cross-examination?
Never ask a question you do not already know the answer to.
2. Where does most of her real work happen?
Months before the trial, in depositions, in a conference room with no judge and no jury.
3. What does she say about objections?
She objects maybe twice in a whole day; a lawyer objecting every minute would lose the jury and annoy the judge.
Listening 2

From the Document Room to the Writers' Room

A former paralegal who now writes legal dramas for television. Sound first — no text.

1. What did he do before he wrote for television, and for how long?
He worked as a paralegal at a firm in Chicago for four years, mostly reviewing documents.
2. What does he change, and what does he refuse to change?
He changes the timeline and compresses months into one episode; he does not change the way lawyers talk to each other.
3. Which scene is he proudest of?
A phone call about a settlement, with no courtroom at all.
