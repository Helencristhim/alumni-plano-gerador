Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Scope -- how far a rule, a duty or an agreement reaches, and where it stops
"The whole argument is about the scope of clause four, not about whether it is fair."
Ambiguity -- wording that can honestly be read in two different ways
"There is no ambiguity in a sentence until somebody has a reason to read it the other way."
A carve-out -- an exception written into an otherwise general rule, to take one thing out of it
"We agreed the general rule in five minutes. The carve-out took two hours."
To stipulate -- to state a requirement clearly, as a condition of the agreement
"The licence stipulates one edition. It does not say the hardback, and that is the problem."
An undertaking -- a formal promise to do, or not to do, one specific thing
"We will give an undertaking not to reprint until this is settled."
A proviso -- a condition attached to something that has otherwise been agreed
"She accepts the figure, with one proviso: the credit appears on every edition."
To supersede -- to replace an earlier version of a document and cancel it completely
"This agreement supersedes everything the two sides signed before today."
Exhaustive -- listing absolutely everything, with nothing left outside the list
"Is that list exhaustive, or are there editions it does not mention?"
Boilerplate -- standard wording copied into every agreement, which almost nobody reads
"Clause seven was boilerplate. That is precisely why it was never read."
Wording -- the exact words somebody chose, considered as something that can be argued about
"Nobody disputes what happened. What we are arguing about is the wording."
To draft -- to write the first version of a legal text, knowing somebody will attack it
"Whoever drafts the clause chooses what it can be made to mean."
Watertight -- so well written that nobody can find a way through it
"It is not a long clause. It is a watertight one, which is a different achievement."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two drafts of the same clause came out of the same firm three days apart. Draft A: the contributors who delivered their work before 1 March shall be paid the full fee. Draft B: the contributors, who delivered their work before 1 March, shall be paid the full fee. Nobody flagged the difference, and the difference is eleven fees.

In Draft A the clause has no commas, so it narrows the group: only the contributors who delivered before the first of March get paid, and the others do not. In Draft B the same words sit between two commas, and a clause between commas narrows nothing. It names the whole group and then adds a fact about all of them — a fact which, in this case, is not even true, because eleven of the nineteen delivered in April. The scope of the two clauses is different, and so is the money.

The rest follows from that. Use who for people, which or that for things, and whose for possession. Never use that between commas. A comma plus which can also refer to a whole sentence: nobody read the licence, which is how we got here. And you can drop the pronoun in a defining clause when it is the object — the email she sent in July — but never when it is the subject. The safest draft is often the shortest one: the full fee shall be paid only to those contributors who delivered on or before 1 March. That version is watertight, and no comma can change it.

1. Under Draft B, how many of the nineteen contributors are covered?
A Only the eight who delivered before the first of March.
B All nineteen, because a clause between commas does not narrow the group.
C None of them, because the clause is badly written.
2. Why can you not write "Clause four, that nobody read, is where the case lives"?
A Because "that" is never used in a clause between commas. It has to be "which".
B Because clause four is a thing, and "that" is only for people.
C Because the clause is too short to take commas.
3. According to the text, what makes the third version safer than either draft?
A It is longer, so it covers more situations.
B It says "only to those contributors who", so there is nothing left for a comma to decide.
C It removes the date, which was the ambiguous part.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Defining and Non-Defining Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

No commas narrows the group. Two commas keeps the whole group and adds a fact.

Narrow the group (defining)no commas — who / that / whichThe contributors who delivered on time will be paid.
Add a fact about all of them (non-defining)two commas — who / which, never thatThe contributors, who delivered on time, will be paid.

Stage 2: Put the Drafting Note in Order

Order

Six steps in a note explaining the problem to a partner. Put them in a logical order.

?"Two drafts of clause four came out of this office three days apart."
?"Draft A pays only the contributors who delivered before the first of March."
?"Draft B, which has two commas, pays all nineteen of them."
?"Eleven of the nineteen delivered in April, so Draft B also states something untrue."
?"This is a question of scope, not of punctuation, and it is worth eleven fees."
?"I propose one watertight version that supersedes both."

Quiz

You want to cover only the contributors who were on time. You write:

B "The contributors who delivered before the first of March will be paid."
C "The contributors, who delivered before the first of March will be paid."
You want to add a fact about clause four, which there is only one of. You write:
A "Clause four, which nobody read, is where the whole case lives."
B "Clause four, that nobody read, is where the whole case lives."
C "Clause four which nobody read is where the whole case lives."
The partner asks whether this is just punctuation. You answer:
A "Yes, but it looks untidy, so I would change it."
B "No. It is a question of scope, and the difference between the two drafts is eleven fees."
C "It depends on how the other side chooses to read it."
You are proposing a version nobody can argue with. You write:
A "The full fee shall be paid to the contributors, subject to delivery."
B "The full fee shall be paid only to those contributors who delivered on or before the first of March."
C "The full fee shall be paid to all contributors, who delivered on time."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.
