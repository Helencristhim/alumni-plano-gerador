NPR -- "The Oxford Comma Goes To Court"

A real case in which a missing comma in a list decided whether lorry drivers were owed overtime. The court read the sentence twice, found both readings honest, and decided in favour of the drivers. Short, and with a transcript.

Tip: listen, then write out the disputed sentence and both readings. Say out loud which group of workers is covered in each version, in one full sentence per version.

Article
CNN -- "A lack of an Oxford comma cost dairy $5 million"

The end of the same story: the company settled for five million dollars, and the state of Maine rewrote the law afterwards, replacing the list with semicolons so that it could never happen again. Two hundred words that make the point of today's lesson better than any grammar book.

Tip: read it once, then write the sentence Maine should have used in the first place. Use "only those" and a defining clause, exactly as you did in class.

TED -- Alan Siegel, "Let's simplify legal jargon!"

Four minutes on rewriting a credit card agreement so that a person can read it, and on why the long version was never protecting anybody. It is the argument of today's second recording, made in front of an audience that had all signed one of those agreements.

Tip: find one clause in any terms of use you have on your phone, in English, and rewrite it in half the words without losing anything. Read both versions out loud and say which one is watertight.
