Lesson 08 -- Pre-class

He Said, She Said -- Carrying Somebody Else Words

A sentence that eleven thousand places attribute to a man who never said it, and the grammar of reporting without dropping anything. Key words: to quote somebody, to summarize, to paraphrase, to assert, to contradict, to verify, to misquote, according to, to put it differently, to take somebody word for it. Structure: reported speech with backshift and reporting verbs.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To quote somebody -- to repeat somebody exact words, unchanged, inside quotation marks
"If you quote him, use his words. If you change them, do not use the marks."
To summarize -- to give the main points of something long, in much less space
"A journalist summarized the lecture in a newspaper piece the following week."
To paraphrase -- to say the same thing in your own words, at roughly the same length
"He paraphrased it accurately, which is not the same as quoting it."
To assert -- to state something firmly as true, without offering proof for it
"The more confidently a sentence is asserted, the less often anybody asks where it came from."
To contradict -- to say the opposite of what somebody has just said
"He admits he cannot contradict her, because they are arguing about different things."
To verify -- to check something against the original until you know it is true
"A quotation is verified by finding the original, not by finding it in three places."
To misquote -- to repeat somebody words wrongly, whether or not you meant to
"Nobody had to misquote anybody deliberately. One set of punctuation marks did the work."
According to -- names the source of a claim, and quietly says it is not yours
"According to a researcher who spent two years on it, the physicist never said it."
To put it differently -- announces that you are about to say the same thing another way
"One of them put it differently, and better than the others."
To take somebody word for it -- to accept what somebody says without checking it yourself
"She was not going to take his word for it over her own thirty years in a classroom."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When you carry somebody else words into your own sentence, English moves almost everything one step back. “I study every day” becomes He said he studied every day. “I have seen it” and “I saw it” both become She said she had seen it — the present perfect and the past simple arrive at the same place. Pronouns change owner, and time words move with them: tomorrow becomes the next day, today becomes that day, now becomes then.

Questions are where almost everybody slips, because the step back is not the only thing that happens. A reported question stops being a question: the do disappears, the word order goes back to normal and there is no question mark. “Where do you live?” becomes He asked me where I lived, never where did I live. For a yes or no question there is no question word to reuse, so English borrows one: She asked whether I agreed. Orders and advice take a different shape again: He told me to push through it — tell, then the person, then to.

Two things worth fixing for good. First: tell takes a person directly and say does not. He told me, or he said that. Never he said me, and never he told to me. Second: not everything has to step back. If the fact is still true as you speak, you can leave it where it is — He said the exam is in January is correct, and stepping it back would quietly suggest it is not any more. And one habit that separates a good report from a flat one: stop using said every time. Explained, admitted, claimed, warned, insisted, pointed out each carry information that said throws away.

1. “Where do you live?” What happens when you report it?

2. Which sentence is correct?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech: One Step Back

GRAMMAR

The verb, the pronoun and the day all move. The question also loses its do and its question mark.

JobFormExample
A statementsay (that) + one step back"I study every day." → He said he studied every day.
Present perfect and past simpleboth become the past perfect"I have seen it." → She said she had seen it.
A question with a question wordask + question word + normal word order"Where do you live?" → He asked me where I lived.
A yes or no questionask + if or whether"Do you agree?" → She asked whether I agreed.
An order or a piece of advicetell · advise + object + to + verb"Push through it." → He told me to push through it.
Time and place move toonow→then · today→that day · tomorrow→the next day"I will call you tomorrow." → He said he would call me the next day.
Beyond saidtold · explained · admitted · claimed · warned · insisted · pointed outShe admitted that she had not checked it.
No step back neededwhen the fact is still true right now"The exam is in January." → He said the exam is in January.

Never: He said me that · He told to me · She asked me where did I live · He said he will call me tomorrow
