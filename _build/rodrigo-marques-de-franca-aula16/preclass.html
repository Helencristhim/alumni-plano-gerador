Lesson 16 -- Pre-class

The Long Sentence -- Decoding the Legal Paragraph

A legal opinion on a late delivery, and the structure that makes English legal sentences so long. Key words: a legal opinion, hereinafter, subject to, pursuant to, to be deemed, whereas, thereof, to set forth, to be bound by, a proviso, to construe, ambiguous. Structure: reduced relative clauses and participle clauses.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A legal opinion -- a formal written answer from a lawyer to a client's legal question
"The client wants a legal opinion before Friday."
Hereinafter -- from this point on in the document
"The Supply Agreement, hereinafter the Agreement, was signed in March."
Subject to -- depending on a condition, or limited by it
"Our view is that it may terminate, subject to one condition."
Pursuant to -- in accordance with, as required by
"Pursuant to Clause 14.1, the notice must be in writing."
To be deemed -- to be treated as true for legal purposes, even if it is not literally so
"A delay of more than thirty days shall be deemed a material breach."
Whereas -- a word that introduces the background facts at the start of a contract
"Whereas the parties wish to cooperate, they agree as follows."
Thereof -- of the thing that was just mentioned
"The fifteen days are counted from receipt thereof."
To set forth -- to state something formally and in detail, in writing
"The notice must set forth the breach."
To be bound by -- to have a legal obligation to follow something
"Both parties are bound by the courier requirement."
A proviso -- a condition added to an agreement or a statement
"He agreed, with the proviso that the cap still applies."
To construe -- to interpret the meaning of words in a particular way
"A court could construe the clause against the drafter."
Ambiguous -- open to more than one interpretation
"The second sentence is ambiguous, so we should redraft it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A Brazilian food company wants to end a contract with a Dutch supplier. The equipment ordered in June arrived forty-five days late, and under the contract any delivery made more than thirty days late is deemed a material breach.

The law firm's opinion answers the question in its second sentence: the company may terminate, subject to one condition. Having reviewed the correspondence, the lawyers see no evidence of force majeure. But the party wishing to terminate must first send a written notice setting forth the breach.

A notice sent by email alone is not enough. Any notice not complying with the courier requirement could be construed as invalid. Each short form in bold replaces a longer clause: which was ordered, who wishes, after they had reviewed.

1. "The equipment ordered in June." What is the long version?
A The equipment that ordered something from the supplier back in June, before the contract was signed.
B The equipment which was ordered in June.
C The equipment ordering in June.
2. "The party wishing to terminate." Who does the wishing?
A The lawyers who wrote the opinion for the company.
B Nobody: it is a passive form.
C The party itself: -ing means the noun does the action.
3. Why is an email not enough?
A Because the contract requires the notice to be delivered by courier.
B Because emails between companies in two different countries are never legally valid anywhere in the world.
C Because the supplier does not read email.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reduced and Participle Clauses

GRAMMAR

A long English sentence is usually several sentences compressed. Each short form below replaces a whole clause.

Short formLong versionExample
noun + past participlewhich / that was + past participle (passive)"the equipment ordered in June"
noun + -ingwho / that + verb (active)"the party wishing to terminate"
not + -ingthat does not + verb"any notice not complying with Clause 14"
Having + past participleafter + had + past participle (the earlier action)"Having reviewed the file, we..."
The subject ruleThe subject after the comma did the participle."Having reviewed the file, we found..." NOT "the contract seemed..."
When not to reduceA single finished active action in the past"the client who called yesterday" NOT "calling yesterday"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are writing to, or talking with, clients and lawyers abroad. Choose the best sentence.

You want to refer to a contract signed last year:
A The contract which it signed last year by both parties in our office is still valid.
B The contract signed last year is still valid.
C The contract signing last year is still valid.
You read the file first, then you found a problem:
A Having read the file, we found a problem.
B Having read the file, a problem was found by the contract and also by our team.
C Having reading the file, we found a problem.
A foreign client asks if they can terminate. The answer is yes, with a condition:
A Yes, you can, subject to one condition.
B Yes, you can, pursuant to subject of one condition that is set forth hereinafter in this message.
C Yes, you can, deemed one condition.
A clause can be read in two different ways:
A The clause is ambiguous.
B The clause is bound by two meanings and construed by both of the parties at the same time.
C The clause is a proviso.
You want to say people who live abroad usually prefer plain English:
A Clients lived abroad usually prefer plain English.
B Clients living abroad usually prefer plain English.
C Clients who living abroad and who work there usually prefer to receive plain English.
