Chapter 4: The Code

Two Endings, and Two Words That Refuse Both

Short adjectives grow a tail. Long ones get a word in front.

Grammar Discovery

Two Grew a Tail. Two Got a Word in Front.

"Her house is bigger and much cheaper than my apartment."

"My street is more crowded and more expensive."

"She says it is the quietest place she knows."

"The traffic is the worst part of her week."

Short adjectives grew a tail: -er to compare two, -est for the one at the top. Long ones could not, so they got a blue word in front instead: more, most. And the last one did neither — bad simply became something else.

JobFormExample
Two things, short adjectiveadjective + -er + thancheaper than · safer than · bigger than (double the g)
Two things, adjective ending in -y-y becomes -iernoisy → noisier · easy → easier · happy → happier
Two things, long adjectivemore + adjective + thanmore crowded · more expensive · more comfortable
The one at the top, shortthe + adjective + -estthe cheapest · the quietest · the safest
The one at the top, longthe most + adjectivethe most expensive · the most comfortable
The two that break every rulegood → better → the best · bad → worse → the worstThe traffic is the worst part of her week.
When they are equalas + adjective + as · not as + adjective + asIt is not as comfortable as the other one.
The trapONE marker per adjective — never bothNot more cheaper, not more better, not the most big. Same rule as the s and the did.

In one line: short adjectives grow a tail, long ones take a word in front, and no adjective ever takes both.

Listening 1 · Two Cities

A Nicer Life, or a Bigger One

A man comparing the two cities he has lived in. Sound first — no text.

1. What three things were better in the first city?
It was cheaper, warmer and much friendlier. He knew the man at the bakery, his dog, and which day he made the good bread.
2. What does he say about the second city, and about his own work there?
It is more expensive, colder and a lot less friendly, but there is something on every night, the trains work, and he is better at his job than he was, because the people around him are better at theirs.
3. How does he answer the question at the end?
The first one was a nicer life and the second one is a bigger one, and he does not think you get both.
Listening 2 · Better at What?

Finish the Sentence

A woman arguing with the word better. Sound first — no text.

1. What three examples does she give of two good things that do not go together?
A car can be faster and less safe. A flat can be cheaper and further from everything. A job can pay more and cost you every Sunday.
2. What is her first question back when somebody asks whether one thing is better?
Better for who, and better for which part of your life.
3. What does she say happens once you finish the sentence?
The choice usually makes itself, and you stop arguing with people who want a different half of the same thing.

Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Two Places You Know, No Notes

Scenario: Compare two places you know well — two neighborhoods, two cities, the place you live now and the place you grew up. Give me at least four comparisons, the best thing about one and the worst thing about the other, and say which you prefer and why. Then ask me one question.

No keywords, no notes, two minutes. Nobody will interrupt you.
