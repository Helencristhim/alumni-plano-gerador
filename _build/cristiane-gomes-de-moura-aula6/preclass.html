Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

Better, Bigger, Best -- Choosing, and Saying Why

Choosing is easy. Explaining the choice is the part that turns an opinion into a conversation. Key words: crowded, noisy, expensive, cheap, safe, comfortable, a view, the countryside, traffic, to prefer. Structure: comparatives and superlatives — short adjectives grow a tail (-er, -est), long ones take a word in front (more, most), and good and bad refuse both.

"The best thing about it is the view. The worst thing is the price."

"She moved to the countryside, four hours from where they grew up."

"The traffic is the worst part of her week."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Putting two things side by side is one of the most useful things you can do in a second language, because it turns "I liked it" into something the other person can argue with. English does it in two ways, and the only thing that decides between them is how long the adjective is. Short adjectives grow a tail: "Her house is bigger and much cheaper than mine." "This street is safer." If the adjective ends in -y, the y changes: noisy becomes noisier, easy becomes easier.

Long adjectives cannot carry a tail, so they take a word in front instead: "My street is more crowded and more expensive than hers." "The other one is more comfortable." And when you want the one at the top of the whole group, the same split happens again: the cheapest, the safest, the quietest for short ones; the most expensive, the most comfortable for long ones. Notice the the: it never disappears in front of a superlative.

Two adjectives ignore all of that. Good becomes better and then the best. Bad becomes worse and then the worst: "The traffic is the worst part of my week." You also have a way of saying two things are equal, which is as ... as: "It is not as comfortable as the other one." And now the trap, which is the same trap you have met twice already: one marker per adjective. Never more cheaper, never more better, never the most big — exactly as the s only lands once and the did only lands once. Finish with the choice and the reason, and then hand the question back: "I prefer this one because the view is better. And you?"

1. According to the text, what decides between a tail and a word in front?
A Whether the sentence is positive or negative.
B How long the adjective is — short ones grow -er, long ones take more.

2. Why is "more cheaper" wrong?
A Because there is one marker per adjective, and cheaper already has it.
B Because cheap is an irregular adjective.
C Because you cannot compare prices in English.
3. The text says this trap is one you have already met twice. Where?
A In the vocabulary of lessons 1 and 2.
B With the third-person s, and with the did of the past — both land once only.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives, Superlatives, and One Marker Only

GRAMMAR

Short adjectives grow a tail. Long ones take a word in front. No adjective ever takes both.

JobFormExample
Two things, short adjectiveadjective + -er + thancheaper than · safer than · bigger than (double the g)
Two things, adjective in -y-y becomes -iernoisy → noisier · easy → easier
Two things, long adjectivemore + adjective + thanmore crowded · more expensive · more comfortable
The one at the top, shortthe + adjective + -estthe cheapest · the quietest · the safest
The one at the top, longthe most + adjectivethe most expensive · the most comfortable
The two that break every rulegood → better → the best · bad → worse → the worstThe traffic is the worst part of my week.
When they are equalas + adjective + as · not as + adjective + asIt is not as comfortable as the other one.
Making it strongermuch / a lot + comparativeIt is much cheaper. It is a lot noisier.
The trapONE marker per adjectiveNot more cheaper, not more better, not the most big.

Never: more noisy · more cheaper · more better · the most big · the baddest part

Stage 2: Put the Argument in Order

Order

This is how you explain a choice. Put it in a logical order.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to say your street has more noise than your friend’s. You say:
A "My street is more noisy than hers."
B "My street is noisier than hers."
C "My street is more noisier than hers."
You want to say one option costs less. You say:
A "This one is much cheaper than the other."
B "This one is more cheaper than the other."
C "This one is the more cheap of the other."
You want to name the single worst thing about your week. You say:
A "The traffic is the baddest part of my week."
B "The traffic is the most bad part of my week."
C "The traffic is the worst part of my week."
Somebody asks which of two places you would take. You answer:
A "The second one."
B "I prefer the second one, because the view is better and I am only going once."
C "I prefer more the second one of the first."
