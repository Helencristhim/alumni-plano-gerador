Lesson 05 — Pre-class

New City, New Street

A friend is coming to visit and she has never seen the place you live now. Before the lesson: eight words for a street — neighborhood, block, crosswalk, doorman, crowded, traffic, rush hour, within walking distance — and the two words that carry every description in English: there is and there are, with the quantity in front of the noun.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Eight words for the street you live on. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Neighborhood -- the streets and buildings around where you live
"My old neighborhood was three blocks from the beach."
Block -- the part of a street between two corners
"The bakery is two blocks from my building."
Crosswalk -- the white lines where people walk across the street
"Wait at the crosswalk, the cars here do not stop."
Doorman -- the person who works at the entrance of a building
"Ask the doorman, he knows everything about this street."
Crowded -- full of people, with not much space to move
"The metro is crowded until nine in the morning."
Traffic -- all the cars and buses moving on the streets
"There is a lot of traffic on this avenue."
Rush hour -- the time of day when the streets are fullest
"At rush hour it takes forty minutes to go four blocks."
Within walking distance -- close enough to get there on foot
"The park is within walking distance, so I never take the car."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the message. The words in bold are how English says what a place has.

Okay, so before you come: my neighborhood is nothing like the old one. There is a doorman in my building and he opens the door before you even ring. There are two crosswalks on my block, which sounds boring until you try to cross without one.

At rush hour there is a lot of traffic and the sidewalk is crowded, so we will go out later. On Sunday there isn't much noise at all. There aren't many trees, I will be honest, and there is no beach — but there are a few good places within walking distance, and one of them opens at six in the morning.

"There is a lot of traffic" — why is and not are, when there are so many cars?
A It is a mistake — it should be are.
B Traffic is one thing you cannot count, so it takes is.
C Because the sentence is about the future.
Why is it "there aren't many trees" and not "there aren't much trees"?
A You can count trees, so plural things take many.
B Both are correct in English.
C Much only works in questions.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

There is / There are — and how much of it

This is how English says that something exists in a place.

  • There is (There's) + one thing, or a thing you cannot count: "There's a doorman." / "There's a lot of traffic."
  • There are + two or more things you can count: "There are two crosswalks."
  • Questions: "Is there a bakery near here?" / "Are there any parks?"

The quantity goes in front of the noun

  • a lot of — works with both: "a lot of traffic" / "a lot of cars"
  • much — things you cannot count, in negatives: "There isn't much noise."
  • many — things you can count, in negatives: "There aren't many trees."
  • a few — three or four countable things: "There are a few shops."
  • no — zero: "There is no beach." / "There are no trees."

The trap. Your first language uses a verb like have in this sentence, so it often comes out as "there have a lot of traffic". In English it is always there is or there are — never there have.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Say each one twice: once slowly, once at speaking pace. In fast speech there is becomes there's, in one sound.

There's a doorman in my building.
There are a lot of shops within walking distance.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Somebody who has never been to your city is standing at your front door. In about ninety seconds, walk them down your street out loud: start at the entrance of your building, then your block, then what is within walking distance. Use there is at least twice and there are at least twice, three different quantities, and finish with one thing your street does not have.
