Lesson 05 — Pre-class

New City, New Street

A friend is coming to visit and she has never seen the place you live now. Before the lesson: eight words for a street — neighborhood, block, crosswalk, doorman, crowded, traffic, rush hour, within walking distance — and the two words that carry every description in English: there is and there are, with the quantity in front of the noun.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Eight words for the street you live on. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Neighborhood -- the streets and buildings around where you live
"My old neighborhood was three blocks from the beach."
Block -- the part of a street between two corners
"The bakery is two blocks from my building."
Crosswalk -- the white lines where people walk across the street
"Wait at the crosswalk, the cars here do not stop."
Doorman -- the person who works at the entrance of a building
"Ask the doorman, he knows everything about this street."
Crowded -- full of people, with not much space to move
"The metro is crowded until nine in the morning."
Traffic -- all the cars and buses moving on the streets
"There is a lot of traffic on this avenue."
Rush hour -- the time of day when the streets are fullest
"At rush hour it takes forty minutes to go four blocks."
Within walking distance -- close enough to get there on foot
"The park is within walking distance, so I never take the car."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the message. The words in bold are what turns a list of things into a description.

Okay, so before you come: my neighborhood is nothing like the old one. There's a doorman in my building who opens the door before you even ring, and two crosswalks on my block, which sounds boring until you try to cross without one.

At rush hour the sidewalk is so crowded that we will go out later. The avenue behind us, which they closed for a year, has just reopened, so there is traffic again. On Sunday you can hear nothing at all.

And the place everybody goes to is a bakery two blocks away that opens at six in the morning. It is within walking distance, which is the only reason I ever see this street empty.

"There's a doorman who opens the door before you even ring." What does the who part do?
A It asks a question about the doorman.
B It puts a second sentence inside the first one, so you say it in one breath.
C It is optional decoration and changes nothing.
"The avenue behind us, which they closed for a year, has just reopened." Why the commas?
A There is only one avenue behind us — the clause just adds something, so it goes between commas.
B Because the sentence is long.
C Commas are always used before which.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Relative clauses — saying it in one sentence

A relative clause puts a second sentence inside the first one. It is the difference between a list and a description.

  • who for people, which for things, that for either — but only without commas
  • where for places: "the place where you buy bread"

The commas change the meaning

  • No commas — it tells you WHICH one: "the bakery that opens at five". Take it out and you do not know which bakery.
  • Two commas — it just ADDS something: "Jorge, who has been here twenty years, is the doorman". Take it out and the sentence still works.
  • After a comma you can use which or who, and never that.

Two shortcuts (no commas only)

  • Drop the pronoun when the clause has its own subject: "the park that everybody goes to"
  • Drop which is / who is altogether: "a café which is called Bonito", "the man who is standing at the crosswalk"

The trap. "There's a bakery who opens at five" — who is only for people. And you cannot drop the pronoun when it is the subject of its own clause: not "a bakery opens at five", which means something else entirely.

Stage 2: From the door to the corner

Order

This is how you describe a street: start at your door and walk outwards. Put the four lines in that order.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Say each one twice: once slowly, once at speaking pace. In fast speech there is becomes there's, in one sound.

There's a doorman in my building.
There are a lot of shops within walking distance.
There isn't much traffic on Sunday.
At rush hour the sidewalk is crowded.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz
A friend asks what your street has. Which sentence is correct English?
A "There have a lot of shops on my street."
B "There are a lot of shops on my street."
C "It has a lot of shops in my street."
It is eight in the morning and nothing is moving. What do you say?
A "There's a lot of traffic — it's rush hour."
B "There are a lot of traffics — it's rush hour."
C "There have many traffic — it's rush hour."
You want to tell your friend about Jorge, and there is only one Jorge. Which one?
A "Jorge that has been here twenty years is the doorman."
B "Jorge, who has been here twenty years, is the doorman."
C "Jorge which has been here twenty years is the doorman."
There is a shop on your corner and you cannot remember its name in English. What do you do?
A Say the word in Portuguese and hope they understand.
B Say "there's a place where you buy bread on the corner".
C Stop talking and change the subject.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Somebody who has never been to your city is standing at your front door. In about ninety seconds, walk them down your street out loud: start at the entrance of your building, then your block, then what is within walking distance. Use there is at least twice and there are at least twice, three different quantities, and finish with one thing your street does not have.
