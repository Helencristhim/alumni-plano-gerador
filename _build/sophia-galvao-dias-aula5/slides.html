The Code

One sentence, not a list

"There's a bakery on the corner."  →  "There's a bakery on the corner that opens at five."

"Jorge knows everything about the building."  →  "Jorge, who has been here twenty years, knows everything about the building."

"The park is ten minutes away."  →  "The park everybody goes to is ten minutes away."

"There's a café which is called Bonito."  →  "There's a café called Bonito."

No commasTwo commas
It tells you which one.
the bakery that opens at five
take it out and you do not know which bakery		It just adds something.
Jorge, who has been here twenty years, ...
take it out and the sentence still works
that / which / whowhich / who — never that

These are relative clauses: defining when there are no commas, non-defining when there are. Two shortcuts, both only without commas: when the clause has its own subject you can drop the pronoun (the park that everybody goes to), and which is / who is can disappear altogether (a café which is called Bonito, the man who is standing at the crosswalk).

Listening 1

Ruby's other street

1. What was on her old street that is not on the new one?
A bakery on the corner, and a lot of children her own age.
2. What is better about the new neighborhood?
The park and the library are within walking distance, and there is not much traffic.
3. How long did it take her to stop hating it?
About three months — and she says the street did not change, she did.

What did Ruby ask?

It has nothing — not even an elevator.
2. What surprises Ruby about the bakery?
That it opens at six in the morning — nothing opens that early where she lives.
3. What does Ruby ask about Saturdays?
Whether the street is crowded.
Listening 2

The walking tour guide

1. How many blocks is the whole walk, and how long does it take?
Six blocks, about forty minutes.
2. What does the guide say about the crosswalk on the second street?
There is only one, so cross there and never in the middle of the block.
3. When does the guide say NOT to do this walk, and why?
At rush hour, between six and eight, because the sidewalks are crowded and there is too much traffic noise.
