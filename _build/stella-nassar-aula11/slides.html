Chapter 4: The Style Code

Saying More by Saying Less

Rhetorical questions and understatement

Discover the Style

Is the Writer Saying More or Less?

"If a prompt is a brushstroke, is a camera a painter?"

"The idea is not unreasonable, but it is incomplete."

"Shredding a painting worth a million pounds was hardly a quiet gesture."

"The new technology is not without its critics."

For each sentence, what is the writer really claiming?

FormWhat it doesExample
rhetorical questionmakes a claim that the listener is invited to completeIs a camera a painter? (implied: no)
question + answer (hypophora)raises a question and answers it at onceSo who is the artist? The person who chooses.
litotesaffirms something by denying its oppositenot unreasonable = reasonable; not without critics = many critics
understatementdeliberately minimizes to create emphasis or ironyhardly a quiet gesture; a minor inconvenience for a disaster
The C2 trapoveruse and ambiguity. Three rhetorical questions in a row sound like a talk show, and litotes can confuse a listener who takes it literally. One question, then your answer.

Chapter 5: Voices at the Table

The Debate, Out Loud

A seminar discussion, a journalist and an artist

Dialogue

The Harkness Table

O
Here is my provocation. Choosing a prompt is a creative act, so an AI image can be art. Who wants to push back?
S
I will. If a prompt is a brushstroke, is a restaurant order a recipe?
O
Nice. But photographers also press a button, and nobody says a photograph is not art anymore.
S
That comparison is not unreasonable. A photographer still chooses where to stand, when to wait and what to leave out.
O
And a person using a generative program chooses among hundreds of images. Is that not a kind of choosing?
S
It is. The problem is the dataset. Those images were learned from artists who never agreed to it.
O
So your objection is not really about art. It is about authorship and consent.
S
Maybe that is the more honest question. Not is it art, but whose work is inside it.
O
Half this table walked in thinking this was a simple yes or no. Hardly anyone thinks so now.
S
I am still ambivalent. But I am ambivalent about better questions.

Listening 1

In the Auction Room

A journalist remembers the evening a painting shredded itself. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. What happened immediately after the auctioneer's hammer came down?
An alarm sounded and the canvas began sliding down through the frame, coming out in strips.
2. How far did the shredding go?
Only about halfway: the lower part was cut into strips and the top remained intact.
3. What does the journalist say about the value of the work afterward?
Rather than destroying its value, the stunt made the half-shredded work far more valuable when it was sold again a few years later.
Listening 2

The Artist Who Works with Code

An artist speaks about using machine learning in his studio. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. How does the artist describe the role of the machine in her work?
As a very fast, very strange assistant that suggests possibilities; he still decides what to keep.
2. What does he say worries him about some AI image tools?
That they were trained on the work of artists who were never asked and are not credited.
3. How does he respond to the question of whether his work is art?
He says the question is not uninteresting, but he prefers to ask what the work makes people notice.
