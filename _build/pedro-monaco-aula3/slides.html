Chapter 3: The Code

The Sentence That Does Not Start Again

Relative clauses — and the comma that changes the meaning

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The clause that protects us is the one nobody reads."

"Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand it in one sentence."

"This is the safeguard we rely on when the numbers look strange."

"Delaware, where the subsidiary was incorporated, has its own company law."

Delete the coloured part of each sentence. Two of them still say something useful. Two of them fall apart. What do the two that survive have in common?

FormWhat it doesExample
defining (no commas)Tells the reader WHICH one you mean. Delete it and the sentence loses its point. No commas — and here that is allowed."The clause that protects us is the one nobody reads."
non-defining (commas)Adds information about something already identified. Delete it and the sentence still stands. The commas are obligatory, and that is never allowed."Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand it."
dropping the pronounWhen the relative pronoun is the OBJECT of its clause, leave it out. This is the single change that makes written English sound spoken."the safeguard we rely on"
not "the safeguard which we rely on"
whose · where · whenwhose = of whom, of which (people AND things). where = in which place. when = at which time."a partner whose filings are late"
"Delaware, where the subsidiary was incorporated"
the prepositionFormal register puts it before the pronoun; everybody else leaves it at the end. Both are correct, and only one of them sounds like a person talking."the rule on which the policy is based"
"the rule the policy is based on"

Portuguese has one word — que — where English chooses between who, which and that, and where a comma decides whether you mean SOME or ALL. The idea is familiar; the choices are new.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Our head of product, that is not a lawyer, has to understand it."
"Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand it."
"The clause which protects us, is the one nobody reads."
"The clause which protects us is the one nobody reads."
"This is the safeguard that we rely on it."
"This is the safeguard that we rely on."

The first is the rule with no exception: after a comma, never that. The second is a comma that cuts the subject away from its verb — the clause is part of the subject, so nothing may separate them. The third is Portuguese arriving intact: a garantia em que a gente confia nela. In English the object appears once, and the relative pronoun already is it.

Practice

Join the Two Sentences

Say it first, then click to check

"The clause ___that protects us is the one nobody reads. (it protects us)"

Chapter 4: What Do You Actually Do?

Two Years in the Same Building

Dana runs product and has never understood what the legal team does

Dialogue

So, What Do You Actually Do?

D
I have been here two years, and I still could not tell anybody what your team actually does.
P
You are not the first to say that. In a nutshell, we decide what the company can sign, and on what basis.
D
That is shorter than I expected. So when you ask me for three documents before a partnership, what is that?
P
That is due diligence. We vet the other side before the first contract, because after signature it costs far more.
D
And the part where my launch waits four days for an approval? Is that the same thing?
P
Fair question. Some of it is me being a gatekeeper, and some of it is a safeguard that exists because it went wrong once.
D
Then give me the version I can repeat to my own team, without the legal words.
P
It boils down to one question: if this goes wrong, who has to explain it? If the answer is us, I want to see it first.

Listening 1

If You Cannot Say It

A general counsel on the test he has used for twenty years. Sound first — no text.

1. Where did the advice come from, and what was it?
From a client who was not a lawyer: if you cannot explain a clause in one sentence, you do not understand it yet — you are only quoting it.
2. What is his test for everything he writes?
He reads it out loud to somebody in another department, and if their face changes, he rewrites it.
3. Which lawyers does he say actually get listened to?
The ones whose colleagues call them before signing instead of afterwards.
Listening 2

What Actually Gets Flagged

A compliance officer on the boring reality of the job. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say actually gets flagged?
Boring things: a supplier whose address changes twice in a month, an invoice just under the second-signature limit, a partner with no website.
2. What is compliance deciding, according to her?
Not whether somebody is guilty, but whether a human being should look at it.
3. What does she say about whistleblowers?
They are almost never people with a grudge — usually somebody who tried the normal channel three times, got nowhere, and had one option left.
