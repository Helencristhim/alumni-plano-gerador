Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

What Exactly Do You Do? — Explaining Compliance to a Non-Lawyer

A dialogue with somebody who is not a lawyer and has never understood what your team does. Key expressions: a safeguard, to flag, a loophole, due diligence, a whistleblower, to vet, a conflict of interest, plain English, to boil down to, a gatekeeper, to sign off on, a rule of thumb, to get bogged down, in a nutshell. Structure: relative clauses — defining and non-defining, the comma that changes the meaning, and the pronoun you can leave out.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen are ways of talking about legal work with somebody who has never read a contract.

A safeguard -- a rule or a measure that exists to prevent a risk
"Every payment above that amount has a safeguard behind it."
To flag -- to point something out so that other people look at it
"I flag anything that looks unusual, even when it turns out to be nothing."
A loophole -- a gap in the rules that lets somebody avoid them legally
"They found a loophole in the wording and used it for two years."
Due diligence -- the checking you do before you sign, buy or hire
"We ran due diligence on the supplier before anything was signed."
A whistleblower -- somebody inside an organisation who reports wrongdoing
"The whistleblower who reported it kept her job, which is rare."
To vet -- to check a person or a company carefully before working with them
"We vet every partner before the first contract is drafted."
A conflict of interest -- a situation in which your duties pull in two directions
"He stepped out of the meeting because he had a conflict of interest."
Plain English -- clear language written for a reader who is not a specialist
"The clause was rewritten in plain English and lost nothing."
To boil down to -- to reduce something complicated to its essential point
"My whole job can boil down to one question: who signs, and on what basis?"
A gatekeeper -- somebody whose approval is needed before anything can go on
"Half of my day is being a gatekeeper, and nobody enjoys that half."
To sign off on -- to give formal approval to something
"Nobody should sign off on a document they have not read."
A rule of thumb -- a rough practical guide that works most of the time
"My rule of thumb is simple: if it is hard to explain, it is hard to defend."
To get bogged down -- to become stuck in detail and slow everything down
"It is easy to get bogged down in wording that nobody will ever read."
In a nutshell -- in a few words, the whole point
"In a nutshell, we decide what the company can sign."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ask a lawyer what they do and you usually get one of two answers: a job title that explains nothing, or four minutes that explain far too much. The useful answer sits between them, and it almost always contains a relative clause. In a nutshell, I decide what the company can sign. The contracts that carry real risk come to me first. Two sentences — and the second one is doing the work, because it tells you WHICH contracts without starting again.

That second sentence is a defining relative clause, and it takes no commas. Take the commas seriously, because they change what you are saying. The clauses that protect us are rewritten in plain English means some clauses protect us, and those are the ones being rewritten. The clauses, which protect us, are rewritten in plain English means all of them protect us and all of them are being rewritten. The comma is not decoration. It is the difference between some and all, and in a contract that difference is the whole argument.

Non-defining clauses are the ones you could delete without damage. They always take commas, and they never take that. Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand the clause in one sentence. Delaware, where the subsidiary was incorporated, has its own company law. A partner whose filings are always late is telling you something before the due diligence gets a chance to.

Two habits will make you sound like somebody who speaks English rather than somebody who writes it. First, drop the pronoun when it is the object of its own clause: the safeguard we rely on, not the safeguard which we rely on. Second, do not name the object twice. The safeguard that we rely on it is the commonest mistake a Portuguese speaker makes here, because in Portuguese the pronoun stays. In English it goes, and the sentence is already finished.

1. What does the text say a useful answer to "what do you do?" almost always contains?

2. What is the difference between "the clauses that protect us" and "the clauses, which protect us"?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

Portuguese has one word — que — where English chooses between who, which and that, and where a comma decides whether you mean SOME or ALL. The idea will feel familiar; the choices are where the work is.

FormWhat it doesExample
defining (no commas)Tells the reader WHICH one you mean. Delete it and the sentence loses its point. No commas — and here that is allowed."The clause that protects us is the one nobody reads."
non-defining (commas)Adds information about something already identified. Delete it and the sentence still stands. The commas are obligatory, and that is never allowed."Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand it."
dropping the pronounWhen the relative pronoun is the OBJECT of its clause, leave it out. This is the single change that makes written English sound spoken."the safeguard we rely on"
not "the safeguard which we rely on"
whose · where · whenwhose = of whom, of which (people AND things). where = in which place. when = at which time."a partner whose filings are late"
"Delaware, where the subsidiary was incorporated"
the prepositionFormal register puts it before the pronoun; everybody else leaves it at the end. Both are correct, and only one of them sounds like a person talking."the rule on which the policy is based"
"the rule the policy is based on"

One habit for this week: every time you write a sentence with and it or and this in the middle, try joining it with which or who instead. Half of your paragraphs will get shorter, and the half that does not was two ideas all along.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are explaining your work to somebody outside the legal team. Choose the version that works.

You are describing a colleague who is not a lawyer:
A "Our head of product, that is not a lawyer, has to understand it."
B "Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand it."
C "Our head of product which is not a lawyer has to understand it."
You want to identify WHICH clause you mean:
A "The clause that protects us is the one nobody reads."
B "The clause, that protects us, is the one nobody reads."
C "The clause, which protects us is the one nobody reads."
You want to sound like a person talking, not a document:
A "This is the safeguard on which we rely."
B "This is the safeguard we rely on."
C "This is the safeguard that we rely on it."
Somebody asks what your job is and you have ten seconds:
A "I am responsible for the legal analysis of contractual instruments."
B "In a nutshell, we decide what the company can sign, and on what basis."
C "I do compliance, and contracts, and some other things as well."
The other side is always late with paperwork and you want to say it in one clause:
A "I work with a client which filings are always late."
B "I work with a client that his filings are always late."
C "I work with a client whose filings are always late."
