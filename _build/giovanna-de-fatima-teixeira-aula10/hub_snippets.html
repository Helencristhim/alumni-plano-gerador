Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Nine words you already met in lessons 1 to 7. Three are about who you are, three about what you do, and three about your time. Listen to each one and read the example.

Background -- the studies and the jobs you had before this one
"My background is not the one people expect."
Degree -- what the university gives you when you finish -- the force is on the second part: di-GREE
"I have a degree in geology, not in engineering."
Commute -- the trip between your home and your work, every day
"My commute takes fifty minutes each way."
Field engineer -- an engineer who works on the site, not in the office
"I am a field engineer on a metro line."
Supervisor -- the person who checks your work and makes the decisions
"My supervisor said one thing only: everybody out."
Schedule -- the plan that says when each thing happens -- in American English it starts with a K sound: SKED-jool
"They made a new schedule on Friday morning."
Routine -- the things you do every day, in the same order
"First the alarm, then the traffic: that is my routine."
Overtime -- the extra hours you work after the normal end of the day
"When there is a problem underground I do overtime."
Weekend -- Saturday and Sunday, when most people do not work
"At the weekend I am tired, so I do very little."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the text out loud, then answer the questions below. It is somebody else's ten minutes, written down -- in class you say yours. Read slowly; nobody is timing you.

My name is Karen Booth and I am a tunnel engineer in London. People always ask about my background, so I start there: I have a degree in geology, not in engineering, and I changed after three years. I started in this company in 2019.

My day always starts at six. First I make coffee, then I take the train, and after that I walk to the gate. My commute takes fifty minutes each way, so I read on the train. That is my routine, every day. I usually get home at seven, but when there is a problem underground I do overtime and I get home at ten.

Right now we are working on the second platform, so the whole crew is down there. I can read a drawing and I can operate the small machines, but I cannot drive the big one yet. Last month I went to the north for two weeks and it rained every day. Did I enjoy it? Not really. At the weekend I am tired, so I do very little.

1. The text says "my day always starts at six" and "right now we are working on the second platform". Why are the two verbs in different forms?
A Because the first one is about Karen and the second one is about the crew.
B Because the first one is every day, and the second one is only right now, this week.
C Because the second one is in the past and the first one is not.
2. Look at "at six", "in 2019" and "in London". Why does Karen use at in one and in in the others?
A At is for an exact point, in is for something big around you: a year, a city.
B At is always for time and in is always for place.
C There is no rule; both are possible in all three sentences.
3. In the last paragraph Karen writes "Did I enjoy it?" and not "Did I enjoyed it?". Why?
A Because the question is about her and not about somebody else.
B Because enjoy is an irregular verb and irregular verbs never take -ed.
C Because did already carries the past, so the verb goes back to the dictionary form.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Nine Lessons in Two Tables

Grammar

There is nothing new in this lesson. The first table is one row per lesson, from lesson 1 to lesson 9 -- it is the whole list of things you can already say. The second table is the one thing worth keeping in your head: in, on and at.

When you use itThe formExample
Every day, alwaysI workI work on a metro line.
Somebody else, every dayshe worksMy supervisor checks the report on Friday.
How oftenalways, usually, sometimes, neverI usually get home at seven.
Right now, todayI am workingRight now I am measuring the lower area.
Where things arethere is / there areThere is one gate for the trucks, next to the fence.
Ability and permissionI can / I cannotI can read a drawing, but I cannot drive the big machine.
Finished, regular verbI workedLast Monday I worked twelve hours.
Finished, irregular verbI went, took, made, saidLast month I went to the north for two weeks.
Asking about the pastDid you...? / What did you...?Did you work last weekend? Yes, I did.
Nine rowsOne row per lesson, from lesson 1 to lesson 9. This is what ten minutes is made of.
TimePlace
inthe big box: in March, in 2019, in the morninginside something: in Brazil, in the tunnel
onthe day: on Monday, on Friday morningon a surface: on the wall, on the second platform
atthe exact point: at seven, at middaythe exact point: at the gate, at the site
The funnelBig to small: in the year, on the day, at the hour. The same order works for places.
No prepositionNever before next, last, this or every: last Monday, next week, every day. Not "in last Monday".

Quiz

In the middle of your ten minutes, a word disappears. The best thing to do is:
A Stop, say the word in Portuguese, and wait for the translation.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record your answer. There is no right or wrong here, and nobody corrects anything. This is your rehearsal: in class you do the same thing for ten minutes.

In three minutes, answer the five questions people always ask: what is your background, what exactly do you do on a normal day, how long is your commute, what are you working on at the moment, and what did you do last weekend. About four sentences for each one. If a word disappears, say "let me start again" in English and keep going.
