Lesson 24 -- Pre-class

Risk Assessment -- Reading the Matrix

Present a legal risk register in English: score likelihood and impact as two separate numbers, escalate whatever sits above the threshold, and leave risk appetite to the board. New vocabulary: likelihood, impact, mitigate, exposure, severity, threshold, escalate, contingency, remediation, risk appetite. Grammar: the modals of probability -- may, might, could and be likely to, never "will".

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Likelihood -- how probable it is that something will happen
"The likelihood is high, because nobody has checked the files since 2019."
Impact -- how much damage something would cause if it happened
"The impact is moderate, so the item sits in the middle of the matrix."
Mitigate -- to make a risk smaller or less serious, without removing it
"Below the line, the department mitigates the risk itself."
Exposure -- the amount a company could lose if the risk actually happens
"Our exposure would be the whole value of the transaction."
Severity -- how serious a problem is, if and when it happens
"The likelihood is low, but the severity is very high."
Threshold -- the level above which action must be taken
"That is above the board threshold, so you have to escalate it."
Escalate -- to pass an issue up to a more senior level for a decision
"Above the line, the risk must be escalated to the committee."
Contingency -- a plan or a reserve kept ready in case something goes wrong
"The committee will ask you for a contingency plan, not an opinion."
Remediation -- the work done to fix a problem that has been found
"Record the remediation, with a date and an owner."
Risk appetite -- how much risk a company is willing to accept -- a board decision
"Risk appetite is their decision, not ours. We measure it, they take it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

On Thursday Simone presents three items to the risk committee. Every item carries two separate scores: the likelihood, which is how probable it is, and the impact, which is how much damage it would do. Item one is the property files: the likelihood is high, the impact is moderate, and the item sits below the line, so the department mitigates it and records the remediation with a date and an owner. Item two is the reverse. The likelihood is low, but the severity is very high and the exposure would be the whole value of the transaction, which puts it above the board threshold. Simone escalates it and brings a contingency plan rather than an opinion. Her language is careful all the way through. She does not say the liability will appear; she says it could cost the group the full exposure. She does not say the files will delay the closing; she says they are likely to delay it by two weeks. And she says the regulator may ask for the full file, which is a real possibility and not a plan. One question she does not answer: how much the group should be prepared to carry. That is risk appetite, and it belongs to the committee.

1. Why does item two go to the committee even though the likelihood is low?
A Because the department has no time to handle it.
B Because the severity puts it above the board threshold.
C Because the likelihood is the only score that matters.
2. Why does Simone say "could cost" instead of "will cost"?
A Because it is remote but serious, and a risk is a probability, not a promise.
B Because she is not sure what the words mean.
C Because the committee prefers shorter sentences.
3. Which question does she deliberately not answer?
A How probable item one is.
B What the remediation date is.
C How much exposure the group should be prepared to carry.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Probability

GRAMMAR

A risk that will happen is not a risk any more -- it is a fact, and the committee will hold you to it. Everything on a register carries a level of probability, and English puts that level in one small word. Use be likely to when you have a number behind the sentence, may or might for a genuine possibility, and could for something remote that would be serious if it happened. Every one of them takes the base verb, and likely always keeps its to.

FormHow probableExample
be likely to + base verbhigh, with evidence behind it"The files are likely to delay the closing."
may + base verba real possibility"The regulator may ask for the full file."
might + base verba possibility, slightly weaker"The seller might refuse to extend."
could + base verbremote, but serious if it happens"A hidden liability could cost us the deal."
be unlikely to + base verbthe honest low end of the scale"The board is unlikely to approve it this quarter."

Key point: two traps. Never put to after may, might or could ("may to ask" → "may ask"), and never drop the to after likely ("are likely delay" → "are likely to delay").

Order

Quiz
