Admissions Interview I -- Tell Me About Yourself, Out Loud

An article by a former interviewer on what admissions interviews are really for, the vocabulary of the interview room, and the discourse markers that make a spoken answer easy to follow. Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read it in context. These words describe what happens in an interview room, and what interviewers notice without saying.

Articulate -- able to express ideas clearly and effectively in speech
"Interviewers remember articulate answers, not long ones."
Anecdote -- a short, interesting story about a real event
"A single anecdote can answer tell me about yourself better than a list."
Rapport -- a friendly relationship in which people understand each other well
"Most interviewers want to build rapport in the first few minutes."
Candid -- honest and direct, even when the truth is uncomfortable
"A candid answer about a weakness is more convincing than a fake one."
Digress -- to move away from the main subject while speaking or writing
"It is fine to digress briefly if you come back to the question."
Elaborate -- to add more detail or explanation to something already said
"The interviewer may ask you to elaborate on a single word."
Succinct -- expressed clearly in few words
"Start with a succinct answer, then offer to say more."
Poise -- calm, confident control of your behavior, especially under pressure
"Poise is not the absence of nerves; it is what you do with them."
Rehearsed -- practiced so often that it sounds memorized and unnatural
"A rehearsed answer often sounds less confident than a thoughtful pause."
Segue -- a smooth transition from one topic to another
"Use the interviewer's question as a segue to your anecdote."
Composure -- the state of being calm and in control of yourself
"She regained her composure after losing her train of thought."
Authenticity -- the quality of being real and genuine, not performed
"Interviewers value authenticity over polish."
Follow-up question -- a question that asks for more about something just said
"The best answers invite a follow-up question."
Self-deprecating -- making gentle fun of yourself in a modest way
"A little self-deprecating humor can build rapport."
Earnest -- sincere and serious in intention
"Her earnest answer about the museum guard made the interviewer smile."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

A reader who loses the thread of an essay can glance back at the previous paragraph. A listener cannot. Everything a speaker says disappears the moment it is spoken, and the listener must hold the structure of an answer in memory while the answer is still arriving. This is why the most admired speakers rarely sound like the most admired writers. They build their answers differently, and they use a small set of phrases that tell the listener where the answer is going.

These phrases are called discourse markers. Some announce structure. The short answer is promises that a longer one will follow and invites the listener to relax. There are really two reasons tells the listener to count. Others reformulate. What I mean by that is and to put it another way signal that the speaker is about to narrow or clarify a claim, which is exactly what a good interviewer is hoping to hear.

A third group qualifies without confrontation. That said and having said that introduce a limitation or a contrast gently, and they sound far less combative in conversation than the however of an academic essay. Finally, closing markers such as so, to come back to your question show the listener that the speaker has not been lost in a digression, even after a long anecdote.

None of this should be confused with filler. Basically, like and you know occupy the same position in a sentence as discourse markers, but they carry no direction. They are what speakers produce when they need time and do not want to admit it. The alternative is not a faster answer but an honest pause. That is a good question; let me think for a second sounds, to almost every listener, more confident than three fillers in a row.

Interestingly, the density that makes academic writing persuasive can work against a speaker. Nominalizations, long participle clauses and stacked concessions are admirable on the page and exhausting across a table. The skill of a truly proficient speaker is not to abandon those resources, but to know when to leave them in the essay.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Discourse Markers and Signposting

GRAMMAR

Spoken answers need signposts. Discourse markers tell a listener the shape of an answer, when a claim is being narrowed or qualified, and when the speaker is returning to the question.
