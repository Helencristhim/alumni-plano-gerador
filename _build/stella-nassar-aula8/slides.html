SPOKEN

Both are true. The first is an excellent sentence to read and a strange one to hear across a table.

Signposts for a Listener

Discover the Style

What Is the Listener Being Told?

"The short answer is that I look at paintings; the longer answer involves a museum guard."

"What I mean by that is I care less about famous names than about how people look."

"I love the big museums. That said, the painting that changed me was tiny."

"So, to come back to your question, that is why I want to study art history."

Say each sentence without the colored phrase. What does the listener lose?

FormWhat it doesExample
structuringtells the listener the shape of the answerThe short answer is... There are two reasons...
reformulatingclarifies or narrows what you just saidWhat I mean by that is... In other words...
contrasting softlyadds a qualification without sounding combativeThat said... Having said that...
returning and closingshows you have not lost the questionSo, to come back to your question... Which is why...
buying time honestlya real pause, named, instead of fillerThat is a good question; let me think for a second.
The C2 trapfillers pretending to be markers. "Basically", "like" and "you know" point nowhere. A two-second silence sounds more confident than all three.

Chapter 5: Voices from the Interview

The Interview, Out Loud

A mock interview, an admissions officer and a student story

Dialogue

The Mock Interview with Mr. Hale

H
Thanks for making time on a school night. So, to get us started, tell me a little about yourself.
S
Sure. The short answer is that I spend a lot of time looking at paintings. The longer answer starts with a museum guard who kept asking me to step back.
H
Now I have to hear the longer answer.
S
I was standing too close to a small Vermeer. He warned me four times. What I mean by that is I was not being rude, I just could not see what I wanted to see from behind the line.
H
And what did you want to see?
S
How he painted the light on the bread. That said, the guard was right, and I did eventually step back.
H
I studied engineering, so forgive me if this is a naive question. What does looking that closely actually teach you?
S
That is a good question. Let me think for a second. I would say it teaches you that every effect is a decision somebody made.
H
That is a better answer than most engineers would give about their own bridges.
S
So, to come back to where we started, that is why I want to study art history: to understand the decisions behind what we see.
Comprehension

About Mr. Hale

1. How does Mr. Hale react to Stella's short answer?
He says that now he has to hear the longer answer.
2. What did Mr. Hale study, and how does he introduce his question?
Engineering. He asks her to forgive him if the question is naive.
3. How does Mr. Hale respond to Stella's answer about decisions?
He says it is a better answer than most engineers would give about their own bridges.
Listening 1

What the Report Actually Says

A director of an interview program speaks to applicants. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. Who usually conducts the interviews, according to the speaker?
Volunteer graduates of the college, who meet applicants in or near their own region, often online.
2. How important does she say the interview is in the overall decision?
It is one piece among many; it rarely decides an application on its own, but it can add real color to a file.
3. What does she say interviewers most often write in the strongest reports?
That they would have happily kept talking, and that the student asked them a genuine question.
Listening 2

The Student Who Froze

A college student tells a story on a podcast about applying. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. What question made the student freeze, and what did he do first?
What book changed your mind about something. He started listing famous books he thought would impress the interviewer.
2. How did he recover?
He stopped, said that was a good question and asked for a second to think, then told the truth about a comic book.
3. What lesson does he draw from the experience?
That a named pause sounds more confident than a rehearsed answer, and that honesty started a real conversation.

The Artwork That Changed You

Task: Mr. Hale asks: "Tell me about a work of art that changed how you think." Answer in about two minutes, then handle two follow-up questions. You may not use basically, like or you know.

Support:

anecdotecandidThat said...To put it another way...Let me think for a second.
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Three Questions, No Warning

Task: Your teacher will ask three interview questions in a row. One of them you cannot prepare for. Use signposts, take an honest pause when you need it, and end each answer on purpose.

No support. Target: every answer has a shape, one honest pause, zero fillers, and one question of your own at the end.
