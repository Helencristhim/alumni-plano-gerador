Getting Around the City

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Directions in English are built from very small words, and each one belongs to one of two families. The first family tells you where something is: the station is on the corner, the crosswalk is between the bank and the hotel, your friend is waiting at the entrance. Nothing moves in those sentences. The second family tells you where you go: walk across the park, go along the avenue, get off at the second stop. Something is travelling from one point to another. When you are not sure which word to use, ask yourself one question: is anything moving here? If the answer is no, you need a word from the first family.

1. In "The station is on the corner", why do we use a place preposition?
A Because corners always take on.
B Because nothing is moving: the sentence says where the station is.
C Because the station is a building and buildings take on.
2. Which sentence uses a movement preposition?
A "My office is between two banks."
B "Walk across the park to the museum."
C "She is waiting at the entrance."
3. According to the text, what is the quickest way to choose the right preposition?
A Learn a list of nouns and the preposition each one takes.
B Ask whether anything is moving in the sentence.
C Use in when you are not sure.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Place and movement

GRAMMAR

Two families of prepositions, and one question that tells them apart: is anything moving?

FamilyWordsExample
Placein, on, at, between, opposite, next toThe station is on the corner.
Movementto, into, across, along, through, up, downWalk across the park.
Public transportget on / get offGet off at the second stop.
Cars and taxisget in / get out ofGet into the taxi at the door.

Two more traps for a Brazilian speaker: you arrive at a station, an airport or an office, and in only for a city or a country. And along, not in, is the word for following a street: "walk along this avenue", never "walk in this avenue".
