Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

What Were You Doing When… -- The Morning It All Went Wrong

How to turn a list of facts into a story: the long action that was already happening, and the short one that cut across it. Key expressions: to be stuck, on my way, to run into, to realise, to hang up, all of a sudden, a traffic jam, to be about to, to make it, luckily. Structure: past continuous versus past simple.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These ten stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

To be stuck -- unable to move or to leave a place
"He said do not worry, I am stuck as well, six cars behind you."
On my way -- travelling towards a place, but not there yet
"I was on my way to the office when the road closed."
To run into -- to meet somebody by chance, with no plan
"I ran into an old colleague at the station and we talked for twenty minutes."
To realise -- to understand something suddenly
"I was reading the email when I realised the meeting was at eight, not at nine."
To hang up -- to end a telephone call
"He hung up before I could explain why I was late."
All of a sudden -- very quickly and with no warning at all
"We were waiting quietly when all of a sudden everybody stood up."
A traffic jam -- a long line of cars that is not moving
"I was sitting in a traffic jam, going nowhere, when my telephone rang."
To be about to -- on the point of doing something, one second away
"I was about to apologise when he told me he was stuck too."
To make it -- to arrive in time, usually with very little to spare
"Luckily I made it before the client did."
Luckily -- by good fortune, and it could easily have gone the other way
"Luckily nobody moved for forty minutes, so we finished the whole meeting."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Janderson was on my way to the office at half past seven — his words, and the grammar is his — when all of a sudden the road closed. There were no signs and nobody explained anything. Two minutes later he was stuck in a traffic jam that did not move for fifty minutes.

He was reading an email from the London office when he realised he had no signal at all. He was about to send a message to say he would be late, and the message never left the phone. Twice he tried to call and twice the line hung up on him.

While he was waiting in the car, something small and good happened. He ran into nobody, obviously, because he was alone in a car, but the client called him — and the client was in the same jam, six cars behind. So they did the whole meeting on the telephone, from two cars that were going nowhere.

Luckily he made it to the office before the client did, and the meeting started only twenty minutes late. His colleague in London was about to call him when he finally walked in. She asked the question that gave this lesson its title: what were you doing all that time?

1. "He was reading an email when he realised he had no signal." Which action started first?
A Reading the email — it was already happening, so it takes was + -ing.
B Realising — because it comes second in the sentence.
C Both started at exactly the same moment.
2. "What were you doing all that time?" Why is there no did in this question?
A Because it is an informal question.
B Because were is already the auxiliary, so nothing needs to be borrowed.
C Because the question is about the future.
3. What does "while he was waiting in the car" tell you?
A That the waiting finished before the next action started.
B That the waiting was the background, and something else happened inside it.
C That he waited only for a second.
4. Why does the text say he was lucky?
A Because the road opened again quickly.
B Because he made it to the office before the client did.
C Because the meeting was cancelled.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

The past continuous is not about time. It is a frame: it paints the scene, and the past simple is what walks into it.

FormWhat it saysExample
was / were + verb-INGThe long action. It was already running when the story starts."I was driving to the office."
Past simpleThe interruption. It happened once, quickly, and it finished."… when the road closed."
when and whileWhen usually introduces the short action; while the long one. Both orders mean the same."I was reading when he called."
"While I was reading, he called."
Two long actionsIf both were happening at the same time, both take -ing."While I was waiting, she was working from home."
Negative and questionMove was or were, or add not after it. Nothing is borrowed — there is no did here."I was not driving."
"Were you waiting long?"
The trapVerbs about the mind still refuse the -ing form, exactly as in the present.NOT "I was not knowing"
"I did not know."

One practical use of this structure at work: it explains a delay without sounding like an excuse. I was on my way when the road closed gives the reason and the timing in six words.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You arrive twenty minutes late to a meeting in English. Choose the best thing to say.

Somebody asks what happened. The best answer is:
A "I drove to the office when the road closed."
B "I was driving to the office when the road closed."
C "I am driving to the office when the road closed."
You want to ask what she was doing at that moment:
A "What did you doing?"
B "What were you doing?"
C "What you were doing?"
You want to say that you almost sent the message:
A "I was about to send it when I lost the signal."
B "I was almost sending it when I lost the signal."
C "I sent it almost when I lost the signal."
You did not know about the change of time. You say:
A "I was not knowing about the change."
B "I did not know about the change."
C "I am not knowing about the change."
You arrived just in time, and only just. You say:
A "I achieved the arrival within the established schedule."
B "Luckily I made it."
C "I was lucky of arriving."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is scoring it.

Record about sixty seconds about a day that went wrong. Where were you going, what were you doing when it happened, what interrupted it, and how did it end? Use one sentence with two verbs in it, and keep the sentences short.
