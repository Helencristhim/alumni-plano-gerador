Chapter 3: The Code

I Work, I Am Responsible For

And the questions that come back

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I work as an HR manager at a retail group."

"I am responsible for culture and engagement."

"What do you do in your company?"

"What are you responsible for?"

Why does the question need an extra word that the answer does not have?

FormUseExample
I / you / we + verbYour job, your routine — things that are always trueI work in HR. I lead a small team.
he / she + verb + sThird person always takes an -sShe works in HR. He leads the team.
am / is / are + responsible forWith responsible you need the verb to be, not workI am responsible for engagement.
Do / Does + subject + verbQuestions need do or does, and the verb goes back to base formDo you work in HR? Does she lead the team?
Two trapsNever I am work — choose one verb. And never What you do? — the question needs do.

The Structure

Three Sentences, Every Time

"What do you do?" is the easiest question to answer badly. This is the shape that works.

1 · The role

"I work as an HR manager at a retail group."

2 · The real work

"I am responsible for culture, performance and engagement."

3 · One real example

"Last year I led the project that gave us our Great Place to Work certificate."

Role → real work → one example. Never a long list with no example.

Chapter 4: The Interview

Meet Mark

Recruiter · a first conversation

Dialogue

A First Interview

M
Good morning, Viviane. Thanks for your time. So, tell me — what do you do?
V
Good morning, Mark. I work as an HR manager at a retail group. I am responsible for workplace culture, performance and engagement.
M
That is a wide area. Can you give me one example?
V
Of course. Last year I led our survey project, and we got our Great Place to Work certificate. It was the first time for the company.
M
Nice. And how do you handle retention in an operation with high store turnover?
V
Sorry, could you repeat that, please?
M
Of course. Turnover is high in the stores. What do you do about it?
V
We work on two things: better onboarding in the first month, and recognition for the store leaders. People do not leave companies. They leave managers.
M
That is a very good answer. Thank you, Viviane — I will come back to you this week.
Comprehension

About Mark

1. What does Mark ask for after the first answer?
One example — because the area she described is very wide.
2. What does he do when she asks him to repeat?
He repeats it, and he makes the question shorter and simpler.
3. How does he end the conversation?
He says it was a very good answer and that he will come back to her that week.
Listening

An HR Manager's Week

She describes what her job really is. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. She says her job is not about paperwork. What is it about?
Culture.
2. What three questions does she ask team leaders?
What is working in your team? What is not working? What do you need from me?
3. What is her main project right now?
Leadership development — a programme for new managers.
4. Why does she say the programme is needed?
Most managers are excellent technically but lost when they have to lead people — nobody teaches that.
Listening 2

What a Recruiter Listens For

He interviews candidates from twenty countries. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Does he expect perfect grammar?
No. He interviews people from twenty countries and almost nobody speaks perfect English.
2. What makes a strong candidate for him?
Three clear sentences about what you actually do, with a real example.
3. What should you do when you do not understand a question?
Just say so: "sorry, could you repeat that?" — every good candidate does this.
4. Who does he remember for the wrong reason?
The candidates who pretend to understand and answer the wrong question.
