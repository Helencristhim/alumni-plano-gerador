Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Back to Work -- The Interview

Eighteen years of experience, a career break and one hypothetical question. Key words: a hiring manager, a role, to be responsible for, a stakeholder, to streamline, a track record, a career break, transferable skills, to take ownership of, a notice period, to be a good fit, onboarding. Structure: would, for situations that have not happened.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A hiring manager -- the person who decides who gets the job, and who you meet after the recruiter
"The hiring manager asked about the eighteen years, not about the gap."
A role -- a job with a clear set of duties, the word companies use instead of position
"The role is hybrid: three days in the office in Washington."
To be responsible for -- to have something as your own duty, so that you are the one who answers for it
"I was responsible for the internal systems of a bank for eighteen years."
A stakeholder -- anybody affected by your work, whose opinion counts even if they are not your boss
"The stakeholders were branch managers, not engineers."
To streamline -- to make a process simpler and faster by taking steps out of it
"We streamlined the approval process and it went from six days to two."
A track record -- everything you have actually done before, seen as proof of what you can do
"Eighteen years in one bank is a track record, not a line on a page."
A career break -- a period when you stop working on purpose and plan to go back later
"I took a career break when the family moved to Washington."
Transferable skills -- the abilities you can carry from one job into a completely different one
"Running three properties from another country is a transferable skill."
To take ownership of -- to make something your responsibility and stay with it until it is finished
"I took ownership of the migration and I stayed until it worked."
A notice period -- the time you keep working after you tell a company you are leaving
"My notice period was thirty days, and I worked all of them."
To be a good fit -- to match what a team needs, in skills and in the way you work with people
"They want to know if I would be a good fit before they make an offer."
Onboarding -- the first weeks in a new company, when they train you and set you up
"Onboarding is two weeks, and most of it is remote."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Paul is the hiring manager for a systems analyst role in Washington. He opens every interview the same way: walk me through your background. Gisele tells him that she was responsible for the internal systems of a bank for eighteen years, and that she took ownership of the migration project. That part is easy, because all of it already happened.

Then he asks the question nobody can rehearse. "What would you do if the team disagreed with you about a deadline?" Nothing here has happened, so the verb changes. Gisele answers: "I would ask what they can see that I cannot. If the date still had to move, I would say it early. I wouldn't wait until the last week." One imagined action after another, and never a past tense after would.

At the end Paul says the last five minutes are hers. She asks two things back: what would onboarding look like in the first month, and who would she work with every day. She does not apologize for the career break. She says it as a fact, and Paul moves on.

1. Why does the verb change in "What would you do if the team disagreed with you?"
A Because it is a polite question, and polite questions always use would.
B Because nothing has happened: the situation is imagined, and would is the verb for that.
C Because the interview happened in the past.
2. "If the date still had to move, I would say it early." Why is there no second would after if?
A Because the if side goes into the past simple, and only the other side takes would.
B Because would cannot be used twice in the same conversation.
C Because the sentence is negative.
3. What does Gisele do with the career break in this text?
A She explains it three times so that Paul understands the reason.
B She hides it and talks about the bank instead.
C She says it as a fact, without apologizing, and the interview moves on.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Would for Situations That Have Not Happened

GRAMMAR

One word, a naked verb after it, and the if side in the past.

UseFormExample
An imagined actionwould + base verbI would start by asking why.
The questionWhat would + you + base verbWhat would you do if the deadline moved?
Spoken, shortI’d / you’d / we’d / they’dI’d talk to each person first.
Negativewould not / wouldn’t + base verbI wouldn’t wait until the last week.
Softer, more carefulwould say / would like / would preferI would say it took about two weeks.
The two sidesif + past simple, would + base verbIf they disagreed, I would ask why.

Never: What would you did · I would to start · If the deadline would move · I wouldn’t waited

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

The hiring manager asks what you would do if a stakeholder asked for something impossible. You answer:
A "I would to explain the limit and offer another way."
B "I would explain the limit and offer another way."
C "I would explained the limit and offer another way."
He asks about the months after the bank. The strongest answer is:
A "I am sorry, I have not worked since May."
B "I took a career break when we moved to Washington, and I am ready to go back."
C "It is a long story, we can talk about something else."
You want to say that a moving deadline would not be a problem. You say:
A "If the date moved, that wouldn’t be a problem for me."
B "If the date would move, that wouldn’t be a problem for me."
C "If the date moved, that wouldn’t been a problem for me."
At the end he says: "Do you have any questions for me?" You say:
A "No, I think you explained everything."
B "Yes. What would onboarding look like in the first month?"
C "Yes. When would you tell me if I am a good fit or no?"
