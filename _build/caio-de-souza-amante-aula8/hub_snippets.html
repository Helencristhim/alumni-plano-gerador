Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A benchmark -- the outside number other companies are measured against
"Every number in the pack is shown against a market benchmark."
Churn -- the share of clients who leave in a period
"Churn is measured on client count, not on revenue."
A margin -- what is left of the revenue once the costs are taken out
"Headcount doubled in three years and the margin did not move."
To forecast -- to say what the numbers will be before they happen
"The forecast was missed by nine percent."
An outlier -- a number so far from the others that it distorts the picture
"One quarter was an outlier and the whole company built a story on it."
To audit -- to check the numbers formally, from outside the company
"The accounts were audited by Vieira and Marsh in March."
A baseline -- the starting number you measure every change against
"We set a baseline of twelve trailing months and showed every number against it."
Recurring revenue -- money that arrives every month without being sold again
"One page of recurring revenue was the page the buyer read twice."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways to report the same fact to a board. My team missed the forecast puts a person in the sentence, and the room immediately starts deciding whether somebody should be blamed. The forecast was missed by nine percent takes the person out, and the room starts deciding what to do next. The facts are identical. The conversation is not, and in a negotiation the difference between those two conversations is worth money.

The form is two verbs, always: a form of be, then the past participle. The forecast was missed. Churn is measured on client count. The accounts were audited in March. The first verb does all the work you can hear: it carries the tense and it agrees with the subject. The second one never changes. This is the piece a Portuguese speaker most often loses, because the same idea is carried in that language by a single short word attached to the verb, and the direct translation produces a sentence with only one verb in it. The forecast missed by nine percent is not English.

When the actor matters after all, it comes back on the end with by: the accounts were audited by Vieira and Marsh. Use it when the name changes what the sentence is worth — an auditor's name does, and a colleague's name usually does not. And when nothing is being done to the subject, none of this applies: two deals moved into the next quarter stays as it is, because nobody moved them.

One warning that has nothing to do with grammar and everything to do with the room. This form can be used to hide, and every experienced listener knows it. A mistake was made tells a board that somebody is not saying who, and you have spent your credibility to save one word. So the rule that goes with the form is short: take the person out when the person is not the point, and never when the person is you.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Passive Voice for Executive Reporting

GRAMMAR

Two verbs, always. The first one carries the tense; the second one never changes.

JobFormExample
The result matters, the actor does notbe + past participleThe forecast was missed by nine percent.
A definition or a standing ruleis / are + past participleChurn is measured on client count.
The actor changes the weight, so name it… by + the actorThe accounts were audited by Vieira and Marsh.
Nothing is done to the subjectkeep it activeTwo deals moved into the next quarter.
The trapbe is not optionalNot the forecast missed by nine percent. Say was missed.
The second trapthe participle, never the plain pastNot was chose. Say was chosen.
The permanent targetbe still agrees with the subjectChurn is measured. The accounts were audited.

Never: The forecast missed by nine percent · Churn are measured · The accounts was audited · was chose

Talk
Hans Rosling — “The Best Stats You've Ever Seen” (TED)

Twenty minutes of a statistician making numbers argue for themselves, in front of an audience that came in believing the opposite. Almost every sentence he says about the data is built in the impersonal form you practised today, and you can hear why: he wants the number in front, not himself.

Tip: watch once for the argument. On the second pass, write down five sentences of his where the actor has been removed, and one where it has not.

Tip: pause when he defines each metric and say the definition back in the impersonal form, out loud, before you continue.
