Say It Again, Better -- One Idea, Two Audiences

Explaining the same finding to a specialist and to a student. Key words: to paraphrase, to refer to, to mean, to imply, jargon, specialist, pain signal, sensory nerve. Structure: who, which, that to define a word.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. Four words help you explain; four are words you will often need to explain.

To paraphrase -- to say the same idea again with different words
"Can you paraphrase that sentence in simpler words?"
To refer to -- to be the name for something; to talk about it
"The term can refer to damage in the thin nerves that carry pain."
To mean -- to have a meaning; to want to say something
"When I say sensitization, what I mean is a nerve that reacts too much."
To imply -- to show an idea without saying it directly
"She didn't say no, but her face seemed to imply that she disagreed."
Jargon -- special words that only people in one field understand
"Too much jargon makes a talk impossible to follow for students."
Specialist -- a person who knows a lot about one subject
"For a specialist, you can use the technical words."
Pain signal -- the message that a nerve sends to the brain when something hurts
"A damaged nerve sends a pain signal even when there is no injury."
Sensory nerve -- a nerve that carries information about touch, heat and pain
"A sensory nerve carries information about touch, heat and pain to the brain."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Dr. Lena Weber teaches pharmacology in Berlin. She has one rule for her first-year students: no word without a definition. "Allodynia is pain which comes from something that should not hurt. A patient who has allodynia can feel pain from a bedsheet. Small fibers are the nerve fibers that carry pain and temperature." When a student looks confused, she does not repeat the same words. She paraphrases, and she uses an image that everybody knows: "Think of a fire alarm which goes off when you light a candle."
Dr. Weber says "a patient who has allodynia". Why "who" and not "which"?
A Because the sentence is about a medical problem that started a long time ago in the past
B Because "patient" is a person
C Because it is a question
What does Dr. Weber do when a student looks confused?
A She says the same words again, but louder and more slowly, until the student understands
B She paraphrases and uses an image
C She gives a test

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Who, Which, That

GRAMMAR

A defining relative clause explains WHICH person or thing you mean. It is the fastest way to define a technical word in the same sentence: X is a Y that...

whopeopleA pharmacologist is a scientist who studies drugs.
whichthingsAllodynia is pain which comes from a light touch.
thatpeople or things (safest when you speak)We need a drug that calms the nerve.
negativethe negative goes in the clausePatients who don't feel heat can burn a hand.
questionthe clause stays the sameIs there a drug that calms the nerve?

No comma, and do not repeat the subject: "a nerve that is damaged", never "a nerve that it is damaged".

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A "It is a chronic condition with peripheral sensitization and central mechanisms of nociception."
B "It is pain that comes from a damaged nerve."
C "Pain. Nerve."
Your listener looks confused. What do you do?
A "Let me put it this way..." and say the idea with an image
B Say exactly the same sentence again, but louder and more slowly than the first time
C Change the subject
Which sentence is correct?
A "A nerve that it is damaged sends the wrong signals to the brain all day."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Sixty seconds. Choose one finding from your research and explain it to a first-year medical student. Define every technical word in the same sentence with who, which or that, use one image from daily life, and finish with "Does that make sense?". No script -- just speak.
