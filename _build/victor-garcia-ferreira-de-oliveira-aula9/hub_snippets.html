Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Connecting Ideas -- Two Facts, One Sentence

A list of short sentences sounds like a form being filled in. One word joins them, and the same information starts sounding like an argument. Key words: a campus, a supervisor, a cohort, a workload, a peer, overwhelming, to settle in, to drop out, a support network, to make the most of something. Structure: relative clauses, defining and non-defining.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A campus -- the buildings and grounds where a university actually lives
"The campus is the size of a small town, which nobody tells you before you arrive."
A supervisor -- the person who is responsible for guiding your work and answering for it
"The supervisor who read my first essay is the reason I did not rewrite it eleven times."
A cohort -- the group of students who start at the same time as you
"My cohort was forty people, and thirty of them were also somewhere new."
A workload -- how much work you are carrying at one time, not how hard any of it is
"The workload in the first term is the thing people underestimate, every single year."
A peer -- somebody at the same level as you, neither above nor below
"A peer will tell you what the supervisor will not, which is why the first friend matters."
Overwhelming -- so much at once that you cannot process it, even when each piece is easy
"Nothing in the first week is hard. All of it at the same time is overwhelming."
To settle in -- to stop feeling new in a place, and start just living in it
"It takes about six weeks to settle in, which is six weeks longer than anybody expects."
To drop out -- to leave a course before it finishes
"Most people who drop out do not fail. They leave in the term when nobody notices them."
A support network -- the set of people you can ask when something goes wrong
"A support network is knowing which door to knock on at four in the afternoon."
To make the most of something -- to get everything possible out of a chance you already have
"The people who make the most of the first year are the ones who ask early."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two minutes made of short sentences sounds like a form being filled in. The same two minutes with the halves joined sounds like somebody thinking, and the information does not change at all. Compare these. I want a university. It has a strong football programme. Now: I want a university that has a strong football programme. The second half tells you which university, so it cannot be removed and it takes no commas. That is the essential kind, and who, which and that all work in it: who for people, which for things, that for either.

The other kind is extra. My supervisor, who is Canadian, had never met a Brazilian student. There is only one supervisor here, so nothing needs identifying; the clause simply adds detail and it sits between two commas. Remove it and the sentence survives. In this kind, that is not allowed: after a comma it has to be who or which. And a comma plus which can also comment on the whole idea before it: the campus is the size of a town, which nobody tells you.

Two more words finish the set. Where joins a place, with no preposition afterwards: the city where I lived, never where I lived in. And whose shows possession: a student whose brother plays for the same club, never a student his brother plays. Three traps, then, and they are always the same three: which used for a person, that used after a comma, and a preposition left hanging at the end of a where clause. Fix those and your sentences double in length without getting harder to follow.

1. Why does "I want a university that has a strong football programme" take no commas?
A Because the sentence is too short for commas.
B Because the second half says which university, so it is essential and cannot be removed.
C Because "that" never takes commas in any sentence.
2. According to the text, what is not allowed after a comma?
A "that" — after a comma it has to be "who" or "which".
B "who" — it can only be used at the start of a sentence.
C "which" — it can only be used for people.
3. Which sentence does the text say is correct?
A "The city where I lived in was smaller."
B "I met a student his brother plays for the same club."
C "I met a student whose brother plays for the same club."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

No commas means the clause says WHICH one. Two commas means it is extra, and the sentence survives without it.

JobFormExample
Says WHICH one (essential)noun + who / which / that + clause, no commasThe supervisor who read my essay is the reason I stayed.
Adds extra detailnoun, who / which + clause, (two commas)My supervisor, who is Canadian, had never met a Brazilian.
People and thingswho for people · which for things · that only in the essential kindA university that has a programme.
Placeswhere + clause, with no preposition afterwardsThe city where I lived was smaller than mine.
Possessionwhose + nounA student whose brother plays for the same club.
Commenting on the whole idea, which + clauseThe campus is huge, which nobody tells you.
Leaving the pronoun outpossible when it is the object of the clauseThe essay (that) I submitted last week.

Never: the supervisor which read my essay · my supervisor, that is Canadian · the city where I lived in · I met a student his brother plays.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You are identifying which supervisor you mean. You say:
A "The supervisor which read my essay was from Vancouver."
B "The supervisor who read my essay was from Vancouver."
C "The supervisor, that read my essay, was from Vancouver."
There is only one supervisor and you are adding a detail. You say:
A "My supervisor, who is Canadian, had never met a Brazilian student."
B "My supervisor that is Canadian had never met a Brazilian student."
C "My supervisor, which is Canadian, had never met a Brazilian student."
You are talking about the city you lived in. You say:
A "The city where I lived in was smaller than mine."
B "The city where I lived was smaller than mine."
C "The city which I lived was smaller than mine."
You want to say something about a student and his brother. You say:
A "I met a student his brother plays for the same club."
B "I met a student who his brother plays for the same club."
C "I met a student whose brother plays for the same club."

Reflection
