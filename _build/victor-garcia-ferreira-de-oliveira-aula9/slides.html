Chapter 3: The Code

The Half You Cannot Remove, and the Half You Can

One structure, two jobs, one comma

Grammar Discovery

Cover the Red Part

"The supervisor who read my first essay is the reason I stayed."

"My supervisor, who is Canadian, had never met a Brazilian student."

"I want a university that has a strong football programme."

"The campus is the size of a town, which nobody tells you."

Cover the red parts and you no longer know which supervisor or which university. Cover the blue parts and you lose nothing except detail. The punctuation is not decoration.

JobFormExample
Says WHICH one (essential, no commas)noun + who / which / that + clauseThe supervisor who read my essay is the reason I stayed.
Adds extra detail (two commas)noun, who / which + clause,My supervisor, who is Canadian, had never met a Brazilian.
People and thingswho for people · which for things · that only in the essential kindA university that has a programme · my supervisor, who is Canadian
Placeswhere + clause (no preposition afterwards)The city where I lived was smaller than mine.
Possessionwhose + nounA student whose brother plays for the same club.
Commenting on the whole sentence, which + clauseThe campus is huge, which nobody tells you.
The three trapswhich for a person · that after a comma · a doubled prepositionNot the person which: say who. Not my supervisor, that: say who. Not where I lived in: say where I lived.

In one line: no commas means the clause tells you WHICH one; two commas means it is extra, and the sentence survives without it.

The Place You Are Applying To

Dialogue

Coffee With Somebody in Her Second Year

P
So you are applying for September. The thing nobody tells you is that the campus is the size of a small town, which changes how you plan a Tuesday.
V
That is the kind of detail I cannot get from a website. What was the part that surprised you most in the first term?
P
The workload, which is not difficult, only constant. Nothing in week one is hard. All of it at once is overwhelming.
V
And the people who drop out — is it because of the marks?
P
Almost never. Most of the students who leave are the ones nobody noticed, which is a much sadder sentence than the one about marks.
V
So the thing that actually matters is the support network, not the timetable.
P
Exactly, and it is not friends. My supervisor, who is impossible to find on a Monday, answers everything on a Thursday. That is the sort of thing a peer tells you and a handbook never does.
V
How long did it take you to settle in, honestly? And what would you do differently if you started again in September?
Comprehension

About Priya

1. What does Priya say about the workload, and how does she describe it exactly?
That it is not difficult, only constant. She says nothing in week one is hard, and all of it at once is overwhelming.
2. According to Priya, who are the students who leave?
Almost never the ones with bad marks. She says most of the students who leave are the ones nobody noticed.
3. What does Priya say about her own supervisor, and what point is she making with it?
That he is impossible to find on a Monday and answers everything on a Thursday. Her point is that a peer tells you that kind of thing and a handbook never does.
Listening 1 · Student Services

The Term Where People Disappear

A student services coordinator on the first term. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say about the students who leave in the first term?
That they are almost never the ones who are failing. They are the ones who have not spoken to anybody, and she says that is a different problem entirely.
2. What does she say about international students specifically?
That they wait longer before asking for help, because asking in a second language feels like proving you should not be there. She says the delay is the real risk, not the language.
3. What is the one thing she asks new students to do in the first two weeks?
Speak to three people whose job it is to help, before there is a problem, so that the first conversation is never an emergency.
Listening 2 · Second Year

How He Built the Network

An international student on his first six weeks. Sound first — no text.

1. What did he do in his first week that he says made the difference?
He joined a team, which put him in a room twice a week with people who were not on his course. He says the network came from that and not from his classes.
2. What does he say about the people he asks for help now?
That there are four of them and each one is for a different thing, and that no single person can be the whole network without eventually getting tired of you.
3. What does he say he would tell somebody arriving in September?
That six weeks is how long settling in takes, so do not judge anything before then, and that the people who make the most of the first year are the ones who ask early.

1. Two of these people do a similar job. Which two, and what is the difference between them?
The academic adviser and the supervisor. The adviser signs the course choices, and the supervisor reads the work and answers for it, so one is about what you take and the other about what you produce.
2. Why does the handbook say to book the writing centre five days ahead in December, and not in October?
Because in December everybody has deadlines at the same time, so a service that is free and immediate in October becomes a queue.
