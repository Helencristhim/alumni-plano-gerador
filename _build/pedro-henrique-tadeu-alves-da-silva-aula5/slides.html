Chapter 3: The Code

The Way It Used to Work

Two ways to say the old normal, and one of them has a limit

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"We used to run the auditions on paper."

"A team of six would sit in a room for three weeks."

"There used to be one person who watched everything first."

"Four thousand people would send a video every season."

All four describe the old normal. Three of them could be written with would. The blue one cannot, and the reason is not the tense: it is what kind of verb it is.

JobFormExample
A past habit or a past state, finished nowused to + base verbWe used to run the auditions on paper. There used to be one person.
A repeated past ACTION, finished nowwould + base verbA team would sit in a room. Four thousand people would send a video.
The limit of wouldstates take only used toNot there would be one person. Say there used to be. Same for be, have, know, live.
Negativedid not use to + base verbWe did not use to record anything. (No -d after did.)
QuestionDid ... use to ... ?Did you use to print the sheets by hand?
Opening a storystart with used to, continue with wouldWe used to run it on paper. Every season a team would sit down and would read four thousand sheets.
The traptwo different structures that look alikeI used to work there = it is over. I am used to working there = it feels normal to me now.

In one line: used to opens the old normal, would keeps it running, and a state can only take used to.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Pitch, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one brief

Dialogue

Two Minutes Before She Has to Leave

N
I have about two minutes before my next call, so start wherever you want. What is this?
P
Then let me start with how it works today. We used to run the auditions on paper, and honestly we still do. Four thousand videos, and a team of six would sit in a room for three weeks.
N
Three weeks. Is that the reading, or the waiting?
P
Mostly the waiting. The bottleneck is one person: nobody can start until she has watched everything. So the lead time is three weeks even when the work is four days.
N
Right. And you want money to fix it.
P
Not yet. I built a proof of concept on two weekends, for one category, with real videos. It is running. I would like you to watch it for four minutes, and then we talk about the budget.
N
That is the right order, and almost nobody does it. What does it cost me if I say yes?
P
One editor, two days a week, for one season. That is the trade-off: we lose him now and we get three weeks back every season after that. And I would roll it out beside the paper, not instead of it.
N
Somebody will push back and say the team will never use it. They said that about the last three things.
P
They are right to. That is why it runs beside the old one for a season: approval is not buy-in. Who has to sign off on it, apart from you?
Comprehension

About Nina

1. How much time does Nina have, and what is the first thing she wants to know about the three weeks?
About two minutes, before her next call. She asks whether the three weeks are the reading or the waiting.
2. What does Nina say is the right order, and what does she say about other people?
Showing the working proof first and talking about the budget afterwards. She says that is the right order and almost nobody does it.
3. What objection does Nina predict, and what does she say about the last three things?
That somebody will push back and say the team will never use it. She says they said exactly that about the last three things.
Listening 1 · The Other Side of the Table

What I Listen For in the First Thirty Seconds

Somebody who approves or refuses ideas for a living, being unusually honest. Sound first — no text.

1. What is she NOT listening for in the first thirty seconds, and what is she listening for instead?
Not the idea. Whether you understand the thing you want her to change, including the boring part everyone forgets.
2. What happens, according to her, if you open with the solution?
She spends the rest of the meeting checking whether you actually know the problem, and she stops hearing you.
3. What are the second and third things she listens for?
What it costs her, which is not necessarily money but time, attention or one person she would have to move off something else. And whether you have thought about what happens when it does not work, because nobody signs off on something that has only one ending.
Listening 2 · The Way It Used to Work

Nobody Has Printed a Sheet Since

Four thousand videos, three weeks and a system that was replaced without a budget. Sound first — no text.

1. How did the auditions used to work, and what was the bottleneck?
On actual paper: four thousand people would send a video and a team of six would sit in a room for three weeks with printed sheets. The bottleneck was that one person had to watch everything before anyone else could start.
2. What does he call “the whole trick”, and what did he do instead of asking for money?
Not asking for a budget first. He built the proof of concept on two weekends, for one category only, showed it running with real videos, and asked for the money afterwards.
3. What was the first answer, what was the objection, and what did they do about it?
The first answer was still no. Somebody pushed back and said the team would never use it, which he calls a fair objection, so they ran the new system beside the paper for one season.

If it does not work: the paper process has not been switched off, so nothing has to be rebuilt. The proof of concept is already running and can be watched in four minutes.
1. The Cost line comes before the Benefit line. Why would somebody write it in that order?
Because the person deciding is going to look for the cost anyway. Naming it first means they do not find it later on their own, and it buys credibility for everything that follows.
2. Two numbers on this page are in tension: three weeks and four days. What is the argument being made?
That most of the lead time is waiting, not working. The process is not slow because the job is big; it is slow because of one narrow point.
3. What does the note at the bottom do for the reader?
It answers the question nobody asks out loud: what happens if this fails. Nothing has been switched off, so there is a way back, and the working version can be seen in four minutes.
