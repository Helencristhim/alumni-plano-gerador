Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

Pitching the Idea

Two minutes with the one person who can say yes, and the first thirty seconds decide everything. Key words: a deck, a decision-maker, lead time, a bottleneck, a proof of concept, to scale, to sign off on something, to push back, a trade-off, to roll out, buy-in, a budget. Structure: used to opens the old normal, would keeps it running, and a state only takes used to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A deck -- the set of slides you show when you present an idea
"The deck is six slides, and the first one is the problem."
A decision-maker -- the person who can say yes without asking anybody else
"Find the decision-maker before you build the deck, not after."
Lead time -- the time between asking for something and actually getting it
"The lead time was three weeks, and the show does not wait three weeks."
A bottleneck -- the one narrow point that slows the whole process down
"The bottleneck was one person: nobody could start until she had watched everything."
A proof of concept -- a small version built only to show that the idea works
"I built the proof of concept on two weekends and did not ask for money first."
To scale -- to make something work at a much bigger size without breaking
"It works for one category. The question is whether it scales to twelve."
To sign off on something -- to give the official approval that lets it happen
"Nobody signs off on something that has only one ending."
To push back -- to say no, or not yet, and give a reason
"Somebody pushed back and said the team would never use it. That was fair."
A trade-off -- when you gain one thing and lose another at the same time
"The trade-off is honest: we lose a week now and save three every season."
To roll out -- to put something into use, step by step, across a whole operation
"We rolled it out one category at a time, beside the old system."
Buy-in -- the agreement of the people who have to live with the decision
"Approval is not buy-in. Without buy-in, the old system comes back in six weeks."
A budget -- the money set aside for something, decided before the work starts
"I asked for the budget after the demo, not before it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Almost every pitch that works opens in the past, because before anybody hears your idea they need to believe that you understand the thing you want to change. So the first thirty seconds describe the old normal. We used to run the auditions on paper. Four thousand people would send a video, and a team of six would sit in a room for three weeks. Both forms say the same thing — that this was the routine and it is over — but they are not interchangeable. Used to works with anything. Would only works with repeated ACTIONS.

That limit is the whole grammar of this lesson. There used to be one reviewer who watched everything first, and you cannot say there would be, because be is a state and not an action. The same applies to have, know and live. The negative and the question hide a second trap: after did, the -d disappears. We did not use to record anything. Did you use to print the sheets by hand? And a third one, which is a different structure altogether: I used to work here means it is finished, while I am used to working here means it feels normal to me now.

Once the old way is on the table, the rest of the pitch is three moves. Name the bottleneck, because the lead time is usually waiting and not work. Show the proof of concept before you ask for the budget — that order is the one thing almost nobody does. Then name the trade-off yourself, out loud, before the decision-maker finds it. Expect somebody to push back; a fair objection deserves a design, not an argument, which is why you roll out the new system beside the old one. And remember that a signature is not buy-in: the people who have to live with the change are the ones who decide whether it survives.

1. Why can you not say "there would be one reviewer who watched everything first"?
A Because "would" is only used in questions.

B They mean the same thing, and the second one is more formal.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Used To and Would

GRAMMAR

One decision: is this a repeated action, or a state?

JobFormExample
A past habit or a past state, finished nowused to + base verbWe used to run it on paper. There used to be one reviewer.
A repeated past ACTION, finished nowwould + base verbA team would sit in a room. Four thousand people would send a video.
The limit of wouldstates take only used toNot there would be one reviewer. Same for be, have, know, live.
Negativedid not use to + base verbWe did not use to record anything. (No -d after did.)
QuestionDid ... use to ... ?Did you use to print the sheets by hand?
Opening a storystart with used to, continue with wouldWe used to run it on paper. Every season a team would sit down and would read four thousand sheets.
The traptwo structures that look alikeI used to work there = it is over. I am used to working there = it feels normal now.

Never: we did not used to record · there would be one reviewer · I am used to work on paper · I used to work there for five years · did you used to print them?

Stage 2: Put Your Pitch in Order

Order

You have two minutes with the person who can say yes. Put your pitch in the order that works.

?"Let me start with how it works today, including the part everybody forgets."
?"We used to run the auditions on paper, and a team of six would sit in a room for three weeks."
?"The bottleneck is one person: nobody can start until she has watched everything."
?"I built a proof of concept on two weekends, with real videos, and it is running."
?"The cost is one editor, two days a week, for one season. That is the trade-off."
?"Who has to sign off on it, apart from you?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You have two minutes with the decision-maker. You open with:
A "I have an idea that will save three weeks every season, it is very good."
B "Let me start with how it works today. We used to run the auditions on paper, and we still do."
C "Sorry to take your time, I know you are busy, it is only a small thing."
You want to describe the routine of the old system. You say:
A "Every season a team of six would sit in a room for three weeks."
B "Every season a team of six was sitting in a room for three weeks."
C "Every season a team of six is used to sit in a room for three weeks."
She asks what it costs her. You say:
A "Almost nothing, it is very cheap and very fast."
B "One editor, two days a week, for one season. That is the trade-off, and it is honest."
C "I did not calculate this yet, but I think it is not a problem."
Somebody says the team will never use it. You say:
A "That is not true, the team will love it when they see it."
B "Ok, then maybe it is better to forget this idea."
C "That is fair. It runs beside the old system for one season, because approval is not buy-in."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Pitch something you have actually built, or something you would build if somebody said yes. Two minutes: how it used to be done, where it slows down, what you have already shown works, the honest cost, and what happens if it fails. Name the cost before anybody asks.
