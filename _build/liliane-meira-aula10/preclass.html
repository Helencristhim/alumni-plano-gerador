Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

The Limits of a Project -- What We Do Not Know Yet

A reviewer's report on an art and health project, and the grammar of saying how sure you are. Key words: a reviewer, a limitation, speculative, inconclusive, a sample, anecdotal, to corroborate, robust, rigor, plausible, a gap in the literature, to account for. Structure: modals of deduction (present and past).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A reviewer -- a person asked to assess a project or a text and give a written opinion
"The second reviewer was far more generous than the first."
A limitation -- a weakness or boundary that restricts what a project can claim
"Every honest report has a paragraph on its limitations."
Speculative -- based on guesswork rather than on evidence
"The last section is speculative, and the reviewer says so."
Inconclusive -- not leading to a firm result or decision
"The first round of interviews was inconclusive."
A sample -- a small group chosen to represent a much larger one
"Twelve caregivers is a small sample for a claim about a whole hospital."
Anecdotal -- based on personal stories rather than on systematic evidence
"The evidence is powerful, but it is anecdotal."
To corroborate -- to confirm something with further, independent evidence
"Nothing in the hospital records corroborates that date."
Robust -- strong and reliable, able to survive close criticism
"The method is robust enough for a second phase."
Rigor -- careful, precise and thorough work
"The panel praised the rigor of the transcripts."
Plausible -- reasonable and likely to be true, although not proven
"It is a plausible reading, but it is not the only one."
A gap in the literature -- a question that nobody has studied properly yet
"The project sits in a real gap in the literature."
To account for -- to explain something, or include it in your analysis
"The report does not account for the patients who refused."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The reviewer was never in the hospital, so almost everything she writes about what happened there is a deduction. The artist must have spent months on the ward, she writes, because nobody gets testimony this candid in one visit.

She is equally clear about the limitations. A sample of twelve caregivers cannot be representative of a whole hospital, and the evidence is anecdotal. Some families might have told the artist what they thought she wanted to hear.

One date cannot be right, because nothing in the records corroborates it; the artist may have been relying on one memory. Still, the method is robust and the reading is plausible, and the reviewer recommends funding.

1. "The artist must have spent months on the ward." Does the reviewer know this?
A Yes, the artist told her the exact dates.
B No, but she is almost certain, and she is talking about the past.
C No, and she thinks it is impossible.
2. "A sample of twelve cannot be representative." What does cannot be mean here?
A The reviewer thinks it is impossible.
B The artist is not allowed to use it.
C The reviewer is not sure.
3. Which sentence is only a possibility?
A The artist must have spent months on the ward.
B The date cannot be right.
C Some families might have told her what she wanted to hear.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Deduction (Present and Past)

GRAMMAR

How to say how sure you are, about now and about then.

RuleFormExample
Almost certainmust + verb / must have + past participleIt must be small. She must have spoken to more people.
Possiblemight / may / could (+ have + past participle)They might have told her what she wanted to hear.
Impossible, in my viewcan’t / cannot (+ have + past participle)It cannot be representative. He can’t have read it.
Now or thenmodal + verb = now · modal + have + participle = pastmust be · must have been
The negative, herecan’t, never mustn’tmustn’t means it is forbidden
No to after the modalmodal + base verbmust be, not must to be

Never: must to be · must have speak · mustn’t be (for impossible) · can’t read (for a guess about the past).

Stage 2: Put the Limitations Paragraph in Order

Order

Six sentences of an honest limitations paragraph. Put them in the order a panel expects to read them.

?"This project is based on interviews with twelve caregivers in one hospital."
?"A sample of this size cannot be representative of the whole hospital."
?"The evidence is anecdotal by design, and I do not claim it speaks for everybody."
?"Some families might have told me what they thought I wanted to hear."
?"Six people refused to be recorded, and I account for their silence in the work."
?"The second phase will test whether the hospital records corroborate these accounts."

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen, then record yourself. In must have and might have, the have is weak: mus-tuv, my-tuv.

A sample of twelve cannot be representative of a whole hospital.
Some families might have told me what they thought I wanted to hear.
The reviewer must have read the first version.
It is plausible, but we have nothing to corroborate it yet.
The main limitation is that the evidence is anecdotal.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the sentence you would actually say.

A panel member asks if twelve interviews are enough. You say:
A "Twelve interviews cannot be representative of the whole hospital, and I say so."
B "Twelve interviews mustn’t be representative."
C "Twelve interviews must to be enough."
You think a reviewer read only your first version. You say:
A "She must read the first version."
B "She must have read the first version."
C "She must have readed the first version."
You want to admit a possible bias. You write:
A "The families told me the truth, obviously."
B "The families must lie."
C "Some families might have told me what they thought I wanted to hear."
A claim in your application is not proven yet. You say:
A "It is plausible, but we have nothing to corroborate it yet."
B "It is proven, because it is plausible."
C "It is speculative, so it must be true."
