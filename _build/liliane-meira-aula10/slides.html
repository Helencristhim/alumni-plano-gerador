Chapter 4: What Must Have Happened

Sure, Fairly Sure, Not Sure

The grammar of a careful reader

Grammar Discovery

How Sure Is She?

"The artist must have spoken to far more than twelve caregivers."

"A sample of twelve cannot be representative of a whole hospital."

"Some families might have told her what they thought she wanted to hear."

"The second phase could be the stronger one."

Nobody in these sentences saw it happen. The red words say how sure the writer is, and whether she means now or then.

RuleFormExample
Almost certainmust + verb / must have + past participleIt must be a small sample. She must have spoken to more people.
Possiblemight / may / could + verb, or + have + past participleThey might have told her what she wanted to hear.
Impossible, in my viewcan’t / cannot + verb, or + have + past participleIt cannot be representative. She can’t have checked every record.
Now or thenmodal + verb = now · modal + have + participle = pastmust be (now) · must have been (then)
The negative of must, herecan’t, never mustn’tmustn’t means it is forbidden, not that it is impossible
The trapno to after the modalnot must to be, not must have speak

In one line: the modal says how sure you are; have plus the participle moves the guess into the past.

Listening 1 · The Panel

I Read the Limitations First

Somebody who funds projects between the arts and medicine, on the paragraph she reads first. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the first thing she reads in an application, and what does she conclude if it is missing?
The paragraph on limitations. If it is missing, the applicant cannot have thought very hard about the work, or must be hoping the panel will not notice.
2. What three limits did the best application this year admit?
Twelve families cannot be a claim about the whole city; some might have said what they thought the team wanted to hear; and they do not yet know whether the effect lasts beyond six months.
3. How does she describe admitting what you do not know?
Not as weakness, but as the clearest sign of rigor a panel has.
Listening 2 · The Researcher

Not a Verdict, a Conversation

Somebody from the humanities side of medicine, on how to answer a hard review. Sound first — no text.

1. What do people outside research imagine a review is, and what does he say it is closer to?
A verdict. He says it is closer to a conversation with somebody who has read your work more carefully than anybody else will.
2. When a reviewer says a claim must be overstated, what is the useful response?
Not to defend it, but to ask what evidence would corroborate it and whether you have it. If you do not, the claim has to become smaller and more honest.
3. Why does he say anecdotal evidence is not a flaw in his field?
Because it is anecdotal by design. It is a limitation they name, and naming it is what allows the stories to be taken seriously.
