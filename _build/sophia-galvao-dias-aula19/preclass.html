Lesson 19 — Pre-class

Hardly Any Sun

About ten minutes. Eight cold-weather words — chilly, freezing, fog, humid, layers, hoodie, heater, breeze — and the words that say how much there is: a few, few, a little, little, plenty of, hardly any.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud.

Chilly -- a little cold, not freezing
"There are a few chilly mornings, even in Rio."
Freezing -- very, very cold
"I'm freezing, and there's hardly any sun today."
Fog -- a cloud near the ground that makes it hard to see
"There's so much fog that I can't see the street."
Humid -- when the air feels wet and heavy
"Rio is humid, and there is plenty of sun."
Layers -- clothes you wear one on top of another
"You need plenty of layers in July."
Hoodie -- a warm top with a hood for your head
"I have only a few hoodies, and I wear them all."
Heater -- a machine that makes a room warm
"There is little heat in my room without the heater."
Breeze -- a soft, light wind
"There is a little breeze by the beach."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read Sophia's message to a friend in Rio. The words in bold say how much there is.

Winter in São Paulo — It's July and I'm freezing! There is hardly any sun in the morning, and there is plenty of fog on the way to school.

In Rio there were a few chilly days, but here I wear three layers. There is little heat in my room, so the heater is on all night.

The good news? There are a few sunny days next week, and I still have a little hot chocolate!

"There is hardly any sun in the morning." How much sun is there?

B A lot of sun, because the mornings in São Paulo are always bright.

A Bad news, because she does not like sunny days and wants more fog.
B Good news.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

How much is there?

  • a few / few + things you can count: There are a few sunny days (good news). Few people go to the beach in July (bad news).
  • a little / little + things you cannot count: There's a little sun (good news). There's little heat in my room (bad news).
  • plenty of = more than you need. hardly any = almost zero. Both work with any noun.

Watch out

  • hardly is already negative: hardly any time, never hardly no time. And it is plenty of, never just plenty.

Remember: your life up to now is still have: Have you ever seen snow? Yes, I have.
