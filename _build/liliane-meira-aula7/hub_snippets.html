Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

Describing What You See -- Talking About an Image in Real Time

The label tells you what a work is made of. It never tells you what it looks like. Key words: to loom, washed out, grainy, to recede, cropped, a vantage point, muted, stark, to hover, to draw the eye, to catch the light, tucked away. Structure: present participles and -ing clauses.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To loom -- to rise above you and seem larger than it really is
"The piece looms over the visitor, taking up the whole end of the room."
Washed out -- pale, as if the color had been drained away
"From the side the whole surface goes washed out."
Grainy -- full of tiny visible specks, like old film
"The print is grainy, and the grain is clearly a decision."
To recede -- to seem to move back, away from the viewer
"The floor recedes into a gray that carries no detail."
Cropped -- cut off at the edge, so part of the subject is missing
"A figure at the far right, cropped at the shoulder."
A vantage point -- the position the viewer is looking from
"The vantage point is low, so the room rises above you."
Muted -- soft and low in intensity, never loud
"Everything else is a muted gray with no detail in it."
Stark -- bare and severe, with nothing softening the contrast
"The room is stark: white walls, one object, no bench."
To hover -- to stay suspended in the air, touching nothing
"A small lamp hovers at the top edge of the canvas."
To draw the eye -- to pull the viewer’s attention to one place first
"The lamp draws the eye first, every single time."
To catch the light -- to reflect light so that a surface suddenly shows
"The varnish catches the light in bands as you walk past."
Tucked away -- placed in a corner where you almost miss it
"The lamp is tucked away at the top edge, where you nearly miss it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The room is stark: white walls, one painting, no bench. The painting is three meters across and hung low, so it looms over you the moment you come in. The vantage point is almost at the floor.

Hanging at knee height, the canvas catches the light in bands, and the varnish moves as you move. From the side the whole surface goes washed out. At the far right a figure stands, cropped at the shoulder, facing away. The rest is empty floor, receding into a muted gray that carries no detail. A small lamp hovers at the top edge, tucked away in the corner of the canvas.

What draws the eye first is the lamp, every time. Then the cropped shoulder. The figure arrives last. The print beside it, grainy and much smaller, shows the same room with the projector running, leaving the far wall in light.

1. "A figure stands, cropped at the shoulder, facing away." What are the two -ing and -ed clauses doing?
A They are two separate sentences joined by commas.
B They hang extra detail on the figure, so no new sentence is needed.
C They tell you the order in which the two things happened.
2. "Hanging at knee height, the canvas catches the light in bands." Who or what is hanging?
A The canvas, because the -ing clause must belong to the subject of the sentence.
B The light, because it is the nearest noun.
C The viewer, because the viewer is described everywhere else.
3. "...with the projector running, leaving the far wall in light." What does this structure let the writer do?
A Say that one thing happened before the other.
B Ask the reader a question without a question mark.
C Stack a second action and its result onto the same sentence, at the same moment.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Participles and -ing Clauses

GRAMMAR

One form, four jobs. All four let you keep describing instead of stopping to start a new sentence.

JobFormExample
Cuts a relative clausenoun + verb-inga figure standing at the far right (= who is standing)
Sets the scene firstverb-ing clause, + main clauseHanging from the ceiling, the piece turns.
Adds what follows from itmain clause, + verb-ingThe light falls, leaving the corner dark.
Two things at the same timewith + noun + verb-ingwith the projector running
Passive versionnoun + past participlea figure, cropped at the shoulder
The trapthe -ing subject must be the subject of the main clauseStanding in the doorway, I blocked the light.

Never: Standing in the doorway, the light was blocked · a figure standing at the far right, it is cropped · the light falls, it leaves the corner dark · with the projector was running.

Stage 2: Put the Description in Order

Order

Six lines of one description. Put them in the order that lets a listener build the work in their head.

?"It is about three meters across and hung low, so it looms over you."
?"The surface is varnished in bands, catching the light as you move."
?"From the side the whole thing goes washed out."
?"At the far right a figure stands, cropped at the shoulder."
?"The rest is empty floor, receding into a muted gray."
?"A small lamp hovers at the top edge, tucked away where you nearly miss it."

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen, then record yourself. Let the -ing endings stay soft, never -ingue.

It is about three meters across, so it looms over you rather than sitting at eye level.
Standing in front of it, you see the varnish catching the light.
A figure standing at the far right, cropped at the shoulder.
The floor recedes into a muted gray, leaving the far corner almost empty.
The lamp draws the eye first, tucked away at the top edge.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that lets the other person see the work.

A curator on a call has a useless photograph and asks you to start. You begin with:
A "It is a very powerful piece about memory."
B "It is about three meters across and hung low, so it looms over you."
C "It is interesting, you would have to see it."
You want to add, in the same sentence, that the figure has no shoulder in the frame. You say:
A "A figure standing at the far right, cropped at the shoulder."
B "A figure standing at the far right, it is cropped at the shoulder."
C "A figure who stands at the far right and that is cropped in the shoulder."
You blocked the light because you were standing in the doorway. You say:
A "Standing in the doorway, the light was blocked."
B "Standing in the doorway, I blocked the light."
C "Standing in the doorway was blocking the light of me."
A visitor with headphones on cannot see the work. Which sentence belongs in the access track?
A "The sky is ominous and the whole thing feels lonely."
B "It is one of the strongest paintings in the room."
C "A small lamp hovers at the top edge, tucked away in the corner of the canvas."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. Time yourself: ninety seconds, out loud, no notes.

Choose one work of your own and describe it for ninety seconds to somebody who cannot see it. Start with the size against a body, then the surface, then what is actually in it. Use at least three -ing clauses, and do not use a single word that tells the listener what to feel.

Talk
Tracy Chevalier -- "Finding the story inside the painting" (TED, 13 min)

A novelist stands in front of three paintings and describes them out loud, one detail at a time, before she allows herself a single interpretation. It is the exact sequence you practiced today, performed by somebody who does it for a living.

Tip: count how many of her sentences hang detail on a noun with an -ing clause. Then describe one work of your own the same way, out loud.
