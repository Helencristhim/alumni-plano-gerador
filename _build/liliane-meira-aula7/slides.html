Chapter 3: The Code

Hanging Detail on a Noun

a figure standing · hanging from the ceiling · leaving the corner dark · with the projector running

Grammar Discovery

One Form, Four Jobs

"A figure standing at the far right looks away."

"Hanging from the ceiling, the piece turns very slowly."

"The light falls across the floor, leaving the far corner dark."

"She filmed the room with the projector running."

The blue verb is the sentence itself. Every red word ends in -ing and carries detail that would otherwise need a whole sentence of its own.

JobFormExample
Cuts a relative clausenoun + verb-inga figure standing at the far right (= who is standing)
Sets the scene firstverb-ing clause, + main clauseHanging from the ceiling, the piece turns.
Adds what follows from itmain clause, + verb-ingThe light falls, leaving the corner dark.
Two things at the same timewith + noun + verb-ingwith the projector running
Why it matters hereno new sentence neededYou keep describing instead of stopping to start again.
The trapthe -ing subject must be the subject of the main clauseNot Standing in the doorway, the light was blocked.

In one line: the -ing clause hangs off a noun, and that noun has to be the one doing it.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

To Somebody Who Cannot See It

One call, three recordings, one access script

Dialogue

The Photograph Is Useless

D
The installation shot I have is useless. It is a phone photo, taken against the window. Start with the size. Is it bigger than me?
L
Much bigger. It looms over you: about three meters across, hung low, so the bottom edge sits at your knee.
D
Good. Now the surface, before the subject. Is it matte, or does it catch the light?
L
It catches the light in bands. Standing in front of it, you see the varnish moving as you move. From the side the whole thing goes washed out.
D
And what is actually in it?
L
A figure standing at the far right, cropped at the shoulder. Everything else is empty floor, receding into a muted gray. The only warm thing is a lamp hovering at the top edge, tucked away where you nearly miss it.
D
What draws the eye first? Honestly.
L
The lamp, every time. Then the cropped shoulder. The figure arrives last, and I think that is the point: the painting keeps you looking before it lets you read it.
Comprehension

About Daniel

1. Why is the photograph Daniel has useless, and what does he ask for first?
It is a phone photo taken against the window. He asks for the size first, and whether the work is bigger than he is.
2. What does Daniel want to hear about before the subject, and what two options does he offer?
The surface. He offers matte, or a surface that catches the light.
3. What is Daniel’s last question, and what single word does he add to it?
He asks what draws the eye first, and he adds the word honestly.
Listening 1 · An Access Writer

The Sentence Has to Move the Way the Eye Moves

Somebody who writes the descriptions visitors hear on headphones. Sound first — no text.

1. What rule does she work with when she writes a description?
That the sentence has to move the way the eye moves.
2. In what order does she write, and what reason does she give for it?
The whole thing first: how big it is against a body and what it is made of, and only then the detail. Standing in front of a canvas, most people take in the light before they take in the subject.
3. What does she say the hardest part is, and what example does she give?
Resisting the urge to interpret. Instead of a lonely figure she writes a figure standing alone at the far edge, and lets the listener decide whether that is loneliness.
Listening 2 · A Gallerist

Never Start with the Story

Somebody who sells work to people looking at it on a phone, two time zones away. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say the mistake everybody makes is, and why is it a mistake?
Starting with the story. Nobody can hold a story about something they cannot picture yet.
2. What order does he use, and why does the surface matter so much?
Scale first, then the surface, then what the work is of. The surface matters because a photograph destroys it: whether it is matte, or whether it catches the light and moves as you move.
3. Which word does he refuse to use, and what does he say it tells the buyer?
Interesting. He says it tells the buyer nothing, and tells them he has run out of words.
Listening 3 · The Introduction

Ominous Is a Verdict, Not a Description

A host opening an episode, and the exercise she failed before recording it. Sound first — no text.

1. What exercise did the guest make the host do before they recorded?
Look at an image for sixty seconds, then say what is there without saying what it means.
2. How did the host fail, and what did the guest point out?
Within about nine words she had called the sky ominous. The guest pointed out that ominous is a verdict and not a description.
3. What does the host say we are fluent in, and what are we almost mute about?
We are fluent in what a picture makes us feel, and almost mute about what is actually in it.
Real Document

The Script for Somebody Who Cannot See It

ACCESS · VERBAL DESCRIPTION
Track 4 of 12 · recorded, 2 min 10 s
WorkUntitled (Lamp), 2026
ArtistLiliane Meira
Dimensions180 × 300 cm, hung 40 cm from the floor
Written byAccess team, with the artist
Three meters wide and hung low, the painting meets you at the knee. The surface is varnished in bands, catching the light as you walk past and going almost washed out from the side. At the far right a figure stands, cropped at the shoulder, facing away. The rest is empty floor, receding into a muted gray that carries no detail. A small lamp hovers at the top edge, tucked into the corner of the canvas, and it is the warmest thing in the work.
1. Why does the script give the size before anything else?
Because scale is what a recording cannot carry. The listener has to place the work against their own body before any detail means anything.
2. Which sentence lets the listener hear two things happening at once, and how does it do it?
The surface is varnished in bands, catching the light as you walk past. The -ing clause runs alongside the main clause instead of waiting for a new sentence.
3. The script never says what the work means. Which word comes closest, and why is it still a description?
Warmest. It reports the color temperature of the lamp against the gray, which anybody looking can check. It does not tell the listener what to feel.
