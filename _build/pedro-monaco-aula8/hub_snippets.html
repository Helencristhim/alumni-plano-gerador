A Judgment in Plain English

A feature on the distance between what a court held and what the report says it held. Key expressions: a ruling, to uphold, to overturn, a precedent, a dissent, an appeal, to defer to, a plaintiff, a defendant, to hold, standing, the merits, to strike down, unanimous. Structure: reported speech — the backshift, when NOT to backshift, say against tell, and the reporting verb that carries a claim of its own.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen appear in the text you will read in class, and in the first paragraph of almost every decision in English.

A ruling -- a decision made by a court
"The ruling was unanimous, which is rare in that court."
To uphold -- to confirm a decision that was made before
"An appeal court can uphold a decision without agreeing with a word of it."
To overturn -- to reverse a decision made by a lower court
"A single sentence can overturn twenty years of practice."
A precedent -- an earlier decision that later courts are expected to follow
"The case sets a precedent for every regulator in the country."
A dissent -- a written disagreement by a judge in the minority
"The dissent is quoted more often than the decision itself."
An appeal -- a request for a higher court to review a decision
"An appeal was filed within the thirty-day window."
To defer to -- to accept somebody else’s judgment as the authoritative one
"Courts used to defer to the agency on technical questions."
A plaintiff -- the person or company that starts a case
"The plaintiff argued that notice had never been given at all."
A defendant -- the person or company a case is brought against
"The defendant admitted the delay but denied the breach."
To hold -- to state something as the legal conclusion of a case
"A court can hold that a rule goes too far without saying what would be enough."
Standing -- the right to bring a case at all
"The claim failed on standing, before anybody looked at the substance."
The merits -- the substance of a case, as opposed to the procedure
"The merits were never argued, which is why the case decides so little."
To strike down -- to cancel a rule or a law entirely
"The court did not strike down the rule; it narrowed it."
Unanimous -- agreed by every member of the court
"The decision was unanimous, and the reasoning was not."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Reporting what somebody said looks like the simplest thing in English and is where careful professionals lose the most. The mechanics are easy enough: the reporting verb goes into the past, and everything inside it steps back one. The agency has gone too far becomes the court held that the agency had gone too far. Present becomes past, past becomes past perfect, will becomes would, tomorrow becomes the following day.

There is one large exception and it matters more in law than anywhere else. When what was said is STILL true, keeping the present is better: the court held that the rule applies only to filings after that date. A ruling does not stop being the rule when the hearing ends, and backshifting it can suggest, quietly, that it has been overtaken.

Then the two mechanical traps. Tell takes a person immediately after it and say never does: she told me that..., she said that..., never she said me. And a reported question keeps statement word order, with no do, does or did: he asked what I thought, not what did I think. For a yes/no question, if or whether does the joining.

The part that is not mechanical at all is the verb. English has a long scale of reporting verbs and each one is a claim about how much was decided. A court that noted something has done less than a court that held it, and one that held it has done less than one that struck down the rule. Choosing a stronger verb than the original makes the summary more useful and less true, which is a trade nobody should make twice.

1. What happens to the tense inside reported speech?
A It steps back one: present to past, past to past perfect, will to would.
B It stays exactly as it was.
C It always becomes the present.
2. When is it better NOT to backshift?
A When the sentence is long.
B When what was said is still true.
C When the speaker was a judge.
3. Which is correct?
A "She said me that the appeal was late."
B "She told me that the appeal was late."
C "She told that the appeal was late to me."
4. Why does the choice of reporting verb matter, according to the text?
A Because it makes the writing more elegant.
B Because each verb is a claim about how much was actually decided.
C Because some verbs are more formal than others.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech

GRAMMAR

Portuguese reports with a much smaller set of verbs and lets the tense stay put far more often, so two habits tend to arrive together: no backshift, and say used for everything. The first only sounds foreign. The second can change what a court decided.

FormWhat it doesExample
the tense goes back one stepPresent becomes past, past becomes past perfect, will becomes would. The reporting verb is in the past, so everything inside it follows."The agency has gone too far" → "the court held that the agency had gone too far"

One habit for this week: every time you write the court said, stop and check what verb the judgment actually used. Nine times out of ten there is a better one, and the tenth time you have just avoided a problem.

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to how a decision travels from the judgment to your summary, then put the steps in order.

?The judgment is handed down and runs to ninety pages.
?The last pages are read first, because that is where the court says what it decided.
?One sentence is identified: the one that would change if the case had gone the other way.
?The dissent is labelled as a dissent, so that nobody quotes it as the decision.
?The summary is written with the verb the court used, and never a stronger one.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are summarising a decision for a colleague. Choose the version that is accurate.

The judgment says: "The agency has gone too far." You are reporting it:
A "The court said the agency has gone too far."
B "The court held that the agency had gone too far."
C "The court struck down the agency."
The judge merely observed that the notice was late:
A "The court held the notice invalid."
B "The judge noted that the notice was late."
C "The court decided the notice was worthless."
The other side said: "I will file the appeal next week."
A "He said me he will file the appeal next week."
B "He told me he would file the appeal the following week."
C "He told that he will file the appeal."
Somebody asked you: "What do you think of the dissent?"
A "She asked me what did I think of the dissent."
B "She asked me what I thought of the dissent."
C "She asked me what I think did about the dissent."
The rule still applies today, and the court said so:
A "The court held that the rule applied only to later filings, but that has changed."
B "The court held that the rule applies only to later filings."
C "The court says the rule apply only to later filings."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Take a decision, a regulation or an internal ruling you have actually read this year and summarise it out loud in about ninety seconds: who held what, who disagreed, what it does NOT decide, and what you would do about it. Use at least four different reporting verbs, and make sure not one of them is stronger than what the original document actually said.
