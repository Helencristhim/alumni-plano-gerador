Chapter 3: The Code

The Verb Carries a Claim

Reported speech, and the choice nobody notices making

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The court held that the agency had gone too far on that record."

"The judge noted that the notice was late, without deciding anything about it."

"She told me the appeal would be filed the following week."

"He asked what I thought of the dissent."

Turn each of these back into the words the person actually used. Two things change every time, and in the last one something disappears. What are they?

FormWhat it doesExample
the tense goes back one stepPresent becomes past, past becomes past perfect, will becomes would. The reporting verb is in the past, so everything inside it follows."The agency has gone too far" → "the court held that the agency had gone too far"
unless it is still trueWhen what was said is still the case, keeping the present is not only allowed, it is better. This matters for holdings, which do not expire when the hearing ends."The court held that the rule applies only to filings after that date."
say · tell · and the restTell takes a person straight after it; say never does. And beyond both there is a whole scale: note, observe, argue, hold, conclude, strike down."She told me that..." · "She said that..."
never "she said me"
the verb is an argumentA court that noted something did less than one that held it, which did less than one that struck down the rule. Reporting with a stronger verb than the original is not a style choice."The judge noted that the notice was late."
vs "The court held the notice invalid."
reported questionsNo inversion and no do/does/did — the same rule you already use for indirect questions. For yes/no, add if or whether."He asked what I thought."
not "what did I think"

Portuguese reports with a much smaller set of verbs and lets the tense stay put far more often, so two habits arrive together: no backshift, and say used for everything. The first sounds foreign. The second quietly changes what a court decided.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"He said me that the appeal was late."
"He told me that the appeal was late."
"She asked me what did I think of the ruling."
"She asked me what I thought of the ruling."
"He said that he will file the appeal tomorrow."
"He said that he would file the appeal the following day."

The first is the one every Portuguese speaker makes: tell takes a person, say does not. The second keeps the inversion and the auxiliary inside a reported question, where neither belongs. The third leaves the tense and the time word where the original speaker left them — if the reporting verb is in the past, will becomes would and tomorrow becomes the following day.

Listening 1

The Sentence That Was the Decision

A court reporter on how she gets ninety pages down to four hundred words. Sound first — no text.

1. Where does she start, and why?
With the last three pages, because that is where the court says what it actually decided; everything before is the argument for it.
2. What sentence is she looking for?
The one that would change if the case had come out the other way — that sentence is the decision.
3. What mistake did she make for years?
Quoting the best line she found — which is very often in the dissent, because dissents are written to be read.
Listening 2

Never a Stronger Verb

A litigator on the one rule he gives every junior. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the one rule he gives every junior?
Never report with a stronger verb than the court used — if the judgment says noted, you write noted.
2. Why is the temptation so strong?
Because a memo saying the court HELD is more useful to the reader than one saying it NOTED, and for about a week nobody finds out.
3. What eventually happens?
Somebody relies on that sentence in front of a client or another court, and it comes back to the paragraph where the verb was upgraded.
