Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Sentence That Changes Tense -- Twenty Years of Small Differences

A forensic accountant tells her career in two tenses, on purpose. Key words: a turning point, to come across, a pattern, to look into, a hunch, to rule out, painstaking, to hold up, to piece together, a discrepancy. Structure: the past simple against the present perfect.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A turning point -- the moment when a situation starts to change direction
"The turning point was a single invoice that arrived twice."
To come across something -- to find something by accident, without looking for it
"She came across the second invoice while she was checking something else."
A pattern -- something that repeats in a way you can predict
"One mistake is a mistake. Nine of them on the same day is a pattern."
To look into something -- to examine something carefully in order to find out the truth
"Nobody asked her to look into it. She looked into it anyway."
A hunch -- a feeling that something is true, before you have any proof
"It started as a hunch, and it took her four months to prove it."
To rule something out -- to decide that something is not possible, and stop considering it
"They ruled out a system error in the first week."
Painstaking -- done very slowly and carefully, with attention to every detail
"It was painstaking work, and there was no faster way to do it."
To hold up -- to stay true or correct when somebody examines it closely
"Her numbers held up in front of three different lawyers."
To piece something together -- to understand something slowly, by putting small facts together
"She pieced the whole thing together from eleven emails and one calendar entry."
A discrepancy -- a difference between two things that should be the same
"A discrepancy of four dollars is still a discrepancy."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences, both correct, and they do not mean the same thing. She worked at the firm for twenty years. She has worked at the firm for twenty years. The first one says she left. The second one says she is still there, at her desk, today. Nothing in the words tells you that. The tense does.

This is the choice English makes that Portuguese does not, and it is the reason a fluent speaker can still write a sentence that says the opposite of what was meant. The past simple closes a period. It goes with the words that name a finished moment: in 2009, last quarter, three days later, ago. The present perfect leaves the period open onto now, and it goes with the words that refuse to close it: since, so far, already, yet, ever, never.

Put the two together and the sentence breaks. I have sent the report on Tuesday is not English, because Tuesday closes a period that have sent is trying to keep open. Say I sent the report on Tuesday if the day matters, and I have sent the report if the only thing that matters is that it is done and nobody has to chase it. The second one is what a manager wants at eight in the morning.

The same rule runs through an entire story. When Dana Reyes came across the first duplicated receipt, she corrected it and moved on — three finished events, in order, all in the past simple. When she describes what those weeks left behind, she switches: I have never seen a large discrepancy that did not have a small one in front of it. That sentence has no date in it, and it is not supposed to. It is the result the twenty-two years produced, and it is still true this morning.
