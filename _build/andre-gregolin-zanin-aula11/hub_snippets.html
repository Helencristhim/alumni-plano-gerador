The Language of Argument -- Concede, Then Counter

How to agree with the best point on the other side and still win the argument. Key words: a counterargument, to concede, to rebut, a stance, nuance, to reinforce, to undermine, to be fair, that being said, to stand by something. Structure: discourse markers for contrast, addition, concession and result.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A counterargument -- a reason that goes against the point somebody is making
"Before you finish, answer the strongest counterargument yourself."
To concede -- to admit that the other side is right about one particular thing
"I concede that the exam is objective. I do not concede that it is fair."
To rebut -- to answer a point and show that it is wrong or incomplete
"She did not ignore his point. She rebutted it, calmly, with one example."
A stance -- the position you take on a question, and are ready to defend
"My stance on the vestibular has changed twice this year."
Nuance -- a small but important difference that a simple answer leaves out
"Yes or no is easy. The nuance is in the middle."
To reinforce -- to make an argument or an idea stronger by adding support
"Your second example reinforces the first one. Keep it."
To undermine -- to make an argument weaker, often from inside, without attacking it directly
"One exaggerated number can undermine an entire argument."
To be fair -- softens what comes next, admitting that the other side has a point
"To be fair, basketball taught me more discipline than school did."
That being said -- turns the sentence back to your own point, after admitting something
"The exam is objective. That being said, it measures speed more than thinking."
To stand by something -- to keep defending a decision or an opinion, even under pressure
"I chose medicine two years ago, and I stand by it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Most people join their ideas with three words: and, but and so. They are correct, and in a short conversation they are enough. In a long opinion, however, they hide the shape of the argument, because the listener cannot tell which idea is the main one. Connectors fix that. Each one announces its job before the idea arrives. However and on the other hand announce a contrast. What’s more and on top of that announce something extra. As a result announces a consequence.

Position matters. However, what’s more and as a result start a new sentence and take a comma: The exam is objective. However, it measures speed. Although works differently. It joins two ideas inside one sentence, so it never needs a second connector. Although I miss basketball, I do not regret leaving it is complete. Adding but is the most common error Brazilian speakers make with it, because in Portuguese the two words often travel together.

The strongest speakers combine these into one move. They concede the best point on the other side, pause, and then counter it: Granted, the cursinho is exhausting. That said, it is the fastest way in. It sounds fair, and that is exactly why it is hard to rebut.

1. According to the text, what is the problem with and, but and so in a long opinion?
A They hide which idea is the main one.
B They are grammatically incorrect in formal English and in any kind of writing.
C They are too informal for speech.
2. Why is “Although I miss basketball, but I do not regret it” wrong?
A Although has to start a new sentence and always needs a comma after it.
B Although already joins the two ideas, so but is extra.
C Regret cannot follow although.
3. What does the strongest speaker do, according to the text?
A Never admits anything.
B Uses however at the start of every single sentence, to sound more formal and convincing.
C Concedes the best point, pauses, then counters it.

GRAMMAR

Order

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A friend says sport teaches more than school. You partly agree. You say:
A Although sport teaches discipline, but school teaches you how to think.
B To be fair, sport teaches discipline. That said, school teaches you how to think.
C You are wrong.
You want to add a second reason in a formal written answer. You write:
A Furthermore, the exam is very expensive to prepare for.
B On the other hand, the exam is also very expensive for most families to prepare for.
C As a result, expensive.
You want to connect a cause and its consequence. You say:
A I moved to São Paulo, however I stopped training.
B I moved to São Paulo. Even so, I stopped training completely after the first month there.
C I moved to São Paulo. As a result, I stopped training.
Somebody has argued well against your decision, and you still believe in it. You say:
A I concede everything you said.
B That is a fair point, but I stand by my decision.
C I rebut you, so you are wrong about everything you said about my decision.
