Forty-Five Seconds of Opinion -- Concession and Contrast

Four questions, no preparation. Give a clear opinion, accept the other side in one sentence, and come back to your position. Key words: A drawback, A benefit, To outweigh, A trade-off, To be torn between, In the long run, A workload, To cram, Overwhelming, Worthwhile, To take for granted, A double-edged sword, To make a case for. Structure: concession and contrast with although, despite, whereas and however.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A drawback -- a disadvantage or a problem that makes something less attractive
"The main drawback of online classes is the lack of contact."
A benefit -- an advantage or a helpful effect of something
"One benefit of studying the night before is that the class makes more sense."
To outweigh -- to be more important or greater than something else
"For me, the benefits outweigh the drawbacks."
A trade-off -- a situation where you accept losing one thing in order to gain another
"Fewer classes per week is a trade-off: less pressure, but a longer course."
To be torn between -- to find it very difficult to choose between two options
"I am torn between London and New York for the exchange."
In the long run -- after a long period of time, not immediately
"In the long run, reading every day makes the difference."
A workload -- the amount of work a person has to do
"My workload doubles in the exam period."
To cram -- to study a lot of information in a very short time, usually just before an exam
"I crammed for the test, and I forgot everything a week later."
Overwhelming -- so great or so much that it is difficult to deal with
"The reading list was overwhelming in the first semester."
Worthwhile -- worth the time, money or effort that you spend on it
"The exchange is expensive, but it is worthwhile."
To take for granted -- to expect something to always be there, without appreciating it
"I took my school English classes for granted until I went to Scotland."
A double-edged sword -- something that has both good and bad effects
"Social media is a double-edged sword for language learners."
To make a case for -- to give arguments that support an idea or a decision
"She made a strong case for starting the exchange earlier."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The strongest spoken opinions are not the most extreme ones. They are the ones that accept one point from the other side and still reach a clear conclusion. English has a small set of words for that move, and each one follows its own rule.

Although and even though introduce a full sentence, with a subject and a verb: “Although online classes are flexible, I learn more in person.” A very common mistake is to add but in the second part, because many languages allow both words in one sentence. English does not: the although already does the job.

Despite and in spite of are followed by a noun or an -ing form, never by a full sentence, and there is no of after despite: “Despite the heavy workload, the program is worthwhile.” To compare two facts side by side, use whereas: “My brother learned from games, whereas I learned from videos.”

However is different: it connects two separate sentences, so it usually comes after a period and before a comma: “Cramming works for one exam. However, it does not work in the long run.” Used well, one concession gives your answer a mature voice, and ten extra seconds to think about what comes next.

1. According to the text, what is wrong with “Although it is hard, but I like it”?
A Although needs a noun after it.
B English does not use but after although.
C Hard should be difficult.
2. What follows despite?
A A noun or an -ing form.
B A full sentence with a subject and a verb.
C The word of.
3. Where does however usually go?
A In the middle of one sentence, after although.
B Before despite.
C After a period, at the start of a new sentence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Concession and Contrast

GRAMMAR

Although + sentence, despite + noun or -ing, whereas to compare, however to start a new sentence.

JobFormExample
Concessionalthough / even though + subject + verbAlthough it is hard, I like it.
Concession with a noundespite / in spite of + nounDespite the cost, it is worthwhile.
Concession with a verbdespite + -ingDespite studying hard, I was nervous.
Compare two factsclause, whereas + clauseHe likes games, whereas I like books.
Contrast two sentencesSentence. However, sentence.It is fast. However, it is expensive.
Strong surpriseeven though + subject + verbEven though I read a lot, I get stuck.

Never: although it is hard, but I like it · despite of the workload · despite it is expensive · it works, however it does not last

Stage 2: Put the Answer in Order

Order

An interviewer asks whether students should study a topic before the class. Put your forty-five-second answer in a logical order.

?"In my opinion, students should study the topic before the class."
?"The main reason is that the class makes much more sense."
?"At my university, for example, we read every case the night before."
?"Although the workload can be overwhelming, you learn more in the long run."
?"Some students prefer to review after the class, whereas I need the preparation first."
?"So, for all these reasons, I still believe studying before is worthwhile."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to accept a point and then disagree. You say:
A "Although it is expensive, but I think it is worthwhile."
B "Although it is expensive, I think it is worthwhile."
C "Despite it is expensive, I think it is worthwhile."
You want to use despite correctly. You say:
A "Despite the heavy workload, I enjoy the course."
B "Despite of the heavy workload, I enjoy the course."
C "Despite the workload is heavy, I enjoy the course."
You want to compare how you and your brother learned. You say:
A "I learned from videos, despite he learned from games."
B "I learned from videos, however he learned from games."
C "I learned from videos, whereas he learned from games."
The interviewer asks a hard question and you need a second. You start with:
A "Your position, in one clear sentence."
B "A long silence until you find a perfect example."
C "I do not know, sorry."
