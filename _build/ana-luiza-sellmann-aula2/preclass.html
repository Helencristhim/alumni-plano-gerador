Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Experience Without a Date -- The Present Perfect

Talking about what you have done, where you have been and what you have not done yet. Key words: to get the hang of, to settle in, homesick, a highlight, overrated, to pull off, a brief, a launch. Structure: present perfect (have and has plus the participle) against the past simple, with ever, never, just, already, yet, for and since.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. Do not translate — the example sentence is the meaning.

To get the hang of -- to learn how to do something after some practice
"I've finally got the hang of the backhand."
To settle in -- to start feeling comfortable in a new place
"They've settled in well after two years in Shanghai."
Homesick -- sad because you miss your home and the people there
"He says he still gets homesick on Sundays."
A highlight -- the best part of a trip or an experience
"The snow in Texas was the highlight of the whole trip."
Overrated -- not as good as people say it is
"Honestly, I think that restaurant is a little overrated."
To pull off -- to succeed at something difficult
"They pulled off a surprise party for forty people."
A brief -- the document that says what the client wants
"The brief has already arrived, so we start on Monday."
A launch -- the day a product or a campaign goes public
"We've been to three launches this year."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

My mother and my brother have lived in Shanghai since 2024. I have never been to Asia, so everything I know about the city comes from their photos. My brother says they have finally settled in: he has got the hang of the metro, and my mother has already made more friends than he has. He still gets homesick on Sundays, and he has not spent two Christmases away from home before.

In January I went to New York and then to Texas, and I saw snow for the first time. That week was the highlight of my year. It is a strange feeling: I have travelled quite a lot for work, but I have not visited my own family yet. My mother brings it up every single week.

1. Why does the text say "they have lived in Shanghai since 2024" and not "they lived"?
A Because they are still there now: the situation started in 2024 and has not finished.
B Because 2024 is a recent year, and recent years take have or has.
C Because there are two people, and two people always take have.
2. The text says "In January I went to New York" and not "I have gone". Why?
A Because the trip was short, and short trips take the past simple.
B Because the sentence names a finished time, January, and a closed date takes the past simple.
C Because you never use have or has with the verb to go.
3. What does "I have not visited my own family yet" tell you?
A That she visited them a long time ago and does not want to go back.
B That the visit is impossible now.
C That the visit has not happened up to now, and it is still expected.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Does the Date Matter?

GRAMMAR

One question, two answers. That is the whole decision in this lesson.

FormUseExample
Present perfect
have / has + participle		The past when the day does not matter, or when the situation is still true now.I have worked on festivals.
She has lived here since 2024.
Past simple
worked / went / saw		A finished moment, when the sentence names the time or the time is understood.I went to New York in January.
We launched it in March.
Negativehave not and has not for the perfect; did not for the past simple.I have not been to Asia.
I did not go last year.
QuestionHave or Has plus the participle for experience; Did plus the base verb for a finished moment.Have you ever been to Brazil?
Did you go in January?
The six little wordsever and never for a whole life, just for a minute ago, already for sooner than expected, yet for still missing, for and since for how long. All of them live with have and has.
Participles worth knowinggo becomes been or gone, see becomes seen, do becomes done, write becomes written, eat becomes eaten, take becomes taken. Regular verbs simply add -ed.

If you are not sure, ask yourself one thing: does the sentence say when? If it does, use the past simple. If it does not, you do not have to invent a date — that is exactly what this form is for.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Someone is asking about your life and your work. Choose the best thing to say.

Someone asks: "Have you ever been to Asia?" You answer:
A "No, I've never been, but my family lives in Shanghai."
B "No, I never went there since my family lives in Shanghai."
C "No, I am never going there before now."
You want to say your family moved to Shanghai in 2024 and is still there. You say:
A "They are in Shanghai since 2024."
B "They've been in Shanghai since 2024."
C "They were in Shanghai since 2024."
A recruiter asks what you have worked on. The strongest opening is:
A "I've worked on brand activations for a few years now, mostly music festivals."
B "I am working on brand activations since a few years."
C "I have worked on brand activations in 2022."
You are telling the story of one specific trip. You say:
A "I have seen snow for the first time in January."
B "I saw snow for the first time in January, in Texas."
C "I am seeing snow in January for the first time."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is correcting this one.

You are on a fifteen-minute call and the person asks: "What have you worked on that you are most proud of?" Speak for about ninety seconds. Start with the big picture, using have and has and no dates. Then pick ONE project and tell that story with the past simple, with the year, the client and what went wrong. If a sentence breaks in the middle, say "let me start that again" out loud and keep going.
