Lesson 23 -- What We Should Have Done

Film
Sully (2016) -- the NTSB hearing scenes

A pilot lands a plane on the Hudson River and saves everyone on board. Then the investigation starts, and the room fills with people saying what he could have done instead: he could have turned back, he should have reached the airport. The film is, from beginning to end, a post-mortem -- and the turning point is the moment the hearing stops asking who made the mistake and starts asking what the process left out. Connection to Lesson 23: this is your grammar under pressure, spoken by people who are deciding a career with it.

Tip: watch the hearing scenes with English subtitles. Write down 5 sentences with should have or could have + past participle, then mark which ones point at a person and which ones point at the process. Rewrite the first group in the passive.

Podcast
Google SRE Prodcast -- "Postmortems with Ayelet Sachto"

Ayelet Sachto is a site reliability engineer at Google in London, and in twenty-eight minutes she explains how to run exactly the meeting you practised in class: how to write a post-mortem that is actionable, transparent and blameless, and why the moment a name goes into the document the learning stops. Connection to Lesson 23: every word of today's vocabulary lives here -- retrospective, blameless, gap, action item, accountability -- in the mouth of somebody who does it for a living.

Tip: listen once for the story. Listen again and write down 5 sentences where she says what a team should have done or could have done differently. Say each one out loud, and check that "should" and "could" are always followed by "have" + past participle.

YouTube
"Past Modals - Should Have, Could Have, Would Have (Intermediate English Grammar)" -- Virtually Fluent

A short, clear lesson on exactly today's structure. The teacher separates the three modals that Brazilian speakers mix most often, shows why the verb after them is always "have" and never "had", and drills the contracted pronunciation -- should've, could've -- that makes the difference between sounding fluent and sounding like a textbook. Connection to Lesson 23: the grammar behind every sentence you said in the retrospective, explained slowly.

Tip: copy the structure table into your notebook. Then write 5 sentences about a real problem at one of your accounts: what should have been done, what could have been done faster with the number of minutes, and what should not have been assumed.
