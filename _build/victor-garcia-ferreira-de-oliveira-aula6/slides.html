Chapter 4: The Code

Two Words Apart, Opposite Meanings

Used to, and used to

Grammar Discovery

Which Two Are Over?

"I used to train three times a week, before I went."

"After a month I got used to training in the dark at six."

"I did not use to read anything in English."

"I am used to reading in English now, and I forget that it is English."

The red ones and the blue ones share three letters and nothing else. Look at what comes immediately after the words used to in each pair.

JobFormExample
A past habit that is overused to + base formI used to train three times a week.
A past state that is overused to + be / have / liveI used to be the quiet one on any team.
Negative and questiondid not use to · Did you use to...? (no -d)I did not use to read in English. Did you use to play?
Becoming adaptedget used to + noun or verb-ingI got used to training in the dark.
Being adapted nowbe used to + noun or verb-ingI am used to reading in English now.
A repeated past action, in a storywould + base form (never for states)Every Saturday we would take the same bus.
The two trapsget used to + infinitive is wrong · keep the -d only in the affirmativeNot I got used to train. Say to training. Not I did not used to. Say did not use to.

In one line: used to + base form is a habit that ended; be or get used to + -ing is a habit that became normal.

Your Week, Before and After

Real Document

Two Weeks, Same Person

WEEKLY TIMETABLE — BEFORE AND AFTER
Victor Garcia Ferreira de Oliveira · kept for the exchange programme
Training3 evenings → 4 evenings, one at six in the morning
Language of the dayPortuguese → English, including on the bus
Homeworkafter dinner → after ten at night
Mealscooked at home → cooked by me, badly, twice a week
Saturdayfree → match day, home or away
Sundayfamily → one video call, and the rest alone
Programme note: students are asked to fill this in twice, once in the first week and once in the last. Almost nobody predicts the Sunday line, and it is the line most of them talk about in the final interview.
1. Which changes on this table did the student choose, and which ones simply happened to him?
He chose the training and the match days by joining the team. The language of the day, the late homework, the cooking and the Sunday were consequences he did not choose.
2. The homework line moves from after dinner to after ten at night. What does that tell you about the rest of the table?
That the evening was full, so the studying had to move to the only hours left. One choice pushed everything else later.
3. According to the programme note, why is the Sunday line the one students talk about at the end?
Because almost nobody predicts it. The table is filled in twice, and the Sunday is the change nobody expected when they wrote the first version.
Listening 1 · The Researcher

What Actually Changes, and What People Invent

A researcher who follows exchange students after they come home. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say almost every student reports, and why is she careful about it?
That they became more independent. She is careful because it is the sentence they were given before they left, so she cannot tell how much of it is memory and how much is the phrase.
2. What is the change she says shows up in the data but almost never in the interviews?
Tolerance for not understanding. She says students stop needing to understand every word before they act, and that almost none of them mention it because it does not feel like an achievement.
3. What does she say about the students who describe a single turning point?
That the story is usually built afterwards. The data shows change happening gradually across weeks, and the single dramatic moment is how people organise it when they look back.
Listening 2 · Two Years Later

The Habit He Could Not Keep

Somebody who came back two years ago. Sound first — no text.

1. What habit does he say he lost within two months of coming home, and why?
Speaking English every day. He says nobody around him needed it, so the habit had nothing holding it up once the situation stopped forcing it.
2. What does he say about the difference between self-discipline abroad and self-discipline at home?
That abroad he did not need much, because the situation decided for him. At home nobody decides for him, and that is when self-discipline actually starts.
3. What is the one thing he says he never lost?
Not being afraid to start a sentence he does not know how to finish. He says that one survived because it is not a habit, it is a decision he made once.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can describe the week I had before, and the week I have now, without mixing them up.
I use used to plus the base form for a habit that ended, and get used to plus -ing for something that became normal.
I say did not use to and did you use to, without the extra d.
I can say whether a change happened gradually or at a turning point, and mean it.
I know the words: a routine, a comfort zone, homesick, gradually, a turning point, independent, self-discipline, to take something for granted, to get used to something, to look back on something.
