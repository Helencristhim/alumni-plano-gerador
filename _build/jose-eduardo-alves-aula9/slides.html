Chapter 3: The Code

Advice Is Not an Order

Three little words, and who decides

Grammar Discovery

What Comes Right After?

"You should call the admissions office first."

"You should not ask a client for that letter."

"You could consider the January intake."

"If we sign this clause, we might lose the choice of jurisdiction."

After every one of these words the verb is bare: no to, no -s, no -ed. The red ones say what you think is best. The blue ones only open a door.

JobFormExample
Strong adviceshould + bare verbYou should call the office first.
Advice againstshould not + bare verb (shouldn't)You should not ask a client for that letter.
A soft suggestion, one option among otherscould + bare verbYou could consider the January intake.
Something possible, not certainmight + bare verbWe might lose the choice of jurisdiction.
Asking for adviceShould + subject + bare verbShould I include a cover letter?
Purpose: why you do itverb + to + bare verbI called them to confirm the deadline.
Softening your own opinionI would recommend / I would suggest + -ingI would suggest calling before you write.
The two trapsnever should to call, and never must where you mean should — must is an order, not advice

In one line: should says what you think, could opens a door, might warns — and after all three the verb stays bare.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Fifteen Minutes With Admissions

One conversation, two recordings, one checklist

Dialogue

Two Weeks Before the Deadline

E
I have two weeks before the deadline. What should I do first?
G
You should call this office before you write anything. Most people write first and ask later.
E
And the cover letter? Should I explain my whole career?
G
No. You should avoid the career summary. We already have your CV. You should answer one question: why this program and why now.
E
I could ask a client for the letter of recommendation. We started working together fifteen years ago.
G
You could, but it is not the strongest choice. A client says you are good at your job. We need somebody who can say you are able to study.
E
So who should I ask?
G
Somebody who taught you, or somebody who writes on your subject. And follow up after ten days — they might forget, and that is not a reason to lose a place.
Comprehension

About Grace’s Advice

1. What does she say he should do before he writes anything, and why?
He should call the admissions office first, because most people write first and ask later.
2. What should the cover letter avoid, and what should it answer?
It should avoid the career summary, because they already have the CV. It should answer one question: why this program and why now.
3. Why is a letter from a client not the strongest choice, and who should he ask instead?
Because a client says he is good at his job, and they need somebody who can say he is able to study. He should ask somebody who taught him, or somebody who writes on his subject.
Listening 1 · Five Pieces of Advice

Four Hundred Applications a Year

Somebody who reads all of them, in five points. Sound first — no text.

1. What are her first two pieces of advice?
Call the office before you write anything, and avoid the career summary in the cover letter — the letter is not a second CV.
2. Why should you not ask a client for the letter of recommendation?
Because a client says you are good at your job, and they need somebody who can say you are able to study.
3. What does she say about silence, and about following up?
You might not hear from them for six weeks, and that is normal — do not read silence as a no. And if a professor promises a letter, follow up after ten days: a polite reminder has never cost anybody a place.
Listening 2 · How to Warn a Client

First the Fact, Then the Silence

A senior partner on telling a client he is about to make a mistake. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say you should never start with, and what should you start with instead?
Never with the word no. Start with the risk, because the risk belongs to the client and the opinion belongs to you.
2. What is the order he recommends?
First the fact, then the silence, then the recommendation — and only if the client asks what you would do.
3. What does he say most young lawyers do, and what happens?
They do it backwards, and then they wonder why nobody follows their advice.

1. Turn the first three lines into advice, using the same modal for all three.
You should call the admissions office, you should write a one-page cover letter, and you should ask a professor for the letter of recommendation.
2. Now the two optional lines. Which modal, and why?
Could. You could ask a second professor as a backup, and you could sit the language test again — they are options, not recommendations.
3. The last two lines are marked RISK. Why do they need a different word?
Because they are not tasks: they are things that might happen. The professor might forget, and the university might not answer until January.
