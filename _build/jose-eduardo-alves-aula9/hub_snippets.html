Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To recommend -- to tell somebody clearly what you think is the best choice, and let them decide
"I would recommend the other route, and here is why."
To consider -- to think about something carefully before you decide
"You could consider the January intake, but I would not decide today."
To suggest -- to put an idea on the table, without saying it is the best one
"I would suggest calling the office before you write anything."
To avoid -- to stay away from something on purpose, because it costs you later
"You should avoid the career summary. They already have your CV."
A risk -- something bad that can happen, but has not happened yet
"Start with the risk, because the risk belongs to the client."
An admissions office -- the team at a university that reads every application and answers the phone
"You should call the admissions office before you write anything."
A cover letter -- the one page that answers why this program and why now
"The letter is not a second CV, and it is not your career summary."
A letter of recommendation -- a letter from somebody who can say you are able to do the work
"A client says you are good. A professor says you can study."
A shortlist -- the reduced list of candidates who are still in the running
"About sixty candidates make the shortlist, out of four hundred."
To follow up -- to write again, politely, when nobody has answered you
"You should follow up after ten days. A polite reminder never cost anybody a place."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Three small words carry almost all the advice in professional English, and the difference between them is not force. It is who decides. Should says what you think is best: "You should call the admissions office first." Could opens a door without recommending it: "You could consider the January intake." Might warns about something possible, not certain: "If we sign this clause, we might lose the choice of jurisdiction."

All three behave the same way, and it is the easiest grammar of the whole block. The verb after them is bare: no to, no -s, no -ed. It is he should ask, never he should to ask and never he shoulds ask. The negative puts not straight after: "You should not ask a client for that letter." The question moves the modal to the front, and it never calls do: "Should I follow up after ten days?"

Now the part that is about register and not about grammar. Must is also correct English, and it is almost always the wrong choice here. You must send me the document today is an order, and you do not give orders to a client or to a university. The same idea with should is just as clear and costs you nothing. English marks the distance between advising and commanding inside the verb, and Portuguese does not, so the hand reaches for the strong one without noticing.

Two more tools you will use every week. To say WHY you did something, use to plus the bare verb: "I called them to confirm the deadline." And to soften your own opinion, put it behind I would: "I would recommend the other route", "I would suggest calling first". Notice the last one: after suggest, the next verb takes -ing.

1. What is the real difference between should, could and might?
A How polite you are being.
B What each one does: recommend, open an option, or warn about a possibility.
C Whether the sentence is about the past or the future.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "He should to ask a professor, not a client."
B "He should ask a professor, not a client."
C "He shoulds ask a professor, not a client."
3. Why is "you must send me the document today" a bad choice in an email to a client?
A Because must is not correct English.
B Because the verb after must needs to.
C Because it is an order, and you do not give orders to a client.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Should, Could and Might

GRAMMAR

After all three, the verb stays bare. No to, no -s, no -ed.

JobFormExample
Strong adviceshould + bare verbYou should call the office first.
Advice againstshould not + bare verb (shouldn’t)You should not ask a client for that letter.
A soft optioncould + bare verbYou could consider the January intake.
Possible, not certainmight + bare verbWe might lose the choice of jurisdiction.
Asking for adviceShould + subject + bare verb, never with doShould I follow up after ten days?
Why you did itto + bare verbI called them to confirm the deadline.
Softening your opinionI would recommend + noun · I would suggest + verb-ingI would suggest calling before you write.

Stage 2: Put the Advice in Order

Order

A client is about to sign a clause you do not like. Put your advice in a logical order.

?"Before I give you my opinion, let me show you one line in the contract."
?"If we sign this clause, we might lose the choice of jurisdiction."
?"That is the risk. It is yours, not mine, so take a moment."
?"If you ask me, I would recommend the other route."
?"You could also ask them to change only that clause and keep the price."
?"Which of the two would you like me to prepare?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You are writing to a client who has not sent a document. You write:
A "You must send me the document today."
B "You should send me the document today, so that we can file on Friday."
C "You should to send me the document today."
You want to put an option on the table without recommending it. You say:
A "You could consider the January intake, but I would not decide today."
B "You must consider the January intake."
C "You could to consider the January intake."
You want to warn about something possible, not certain. You say:
A "We will lose the choice of jurisdiction for sure."
B "If we sign this clause, we might lose the choice of jurisdiction."
C "We might to lose the choice of jurisdiction."
You want to ask a colleague whether to write again. You say:
A "Do I should follow up after ten days?"
B "Should I follow up after ten days, or is that too soon?"
C "I should follow up after ten days?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

A client is about to sign a contract you do not like. Give him the whole advice: the risk first, then what you recommend, then what might happen if he signs it anyway, and one option he could take instead. Ninety seconds.
