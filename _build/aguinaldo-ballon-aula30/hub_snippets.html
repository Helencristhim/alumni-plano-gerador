Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Scrutiny -- careful and critical examination, often by people with power
"The new concession model is under public scrutiny."
Testimony -- what a person formally says to a court or a committee
"His testimony lasted forty minutes."
An expert witness -- a specialist invited to explain a technical subject to a court or a committee
"The committee invited three expert witnesses."
A committee -- a small group of people chosen to study a subject and decide or advise
"The infrastructure committee meets on Tuesdays."
An executive summary -- a short text at the start of a long report, with only the main points
"Most people only read the executive summary."
To condense -- to make a long text or speech shorter, keeping the important parts
"He condensed a two-hundred-page report into three minutes."
To substantiate -- to give evidence that shows a statement is true
"Can you substantiate that number?"
Rigorous -- very careful and exact, following every rule and detail
"The study is rigorous, but the sample is small."
A loaded question -- a question that hides an unfair idea, so any direct answer looks bad
"That is a loaded question, so let me answer the real issue."
To withstand -- to resist pressure or criticism without breaking or changing
"The model withstood three years of criticism."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In front of a committee, you have three minutes and a report of two hundred pages. You cannot say everything, so you condense, and nouns help: "The adoption of the model led to a rise in investment." Four events, four nouns, and the committee has the story.

Every number needs a source: "According to the agency's own data, tariffs fell by twelve percent." Use according to for a source, never for yourself: for your own view, say in my view. If you say a wrong number, repair it in one sentence: "Let me be precise: eighty-five percent." And explain rules in plain English: "The operator has to invest."

Then come the questions. Some are loaded: "So you admit the model failed?" Do not say yes or no. Name the hidden idea, "That question assumes the model failed," and answer the real issue. And never guess a number: "I will send it in writing."

1. How do you give your own opinion in a testimony?
A "According to me, the model worked, and I think the numbers in the report show it clearly."
B "The model worked, according to."
C "In my view, the model worked."
2. A senator asks: "So you admit the model failed?" What does the text recommend?
A Name the hidden idea and answer the real issue.
B Say yes, to show that you are honest, and then explain the details of every contract.
C Say no, and change the subject.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A "The adoption in the model led to more investment in the ports over ten years."
B "The adoption of the model led to more investment."
C "The adopt of the model led to more investment."

Stage 2: Put the Opening Statement in Order

Order

You have three minutes in front of the committee. Put your opening statement in order.

?"According to the agency's own data, private investment rose by eighty-five percent."
?"In plain English, my recommendation is to share the demand risk in the first five years."
?"In the same period, tariffs fell by twelve percent in real terms."
?"The adoption of the concession model changed the ports in ten years."
?"Thank you, Madam Chair. I will be brief."
?"Three contracts were renegotiated, mainly because of delays in the right of way."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A member asks where your tariff number comes from. You say:
A "From me. I calculated it myself last year, and I am sure it is correct, more or less."
B "According to me."
C "According to the agency's own data."
A member asks for a number you do not know. You say:
A "It is probably around forty percent, or maybe fifty, I am not completely sure about it."
B "I do not have the exact figure, but I will send it in writing."
C "I do not know."
A senator asks: "So you admit the policy failed?" You answer:
A "That question assumes it failed. The real issue is the timing of the contracts."
B "Yes."
C "No, no, no, it did not fail, absolutely not, the policy was a total success in every way."
You said "it doubled", but it rose by eighty-five percent. You say:
A "It doubled. More or less. It is almost the same thing, so it does not matter very much."
B "Let me be precise: it rose by eighty-five percent."

Talk
TED -- Nancy Duarte, "The secret structure of great talks" (18 min)

A presentation designer on the structure behind the most memorable speeches: what is, what could be, and the call to action. Exactly what a three-minute opening statement needs.

Tip: use her structure to plan your own three-minute statement: one sentence for what is, one for what could be, one recommendation.

Talk
TED -- Pamela Meyer, "How to spot a liar" (19 min)

A researcher on deception and the questions that reveal it. Useful from the other side of the table: it shows what a skeptical committee is looking for when it listens to a witness.

Tip: note two behaviors she describes, and say how a witness can show the opposite: precision, sources and quick corrections.
