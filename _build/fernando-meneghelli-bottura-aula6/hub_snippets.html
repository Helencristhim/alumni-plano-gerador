Drawing the Org Chart Out Loud

Describing a group, a reporting line and a decision in one sentence, without losing the listener. Key words: a parent company, a subsidiary, a business unit, a matrix structure, a reporting line, a dotted line, a shared service center, a joint venture, span of control, a board committee, a stake, to carve out. Structure: defining and non-defining relative clauses.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. Do not translate — the example sentence is the meaning. You will read every one of these in a job description before you ever have to say it in a room.

A parent company -- the company that owns the one you work for
"The parent company is in Brussels and the operating entity is here."
A subsidiary -- a company that is owned by another company
"Four subsidiaries, two of which had never been integrated."
A business unit -- a part of a group that is run with its own numbers
"Each business unit had its own controller, which is where the trouble started."
A matrix structure -- an organization where you report to two people at once
"In a matrix structure the interesting question is who decides when the two disagree."
A reporting line -- the person or function you formally answer to
"My reporting line ran to the group chief executive in London."
A dotted line -- a second, informal reporting relationship
"I had a dotted line to the group finance director, who actually decided things."
A shared service center -- one team doing the same task for several companies
"The shared service center took the same reconciliation from four times a month to one."
A joint venture -- a company that two organizations own together
"The media arm was a joint venture, which made the consolidation interesting."
Span of control -- how many people report directly to you
"Thirty-two in the function, but my span of control was four."
A board committee -- a small group of board members with one specific job
"The audit committee met four times a year and asked better questions than the board."
A stake -- the share of a company that somebody owns
"The group held a sixty per cent stake and consolidated the whole entity."
To carve out -- to separate part of a business so it can be sold or run alone
"We carved out the production arm in nine months, with its own accounts."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The group, which owned nine agencies, reported into a parent company abroad. Three of them were full subsidiaries and one was a joint venture in which we held a sixty per cent stake. Each business unit had its own controller, which sounded sensible on paper and meant in practice that nobody owned a consolidated number.

My reporting line ran to the group chief executive, who sat in London, and I had a dotted line to the finance director, who was the person who actually decided things. The entity that was losing money went first; the two that were breaking even I left until the second year. My span of control was four, although thirty-two people worked in the function.

The idea for the shared service center came from a controller whose team was doing the same reconciliation four times a month, once for each entity. We also carved out the production arm, which the audit board committee had been asking about for two years. None of that was difficult to describe. What was difficult was describing it without ever saying the word difficult.

1. "The entity that was losing money went first." How many entities were there, and what is the clause doing?
A Several. The clause identifies which one — without it the sentence would not tell you.
B One. The clause simply adds a fact about it.
C None — the clause is decorative and could be removed with no change of meaning.
2. Why is it "the group, which owned nine agencies," and not "the group that owned nine agencies"?
A Because "which" is more formal than "that".
B Because there is only one group and the clause just adds information — so it takes commas, and after a comma it is never "that".
C Because "that" cannot be used with organizations.
3. In "a controller whose team was doing the same reconciliation", what does "whose" do?
A It shows possession: the team belongs to the controller.
B It asks a question about the controller's identity.
C It replaces "which" because the noun is a person.
4. What does the last sentence of the text say the real difficulty was?
A Explaining a complicated legal structure in a second language.
B Describing the structure without ever using a word of complaint about it.
C Remembering the ownership percentages accurately.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Two Facts, One Sentence

GRAMMAR

A relative clause hangs a second fact on a noun without starting a new sentence. The comma decides whether that fact is essential or extra — and in speech the comma is a pause.

TypeWhat it doesExample
Defining
no commas		Tells you WHICH one. Remove it and the sentence loses its meaning.The entity that was losing money went first.
Non-defining
commas		Adds a fact about something already identified. Remove it and the sentence still works.The group, which owned nine agencies, reported to Brussels.
that vs whichDefining clauses take that or which. Non-defining clauses take which and never that. For people, who works in both.
whosePossession, for people and for things: the controller whose team did the same reconciliation four times a month.
Quantifier + of whichThe most useful pattern for a group: four subsidiaries, two of which had never been integrated. Also most of whom, none of which, all of whom.
No second subjectThe relative pronoun IS the subject. The entity that was losing money, never the entity that it was losing money — a direct import from Portuguese.
In speechYou cannot hear a comma, but you can hear a pause. A non-defining clause is spoken between two small pauses, on a slightly lower pitch. If you do not pause, the listener hears a defining clause and the meaning changes.

With this structure a whole org chart fits into four sentences. Without it, it takes twelve — and the listener is gone by the fifth.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The panel is asking about structure. Choose the answer that sounds like somebody who has run one.

"Who did you report to?" The strongest answer is:
A "Solid line to the group chief executive, who sat in London, and a dotted line to the finance director, who was the one who decided."
B "It was complicated, because the structure there never really worked properly."
C "To the CEO. And also to the finance director. It depended on the subject."
You want to say that two of the four subsidiaries were never integrated. You say:
A "Four subsidiaries, two of them had never been integrated."
B "Four subsidiaries, two of which had never been integrated."
C "Four subsidiaries that two of them had never been integrated."
Which sentence is correct?
A "The group, that owned nine agencies, reported to Brussels."
B "The entity that it was losing money went first."
C "The idea came from a controller whose team did the work four times."
In a matrix, the question that makes you sound senior is:
A "Who signs my objectives at the end of the year?"
B "When those two disagree, which one actually decides?"
C "Is the matrix here as difficult as it usually is?"
You are asked about a structure that genuinely did not work. The safest way to say it is:
A "Each business unit had its own controller, which meant nobody owned a consolidated number."
B "The structure was chaotic and honestly quite badly designed."
C "It was very difficult to work there because of how they organized everything."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is correcting this one, and there is no score.

Describe the structure of the last group you worked in, in four sentences and about ninety seconds: the formal reporting line, the informal one, how you worked out which was which, and one decision that went a particular way because of it. Use at least two relative clauses with commas, and pause on each side of them. One rule: not a single adjective of judgement about the structure. If one escapes, say the sentence again without it and keep going — do not restart the recording.
