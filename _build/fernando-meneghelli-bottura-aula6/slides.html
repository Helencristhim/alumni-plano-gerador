Chapter 3: The Code

Two Facts, One Sentence

The comma decides whether the extra information is essential or not

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The entity that was losing money went first."

"The group, which owned nine agencies, reported to Brussels."

"I had a dotted line to the finance director, who actually decided things."

"Four subsidiaries, two of which had never been integrated."

Two of these have commas and two do not. What changes about the meaning?

TypeWhat it doesExample
Defining
no commas		Tells you WHICH one. Remove it and the sentence loses its meaning.The entity that was losing money went first.
Non-defining
commas		Adds a fact about something already identified. Remove it and the sentence still works.The group, which owned nine agencies, reported to Brussels.
that vs whichDefining clauses take that or which. Non-defining clauses take which and never that. For people, who works in both.
whoseFor possession, of people and of things: the controller whose team did the same reconciliation four times a month.
Quantifier + of whichThe most useful pattern for describing a group: four subsidiaries, two of which had never been integrated. Also most of whom, none of which, all of whom.
In speechYou cannot hear a comma, but you can hear a pause. Non-defining clauses are spoken between two small pauses and on a slightly lower pitch. That is why this point is as much pronunciation as grammar.

Whole org charts fit in four sentences with this. Without it they take twelve, and the listener is gone by the fifth.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"The group, that owned nine agencies, reported to Brussels."
"The group, which owned nine agencies, reported to Brussels."
"The entity that it was losing money went first."
"The entity that was losing money went first."
"I reported to the finance director, which decided everything."
"I reported to the finance director, who decided everything."

After a comma the word is which for things and who for people, never that. And the relative pronoun IS the subject — there is no second one.

The Move

Four Sentences, No Adjectives

1. The formal line

"My reporting line ran to the group chief executive, who was based in London."

2. The real line

"I had a dotted line to the group finance director, who was the person who decided things."

3. How you knew

"It took me six months and one rejected budget to work that out."

4. One decision

"After that I took the shared service case to him first, and it was approved in a week."

The distance between explaining a structure and complaining about one is a single adjective. Say the facts and let the panel draw the conclusion.

The Client's Structure

A holding company, three subsidiaries, and two lines that disagree

Dialogue

Which Line Actually Decides?

C
Before the panel, you should know the shape of the group. It is a holding company with three subsidiaries, one of which is a joint venture.
F
And the reporting line for this role?
C
Solid line to the Group CFO in Brussels, dotted line to the chief executive here, who runs the Brazilian operation.
F
Then let me ask the question that matters. When those two disagree, which one actually decides?
C
That is exactly the right question, and nobody in this process has asked it yet. Brussels decides on capital, and the chief executive decides on people.
F
Which means a headcount case goes one way and a system case goes the other.
C
Correct. Now describe the structure you came from, in four sentences, and do not tell me it was difficult.
F
My line ran to the group chief executive, who sat in London, and I had a dotted line to the finance director, who was the one who decided. It took me six months and one rejected budget to work that out. After that I took the shared service center case to him first, and it was approved in a week.

2. Who holds the solid line and who holds the dotted line?
Solid line to the Group CFO in Brussels, dotted line to the chief executive who runs the Brazilian operation.
3. When the two disagree, how is it decided?
Brussels decides on capital and the chief executive decides on people.
Listening

What They Are Really Checking

A consultant on the org chart question. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does he say the reporting line question is not a check on the facts?
Because the fact is already on the CV. They are testing whether the candidate understands where power actually sat.
2. In a matrix, what does he say the interesting question is?
Not who signs your objectives, but who you go to when the two bosses disagree.
3. What happens the moment a description turns into a complaint?
They stop hearing an executive and start hearing somebody who did not fit.
Listening 2

A Whole Group in Six Sentences

A CFO describing what she walked into. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the shape of the group, and what was unusual about two of the subsidiaries?
A holding company with four subsidiaries; two of them had been acquired eighteen months earlier and never really integrated.
2. What was the difference between her formal line and her dotted line, and what did it cost her?
The formal line was to the group chief executive in London; the dotted line was to the group finance director, who actually decided. Working that out cost her about six months.
3. Whose idea was the shared service centre, and why does she say so clearly?
One of the controllers, whose team was doing the same reconciliation four times a month — and she says it because people always assume it was hers.
