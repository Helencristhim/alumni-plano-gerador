One Clause, Three Jobs

A Whole Clause Where a Word Would Go

"The trouble is the definition."

A noun. Short, and it says almost nothing.

"The trouble is that nobody agrees on a definition."

A clause in the same seat, carrying an argument.

"What matters is the audience."

Now the clause is the subject, at the front.

"It depends on what you call a sport."

And here it follows a preposition, like any noun would.

Anywhere English accepts a noun, it accepts a clause instead. That is the whole idea, and it is what lets one sentence hold a position and a reason at the same time.

Chapter 3: The Clause

Where the Clause Sits

Subject, object, complement, and after a preposition

Grammar Discovery

Four Clauses Doing a Noun's Job

What matters is the audience, not the sweat.

Everyone knows that they train ten hours a day.

The problem is whether the record can survive a patch.

It all depends on what you call a sport.

Cover the coloured part of each sentence with your hand and read what is left. Then look at the word that opens each coloured block, and at where the block is sitting.

Where the clause sitsHow it opensExample
SubjectWhat / That / Whether"What matters is the audience." / "That they train is undeniable."
Object of the verbthat (often left out) / wh- / whether"Everyone knows (that) they train." / "Nobody knows what the committee will decide."
Complement, after bethat / whether / wh-"The problem is whether the record survives a patch."
After a prepositionwhat / whether — never that"It depends on what you call a sport." / "The question of whether it belongs there."
The word order insideStatement order, always: subject then verb. "Nobody knows what the committee wants", never "what does the committee want".
whether vs ifThey swap only as the object of a verb. Only whether works after a preposition, before to, and at the front of the sentence.

The Tool

Three Places It Goes Wrong

Trap 1 · "what", on its own

Portuguese opens with two words and English opens with one. Say "What matters is the audience" — never "the what matters" and never "that matters is". The word what is already the subject of its own clause.

Trap 2 · nothing follows a preposition except what or whether

"It depends on what you call a sport." "The question of whether it belongs there." A that clause can never sit after on, of, about or in.

Trap 3 · whether is the safe one

As the object of a verb, whether and if both work. Everywhere else only whether survives: after a preposition, before to, and at the front of the sentence.

One clause at the front of a sentence is what turns a list of opinions into a case. Use it on the line you want remembered.

Dialogue

Arguing Without Shouting

N
You are voting against them. Give me your stance in one sentence.
M
What decides this for me is not the sweat. It is whether the record still means something in ten years.
N
That is a valid point. What is your evidence that it does not?
M
A game is patched every three weeks. What that means is that the version somebody won on in July is gone by October.
N
So the question of whether they are athletes is not even your point.
M
It is not. That they train ten hours a day is undeniable, and anybody arguing otherwise has not looked.
N
Then your assumption is that a sport has to freeze. Chess changed its clock rules and survived.
M
I concede that one, honestly. What I would still want is a separate competition, with a history of its own.
N
Then we disagree about the room, not about the players. I can work with that.

About Nina

His stance, in one sentence — she wants the position on the table first.

As a disagreement about the room, not about the players — and she says she can work with that.

Chapter 5: Practice

Building the Case

Spot the Error

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "The what matters is the audience, not the sweat."
"What matters is the audience, not the sweat." (in English "what" already carries the subject; nothing goes in front of it)
2. "It all depends on that you call a sport."
"It all depends on what you call a sport." (a "that" clause can never follow a preposition)
3. "The question of if it belongs there is still open."
"The question of whether it belongs there is still open." (only "whether" survives after a preposition)
4. "Nobody knows what does the committee want."
"Nobody knows what the committee wants." (inside the clause the order is a statement, not a question)
5. "I am agree that the training is real."
"I agree that the training is real." ("agree" is a verb on its own in English; it never takes "am", "is" or "are")
The Real Thing

A Case in Five Lines

Debate card — five lines
Mark · esports at the Olympics
Against
with one concession
The stanceWhat decides this is not the sweat, and I am against them joining the Games.
The reasonThe problem is whether the record survives a patch, and a game is patched every three weeks.
The evidenceEveryone knows that the July version is gone by October, so there is nothing left to compare.
The concessionI concede that they train ten hours a day. My case has never depended on what their bodies do.
Your cardFive lines on a question you actually argued about this year. Every line anchored on a clause, and the concession is not optional.
1. Why does the card open with "What decides this is..." instead of "I think..."?
Because it puts the criterion in the subject position, before any opinion. The listener then knows what would have to be proved to change your mind.
2. What does the concession line do for the argument?
It removes the opponent's strongest weapon by agreeing with it, and shows that the case never rested on that point in the first place.
