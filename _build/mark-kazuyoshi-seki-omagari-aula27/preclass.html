Lesson 27 -- Pre-class

What Matters Is... -- Noun Clauses for Opinions

Anywhere English accepts a noun, it accepts a whole clause instead: what matters is the audience, the problem is that nobody agrees, it depends on what you call a sport, the question of whether it belongs there. Three positions, three traps, and one opinion that finally holds when somebody pushes back. Key words: argument, assumption, evidence, stance, concede, counterargument, reasoning, valid, flawed, debatable, justify, undeniable.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. These let you build a position instead of just repeating that you disagree.

Argument -- a reason, or a set of reasons, you give to support what you think
"The strongest argument for esports has nothing to do with sweat."
Assumption -- something you accept as true without ever checking it
"My assumption is not that these athletes are soft."
Evidence -- the facts that show whether something is true or not
"That is where the evidence points the other way."
Stance -- the position you take on a question, said out loud
"So let me put my stance on the table first."
Concede -- to accept that part of what the other person says is right
"I will concede the strongest counterargument, because pretending it does not exist is how you lose."
Counterargument -- the reason somebody gives against the point you just made
"The counterargument is that the training is not real, and it is simply false."
Reasoning -- the thinking that connects your evidence to your conclusion
"The evidence was right and the reasoning was not, which is worse."
Valid -- well built, so that it still holds when somebody tests it
"It is a valid point, and it is not the point that decides this."
Flawed -- with a mistake inside it that weakens the whole thing
"The comparison is flawed, because a game changes every three weeks."
Debatable -- not settled, so reasonable people can still disagree about it
"Whether it counts as a sport is debatable, and whether they train is not."
Justify -- to show that something is fair or reasonable, with a reason
"Nothing in the numbers justifies leaving them out of the Games."
Undeniable -- so clear that nobody can seriously argue against it
"That the top players practice ten hours a day is undeniable."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the opinion piece and answer the questions. Notice where each clause in bold is sitting in its sentence.

Every few years somebody asks whether esports belong at the Olympic Games. Let me put my stance on the table first. What decides this is not whether the players sweat: shooting, sailing and archery have already survived that test, and it is the weakest one anybody has proposed.

The problem is whether the thing being tested stays the same over time. A hundred meters in nineteen sixty is a hundred meters today, and that is why the record means something. A game is patched every three weeks, and what that means is that the July version does not exist in October. It all depends on what you call a competition.

Now I will concede the strongest counterargument, because pretending it does not exist is how you lose a debate. That the top players practice ten hours a day is undeniable, and everybody knows that they have coaches, analysts and physiotherapists. The reasoning that this is only a hobby is flawed, and anybody who uses it has not looked at the training.

So my assumption is not that these athletes are soft. My assumption is that a sport has to be stable enough to compare across generations. What I would like is a separate competition with a history of its own. Whether anybody wants to hear that is another matter.

1. In "What decides this is not whether the players sweat", where is the first clause sitting?
A In the subject position, before the verb "is".
B In the object position, after the verb.
C After a preposition, like "on" or "of".
2. Why is it "depends on what you call a competition" and not "depends on that you call a competition"?
A Because "depend" is followed by "on" and never by a clause.
B Because a "that" clause can never follow a preposition; only "what" or "whether" can.
C Because the sentence is a question in disguise.
3. "That the top players practice ten hours a day is undeniable." What job is the bold clause doing?
A It is the subject of "is undeniable".
B It is the object of "practice".
C It is a relative clause describing "players".
4. Why does the writer concede the counterargument instead of ignoring it?
A Because he has changed his mind about the whole question.
B Because agreeing with the strongest objection shows his case never depended on it.
C Because a debate requires you to give at least one point to the other side.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Noun Clauses

GRAMMAR

Anywhere English accepts a noun, it accepts a whole clause instead. What changes is where the clause sits and which word opens it.

Where the clause sitsHow it opensExample
SubjectWhat / That / Whether"What matters is the audience." / "That they train is undeniable."
Object of the verbthat (often left out) / wh- / whether"Everyone knows (that) they train." / "Nobody knows what the committee will decide."
Complement, after bethat / whether / wh-"The problem is whether the record survives a patch."
After a prepositionwhat / whether — never that"It depends on what you call a sport." / "The question of whether it belongs there."
Word order insideStatement order, always: subject then verb. "Nobody knows what the committee wants", never "what does the committee want".
whether vs ifThey swap only as the object of a verb. Only whether works after a preposition, before to, and at the front of the sentence.
The Portuguese trap"What" already carries the subject of its own clause, so nothing goes in front of it: "What matters is...", never "the what matters".

Stage 2: Put the Case in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the five parts of an argument in order.

?Name the evidence, so the listener can check you instead of trusting you.
?Close by saying what matters most, so the last sentence carries the whole case.
?Put your stance on the table in one sentence.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

Take a question you actually argued about this year -- at school, at training or at home. Build the case in five sentences: the stance, the strongest reason, the evidence, the counterargument you concede, and the line that closes it. Start at least two sentences with "What" or "That", and use "whether" at least once. Finish by saying what would have to be true for you to change your mind.
