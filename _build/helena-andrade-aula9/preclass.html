Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Precision Reading -- Hedging Language and the Words an Investigation Uses to Be Careful

The vocabulary of a formal inquiry and the hedging that separates what a text states from what it suggests. Key words: negligence, testimony, investigation, finding, recommendation, contributing factor, root cause, corrective action, witness, sequence of events. Structure: hedging language and the modals would rather, had better and may as well.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read it inside a real sentence. These are the words the exam text will use.

Negligence -- the legal failure to take the care a reasonable person would take
"A report will rarely state that a person was negligent."
Testimony -- what a person formally says as evidence, whether or not it is true
"Testimony is what a person said, not what happened."
Investigation -- the formal inquiry that establishes what happened and why
"The investigation was opened within forty-eight hours."
Finding -- a conclusion the inquiry treats as established by the evidence
"Only the first sentence is a finding."
Recommendation -- the action the inquiry asks somebody to take so it does not happen again
"The recommendation is promoted once the corrective action is agreed."
Contributing factor -- something that helped cause the event without being the reason for it
"A root cause is often described as a contributing factor first."
Root cause -- the underlying reason without which the event would not have occurred
"If it would not prevent the next occurrence, it is not the root cause."
Corrective action -- the change made afterwards to stop the same thing happening again
"Corrective action is agreed before the finding is promoted."
Witness -- a person who saw or heard the event and can be questioned about it
"The witness stated that he had closed the valve."
Sequence of events -- the ordered account of what happened, minute by minute
"A report may state that the sequence of events is established."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

It would appear that the valve was closed before the transfer began. The witness stated that he had closed it, which is testimony and not a finding. More often than not, a root cause is recorded as a contributing factor first, and to some extent that is a matter of convention rather than of evidence.

Investigators would rather understate a cause they cannot fully evidence than overstate one they can. If two explanations fit the evidence equally, you had better write both. If one of them can be tested, you may as well test it before you write anything. And if your recommendation would not prevent the next occurrence, it seems that you have not yet found the root cause.

2. Why is you had better write both not about the past?
A Because had better is present advice with a consequence, not the past of have.
B Because better cannot be used in the past.

3. Which of these would the paper describe as something the text implies rather than states?
A The sequence of events is established.
B It seems that the root cause has not been found.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging -- The Distance Between the Writer and the Claim

GRAMMAR

A hedge is not a softer way of saying the same thing. It changes what the text claims, and it is the only mark on the page that separates the text states from the text implies.

FormWhat it doesExample
it would appear / it seems thathedge: the evidence supports the claim without compelling itIt would appear that the valve was closed.
to some extent / more often than nota claim that carries its own limit inside itMore often than not, a root cause is called a contributing factor first.
would rather X than Ya stated preference between two available coursesInvestigators would rather understate a cause than overstate one.
had better + bare infinitivestrong advice with a consequence behind it. It is not the past of haveyou had better write both
may as well + bare infinitivethere is no reason not to, and some reason toyou may as well test it first
The reading trapa hedge is not a softer way of saying the same thing: it changes what the text CLAIMS. The text states and the text implies are two different questions, and the hedge is the only mark on the page that tells them apart.

Every wrong option in a comprehension question is built by moving a claim one step closer to the reader or one step further away. Hedges are where that move happens, and they are three words long.

Stage 2: Put the Method in Order

Order

How a claim travels from evidence to published finding.

?It is recorded as a contributing factor, at a deliberately lower level.
?The evidence supports an explanation without settling it, so it is written as a hedge.
?The finding is promoted, and the flat sentence is written at last.
