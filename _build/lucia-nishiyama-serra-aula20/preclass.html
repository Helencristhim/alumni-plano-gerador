Lesson 20 -- Pre-class

Writing with Authority -- The Email He Reads at Six

The follow-up email to Thursday's interview, and the two decisions that make written English sound senior. Key words: subject line, bottom line, recipient, concise, wordy, to acknowledge, sign-off, to follow up. System: nominalization -- the verb becomes a noun to package what you both already agree on, and the verb comes back, with a person in front of it, the moment somebody has to answer for something.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Subject line -- the short line that tells the reader what a message is about before they open it
"A subject line is a label, not a greeting: it has to say what is inside."
Bottom line -- the one point you would keep if you could keep only one
"The bottom line: I would take the role, and I could start in six weeks."
Recipient -- the person a message is written for, and whose time decides how long it should be
"Every line has to earn its place with the recipient, not with the writer."
Concise -- short because nothing unnecessary was left in, not because something is missing
"Short means few words. Concise means no wasted ones. They are not the same."
Wordy -- using more words than the idea needs, so the reader has to work to find the point
"A wordy paragraph is not more polite. It just moves the work to the reader."
To acknowledge -- to tell someone in writing that their message arrived and was read
"Two lines to acknowledge the message on Friday are worth more than a perfect answer on Tuesday."
Sign-off -- the closing line before your name, which sets the distance between you and the reader
"Best regards is safe with someone you met once. Best is warmer, and normal in an American office."
To follow up -- to write again after a meeting or a call, so that the conversation produces something
"I follow up inside twenty-four hours, while the conversation is still in his head."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

It is Friday, and Lucia has one hour to write the email that follows Thursday's interview. She starts where the reader starts: the subject line. Not a greeting, a label -- Follow-up: AI safety role, Thursday interview -- because the recipient reads it on a phone before six in the morning and decides in eight seconds. Her first sentence picks up something he said, so the message is a continuation and not a broadcast. Then, in one line, comes the bottom line: she would take the role and could start in six weeks. Everything after that exists to support it. In the evidence she uses the move that makes written English sound senior: the verb becomes a noun. "We replaced the platform" turns into "the platform replacement", and "the board approved it" turns into "the approval" -- a whole event, packed into a subject, calm and short. That move is called nominalization, and it has one price: a noun does not need an actor. So when she reaches the failed program, she puts the verb back and a person in front of it: "we told the client late, and that one is on me." Every line is concise, which is not the same as short, and nothing in the message is wordy. She asks for one thing only, chooses her sign-off, and sends it before lunch. The recruiter, who only needs to acknowledge the interview, gets three lines and a different question.

1. Why does Lucia put the bottom line near the top instead of at the end?
A Because the reader decides in seconds, so he has to be able to stop after one sentence and still know what she wants.
B Because a conclusion at the end would be grammatically incorrect in English.
C Because the evidence is not important and can be left out completely.
2. What happens to the verb in "we replaced the platform" when she writes "the platform replacement"?
A It moves to the end of the sentence and keeps the same form.
B It becomes a noun, so a whole event fits into the subject of one short sentence.
C It becomes a question, because written English prefers questions.
3. Why does she use "we told the client late" instead of a noun there?
A Because nouns are never correct when you write about the past.
B Because the sentence would be too short with a noun in it.
C Because somebody has to answer for that one, and a noun would hide the person who did it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Nominalization

GRAMMAR

The whole mechanism in five lines. The verb becomes a noun -- and you decide when to let it.

Spoken (verb)Written (noun)How the noun is built
we decided to move itthe decision to move it-sion / -tion: decide, discuss, implement, complete.
the vendor failed twicetwo vendor failures-ure / -ment: fail, agree, replace, improve.
I reviewed the contractmy review of the contractSame word, no ending -- and of carries the object.
the board approved itthe board approval-al: approve, refuse, propose, renew.
The priceA noun does not need an actor. The delay was not communicated hides everyone; we did not tell the client names the writer.
The ruleUse the noun to package what you and the reader already agree on. Bring the verb back, with a person in front of it, the moment somebody has to act or answer for something.
