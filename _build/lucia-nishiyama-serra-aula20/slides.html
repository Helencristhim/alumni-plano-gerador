Chapter 4: The Noun That Carries It

One Move, Two Directions

Verb into noun — and back again when it matters

The Writing Map

Five Moves That Sound Senior

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"We decided to move the deadline." → "The decision to move the deadline..."

"The vendor failed twice." → "Two vendor failures..."

"I reviewed the contract on Monday." → "My review of the contract..."

"The team approved the budget." → "The budget approval..."

The right side is shorter every time. What happened to the verb — and to the person who did it?

Spoken (verb)Written (noun)How the noun is built
we decidedthe decision-sion / -tion: decide, discuss, implement
the vendor failedtwo vendor failures-ure / -ment: fail, agree, replace
I reviewed itmy review of itsame word, no ending — and of carries the object
they approved the budgetthe budget approval-al: approve, refuse, propose
The move is called nominalization: the verb becomes a noun, so a whole event fits into the subject of one calm sentence. The price is that a noun does not need an actor. Use the noun for what everybody already agrees on. The moment somebody has to act, or answer for something, bring the verb back and bring the person with it.

Written English is not spoken English with longer words. It is the same events, packed differently — and the packing is a choice you make sentence by sentence.

Focus

One Event, Four Sentences

The event: your team told the client about the delay two days late. Say each version out loud, then tap to see what it does to the reader

"There was a delay in client communication." ___Nobody is in the sentence. Nothing can be answered, and the reader stops trusting the rest.
"The client was informed two days late." ___The fact is there, the actor is not. Useful only when who did it is genuinely not the point.
"We told the client two days late." ___Verb and actor together. Six words, and the reader knows exactly whom to answer.
"The two-day delay is on us, and here is what changed since." ___The noun packages the agreed fact and the verb carries the action. This is the senior version.

Same event, four different rooms. The grammar chose all of it.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

Subject: Thank you for your time — a label that tells the reader nothing
Subject: Follow-up — AI safety role, Thursday interview
"I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to follow up on our meeting." — two sentences, nothing said
"You mentioned the first year would be judged on the review cycle. That is the part I kept thinking about."
"The delay was not communicated to the client." — the noun hides everyone
"We did not tell the client about the delay for two days."

In Portuguese, two lines of courtesy before the point are good manners. In an American inbox they are two lines the reader paid for and did not use. The politeness moves to the sign-off, where it costs nothing.

Better. Now your first sentence is about you. Open with something he said, or the whole thing reads like a broadcast.

Did You Catch It?

About the Draft Review

1. What does Ray say a subject line is, and what does he say it is not?
A label, not a greeting: it has to tell the reader what is inside the message.
2. Why does Ray reject a first sentence that is about the writer?
Because the email then reads like a broadcast; he wants it to open with something the reader said.
3. What is Ray's rule for choosing between the noun and the verb?
Keep the noun for what both people already agree on, and keep the verb wherever somebody has to answer for something.
Listening 1

The Editor Who Deletes First Paragraphs

An executive communications editor on the sentence that decides everything. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she say she does first with almost every email she is given?
She deletes the first paragraph, because the real first sentence is usually the second one.
2. How does she explain the difference between short and concise?
Short is about the number of words; concise is about waste. A short email can still be wordy, and a long one can be concise.
3. What test does she give for deciding whether a line stays?
Delete the line and ask whether the reader would miss anything. If nothing is missing, it never goes back.
Listening 2

What He Archives Before the Second Line

A vice president records a note for his team about his own inbox. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. Where and when does he say he reads most of his messages?
On his phone, standing in the kitchen before six, before anybody in the office is awake.
2. What does he say makes him archive a message without answering it?
When two lines go by and he still does not know what is being asked of him.
3. What does he ask his team to do about the number of questions in one email?
To ask one thing, because two questions in one message get one answer, and it is always the easy one.
The Real Thing

Friday, 08:12

NEW MESSAGE
To: Marcus Hale <m.hale@example.com>
Subject: Follow-up — AI safety role, Thursday interview

Marcus,

You said the first year would be judged on how fast the review cycle moves. That is the part I kept thinking about on the drive home.

The bottom line: I would take the role, and I could start in six weeks.

Two numbers from Thursday, in case they help. The platform replacement I described ran for nineteen months and cut the release cycle from six weeks to nine days. The failed program you asked about cost us two quarters, and we told the client late — that one is on me, and it is why I now write the risk down in week one.

Could you let me know whether the peer conversation changes the timeline on your side?

Best regards,
Lucia Nishiyama Serra

1. Which sentence is the bottom line, and where is it?
"I would take the role, and I could start in six weeks" — third line, before any evidence.
2. Find one noun that packages an event, and one verb that keeps the actor.
The noun: "the platform replacement". The verb: "we told the client late" — with "that one is on me".
3. How many things does the email ask for?
One: whether the peer conversation changes the timeline. Compensation is not in this message at all.
