Yes, But — the Language of Concession

An economic policy nobody defends entirely, and the words that let you argue about it honestly. Key expressions: a trade-off, unintended consequences, to bear the brunt of, a false economy, to roll out, a stopgap, means-tested, across the board, to claw back, self-defeating, politically untenable, a windfall, to scale back, to be phased in, to offset, regressive, ring-fenced, a blunt instrument. Structure: concessive discourse markers — granted, admittedly, that said, even so, for all, albeit, much as.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

A trade-off -- a loss you accept in order to gain something else
"Every policy in this area is a trade-off, and pretending otherwise is dishonest."
Unintended consequences -- results nobody wanted and nobody predicted
"The unintended consequences appeared in the rental market within a year."
To bear the brunt of -- to suffer the worst part of something
"Small towns bore the brunt of it, and they were not consulted."
A false economy -- a saving that costs you more later than it saved you now
"Cutting the inspections was a false economy and everybody knew it."
To roll out -- to introduce something gradually across a whole system
"They rolled it out in four regions before going national."
A stopgap -- a temporary solution meant to last until a real one arrives
"It was designed as a stopgap and it has been running for eleven years."
Means-tested -- given only to people below a certain level of income
"Once it is means-tested, half the eligible people never apply."
Across the board -- applying to everybody equally, with no distinctions
"An across-the-board cut is simple to explain and hard to defend."
To claw back -- to recover money that has already been paid out
"The treasury clawed back most of it through the tax code."
Self-defeating -- undermining the very purpose it was created to serve
"A rule that complicated is self-defeating, however good the intention."
Politically untenable -- impossible to defend in public, whatever the merits
"The economics were sound and the position was politically untenable."
A windfall -- an unexpected gain that nobody worked for
"For the largest firms it was a windfall, and that was not the plan."
To scale back -- to reduce the size or the ambition of something
"They scaled it back twice and then quietly stopped mentioning it."
To be phased in -- to be introduced in stages over a period of time
"It is being phased in over six years, which is politically convenient."
To offset -- to balance a cost in one place against a gain in another
"The rebate was meant to offset the increase, and it did, partly."
Regressive -- hitting those with least the hardest, in proportion to what they have
"Any flat charge is regressive, whatever else it is."
Ring-fenced -- protected so that the money cannot be spent on anything else
"The fund is ring-fenced, at least on paper."
A blunt instrument -- a measure too crude for the problem it is aimed at
"Interest rates are a blunt instrument, and everybody says so."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Granted, the first two years were expensive, and the people who said so at the time were right. Admittedly, nobody predicted what would happen in the rental market, and the unintended consequences there took another year to measure properly. Anybody defending the scheme who begins somewhere else is not arguing in good faith.

That said, the case for it survives all of that. It was rolled out in four deliberately different regions before anybody committed to more, and it is now being phased in nationally over six years — a period which is, admittedly, a political number rather than an economic one. Waiting times fell. That is measured, and it is not a rounding error.

The strongest objection is not the cost. It is that the design is regressive at the bottom of the scale, so that the households with least bear the brunt of it in proportion to what they have. The rebate was built to offset that, and it does, mostly. Even so, mostly is doing work in that sentence, and a scheme that needs a second scheme to correct it is halfway to being self-defeating.

The honest summary is a trade-off. For all its faults it did what it was built to do, albeit slowly and at a cost that a means-tested version would have avoided. Much as the critics dislike hearing it, targeting is administratively hard and politically untenable in most years, which is why the blunt instrument keeps winning. While that is not a defence of the design, it is an explanation of it, and the two are not the same thing.

1. Why does the text open with Granted and Admittedly rather than with the case in favour?
A Because the writer has changed sides.
B Because conceding the objections first makes the rest of the argument credible.
C Because the objections are the main point of the article.
2. "That said, the case for it survives all of that." What is that said doing?
A Cancelling everything written before it.
B Adding a further objection.
C Pivoting: what came before stands, and what follows is the writer's actual position.
3. "It did what it was built to do, albeit slowly." What does albeit attach to?
A A single word or short phrase, not a full clause.
B A complete sentence with its own subject and verb.
C Nothing — it is a filler.
4. What does the text mean by saying the explanation is not a defence?
A That the design cannot be explained.
B That saying why something happened is different from saying it was right.
C That the critics are dishonest.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Concession — Conceding Without Losing

GRAMMAR

Concession is a two-part structure: you hand the other side something real, and then you pivot. The pivot is what stops the concession from being a surrender, and English marks it with a very small number of words.

FormWhat it doesExample
Granted · Admittedly
True enough · Of course		Concede BEFORE the objection is raised. It removes the other side's best line and tells the room you have already thought about it."Granted, the first two years were expensive."
That said · Even so
All the same · Still		The pivot. Everything before it stands; everything after it is what you actually claim. Without this, a concession is just agreement."That said, the alternative was worse."
although / though / while
+ CLAUSE		A subject and a verb follow. Though can also come at the end of a sentence in speech, where it is very natural."Although it is expensive, I would keep it."
"It is expensive. I would keep it, though."
despite / in spite of
+ NOUN		No verb follows. If you need a verb, English forces despite the fact that — which is heavy, and usually a sign you wanted although."Despite the cost, I would keep it."
NOT "Despite it is expensive"
for all + noun
albeit + word
much as + clause		The compressed forms. For all concedes a whole category; albeit qualifies a word; much as concedes a feeling. All three sound considerably more fluent than they are difficult."For all its faults…"
"It worked, albeit slowly."
"Much as I dislike it…"

The single most common error above B1: pairing although with but. Portuguese allows the pair; English uses one or the other and never both. Although it is expensive, I would keep it — or It is expensive, but I would keep it. Never the two together.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are arguing about a policy you support with reservations. Choose the sentence that concedes without surrendering.

You want to concede and then hold your position:
A "Although it is expensive, but I would keep it."
B "Granted, it is expensive. That said, I would keep it."
C "It is expensive, although but I would keep it."
You want to use despite correctly:
A "Despite it is expensive, I would keep it."
B "Despite the cost, I would keep it."
C "Despite that it is expensive, I would keep it."
You want to concede a whole category of faults in two words:
A "For all its faults, it reduced waiting times."
B "For all of it faults, it reduced waiting times."
C "By all its faults, it reduced waiting times."
You want to qualify a single word:
A "It worked, albeit it was slow."
B "It worked, albeit slowly."
C "It worked, albeit that slowly."
Somebody says the objection settles the argument. You concede it and refuse the conclusion:
A "You are splitting hairs."
B "That is real and measurable. Even so, it does not settle it, because the alternative was worse in the same direction."
C "Despite it is real, it is not important."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Take a policy you support with reservations — economic, social, environmental, anything except your own field. In about ninety seconds, argue for it the way somebody honest would: concede the strongest objection FIRST, name what is genuinely real about it, pivot, and finish by saying what evidence would make you change your mind. Use at least three of today's markers. The test: would an opponent listening feel that their best point had been heard?
