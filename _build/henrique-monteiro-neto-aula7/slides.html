Chapter 3: The Code

Agreeing With Half of It

The words that let you concede and still hold the line

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Granted, the first two years were expensive."

"Admittedly, nobody predicted the effect on rents."

"It is a blunt instrument. That said, the alternative was worse."

"The scheme is expensive. Even so, I would keep it."

In each of these the speaker agrees with something. What happens immediately afterwards — and why would you ever hand the other side a point on purpose?

FormWhat it doesExample
Granted · Admittedly
· True enough		Opens by conceding, before the objection is even made. It removes the other side's best line and signals that you have thought about it."Granted, the first two years were expensive."
That said · Even so
· All the same		The pivot. Everything before it stands; everything after it is what you actually think. This is the hinge of the whole structure."That said, the alternative was worse."
while / whereas
· much as		Concession inside a single sentence rather than across two. Much as is the most elegant and the least used."While the cost is real, it is not the objection I would make."
"Much as I dislike it, it works."
for all + noun
· albeit		Compressed concession. For all its faults concedes a whole category at once; albeit attaches a qualification to a word rather than a clause."For all its faults, it reduced waiting times."
"It worked, albeit slowly."
The trap:
although … but		English uses ONE of the two, never both. Portuguese allows the pair, and this is the commonest concession error at every level above B1.NOT "Although it is expensive, but I would keep it."

Conceding is not weakness and it is not politeness. It is the move that makes the rest of your argument credible: the listener stops waiting for you to be unreasonable, and starts listening for what you actually claim.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Although the scheme is expensive, but I would keep it."
"Although the scheme is expensive, I would keep it."
"Despite it is expensive, I would keep it."
"Despite the cost, I would keep it."
"In spite of the scheme was rolled out slowly, it worked."
"In spite of the slow rollout, it worked."

One rule underneath all three. Although and while introduce a CLAUSE, so a subject and a verb follow. Despite and in spite of introduce a NOUN, so no verb follows — and if you want a verb, English makes you say despite the fact that. And never pair although with but: one concession marker per sentence.

Chapter 4: The Policy Nobody Defends Entirely

The Economist Who Has Doubts

She has defended the policy for six years, and she can list its costs faster than you can

Dialogue

Defending What You Half Believe

A
People assume that because I defend the scheme in public I think it is perfect. I have spent six years listing its faults in private, which is a strange way to live.
H
Then start with the strongest objection. Not the one you find easiest to answer.
A
Fair. The strongest is that it is regressive at the bottom of the scale. Granted. That is real, it is measurable, and my side tends to change the subject when it comes up.
H
And your answer to it?
A
That the rebate offsets most of it, though not all, and that the alternative on the table was worse in the same direction. Both of those are defensible. Neither of them makes the objection go away.
H
Admittedly that is more honest than most of what I read about it. Although I notice you have not said whether you would vote for it.
A
I would, and with reservations I would say out loud. For all its faults it did the thing it was built to do. That said, if you asked me to design it again, I would not design that.
H
Which is a sentence I wish I heard more often from people who are paid to be certain.
Comprehension

About Alice

1. What does Alice say people assume about her?
That because she defends the scheme in public she thinks it is perfect.
2. What does she say is the strongest objection?
That the scheme is regressive at the bottom of the scale.
3. What does she say her own side tends to do about that objection?
Change the subject when it comes up.
4. Would she vote for it, and with what qualification?
Yes, with reservations she would say out loud — and she would not design it that way again.
Listening 1

Four Regions, Six Years

A briefing on how the scheme was introduced. Sound first — no text.

1. How was the scheme introduced?
Rolled out in four regions first, then phased in nationally over six years.
2. What happened in the rental market?
An unintended consequence appeared within the first year.
3. What did the treasury do about the largest firms?

Listening 2

The Case Against

A critic, arguing carefully. Sound first — no text.

1. What does the critic concede at the start?
That waiting times fell, and that the fall is real and measurable.
2. What is his central objection?
That it is a blunt instrument, and that a targeted scheme would have cost less.
3. What does he say he is not arguing?
That the policy was made in bad faith.
