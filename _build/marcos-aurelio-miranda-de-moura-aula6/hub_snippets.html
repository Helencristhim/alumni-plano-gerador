How Does It Compare? -- Bigger, or Better?

Two segments, one budget. One of them is four times bigger and the other one makes more money. Key words: a margin, a segment, volume, a benchmark, coverage, a gap, to narrow, to outperform, efficient, a ranking. Structure: comparatives and superlatives.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A margin -- what is left from the price after the cost is taken out
"The workshop segment is small, and it carries the best margin we have."
A segment -- one part of the market, made of customers who behave in the same way
"Two segments, one budget, and they do not look alike on any line."
Volume -- how much you sell, counted in units or litres, and not in money
"The distributor segment moves four times the volume, and always has."
A benchmark -- a number from outside the company that you measure yourself against
"The benchmark is uncomfortable. Our competitor reaches twice as many workshops."
Coverage -- the part of the shops or workshops your product actually reaches
"We reach fewer than one workshop in five, so coverage is the reason."
A gap -- the distance between where you are and where you want to be
"The gap has been getting wider, not narrower, for three years."
To narrow -- to make the distance between two numbers smaller
"Two years of coverage would narrow the gap. Nothing else would."
To outperform -- to do better than somebody else over the same period
"They outperformed us in that segment for three years in a row."
Efficient -- getting the same result with less money, less time or fewer people
"The most efficient use of the budget is not the segment where we are strongest."
A ranking -- the list of companies in order, from the biggest to the smallest
"In the distributor ranking we are second. In the workshop ranking we are fifth."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two segments, one budget, and every sentence that decides between them is a comparison. Start with the short adjectives, because they take an ending: "The distributor segment is bigger than the workshop segment." Bigger, smaller, faster, higher, lower — one or two syllables, add er, and follow it with than. Never than replaced by that, and never of.

Long adjectives refuse the ending and take a word in front instead: "The workshop segment is more profitable than the distributor segment." More efficient, more competitive, more expensive. The one thing English will not accept is both at once. More bigger and more better are the two most common mistakes a Portuguese speaker makes here, and they happen for a good reason: in Portuguese the word for more comes along every time.

When you compare not two things but all of them, the word the appears and the ending changes: "First sales carry the best margin we have." "That is the most efficient use of the budget." Short adjective, the plus est; long adjective, the most. Three adjectives are irregular and you simply learn them: good becomes better and the best, bad becomes worse and the worst, far becomes further. So: our coverage in workshops is worse than in distributors, the gap has not narrowed, and our competitor has outperformed us in that ranking for three years. Every one of those is a comparison, and none of them is an argument until you add the last sentence: so this is where I would put the money.

1. Why is it "bigger than" and not "more big than"?
A Because big is an irregular adjective.
B Because short adjectives take the ending, and long ones take more.
C Because the sentence compares two things and not all of them.
2. What is wrong with "more better"?
A Better is already the comparative form, so more cannot come with it.
B Better can only be used about people.
C Nothing. It is correct in a formal register.
3. What does the word "the" tell you in "the most efficient use of the budget"?
A That two options are being compared.
B That the sentence is about the past.
C That all the options are being compared, not just two.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Short adjectives take the ending, long adjectives take more, and the marks that you are comparing everything.

JobFormExample
Two things, short adjectiveadjective + er + thanbigger than · higher than · lower than
Two things, long adjectivemore + adjective + thanmore profitable than · more efficient than
All of them, short adjectivethe + adjective + estthe highest · the biggest
All of them, long adjectivethe most + adjectivethe most efficient · the most competitive
Irregulargood, bad, fargood → better → the best · bad → worse → the worst
Spelling-y becomes -ier · short words double the last lettereasier · bigger · wider
Saying how much biggerfour times bigger · twice as many · far moreIt moves four times the volume.

Never: more bigger than the workshops · more better than here · lower that our competitor · most efficient use of the budget · the more profitable of them all.

Stage 2: Put the Comparison in Order

Order

The board asks you to compare the two segments. Put your two minutes in a logical order.

?"Three numbers, and then a recommendation."
?"The distributor segment is about four times bigger in volume."
?"The workshop segment is more profitable, and the gap is getting wider."
?"Our competitor reaches nearly twice as many workshops as we do."
?"We are second in one ranking and fifth in the other."
?"So the most efficient use of the budget is the workshop segment."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The board asks which segment is bigger. You answer:
A "The distributors, they are more bigger in volume."
B "The distributor segment. It is about four times bigger in volume."
C "The distributors have a volume more big that the workshops."
You want to say the smaller segment earns more. You say:
A "The workshop segment is more profitable than the distributor segment."
B "The workshop segment is more profitabler."
C "The workshop segment has more profit that the other."
You want to say one number is the highest of all. You say:
A "First sales carry more best margin."
B "First sales carry best margin we have."
C "First sales carry the best margin we have."
You have finished comparing and the room is waiting. You say:
A "Those are the numbers. What do you think?"
B "So the most efficient use of the budget is the workshop segment."
C "Both segments are important for the company."
