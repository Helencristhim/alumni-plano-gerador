Lesson 08 -- Pre-class

If I Were Running the Session

Ask somebody what they did wrong and they defend themselves. Ask what they would do differently and they tell you the truth. Key words: a constraint, to be stuck with something, leverage, to swap something, from scratch, a compromise, realistically, in hindsight, to be worth it, a clean slate. Structure: the past form here measures distance from reality, not distance in time.

To swap something -- to replace one thing with another of the same kind
"If I could swap one thing, it would be the order of the week, not the equipment."
From scratch -- starting again from nothing, with no part of the old version
"Everybody says they would start from scratch, and almost nobody means it."
A compromise -- a solution where both sides give something up
"Every useful compromise started as somebody describing the better version clearly."
Realistically -- used before saying what would actually happen, not what should
"Realistically, nobody gets a clean slate, and the budget does not change."
In hindsight -- looking back, now that you know how it turned out
"In hindsight, I would not have argued about it."
To be worth it -- to give enough back to justify what it costs
"Four hours in the right place would be worth it. Two days would not happen."
A clean slate -- a situation with no history and no old decisions attached to it
"If you had a clean slate and the same budget, what would you do differently?"

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is one question worth asking anybody who has done a job for five years: if you had a clean slate and the same budget, what would you do differently? Almost everybody answers that they would start from scratch, and almost everybody is wrong — what they describe next is never the whole system, only two or three decisions made badly in the first month. Look at the shape of that sentence, because it is the grammar of the whole lesson: if plus a past form, then would plus the base verb. Nothing in it is about the past.

The past form here measures distance from reality, not distance in time. If I had more time, I would rehearse for two days — I do not have more time. If the budget were bigger, I would hire a second engineer — it is not. With the verb be, this structure prefers were for every subject: if I were you, if it were up to me, if the room were bigger. And the second half does not have to be would: if we swapped the order of the week, we might lose a day of editing.

Two warnings, and one of them is the reason this lesson exists. First: would never appears in the if half. Not if I would have more time — ever. Second: do not confuse this with last week. If the track drops out, I will patch it is real and likely, tonight. If I ran the show, I would change it is imaginary. Same word, two different distances.

And then the useful part. Realistically, nobody gets a clean slate, you are stuck with old decisions, and the constraints do not move. What the hypothetical buys you is leverage: once you can describe the better version in two sentences, you can trade for a piece of it. You will not get two days. You might get four hours, and if they are the right four hours it is worth it. Every useful compromise started that way. In hindsight, the people who got what they wanted were never the ones who complained loudest — they were the ones who could say precisely what they would do if it were up to them.

1. "If I had more time, I would rehearse for two days." Is this sentence about the past?
A Yes -- "had" is a past form, so the sentence is about something that already happened.
B No -- the past form measures distance from reality, not distance in time.
C Only if the speaker used to have more time in the past.
2. What is the difference between "if the track drops out, I will patch it" and "if I ran the show, I would change it"?
A The first is real and likely; the second is imaginary.
B The first is about work and the second is about opinions.
C They mean the same thing; the second is just more polite.
3. According to the text, what does the hypothetical version actually buy you?
A A clean slate, if you ask for it clearly enough.
B The right to complain about the constraints.
C Leverage: once you can describe the better version precisely, you can trade for a piece of it.

You have described the perfect version, and somebody asks what you would actually settle for. You say:
A "Nothing less than two days, or it is not worth to do."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Take a real season or project. Name two constraints you cannot change and two decisions you are stuck with. Describe the ideal version in two sentences. Then name the affordable half, with a number, and say why it would be worth it. Finish with one thing you would have done differently.
