One Question

Note: fill this in alone before the meeting. In the meeting, only the Affordable half line is negotiable, and it is the only line anybody will argue about.
1. What is the difference between the Constraints line and the Decisions line?
The constraints cannot be changed by anybody: the studio size, the slot, the delivery day. The decisions could have been made differently, and were not, so now the team is stuck with them.
2. Why does the page ask for an Ideal and an Affordable half, instead of just one plan?
Because the ideal is what makes the thinking precise, and the affordable half is what can actually be traded for. Without the ideal you do not know which four hours matter; without the affordable half you get nothing.
3. According to the note, which line will people argue about, and why fill the page in alone first?
Only the Affordable half is negotiable, so that is the line people argue about. Filling it in alone first means you arrive with the ideal already thought through, instead of inventing it under pressure.
Chapter 4: The Code

A Past Form That Is Not About the Past

The distance is not in time. It is in probability.

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"If I had a clean slate, I would change the order of the week."

"If the budget were bigger, I would hire a second engineer."

"If I were you, I would ask before Friday."

"If we rehearsed for two days, we might lose a day of editing."

The red verbs are all in a past form, and not one of these sentences is about the past. The blue half never appears on the other side of the comma.

JobFormExample
Imaginary or unlikely situation, nowIf + past form, would + base verbIf I had a clean slate, I would change the order of the week.
The verb bewere with every subjectIf I were you. If the budget were bigger. If it were up to me.
Giving adviceIf I were you, I would ...If I were you, I would ask before Friday.
A less confident resultmight / could instead of wouldIf we rehearsed for two days, we might lose a day of editing.
Negativedid not + base verb · would notIf we did not have the slot, we would not need Thursday at all.
First or second?real and likely · imaginary or unlikelyIf the track drops out, I will patch it. (tonight, real) · If I ran the show, I would change it. (imaginary)
The trapnever would in the if halfNot if I would have more time. Say if I had more time.

In one line: the past form here measures distance from reality, not distance in time.

Listening 1 · The Question at the End

Not What Went Wrong. What Would You Do Differently.

Nine years of asking the same question after every project. Sound first — no text.

1. What question does she NOT ask, and what does she ask instead?
Not what went wrong, because that makes people defend themselves and you learn nothing. Instead: if you did this again next month, with the same money, what would you do differently?
2. What kind of answer does she almost never get, and what do people say instead?
Almost nobody says they would change the big thing. They say they would move one meeting, or that they would have brought the musical director into the room in week one instead of week three.
3. What surprised her for the first three years?
That twenty answers across twenty projects do not give you twenty problems. You get the same four, described by different people who all think they are the only one who noticed.
Listening 2 · The Cheapest Half

Now What Is the Cheapest Half of That?

The trap inside the hypothetical, named by somebody who fell into it for years. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the trap he describes, and what three examples does he give of it?
That describing the perfect version is very satisfying and useless on its own. His examples: if I had three more days, if the room were bigger, if somebody else had made that decision in 2019.
2. What was the one extra sentence he learned, and who taught it to him?
"Fine, now what is the cheapest half of that?" -- from somebody who ran a studio for thirty years and would listen to the whole description first.
3. What is the difference he draws at the end between two reasons for describing the perfect version?
Describe it so that you can find the affordable piece of it -- not so that you can be right about what you were never going to get.
Chapter 6: Say It Back

The Same Version, Three Times

Guided > Semi-free > Free

Role-Play 1 of 3 — Guided

If You Did This Again Next Month

Scenario: Same money, same people, next month. Answer three questions: what you would change first, what you would not touch, and what you would have done differently in hindsight. Every answer is a full conditional.

Keyword chips:

Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Now the Cheapest Half

Scenario: You have just described the perfect version. Now somebody asks for the cheapest half of it. Name a specific number and a specific place in the week, say what it would remove, and say why it would be worth it.

Keywords:

four hours before the key list is locked it would remove the bottleneck it would be worth it
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Your Retrospective, No Notes

Scenario: Take a real season or project. Name two constraints you cannot change and two decisions you are stuck with. Describe the ideal version in two sentences. Then name the affordable half, with a number, and say why it would be worth it.

No keywords, no notes, two minutes. Finish with one thing you would have done differently.
