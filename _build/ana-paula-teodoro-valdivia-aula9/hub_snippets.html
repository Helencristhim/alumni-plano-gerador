Phrasal Verbs in Action -- When Contracts Fall Apart or Come Together

The whole life of a deal, told the way people actually say it: a draft is drawn up, obligations are carried out, open points are ironed out, the term rolls over, somebody backs out, the deal falls through, or a third party takes over. Key verbs: draw up, carry out, back out, fall through, take over, hold up, spell out, iron out, break down, phase out, opt out, roll over. Structure: separable and inseparable phrasal verbs -- where the object stands, and why a pronoun has no choice.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each verb and read the definition. Together these twelve tell the whole life of an agreement -- the goal is to say them out loud, with the stress on the second word.

Draw up -- to prepare and write a formal document
"Their counsel drew up the first version in March."
Carry out -- to do what a plan or an obligation requires
"We carried out every obligation on our side."
Back out -- to leave an agreement before it is finished
"The supplier backed out three days before signing."
Fall through -- to fail before it is completed
"The whole deal fell through in April."
Take over -- to gain control of a company or a role from someone else
"A German group took over the contract."
Hold up -- to delay something or stop it from moving forward
"One clause held up the signature for a month."
Spell out -- to explain something in full detail, leaving no doubt
"The annex spells out who pays for what."
Iron out -- to solve the small problems that are still open
"There are two points left to iron out."
Break down -- to stop working, or to separate something into parts
"The talks broke down over the penalty."
Phase out -- to end something slowly, in stages
"They are phasing out the old pricing model."
Opt out -- to choose not to take part in something
"Either party can opt out of the renewal."
Roll over -- to continue automatically into a new period
"The term rolls over for another year."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Ana has to tell a manager what happened to a contract, and she tells it as a story. Their counsel drew up the first version in March. In fact, they drew it up in one weekend -- and never drew up it, because a pronoun always stands in the middle. Ana's team carried out its part in April. Then one liability clause held the signature up, and the same clause held it up for two more months while both sides were still ironing out the wording. The term rolled over by itself, so the old prices stayed. In the end the supplier backed out of the deal -- never backed the deal out of, because that verb does not open -- and the whole thing fell through. A competitor has offered to take over the same scope, and this time Ana will spell out the liability cap in the first draft. She will spell it out before anybody signs.

1. The text says "they drew it up", never "they drew up it". Why?
A Because "draw up" can never be separated.
B Because "draw up" is separable, and with a pronoun the object must go in the middle.
C Because "it" is not a real object.
2. Both "held the signature up" and "held it up" appear. What does that show?
A That "hold up" changes meaning depending on the position.
B That the writer made a mistake in one of them.
C That with a noun both positions are possible, and with a pronoun only the middle is.
3. Why is "backed the deal out of" impossible?
A "Back out of" is inseparable, so the object always comes after the whole block.
B "Deal" is too long to stand in the middle.
C It is possible, but only in writing.
4. "Fell through" and "rolled over" have no object in the text. Why not?
A The object was left out to make the text shorter.
B These verbs take no object at all -- the thing itself falls through or rolls over.
C The object always comes in the following sentence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Separable and Inseparable Phrasal Verbs

GRAMMAR

One question decides everything: is there an object, and is it a noun or a pronoun?

TypeRuleExample
Separable + nounBoth positions work. It is your choice, and neither is more formal.They drew up the contract = they drew the contract up.
Separable + pronounThe middle stops being optional. The pronoun has to stand there.They drew it up. We ironed them out. I will spell it out.
InseparableThe block never opens. Everything comes after it, noun or pronoun.They backed out of the deal. She opted out of it.
No object at allNothing goes anywhere. The subject does the whole thing by itself.The deal fell through. The talks broke down. The term rolled over.
Today's twelveSeparable: draw up · carry out · take over · hold up · spell out · iron out · phase out. Inseparable or alone: back out (of) · opt out (of) · fall through · break down · roll over.
Four trapsNever "draw up it" or "iron out it" -- a pronoun never stands after the particle. Never open an inseparable verb: "back the deal out of" does not exist. Never translate the particle from your first language ("depend of", "consist in") -- it is learned with the verb, in one block. And never speak a whole call in Latin verbs (execute, terminate, postpone, resolve) -- it is correct, and it sounds like the document reading itself out loud.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

The manager asks who prepared the first version. You answer:
A "Their counsel drew up it in March."
B "Their counsel drew it up in March."
C "The document was executed by their counsel in the month of March."
The supplier has left the agreement before signing. On a call you say:
A "The supplier backed the deal out of."
B "The agreement was unilaterally terminated by the supplier."
C "The supplier backed out, so the deal fell through."
Two small points are still open and you want to say you will solve them. Which is best?
A "There are two points left, and we will iron them out this week."
B "There are two points left, and we will iron out them this week."
C "Two outstanding points remain pending resolution."
Nobody sent a notice, so the contract continued by itself for another year. You say:
A "The term was renewed by operation of the automatic renewal provision."
B "Nobody opted out, so the term rolled over for another year."
C "The term rolled the year over again."
You want the liability cap explained in full detail in the next draft. You say:
A "Please spell out it in the next draft."
B "Please spell the cap out in the next draft, so nobody has to guess."
C "Please proceed to the detailed specification of the said cap."
