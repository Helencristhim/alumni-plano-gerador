Lesson 17 -- Pre-class

Presenting to the Board

Five minutes in front of the chair of the board, one request, and the questions that come after it. Key expressions: a slide deck, to set the scene, to field a question, a hostile question, to buy time, to get to the point, to make the case for, buy-in, a return on investment, to rein in, to stand by, on the spot, to drive home, a mandate. Structure: cleft sentences for emphasis -- what we need is, it was... that, all I am asking for is.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to a presentation to a board: the slides, the request and the hard questions that follow it.

A slide deck -- a set of slides prepared for a presentation
"I cut the slide deck from forty pages to seven."
To set the scene -- to give the background a listener needs before the main point
"Take one minute to set the scene, then go straight to the request."
To field a question -- to deal with a question, especially a difficult one, as it comes
"She can field a question from the CFO without looking at her notes."
A hostile question -- a question asked to attack or challenge you, not to get information
"Every board has one member who saves a hostile question for the end."
To buy time -- to delay something so that you have more time to think or act
"Saying that is a good question is a polite way to buy time."
To get to the point -- to say the important thing directly, without a long introduction
"The board has ten minutes, so get to the point in the first sentence."
To make the case for -- to give the reasons why something should be done or approved
"You have five minutes to make the case for the new system."
Buy-in -- agreement and active support from the people who must approve or act
"Without buy-in from the CFO, the proposal will not survive the meeting."
A return on investment -- the benefit you get compared with the money you spend
"What the board wants to see is a return on investment, not a list of features."
To rein in -- to control or limit something, especially costs
"The new process will help us rein in the cost of outside counsel."
To stand by -- to keep supporting something you said or decided, even under pressure
"I stand by the number, even if it is higher than you expected."
On the spot -- immediately, without time to prepare, and often under pressure
"A director asked me for the exact figure on the spot."
To drive home -- to make a point so clear that nobody can forget it
"Use one number to drive home the size of the risk."
A mandate -- the official authority to do something, given to you by somebody else
"The committee gave us a clear mandate to review every supplier."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In a meeting in Portuguese, you stress the important part with your voice, or by moving the words around. In English, especially in a presentation, speakers often build the stress into the sentence itself. They split one sentence into two parts and push the important part to the end: We need a mandate becomes What we need is a mandate. This is called a cleft sentence.

The what-cleft makes the listener wait for the focus. The it-cleft does something different: it points at one thing and excludes the others. It was the near miss in March that changed my mind means: not the audit, not the cost, the near miss. Use who instead of that when the focus is a person: It is the CFO who has to approve it.

Two more patterns are useful in front of a board. All I am asking for is six months makes a request sound small and reasonable, because all means the only thing. And when the focus is an action, use do: What we did was review every contract, with the base form of the verb after was and no second subject.

A warning: a cleft is a spotlight. In a five-minute presentation, use two or three, for the sentences you want the board to repeat after you leave. Use one in every sentence and nothing stands out.

1. What does a what-cleft do with the important part of the sentence?
A It moves it to the beginning, before the verb.
B It pushes it to the end, where the listener is waiting for it.
C It removes it from the sentence so that the board has to ask about it in the questions.
2. What does It was the near miss that changed my mind suggest?
A That the near miss, and not something else, changed his mind.
B That the near miss changed his mind only a little.
C That several different things, including the audit and the cost, changed his mind at the same time.
3. Why does a speaker say All I am asking for is six months?
A To make the request sound bigger and more urgent than it really is in practice.
B To make the request sound small and reasonable.
C To show that the request is not important.
4. According to the text, how many cleft sentences should a five-minute presentation have?
A Two or three, for the key sentences.
B One in every sentence.
C None at all, because cleft sentences sound too informal for a board of directors.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences for Emphasis

GRAMMAR

Your first language builds emphasis with the voice and with word order. English can do that too, but in a presentation it usually builds the emphasis into the structure of the sentence.

PatternWhen it happensExample
What + clause + be + FOCUSThe new, important part goes at the END."What we need is a mandate."
It + be + FOCUS + that / whoPoints at ONE element and excludes the others."It was the near miss that changed my mind."
All + clause + beAll = the only thing. The request sounds small."All I am asking for is six months."
The reason / thing + clause + beLike the what-cleft, with a more specific noun."The thing that worries me is the timing."
What + did + was + verbAn ACTION in focus: base form after was, no second subject."What we did was review every contract."

One habit for this week: before any meeting where you need a yes, write your request as a what-cleft and your main reason as an it-cleft. Say both out loud twice. Those two sentences are your whole presentation; everything else is detail.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are in front of a board, or preparing to be. Choose the version a professional would actually use.

1. You open your presentation with the request:
A What we are asking for it is a mandate to screen every supplier.
B What we are asking for is a mandate to screen every supplier.
C That what we are asking for is a mandate to screen every supplier.
2. You want to say that the near miss, and nothing else, changed your mind:
A It was the near miss in March that changed my mind.
B It were the near miss in March what changed my mind.
C Was the near miss in March that it changed my mind, more than the audit.
3. You want your request to sound small and reasonable:
A All what I am asking for is a six-month pilot.
B All I am asking for is a six-month pilot.
C All I am asking it is a six-month pilot.
4. A director asks a hostile question and you need a moment:
A That is a fair question. What I can tell you is that the number comes from our own audit.
B I do not know, and I would prefer not to answer that question in front of the whole board today.
C Your question is wrong.
5. You describe the action your team took:
A What we did was we reviewed every contract.
B What we did was review every contract.
C What we did it was to reviewing every contract.

Reflection
