The Extent to Which — Taking an Argument Apart

A dense opinion piece, four studies and the single unexamined sentence the whole thing rests on. Key expressions: a premise, an inference, it does not follow, a corollary, to beg the question, a counterexample, to rest on, a leap, self-evident, an assumption, to take for granted, to generalize from, a false dichotomy, anecdotal, to be a stretch, watertight, a blind alley, to unpack. Structure: advanced relative clauses — the extent to which, by which, none of which, many of whom, whereby, in which case.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

A premise -- a statement the argument assumes and builds on
"Accept the premise and the conclusion is unavoidable, which is why the premise is the target."
An inference -- a conclusion drawn from evidence rather than stated by it
"That is an inference, and a reasonable one, and it is not a finding."
It does not follow -- the conclusion is not supported by what came before
"Both facts are true. It does not follow that one caused the other."
A corollary -- something that follows automatically if the main claim is true
"The corollary is uncomfortable and nobody in the piece mentions it."
To beg the question -- to assume in the argument the very thing you set out to prove
"Calling it a failure begs the question, since that is what we are arguing about."
A counterexample -- a single case that disproves a general claim
"One counterexample is enough, and there are four."
To rest on -- to depend on something for its whole support
"The entire case rests on a definition given in a footnote."
A leap -- a step in the reasoning with nothing underneath it
"Paragraph six is a leap, and it is dressed as a summary."
Self-evident -- presented as needing no argument at all
"Nothing in this field is self-evident, and anything called self-evident is worth checking."
An assumption -- something taken as true without being argued for
"There are three assumptions and only one of them is defended."
To take for granted -- to treat as settled without noticing that you have
"He takes for granted exactly what his critics dispute."
To generalize from -- to draw a wide conclusion from a narrow case
"You cannot generalize from eleven interviews in one city."
A false dichotomy -- presenting two options as though there were no others
"Either growth or fairness is a false dichotomy and it has survived forty years."
Anecdotal -- based on individual stories rather than on systematic evidence
"The opening is anecdotal, which is fine, and the conclusion is not."
To be a stretch -- to go further than the evidence comfortably allows
"Calling that a trend is a stretch, and he almost says so himself."
Watertight -- with no gap anywhere that an objection could get through
"The first section is watertight. That is why the second one works on you."
A blind alley -- a line of enquiry that leads nowhere
"Two of the four studies went down the same blind alley."
To unpack -- to take a compressed claim apart and examine the pieces
"Unpack that sentence and there are three separate claims inside it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The article is a good one, which is what makes it worth taking apart. It cites four studies, its first two sections are watertight, and its author is somebody whose earlier work is widely quoted. That is precisely the difficulty: a strong opening earns the trust that a weak section later spends. Read the beginning generously and the middle suspiciously.

The case rests on a definition given in a footnote, and the extent to which everything depends on that footnote is never acknowledged. Four studies are cited, none of which uses the same definition as the other three, and two of which were designed to answer a different question. That is not dishonesty; it is what happens when a writer knows a field well enough to stop noticing that its terms are contested. The premise is taken for granted rather than defended, and everything after it inherits the problem.

The central move is in paragraph six, where two facts are established, both of them true, and the second is then treated as a consequence of the first. It does not follow. There is a mechanism by which it might, and none is proposed; there is a counterexample from the same decade, and it is not mentioned. Unpack the sentence and it contains three separate claims, of which only the first is supported. Calling the result a trend is a stretch, and the author very nearly says so himself.

None of this makes the conclusion wrong. It may well be right, in which case somebody will have to argue for it properly. What the piece does is present as demonstrated what remains, at best, a reasonable inference — and it does so in a register so calm that disagreeing feels like pedantry. The corollary, which the author does not draw, is considerably harder to defend than the conclusion he does draw.

1. "The extent to which everything depends on that footnote is never acknowledged." What is this structure doing?
A Turning a question of degree into something the sentence can be about.
B Asking a question.
C Introducing a condition.
2. "Four studies are cited, none of which uses the same definition." Why not none of them?
A Because them would leave two sentences joined only by a comma.
B Because them is informal.
C Because studies are things, not people.
3. What does the writer mean by saying a strong opening is a difficulty?
A That the first sections are badly written.
B That the trust the opening earns is spent by the weaker section later.
C That readers stop reading after the first section.
4. "It may well be right, in which case somebody will have to argue for it properly." What does this concede?
A That the conclusion could be true even though the argument fails.
B That the conclusion is certainly false.
C That nobody should write about it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Advanced Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

These structures attach a second idea to a noun without starting a new sentence. They are the backbone of analytical English — and splitting them into short sentences always loses the connection, which is what the analysis actually consists of.

FormWhat it doesExample
the extent to which
the degree to which
the way in which		Makes a question of degree into a noun phrase you can then say something about. Almost impossible to paraphrase, and the single most useful structure in this lesson."The extent to which it was representative is never discussed."
preposition + which
by / in / under / from
/ against which		The preposition goes IN FRONT of which in careful English. The informal alternative strands it at the end, which is acceptable in speech and not in writing."a method by which they can be compared"
"the conditions under which it holds"
quantifier + of which
+ of whom		Attaches a second statement to a list or a group: none / many / two / most / all of which. Use of whom for people."four studies, none of which…"
"sixty people, many of whom…"
wherebyA single word meaning by which, used almost only about systems, rules and arrangements. High register and completely standard in that narrow use."an arrangement whereby each side reviews the other"
in which case · at which
point · for which reason		Attaches a consequence to the whole of what you have just said. Common in careful speech, and it keeps an argument moving where a full stop would break it."They may refuse, in which case the timetable changes."

The error to watch for is one you will make under pressure: after a comma, English needs a RELATIVE pronoun to join the halves. Four studies, none of them used the same definition is two sentences glued together, and English does not permit that. Say none of which. The same applies to many of whom, two of which, all of whom.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are taking a written argument apart, in front of somebody who wrote it. Choose the precise version.

You want to talk about how far something is true:
A "The extent which the sample was representative is never discussed."
B "The extent to which the sample was representative is never discussed."
C "The extent of which the sample was representative is never discussed."
You want to attach a second statement to a list of studies:
A "Four studies, none of them used the same definition."
B "Four studies, none of which used the same definition."
C "Four studies, none of that used the same definition."
The same structure, but about people:
A "Sixty people, many of whom had already left."
B "Sixty people, many of which had already left."
C "Sixty people, many of them had already left."
You want the formal version of "a method they can compare them by":
A "a method by which they can be compared"
B "a method which they can be compared by it"
C "a method whereby they can be compared by"
Somebody says your objection means the conclusion is false. You disagree:
A "Yes, the conclusion is false."
B "No. It may well be right, in which case somebody will have to argue for it properly."
C "The extent to which it is false is self-evident."
