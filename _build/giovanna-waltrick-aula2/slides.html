Chapter 3: The Code

When the Story Is Over

One tense for everything that already happened

Discover the Rule

What Do These Have in Common?

We arrived in Miami at six in the morning.
The officer asked me three questions.
I went through customs with my father.
I did not understand the second question.
Click to reveal the rule
All of them are finished — they happened and they are over. That is the past simple. Most verbs take -ed: arrive → arrived, ask → asked. Some are irregular and you learn them one by one: go → went, take → took, buy → bought, say → said. Two things to watch: after did and did not the verb goes back to normal (I did not understand, never I did not understood), and the past does not change with he, she or it — she went, they went, same word.

Reading

The Line at Passport Control

What Happens at Passport Control

Every traveller who arrives in the United States goes through the same two counters, in the same order. The first one is passport control, and the second one is customs. They are not the same thing, and they do not ask the same questions.

At passport control, an immigration officer decides if you can enter the country. The officer looks at your passport and asks about you: where you are coming from, why you are here, how long you are staying, and where you are staying. Most of these conversations take less than a minute.

At customs, nobody is interested in who you are. They want to know what you are carrying. If you are bringing food, plants or large amounts of money, you have to declare it. If you are not, you say that you have nothing to declare, and you walk out.

Two things surprise first-time travellers. The first one is how short it is: four questions and a stamp. The second one is that being nervous is completely normal and does not matter. Officers talk to hundreds of nervous people every day. What they need is a clear answer, not a confident one.

The Officer's Side of It

Read the questions, then listen.

About four hundred people. Each conversation takes less than a minute.
2. What are the three things he needs to hear?
Why you are here, how long you are staying, and where you are staying.
3. What does he say about people who are nervous?
Almost everybody is nervous, and being nervous is not a problem for him.

G
Good morning. I am coming from Sao Paulo, in Brazil.
O
And what is the purpose of your trip?
G
Tourism. I am here on holiday with my family.
O
How long are you staying?
G
Two weeks. We are going back on the twenty-eighth.
O
Where are you staying while you are here?

O

Comprehension

About the Officer

1. What are the four things the officer asks?
Where she is coming from, the purpose of the trip, how long she is staying, and where she is staying.
2. Does he ask to see the hotel address?
No. She offers it, and he says it will not be necessary.
3. How does he end the conversation?
He says: Enjoy your stay.

The First Time She Did It Alone

Read the questions, then listen.

1. What did she do in the line while she was waiting?
She practised her answers in her head and wrote three words on her phone: tourism, two weeks, and the name of the hotel.
2. What went wrong, and what did she do about it?
She did not understand the third question at all. She asked him to repeat it, and he repeated it more slowly.

It is completely normal, and much better than guessing and answering the wrong thing.
