Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Been There, Done That -- Talking About What You Have Lived

A conversation about everywhere you have already been, and the one verb that lets you tell it without a date. Key words: a destination, a layover, an itinerary, departure, arrival, jet-lagged, a solo traveler, to book a trip, to miss a connection, to take off. Structure: present perfect for experience, with ever, never, already, yet, for and since — and when to go back to the past simple.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A destination -- the place a trip is going to
"Alaska has been on my list of destinations for years."
A layover -- a short stop between two flights, where you wait for the next one
"We had a six-hour layover in Seattle on the way up."
An itinerary -- the written plan of a trip, with dates, places and times
"I have never followed an itinerary all the way to the end."
Departure -- the moment a plane or a train leaves
"Our departure was at two in the morning."
Arrival -- the moment a plane or a train gets there
"Arrival was at noon, and we picked up the RV the same day."
Jet-lagged -- tired and awake at the wrong hours after a long flight
"I have been jet-lagged for four days and I am still waking up at five."
A solo traveler -- somebody who travels alone, because they want to
"She has been a solo traveler since her twenties."
To book a trip -- to pay for the flights and the rooms in advance
"We have already booked the trip for next March."
To miss a connection -- to get to the airport too late for the next flight
"I have never missed a connection, and I would like to keep it that way."
To take off -- to leave the ground, when a plane starts flying
"The plane took off two hours late."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody at the next table asks where you have been. Watch what happens to the verb. "I have been to the United States four times." Nothing there says when, and that is the whole point: the sentence is about the experience, not about the date. It is the answer to a question about your life, and your life is not finished. English keeps that open with have plus the third form of the verb — been, gone, taken, driven.

Then the other person asks the obvious thing: when? And the moment a closed date arrives, the verb changes. "I went there in July. We picked up an RV at arrival and we drove for seventeen days." July is over. The window shut. So the sentence goes back to the past form you already know from the first lesson. This is the swap that costs most speakers their credibility in thirty seconds: I have been there in July is not English. Either the date goes, or the have goes.

Three small words do most of the work. Ever opens a whole life: "Have you ever missed a connection?" Never closes it: "I have never been a solo traveler." Already says it happened sooner than you expected: "We have already booked the next trip." And for something that started in the past and is still true, two words split the job: for gives you the length — "I have worked in operations for four years" — and since gives you the point it started: "I have wanted to see Alaska since I was a teenager."

1. Why does the text say "I have been to Alaska" and not "I went to Alaska"?
A Because Alaska is far away and needs the long form.
B Because the sentence is about the experience, and nobody has said when it happened.

2. The text says "I went there in July" right after. Why does the verb change?
A Because a closed date arrived, and a closed date always takes the past form.
B Because July is a summer month.
C Because the speaker repeated the story twice.
3. What is the difference between "for two years" and "since 2024"?
A They are the same and you can use either one.
B "For" is only used in questions.
C "For" gives the length of the time, and "since" gives the point it started.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect versus Past Simple

GRAMMAR

No date, and the time is still open: have plus the third form. A closed date: the past form.

JobFormExample
An experience, no datehave / has + third formI have been to Alaska.
The same trip, with a datethe past formI went there in July.
Question about a whole lifeHave + subject + ever + third formHave you ever missed a connection?
Zero times, everhave never + third formI have never been a solo traveler.
Sooner than expectedhave already + third formWe have already booked the trip.
Not yet, but expectedhave not + third form + yetWe have not packed yet.
How long, still truefor + length · since + starting pointI have worked here for four years · since 2022.
Third forms to learn by earno rule — hear themgo → gone · take → taken · drive → driven · be → been

Never: I have been there in July · I have went to Alaska · Have you ever drove an RV? · I work here since 2022 · since four years

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Somebody at an airport asks about your travel history in general. You answer:
A "I went to the United States four times."
B "I have been to the United States four times."
C "I am going to the United States four times."
They ask when the last one was. You answer:
A "I have been there last July."
B "I have gone there in July."
C "I went there in July, three weeks ago."
You want to ask about their whole life, not one trip. You say:
A "Have you ever driven an RV?"
B "Did you ever have driven an RV?"
C "Have you ever drove an RV?"
You want to say you have worked in operations since 2022. You say:
A "I work in operations since 2022."
B "I have worked in operations since 2022."
C "I have worked in operations for 2022."
