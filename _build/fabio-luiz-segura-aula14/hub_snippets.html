Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

The gist -- the main idea of what somebody said, without the details
"I missed the numbers, but I got the gist: the paperwork is slower than the money."
An inference -- a conclusion you reach from what you heard, even though nobody said it directly
"He never said the pilot failed. That was my inference from the way he changed the subject."
To paraphrase -- to say what somebody said again, in your own words, to check it
"Let me paraphrase to make sure I'm following: you connect once, and they do the rest?"
A filler phrase -- words like you know or basically that fill time but carry no meaning
"Half of his sentence was filler phrases. The other half was one number."
A false start -- when a speaker begins a sentence, stops, and begins it again differently
"Native speakers make false starts all the time. Do not treat each one as a new idea."
To tune in -- to adjust your ear to a voice, an accent or a room until it becomes easy to follow
"By the second afternoon I had tuned in, and suddenly everybody spoke slower."
To lose the thread -- to stop following a conversation because you missed one part of it
"I stopped on one word I did not know, and I lost the thread for a whole minute."
A context clue -- a word or a structure around a new word that helps you guess what it means
"I did not know the word, but not because of the technology was a context clue."
An anchor word -- a noun or a number you already know that holds the meaning of a fast sentence
"Settlement, corridor, ninety days: three anchor words, and I had the whole sentence."
To fill in the gaps -- to complete the missing parts of something, often later, with new information
"Keep listening. Speakers repeat themselves, and they usually fill in the gaps for you."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

On the first morning of a conference, the problem is rarely that you understood nothing. It is that you understood most of it and missed one piece: a number, a name, a word of jargon. What you say next decides whether the conversation continues or changes tone.

The reflex is to say Sorry, I don't understand. It tells the other person that everything was lost, so they repeat everything, slowly, and now you are a student instead of a partner. The better move is to ask for the one piece you need, and in English the polite way to ask has its own grammar. Compare What did you mean? with Could you tell me what you meant? and When does the pilot start? with Do you know when the pilot starts? After a polite opening, the question turns back into a statement: no did, no does, and the subject comes before the verb.

For a yes-or-no question, the polite form uses if: Could you tell me if the fee includes the exchange margin? And there is a second tool that needs no question word at all. You simply paraphrase what you think you heard and let your voice go up at the end: So you're saying the rollout takes six months? The other person either says yes or fills in the gap. Either way, you are still in the conversation.

The last tool uses an anchor word, the one you caught: What was the figure you mentioned? or What was the timeline you mentioned? It shows that you got the gist and only need one piece. Nobody hears that as weakness. It sounds like somebody who was listening carefully.

1. According to the text, what is the problem with "Sorry, I don't understand"?
A It is grammatically wrong.
B It tells the other person that everything was lost, so they repeat everything slowly.
C It is too informal for a conference.
2. What happens to the question after a polite opening like "Could you tell me"?
A It turns back into a statement: no did or does, and the subject before the verb.
B It keeps did or does, but moves them to the end.
C It needs a question mark in the middle.
3. How does an echo question like "So you're saying the rollout takes six months?" work?
A It uses a question word at the start.
B It uses did before the subject.
C It is a full statement, and the voice goes up at the end.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect and Echo Questions

GRAMMAR

After a polite opening, the question turns back into a statement.

TypeFormExample
Direct questionquestion word + did / does + subject + verbWhat did you mean?
Indirect questionCould you tell me / Do you know + question word + subject + verbCould you tell me what you meant?
Indirect yes/no questionCould you tell me / Do you know + if + subject + verbDo you know if the pilot includes Brazil?
Echo questiona full statement + voice up at the endSo you're saying it takes six months?
Anchor questionWhat was the + anchor word + you mentioned?What was the figure you mentioned?
Negative formCould you tell me why + subject + doesn't / didn't + verbCould you tell me why it doesn't include Brazil?

Never: Could you tell me what did you mean · Do you know when does the pilot start · Could you tell me if do you work with banks · Sorry, I don't understand, can you repeat everything

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

At a booth, a vendor explains his product fast and you miss one number. Put the conversation in a logical order.

?"Our middleware sits in the middle, so you connect once and we do the rest."
?"Just to make sure I'm following, you connect once and they do the rest?"
?"Exactly. A typical bank goes from forty connections to one."
?"I caught most of that. What was the timeline you mentioned?"
?"About eighteen months for the full migration."
?"Could you tell me if the first corridor could be live sooner?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A vendor says something fast and you miss one number. You say:
A Sorry, I don't understand. Can you repeat everything?
B I caught most of that. What was the figure you mentioned?
C What did you said about the number?
You want to know when the pilot starts, politely. You say:
A Could you tell me when does the pilot start?
B Could you tell me when did the pilot start?
C Could you tell me when the pilot starts?
You think the speaker said the rollout takes six months, but you are not sure. You say:
A So you're saying the rollout takes six months?
B The rollout takes six months, I think, no?
C Please repeat the rollout slowly.
On the first morning, you do not know one word in a long sentence. The best thing to do is:
A Stop and think about the word until you remember it.
B Ask the speaker to stop and explain the word immediately.
C Keep listening, use the anchor words, and let the speaker fill in the gaps.
