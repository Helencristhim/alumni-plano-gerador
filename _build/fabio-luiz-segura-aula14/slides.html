Chapter 4: The Polite Question

Ask for One Piece, Not for Everything

Where the verb goes when a question gets polite

Grammar Discovery

Four Ways to Ask for the Missing Piece

"Could you tell me what you meant by reconciliation?"

"Do you know when the pilot starts?"

"So you're saying the rollout takes six months?"

"What was the figure you mentioned for the first corridor?"

In the first two, where is did or does? And the third one has no question word at all. How does the listener know it is a question?

TypeFormExample
Direct questionquestion word + did / does + subject + verbWhat did you mean?
Indirect question (polite)Could you tell me / Do you know + question word + subject + verbCould you tell me what you meant?
Indirect yes/no questionDo you know / Could you tell me + if + subject + verbDo you know if the pilot includes Brazil?
Echo question (paraphrase)a full statement + voice up at the endSo you're saying it takes six months? ↗
Anchor questionWhat was the + anchor word + you mentioned?What was the figure you mentioned?
The trapno did / does / do inside an indirect questionNot could you tell me what did you mean. Say what you meant.

In one line: after a polite opening, the question turns back into a statement — and a statement with the voice going up is a question too.

Chapter 5: Real Speed

Three Accents, No Warm-Up

A booth, a panel, and a keynote before coffee

Listening 1 · The Booth

We Sit in the Middle

A technology vendor explains his product at a booth. Sound first — no text.

1. Where does his product sit, and what does it not touch?
In the middle, as one layer between the bank's core system and the networks. It does not touch the core banking system.
2. What happens to a typical bank's connections after eighteen months?
It goes from about forty point-to-point connections to one.
3. What part does he say people usually miss?
Reconciliation: the matching happens in real time, so the operations team stops spending the morning finding out what happened yesterday.
Listening 2 · The Panel

Instant for Whom?

A European banking executive on a panel. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say is NOT the main thing slowing cross-border payments down?
Technology. She says the technology is mostly there.
2. What did the count last year show?
Around one payment in eight needed a human being somewhere along the way, almost always for a data problem and not for a real risk.
3. What does she mean by "instant for whom"?
The money can move in seconds, but the paperwork around it still moves at the speed of the slowest regulator.
Listening 3 · The Keynote

Three Numbers Before Coffee

The closing minute of a keynote. Sound first — no text.

1. How has the speed of a business payment between continents changed?
Ten years ago it took two to five days. Today the majority arrive within an hour, and many in minutes.
2. What has not changed as fast, and why does it matter?
The cost. For a small business it is still several percent, and that decides whether a small exporter can compete at all.
3. What does he say is the fastest-growing part of the market?
Small and mid-sized companies that were never profitable to serve before.

A Noisy Welcome Dinner

Scenario: A banker from another country talks fast in a noisy room, jumping from settlement times to his weekend. Stay in the conversation: ask for the one piece you need, paraphrase once, and keep it friendly.

Keyword chips:

Sorry, it's a bit loud in here Do you know if the gist I lost the thread for a second
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

The Time You Lost the Thread

Scenario: A real meeting or event where you lost the thread in English. Tell it in two minutes: what was said, what you missed, what you did. Then play it again, fast, and this time get the missing piece with one precise question.

What I Can Do Now

I listen for anchor words and ignore filler phrases, instead of trying to catch every word.
