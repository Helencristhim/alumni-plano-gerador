Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words of the room where the contacts are actually made.

Symposium -- a meeting where specialists present and discuss one subject
"The symposium on trauma care is in October."
Attendee -- a person who goes to an event
"There were four hundred attendees at the conference."
Presenter -- the person who gives a talk at an event
"The presenter took questions for ten minutes."
Panel -- a small group of specialists who discuss a subject in front of an audience
"She was invited to join the panel on artificial intelligence."
Networking -- meeting people in your field to build professional contacts
"The best networking happens in the coffee line, not in the room."
To collaborate -- to work together with someone on the same project
"We collaborate with a hospital in Chicago."
To reach out -- to contact someone, usually first
"I reached out to him after reading his paper."
Colleague -- a person who works with you or in the same field
"A colleague introduced me to the research director."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Paula Nunes is a pediatrician, and last month she went to her first international symposium. Did she enjoy it? Not at the beginning. On the first morning she sat alone at the coffee break, because she did not know what to say. Then a woman sat down next to her and asked three questions: Are you here for the whole symposium? Is this your first time? What did you think of the panel? Twenty minutes later they were still talking, and two months later they are writing a paper together. Paula says the lesson was simple: the person who asks the question controls the silence.
Why is it "What did you think of the panel?" and not "What you thought of the panel?"
A Because English puts the auxiliary in front of the subject in questions
B Because "thought" is an irregular verb
C Because the panel is already finished
What made the difference for Paula at the coffee break?
A She prepared a speech about her work
B Somebody sat down and asked her three questions
C She left the room and came back later

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Questions in English

GRAMMAR

In English a question is not a sentence said with a different tone. It is a sentence with the auxiliary in front of the subject.

yes / no questionauxiliary + subject + verbAre you here for the whole week? Do you work in trauma?
wh- questionwh- word + auxiliary + subject + verbWhat did you think of the panel?
with havehave / has + subject + past participleHow long have you been in this field?
after do / does / didthe verb stays in the base formDid you go last year? (not did you went)

Careful: tone of voice is not enough. "You are here for the whole week?" is understood, but it sounds like surprise, not like a question — and in a room full of strangers that difference is the whole conversation.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz
You sit down next to somebody at the coffee break. What do you say first?
A "What is your name and where do you work?"
B "Is this seat free? I am Stephanie, from Brazil."
C "Hello. I am a doctor from Brazil and I work with data and artificial intelligence."
The other person says something interesting. What keeps the conversation alive?
A "Ah, ok."
B "That is interesting. How long have you worked in that area?"
C "Yes, I know that already."
You have to leave, but you want to keep the contact. What do you say?
A "Goodbye."
B "It was great talking to you. Would it be all right if I reached out by email?"
C "Send me your email."
