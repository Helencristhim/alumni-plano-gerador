Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Ridership -- the number of people who use a train, metro or bus line
"Ridership on the new line was lower than the forecast."
A fare -- the money a passenger pays for one trip
"The fare is the same for every station on the line."
A commuter -- a person who travels to work and back every day
"Most commuters on this line travel more than an hour."
Rolling stock -- the trains and cars that a railway or metro uses
"The concessionaire has to buy all the rolling stock."
A depot -- a place where trains or buses are kept, cleaned and repaired
"The depot is at the end of the line, next to the river."
A right of way -- the legal right to build or run a line across a piece of land
"The right of way was the slowest part of the project."
Congestion -- too many vehicles or people in one place, so everything moves slowly
"The new line should reduce congestion downtown."
A transit hub -- a station where many lines and types of transport meet
"The central station is the biggest transit hub in the state."
To ramp up -- to increase something gradually, step by step, to a full level
"Ridership usually takes three years to ramp up."
Overcrowded -- with too many people in a space that is too small
"At rush hour the trains are overcrowded."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A new field does not need new grammar. It needs the right tool at the right moment. When you talk about a forecast you cannot promise, you hedge: "Ridership is likely to ramp up over three years." When you tell the history of a project quickly, you nominalize: "The signing of the contract came after two years of consultation."

When you say a wrong number, you repair it in two seconds: "Two million a day, or rather, one point eight." And when you explain a contract to people who are not lawyers, you use plain English: "The operator has to buy the trains, as long as the state builds the tunnel."

One warning: do not hedge a fact. If you know that the contract was signed in 2019, say it. Hedging what you know sounds like you do not know your own project.

1. Which tool do you use for a forecast you cannot promise?
A Nominalization, because a forecast is always a noun in a formal interview about a project.
B Plain English, as in has to.
C Hedging, as in is likely to.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "Ridership is likely to grow."
B "Ridership is likely grow when the connection to the central station opens next year."
C "Ridership likely to grows."
3. What does the text say about hedging a fact you know?
A That it is always more polite, so you should hedge every sentence in an interview, even the dates.
B That it sounds like you do not know your own project.
C That it is the best way to start an answer.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Toolbox

GRAMMAR

Four tools from the last weeks. The right one at the right moment.

ToolUse it whenExample
Hedgea forecast or a number you cannot promiseRidership is likely to grow.
Nominalizethe history of a project in few wordsThe opening of the line was delayed.
Repaira wrong number or a word that is too strongtwo million — or rather, one point eight
Plain Englisha contract rule for people who are not lawyersThe operator has to buy the trains.
A factsomething you knowThe contract was signed in 2019.

Never: is likely grow · the signing in the contract · has to buys · as long as it will stay

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A journalist asks how many people will use the line next year. You say:
A "It is likely to be around three hundred and fifty thousand, but it depends on the connection."
B "Exactly four hundred thousand."
C "It is likely be three hundred and fifty."
You said "two million" and it was one point eight. You say:
A "Sorry, sorry, I am very bad with numbers in English, I always make mistakes when I speak."
B "Two million. Yes."
C "Or rather, one point eight million."
A listener asks: "Does the operator pay for everything?" You answer:
A "The Concessionaire shall procure the rolling stock, provided that the Grantor shall build the civil works."
B "No. The operator has to buy the trains, as long as the state builds the tunnel."
C "No. The operator has to buys the trains."
Somebody asks when the contract was signed. You know it was 2019. You say:
A "It was signed in 2019."
B "It was probably signed around 2019, I think, more or less, if I remember well."
C "It is likely that it was 2019."
