Chapter 4: Real Speed

Live, About a Metro Line

An interview, a briefing and an engineer

Dialogue

The Metro Line, on the Radio

J
Aguinaldo, welcome back to the show. Today, a different topic: the new metro line. Should it go to a private operator?
A
It is likely to be the best option, but only with a good contract. In plain English, the operator has to buy the rolling stock and run the trains, as long as the state builds the tunnel.
J
And who pays if nobody uses the line?
A
That is the real question. Ridership tends to ramp up slowly, over three or four years. So the contract usually says that the state pays the difference in the first years.
J
Our listeners say the trains on line two are already overcrowded.
A
They are. Line two carries two million people a day — or rather, one point eight million. The new line should take part of that, and reduce congestion downtown.
J
And the fare? Will it go up?
A
Not in the first five years. The fare can only change if the agency approves it. That is in the contract, and it is binding.
Comprehension

About Joanna

1. What topic does Joanna bring to the show today?
The new metro line, and whether it should go to a private operator.

3. What is Joanna's last question?

Listening 1 · The Press Briefing

Line Six, Year One

The city's transport secretary at a press briefing. Sound first — no text.

1. What was ridership in the first year, compared with the forecast?
Roughly three hundred thousand a day, against a forecast of four hundred thousand.
2. Why does she say that is not a failure?
Because ridership on new lines usually ramps up over three or four years.
3. What will open next year, and why does it matter?
The connection with the central transit hub. It is likely to bring the biggest increase in ridership.
Listening 2 · The Engineer

Why Forecasts Are Wrong

A transport engineer on ridership forecasts. Sound first — no text.

1. In which direction are ridership forecasts usually wrong?
They are usually too high: they overestimate ridership.
2. What is the most common reason, according to him?
The forecast assumes that the connections to other lines and buses will be ready on day one, and they almost never are.
3. What does he recommend for the contract?
To share the demand risk between the state and the operator in the first years, instead of giving all of it to one party.

Concession term30 years
Operator's obligationsBuy the rolling stock; run trains every 4 minutes at rush hour
State's obligationsBuild the tunnel and stations; secure the right of way
Demand riskShared: the state pays the gap if ridership is below 80% of forecast (years 1 to 5)
FareFixed for 5 years; any change subject to agency approval
Year 1 ridership: about 300,000 a day. Forecast: 400,000.
1. Who has to buy the trains, and who builds the tunnel?
The operator has to buy the rolling stock. The state builds the tunnel and the stations.
2. Explain the demand risk in plain English.
In the first five years, if ridership is below eighty percent of the forecast, the state pays the difference.
3. Does the state pay the gap in year one? Why?
Yes. Ridership was about three hundred thousand, which is seventy-five percent of the forecast, so it is below eighty percent.
