The Numbers Behind the Story

Talking about a year of results without sounding like a spreadsheet. Key words: top line, bottom line, gross margin, EBITDA, working capital, overhead, a write-off, variance, billings, revenue recognition, cash conversion, to forecast. Structure: gerunds and infinitives with a change of meaning — where the form decides the fact.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. You have used all twelve of these this month — in Portuguese. This is activation, not learning, so go fast.

Top line -- revenue, the first line of the income statement
"The margin improved on a smaller top line, which is the harder version."
Bottom line -- profit, the last line of the income statement
"Everybody talks about the top line. The bottom line is what you are hired for."
Gross margin -- what is left after the direct cost of the work
"We went into the year with a gross margin of about thirty one per cent."
EBITDA -- profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization
"EBITDA is a useful number and it is not cash, and a good board knows the difference."
Working capital -- the cash tied up between paying suppliers and being paid
"Clients paid in ninety days and media owners wanted thirty. That gap is working capital."
Overhead -- the cost of running the business that no client pays for directly
"An overhead base built for a group twice the size it had become."
A write-off -- an amount you accept you will never recover
"We never took a write-off that year, which in that year is the number I am proud of."
Variance -- the difference between what was planned and what happened
"A variance is only interesting if you can say which of the two numbers was wrong."
Billings -- the total value invoiced to clients, which is not revenue
"Billings were four hundred million and revenue was fifty five. Say it once and move on."
Revenue recognition -- the rule deciding when a sale becomes revenue
"Revenue recognition decides when, not how much, and that is where the arguments are."
Cash conversion -- how much of the profit actually turns into cash
"Cash conversion was ninety per cent, which is the sentence that ends the question."
To forecast -- to state what the numbers will be, and be held to it
"I forecast the second half twice and I was wrong once, in the right direction."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two thousand nineteen is the year I get asked about. We went into it with a gross margin of about thirty one per cent and an overhead base built for a group twice the size it had been two years earlier. In February we lost our largest account, roughly a fifth of the top line.

I remember telling the board we would not chase revenue to replace it, and I remember the silence afterwards. We tried cutting discretionary spend first, which bought us almost nothing, so we stopped doing that and went on to rebuild the overhead base itself. Protecting the margin meant reducing that base by roughly a fifth, and it meant telling people, one at a time, before the market told them.

By the fourth quarter the margin was thirty four and a half, on a smaller top line. Cash conversion went from seventy four per cent to ninety one. And we took no write-offs at all that year, which in a year like that is the number I am actually proud of. I regret not telling the board about the account two weeks earlier, when I first knew, and I have said so.

1. "We tried cutting discretionary spend." Did they cut it?
A Yes — they did it as an experiment, and it did not achieve much.
B No — they attempted it and were unable to do it.
C The sentence does not say either way.
2. What would change if the text said "we stopped to do that"?
A Nothing — the two forms are interchangeable after "stop".
B It would mean they interrupted something else in order to do it — the opposite of ending the activity.
C It would make the sentence more formal but keep the same meaning.
3. "I remember telling the board" versus "I remembered to tell the board". What is the difference?
A The first is a memory of something he did. The second is about carrying out an obligation he had not to forget.
B The first is informal and the second is formal.
C The first is about the present and the second about the past.
4. Revenue fell by a fifth and the margin rose. What does the text say made that possible?
A Cutting discretionary spend, which delivered most of the improvement.
B Rebuilding the overhead base itself, by roughly a fifth.
C Replacing the lost account with two smaller ones.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- When the Form Decides the Fact

GRAMMAR

Most verbs take either the -ing form or the to form and nothing changes. With these six, the choice changes what actually happened — which is why a finance panel notices.

Verb+ -ing+ to
stopYou ended the activity.
We stopped reporting monthly.		You interrupted something else in order to do it.
We stopped to report.
remember / forgetThe memory of a past event.
I remember telling the board.		The obligation, and whether you carried it out.
I remembered to tell the board.
tryYou did it, as an experiment.
We tried cutting spend.		You attempted something difficult.
We tried to cut spend.
meanIt has this consequence.
It meant cutting forty roles.		You intended it.
I meant to cut forty roles.
regretYou are sorry about a past action.
I regret telling them so late.		You are about to give bad news.
I regret to tell you...
go onYou continued the same thing.
We went on reporting weekly.		You moved to the next thing.
We went on to rebuild the base.
The principleThe -ing form points at the activity itself. The to form points at a purpose or an intention. Everything else follows from that, including the cases you have never been taught.

With these six verbs, the wrong form does not sound wrong — it sounds like a different fact. That is a much more expensive mistake in an interview than an accent.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A panel that reads numbers for a living is asking. Choose the answer that ends the question instead of opening three more.

The Group CFO says your last group did four hundred million, so it was bigger than theirs. You say:
A "That figure is billings, not revenue. Revenue was fifty five, and fifty five is the number for this conversation."
B "Yes, it was a very large operation, one of the biggest in the market."
C "Well, it depends how you count it, because in our industry the numbers work differently."
Which sentence says that the activity ENDED?
A "We stopped to report monthly."
B "We stopped reporting monthly."
C "We stopped for reporting monthly."
You are asked about a variance you cannot yet explain. The strongest answer is:
A "I flag it on the day, with the size of it and the date I will have the answer."
B "I wait until the next cycle, because usually these things correct themselves."
C "I ask the controller to look at it and I report it when it is fully understood."
"Protecting the margin ___ reducing the overhead base." The correct form is:
A "meant to reduce" — because it was the intention.
B "meant reducing" — because it was the consequence.
C "meant reduce" — because "mean" takes the base form.
"Which number are you proud of?" The answer that lands is:
A "All of them, honestly. It was a good year in the end."
B "No write-offs in that year. When you lose a fifth of the revenue, that is the number that takes work."
C "The EBITDA, because it went up even with the difficulties we had."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is correcting this one, and there is no score.

"Walk me through a financial year that did not go to plan." Speak for about ninety seconds, in one take and with no notes, in four moves: the numbers you started with, what happened, what you did in order, and where the year finished. Open with a figure, not with a story. Use at least six of today's twelve terms, and at least two of the verbs where the form decides the fact — tried, stopped, meant, went on. If a number does not arrive, say the range out loud and keep going.
