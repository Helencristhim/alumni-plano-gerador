Warm-up — From Lesson 25

Review

In Lesson 25 you listened to a panel and reported what each speaker said: the main point, the evidence and your takeaway. You understood the words. But sometimes the words are not the problem — the culture is. Next month, Daniela spends a week with the US team in Chicago. A short "maybe" in Brazil can be a clear "no", and in Chicago it can sound like a "yes". Today you will learn the words of business culture and how to compare two cultures without stereotypes. (Different is not better or worse. It is just different.)

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Etiquette -- the rules of polite behavior in a group or a place
"Business etiquette in the US is different from Brazil."
Punctual -- arriving or starting at the exact time
"Please be punctual: the meeting starts at eight thirty."
Hierarchy -- the levels of power in a company, from the boss to the junior staff
"In the Chicago office, the hierarchy is less visible."
Feedback -- comments about someone’s work and how to make it better
"On Friday, Daniela will give feedback on the new system."
Straightforward -- honest and clear, saying things in a simple way
"Greg’s team is very straightforward: they say what they think."
Indirect -- not said in a clear way, only with a hint
"In Brazil, a "no" is often indirect."
Assume -- to think that something is true without checking
"Mike assumed that the new date was fine."
Misunderstanding -- when people understand a message in different ways
"The email created a misunderstanding about the date."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Next month, Daniela will spend a week at the US office in Chicago. To prepare, she read a short guide about business etiquette in the United States. According to the guide, Americans tend to be more direct than Brazilians at work. A Brazilian might say "Maybe we can look at the date again," while an American tends to say "This date will not work." Meetings are usually shorter than in Brazil, and they start on time. The hierarchy is also less visible: people use first names with their bosses. But Americans are not as direct about personal topics as they are about work. Daniela laughed at the last tip: in the US, "Let’s grab lunch sometime" is not always a real invitation.

1. According to the guide, how does an American tend to say no at work?
A With a long story about the past, to be polite to the other person first.
B Directly: "This date will not work."
C They never say no.
2. How are meetings in the US different?
A They are longer than in Brazil, because Americans like to discuss every detail.
B They start fifteen minutes late.
C They are shorter, and they start on time.
3. Which sentence is NOT always a real invitation?
A "Let’s grab lunch sometime."
B "Please come to the team dinner on Thursday at seven, at the Italian place."
C "The meeting is at eight thirty."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip — Comparing Two Cultures

GRAMMAR

To compare cultures, you need comparatives, and a careful word: tend to. A culture is a tendency, not a rule for every person.

UseFormExample
Long adjectivemore / less + adjective + thanAmericans are more direct than Brazilians at work.
Short adjectiveadjective + -er + thanMeetings in Chicago are shorter than in São Paulo.
Not the samenot as + adjective + asMy message was not as clear as I thought.
Usual, not alwaystend to + verbAmericans tend to use first names.
Contrastsentence + while + sentenceBrazilians start with small talk, while Americans tend to go straight to the point.
Remember"more direct", never "more directer". "shorter", never "more short". Tend to + base verb: "tend to say", never "tend to saying". And as ... as, never "as ... than".

Quiz
