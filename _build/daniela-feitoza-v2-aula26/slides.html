Chapter 3

The Compare Code

More, less, not as — and the careful word: tend to

Grammar Discovery

How Do We Compare?

"Americans are more direct than Brazilians at work."

"Meetings in Chicago are shorter than in São Paulo."

"My message was not as clear as I thought."

"Americans tend to use first names with their boss."

What do the orange words have in common? Why "tend to" and not "always"?

UseFormExample
Long adjectivemore / less + adjective + thanmore direct than Brazilians
Short adjectiveadjective + -er + thanshorter than in São Paulo
Not the samenot as + adjective + asnot as clear as I thought
Usual, not alwaystend to + verbAmericans tend to use first names.
Contrastsentence + while + sentenceWe start with small talk, while they go straight to the point.

Never "more shorter". Never "as clear than". Tend to + base verb.

Chapter 4

The Misunderstanding

Mike thinks the date is fine. It is not.

Dialogue

A Little Difficult

G
Hi Daniela. Quick question. Mike thinks the new timeline is fine for your team. Is that true?
D
Not really. I wrote to him that maybe the date was a little difficult. I was trying to be polite.
G
I see. That is a classic misunderstanding. Here, people tend to read “a little difficult” as “possible”.
D
Really? In Brazil, that is a clear no. We are more indirect than Americans, especially with new people.
G
Exactly. For Mike, your message was not as clear as you thought. So he assumed it was okay.
D
So what should I say next time? I do not want to sound rude.
G
Be straightforward, but friendly. Say the problem and the reason: “This date will not work for us, because…”
D
Got it. In Chicago, I will be more direct. And punctual, of course!
