Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A witness -- a person who saw something happen
"There were two witnesses, and they remembered two different mornings."
To notice -- to see or become aware of something, often something small
"She noticed that the door was open, and she had walked past it for weeks."
To glance -- to look at something very quickly
"I glanced at the screen, so I would not swear to the number."
An alibi -- proof that you were somewhere else when something happened
"His alibi was a parking ticket printed at eleven forty."
To recall -- to bring something back into your memory
"I cannot recall whether the light was on. I think it was."
Vague -- not clear or exact enough to be useful
"Around ten is vague. Ten forty is an answer."
To contradict -- to say the opposite of what somebody else said
"The second statement contradicted the first one by fifteen minutes."
A timeline -- the order in which events happened, with times attached
"Build the timeline first. Everything else argues with it later."
To take place -- to happen, usually at a planned or known moment
"The meeting took place on the ninth floor, not the tenth."
Straightforward -- simple and clear, with nothing hidden or complicated
"Her answer was straightforward, and nobody liked it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences, four words apart. When I arrived, she was writing the report. When I arrived, she wrote the report. In the first one she had been at it for a while and you walked into the middle of it. In the second one she had not started, and your arrival is what got her going. Same people, same room, and a completely different story about who caused what.

This is the difference the past continuous exists for. Was or were plus the -ing form describes something that was already running: the background. The past simple describes what arrived: the event. That is why while naturally attaches to the background — while she was waiting for the lift — and when naturally attaches to the event — when the phone rang. Put two backgrounds side by side and both go into the continuous: they were presenting while I was checking the numbers.

Two things trip people up. The first is that a finished sequence needs no continuous at all: the meeting took place at nine and finished at ten is three closed events in a row, and nothing was interrupted. The second is that some verbs refuse the -ing form entirely, because they are not actions you can be in the middle of: know, believe, understand, want. You did not say I was knowing. You knew.

Why it matters outside a grammar exercise: a timeline is built out of exactly this. An interviewer asks what you were doing, never what you saw, because the background gives you somewhere to stand. And when your answer is vague — around ten — you will be asked again later, and the second answer will contradict the first. It is cheaper to say you cannot recall the time than to invent one to be helpful.

1. In "When I arrived, she was writing the report", who started first?
A She did. The writing was already running when I arrived.
B I did. She started writing because I arrived.
C Both started at exactly the same moment.
2. Why is "I was knowing the answer" wrong?
A Because "know" is an irregular verb.
B Because "know" is not an action you can be in the middle of, so it refuses the -ing form.
C Because the sentence needs a "while" in front of it.
3. According to the text, why does an interviewer ask what you were doing rather than what you saw?
A Because it is more polite at the start of a conversation.
B Because what people saw is never reliable.
C Because the background gives the person somewhere to stand, and the rest hangs off it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous against Past Simple

GRAMMAR

One question decides it: was it already running, or did it arrive?

JobFormExample
the background, already runningwas / were + verb-ingI was checking the list.
the event that arrivespast simple...when I found the receipt.
the word that marks each onewhile + background · when + eventWhile she was waiting, she noticed it.
two backgrounds at onceboth continuousThey were presenting while I was reading.
a finished sequencepast simple all the wayIt took place at nine and finished at ten.
questions about a backgroundwas / were + subject + -ingWhat were you doing at ten forty?
verbs that refuse the -ingknow, believe, understand, wantNot I was knowing. Say I knew.

Never: I was knowing the answer · When I arrived, she wrote the report (if she had already started) · While they presented, I read the file · I checked the file when my phone rang

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A I checked the quarter file when the system went down.
B I was checking the quarter file when the system went down.
C I was knowing the system went down.
You are not sure of the time and somebody is writing your answer down. You say:
A Around ten, more or less, I think.
B I cannot recall the exact time, and I will not guess.
C Ten forty. Definitely ten forty.

A They were presenting while I was checking the numbers.
B They presented while I checked the numbers.
C They were presenting when I checked the numbers.

A No, I am sure about my time.

Reflection

Lesson 3 — While Something Else Was Happening

Talk
Elizabeth Loftus — “How reliable is your memory?” (TED)

Seventeen minutes from the researcher behind the study in this lesson’s first recording. She spends the talk on cases where a confident, honest witness was completely wrong, and on how easily a question can rewrite what somebody remembers.

Tip: she narrates several cases. Pick one and retell it out loud in five sentences, using was or were plus -ing for the background of each scene.

Podcast
Criminal — “Mantrap” (episode 366)

Thirty minutes of an interviewer doing exactly what the second recording described: opening with what people were doing, never with what they saw, and leaving long silences that the other person fills.

Tip: count how many times the host asks a follow-up question instead of moving on. Then count how many she asks in the first five minutes alone.

Radio
99% Invisible — “Structural Integrity”

The story of an engineer who received one question from a student, went back to his own calculations, and found something he had missed. The whole episode is a timeline: what was already happening, what interrupted it, and what he did next.

Tip: after listening, write the timeline of the episode in six lines with times or dates. It is the same exercise you did in class, on somebody else’s story.
