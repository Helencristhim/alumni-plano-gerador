Chapter 4: The Comma

Four Sentences, Two Kinds of Comma

Which one, and by the way

Grammar Discovery

Which One Could You Delete?

"The customers who come back every fortnight pay the rent." — which customers?

"My neighbour David, who buys two boxes every time, has never asked the price." — by the way

"The week that everything went wrong was the week the sun came out." — which week?

"I made forty boxes, which was ten more than I had ever made." — by the way

The red ones answer which one? and have no commas. Delete them and the sentence stops telling you who it is about. The orange ones sit inside commas and say by the way. Delete them and you lose a detail, not the meaning.

Chapter 5: Real Numbers

Two Traders and One Spreadsheet

Nineteen years, one bad Saturday, and your own figures

Listening 1 · A Market Trader

A Good Day Is Only Weather

Nineteen years on the same stall. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say a good day actually is, and what pays her rent instead?
A good day is weather. What pays her rent is the forty or fifty people who come back every week without being asked, and she could name most of them.
2. What does she say about a customer who buys because you are cheaper?
That the person is not really a customer, because somebody else will be two pence cheaper than you next month.
3. What two things do the traders who last have in common?
They worked out early what each item actually costs them, and then they stopped looking at the stall opposite.
Listening 2 · Somebody Your Age

A Guess Wearing a Suit

The week the sun came out and the stall lost money. Sound first — no text.

1. Why did he make forty boxes, and how many did he sell?
Because he thought a hot Saturday meant more people. It was ten more than he had ever made, and he sold twenty-six.
2. Why were the fourteen boxes a loss and not stock?
Because cake does not keep. Something perishable that is left over is gone, not stock for the next market.
3. What does he say the lesson was NOT, and what was it?
It was not to bake less. It was that he had picked the number before he had looked at anything, and a number you pick before you look is a guess wearing a suit.

Same price, same customers, more boxes. One of these two Saturdays felt better and earned a quarter as much.
1. Which line is the one that explains the whole difference? Answer with a relative clause.
The fourteen boxes that were left over. They cost money to make and brought nothing in, which is why the margin fell from R$ 215 to R$ 58.
2. Which cost did not move at all, and what do we call a cost like that?
The overheads, which stayed at R$ 70 on both Saturdays. An overhead is a cost you pay even in a week when you sell nothing.
3. On the sunny Saturday, did he break even? Say what he did, not just yes or no.
He did better than break even, because the turnover, which was R$ 468, still covered the R$ 410 of cost. He made R$ 58, which is a quarter of what he made on the ordinary Saturday.
Go Deeper · Optional

Two Ways to Say It Shorter

You have the full clause. Written English keeps two shortcuts, and both were on the page you read.

The one that drops the pronoun and the verb. "The boxes which were left over" becomes "the boxes left over". You can only do it when the clause has no comma, and the shorter version is what a report would print.

The one with the preposition in front. "The supplier from whom I buy the flour" is the formal written form. In speech you would say "the supplier I buy the flour from", and both are correct. Only one of them belongs in an essay.

Your turn. Take two sentences about your own stall and write each one twice: once the way you would say it, once the way you would write it. Then tell me which pair sounds most different, and why.
