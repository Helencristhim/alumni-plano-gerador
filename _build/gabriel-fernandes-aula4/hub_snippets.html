Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A supplier -- the person or shop you buy your materials from
“The supplier who sells me the flour raised his price in March.”
A margin -- what is left after you take the cost out of the price
“The margin on a box is small, which is why the number of boxes matters.”
Overheads -- the costs you pay even in a week when you sell nothing
“The table costs the same whether you sell forty boxes or none, so it is an overhead.”
To break even -- to make exactly enough to cover what you spent
“On a bad Saturday you break even, and breaking even is not the same as losing.”
Stock -- what you have made or bought and have not sold yet
“Boxes are stock. Cake that has to be eaten today is not.”
Demand -- how many people want the thing at the price you set
“Demand is not the crowd. It is the crowd that would pay what you are asking.”
To mark up -- to add to the cost in order to set the price
“I mark up from what the box costs me, not from what the next stall charges.”
A regular -- a customer who comes back without being asked
“A regular is worth more than a good day, because a good day is only weather.”
Turnover -- all the money that comes in, before any cost is taken out
“Turnover is what people brag about. Margin is what they take home.”
Perishable -- something that goes bad if it is not sold quickly
“Cake is perishable, so what is left at four o'clock is not stock.”
To undercut -- to sell for less than somebody else in order to take their customers
“Somebody who undercuts you is not your problem, because that customer was never yours.”
A loss -- what you have when the costs are bigger than the money that came in
“The fourteen boxes that were left were not stock, they were a loss.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has one small machine for saying WHICH thing you mean. You take a noun, and you hang a clause on it that narrows it down. The customers who come back every fortnight. Not all the customers, only those. That clause is doing real work, and if you delete it the sentence stops telling you who it is about. It takes no commas, and it is called a defining clause because it defines.

The same machine does a second job, and this is where the commas come in. My neighbour David, who buys two boxes every time, has never asked the price. There is only one neighbour called David, so nothing needs narrowing down. The clause is a comment you could delete without losing the point. Two commas mark it, one on each side, and it is called non-defining. Miss the second comma and the sentence hangs open.

The words are simple once the job is clear. Who for people, which for things, whose for something belonging to somebody, and where for places. That is the useful one and also the dangerous one: it can replace who or which, but only in the defining kind. After a comma, that is simply wrong, and no native speaker writes it.

One last shortcut. When the relative word is the OBJECT of its clause and there are no commas, you can leave it out entirely. The cakes that I made on Friday and the cakes I made on Friday are both correct. You cannot do it when the relative word is the subject: the customers come back is a different sentence from the customers who come back, and dropping who there breaks it. Read the sentence out loud. Where your voice pauses, a comma probably belongs, and where it does not pause, one probably does not.

1. According to the text, what happens if you delete a defining clause?

B The sentence stops telling you who or what it is about.

2. Why does the text say the David sentence takes commas?
A Because the sentence is long.

C Because the clause comes in the middle of the sentence.
3. When does the text say you can leave the relative word out?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give. Read all three before you decide.

1. You mean SOME of the customers, not all of them. You say:
A The customers who come back every fortnight pay the rent.
B The customers, who come back every fortnight, pay the rent.
C The customers, that come back every fortnight, pay the rent.
2. There is only one person called David and you want to add a detail. You say:
A David that buys every time is a regular.
B David, who buys every time, is a regular.
C David, which buys every time, is a regular.
3. You are talking about the flour and the person who sells it to you. You say:
A The supplier which sells me the flour raised his price.
B The supplier who sells me the flour raised his price.
C The supplier what sells me the flour raised his price.
4. You want to say the stall next to yours belongs to a trader who cut his prices. You say:
A The trader whose stall is next to mine undercut me in June.
B The trader who his stall is next to mine undercut me in June.
C The trader which stall is next to mine undercut me in June.
