Lesson 8 -- On the Other Hand

Podcast
TED Radio Hour (NPR) -- "Changing Our Minds: Why we should admit when we're wrong"

An hour built on the movement this lesson trains: what it costs to concede a point out loud, and what it buys. Among the guests is a former congressman describing the argument that changed his position in public, which is the second listening from class, in real life.

Tip: note every sentence where somebody concedes something before answering. Copy three, then rewrite each one with a different connector: although, despite, however.

Talk
Julia Galef -- "Why you think you're right, even if you're wrong" (TED, 11 min)

The distinction between defending a position and finding out what is true, built around a nineteenth-century French court case. For somebody who argues for a living the talk is uncomfortable in a useful way, and the English is unusually clean and well structured.

Tip: apply her test to an argument you are currently making at work. Ask, out loud and in English, what evidence would change your mind. If nothing would, say so and see how it sounds.

Film
The Social Dilemma (Netflix)

A documentary that argues one side hard, and is worth watching for that reason. Everybody interviewed is fluent, fast and completely certain, which makes it a good test of the skill this lesson trains: find the counterargument the film never states.

Tip: write the objection the film refuses to make, in one paragraph, as well as its strongest defender would. That is exactly the exercise from the first listening in class.
