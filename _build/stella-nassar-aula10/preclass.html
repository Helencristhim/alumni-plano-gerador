Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read it in context. These are the words editors use to name what goes wrong in a draft, and naming a problem is the first step to fixing it.

Redundant -- saying the same thing twice, and therefore unnecessary
""Past history" is redundant: all history is past."
Verbose -- using more words than are needed
"The first draft was verbose, not wrong."
Pare down -- to reduce something to what is essential
"She pared the paragraph down to three sentences."
Tighten -- to make writing more concise and controlled
"Tighten each sentence before you cut a whole paragraph."
Excise -- to remove a part completely, often with care
"Excise the sentence that explains what the scene already shows."
Superfluous -- more than is needed; unnecessary
"The final adjective is superfluous."
Economy -- here: the careful use of words so that none is wasted
"Economy of language is not the same as brevity."
Circumlocution -- using many words to say something that could be said simply
""In the event that" is circumlocution for "if"."
Crisp -- clear, sharp and efficient in style
"The revised opening is crisp."
Streamline -- to make something simpler and more efficient
"Streamline the middle paragraph so the turn arrives sooner."
Distill -- to reduce something to its most essential meaning
"Distill the reflection into one sentence."
Extraneous -- not relevant to the main subject
"The paragraph about the bus ride is extraneous."
Padding -- words or material added only to make something longer
"Remove the padding before you remove the ideas."
Cumbersome -- heavy, awkward and slow to use or read
"The sentence is grammatically correct but cumbersome."
Ruthless -- without pity; completely determined
"Be ruthless with your favorite sentences."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Revision has a reputation for being painful, and much of that pain comes from cutting in the wrong order. A writer who must remove a hundred and fifty words usually looks first for something large to delete: a paragraph, an example, an idea. The essay gets shorter, but it also gets poorer. There is almost always a better place to start.

Most first drafts contain a layer of language that carries no meaning at all. Some of it is redundant: each and every, past history, end result, free gift. Some of it is circumlocution, a long route to a short destination: due to the fact that means because, in the event that means if, at this point in time means now. Some of it consists of empty openings, such as it is important to note that or it should be pointed out that, which announce a sentence instead of writing it.

A second layer is subtler. Writers who have learned to value academic density sometimes use a weak, general verb to carry a noun: made a decision, gave consideration to, came to the realization. Each of these can collapse into a single strong verb: decided, considered, realized. This does not contradict the value of nominalization. A noun is useful when it lets a sentence do something a verb cannot, such as becoming the subject of a claim. When it only adds length, it is padding.

Removing these layers is the fastest way to reduce an essay without losing anything the writer meant. It also makes the remaining language more crisp, because every word left on the page is doing work. Only when this process is finished should a writer begin to question whole sentences, and only after that, whole ideas.

One caution applies throughout. Concision is not the same as flatness. A ruthless editor removes padding, not personality, and the sentence with a strange detail or an unexpected rhythm is often the reason the essay deserves to be read at all.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Concision

GRAMMAR

Concision removes language that carries no meaning. Cut in layers: redundancy and empty phrases first, weak verb and noun combinations second, sentences third, ideas last.

redundant pairstwo words for one ideaeach and every → every; end result → result

empty openingsannounce instead of sayIt is important to note that... → (delete)

The C2 trapcutting voice along with padding. Concision is the absence of waste, not the absence of personality. Protect the sentence only you could have written.

A useful test: read each sentence aloud and ask how you would say it to a friend. The spoken version is often the concise one.
