Listening to Complexity -- Follow the Map of the Talk

How expert listeners survive a long, dense hearing in English. Key words: a hearing, a transcript, to recap, a digression, to pick up on, to zone out, the bottom line, a signpost, dense, to lose track of. Tools: signposts, plus focus, hedging and attribution from lessons 21 to 23.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A hearing -- an official meeting where people can give their views on a decision
"The agency held a public hearing on the new toll rule."
A transcript -- the written text of everything that people said
"I read the transcript of the hearing the next day."
To recap -- to repeat the main points in a short way
"To recap, the goal is more maintenance."
A digression -- a part of a talk that leaves the main topic for a moment
"After a short digression, she went back to the timeline."
To pick up on -- to notice something small that is not said clearly
"He picked up on a small change in the minister's tone."
To zone out -- to stop paying attention for a moment without wanting to
"After two hours of numbers, I started to zone out."
The bottom line -- the most important point or result of a discussion
"The bottom line is that tolls will not go up."
A signpost -- a word or phrase that shows where a speaker is going
"First, however and to recap are useful signposts."
Dense -- with a lot of information in very few words
"The report is dense: every sentence has a number."
To lose track of -- to stop knowing what is happening or what someone is saying
"When he spoke fast, I lost track of the numbers."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Aguinaldo attends a three-hour public hearing on a new toll rule. The language is dense, and the director speaks fast.

He does not try to understand every word. He listens for signposts. When the director says first, he knows a new part starts. When she says what the agency wants is, he knows the key idea comes next. When she says a quick digression, he relaxes a little.

Near the end, he almost zones out. Then he hears to recap, and he focuses again: this is the summary. He leaves with the bottom line: part of every toll will go to maintenance. Notice: every tool is a warning about what comes next.

1. What does Aguinaldo listen for?
A Signposts.
B Every single word, because he wants to translate the whole hearing into Portuguese later.
C Only the numbers, because the rest of a public hearing is usually not important.
2. What does Aguinaldo know when he hears "what the agency wants is"?
A That a digression is starting, so he can stop paying attention for a few minutes.
B That the hearing is about to end and the questions from the floor will start.
C That the key idea comes next.
3. What makes him focus again near the end?
A A question.
B The words to recap.
C A number.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Listening for the Tools

GRAMMAR

No new structure today. Every tool from lessons 21 to 23, and every signpost, is a warning about what comes next.

JobFormExample
Focus (lesson 21)What... is / It is... thatThe next words are the key idea.
Hedge (lesson 22)likely to / tend to / to some extentThe next claim is not certain.
Attribution (lesson 23)as X pointed out / according toThe next idea is someone else's.
Order signpostfirst / second / finallyA new part starts.
Digression signposta quick digression / back to the main pointYou can relax, then focus again.
Summary signpostto recap / the bottom line isListen hard: the summary.

Never: Is the maintenance that worries them · likely to being extended · According to me · To resume (= to recap)...

Stage 2: Put the Recap in Order

Order

You recap a long hearing for a colleague. Put your sentences in a logical order.

?"What the agency wants is more maintenance, not higher tolls."
?"Then she explained the timeline."
?"To recap, the consultation closes on November 30."
?"First, the director explained why the agency wants to change the rule."
?"The hearing had three parts."
?"There was a digression about the old contracts, but they are not part of the proposal."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The speaker is too fast and you miss a point. You say:
A "Sorry, I lost track of the last point."
B "Sorry, I lost the track of your last point because you are speaking very, very fast."
C "Sorry, I zoned out the last point, please say it again from the beginning of the hearing."
You summarize the main points at the end of a meeting. You say:
A "To resume, the goal of the new toll rule is more maintenance on the federal highways."
B "For recap, the goal of the new toll rule is more maintenance on the federal highways."
C "To recap, the goal is maintenance."
The speaker says: "Now, a quick digression." What should you do?
A Take notes very fast, because the most important information of the talk always comes in a digression.
B Relax a little and wait for the main point.
C Leave the room.
You want to give the most important point in one sentence. You start with:
A "The bottom line is..."
B "A digression is..."
C "A transcript is..."
