Stage 2: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

The words you have just met, one by one. Listen to each expression and read it in context: you already do all of this at work. Here it gets its English name.

counterpart -- the person who has the same role as you in another country or company
"My counterpart in Italy has the same three entities."
time zone -- the area of the world that shares the same clock time
"We are four hours apart, so the time zone decides the meeting."
how are things on your side -- a natural way to ask about the other person's situation
"How are things on your side after the reorganisation?"
that sounds challenging -- a way to show you understood that something is difficult
"Two portfolios with one analyst? That sounds challenging."
by the way -- used to add something that is not the main subject
"By the way, are you going to the offsite in November?"
speaking of which -- used to connect to something the other person just said
"Speaking of which, how is the new onboarding process working there?"
touch base -- to make short contact with somebody to check something
"I will touch base with finance before Friday."

Stage 4: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

A global call opens. Somebody asks: "How are things on your side?" There are two possible answers, and the difference between them decides the next twenty minutes.

The first answer is "fine, thanks". It is polite, correct and finished. Nothing in it invites another sentence, so the other person tries once more, receives another two words, and then everybody waits in silence for the meeting to start.

The second answer has two parts: one detail, then a question. "Busy, in a good way. We closed the audit this week. How about you?" The detail gives the other person something to hold, and the question gives the turn back. That is the whole mechanism, and it repeats.

When the answer comes back, two moves keep it alive. The first is acknowledgement: "That sounds challenging" shows that you listened, and it costs three words. The second is a follow-up question, which is a question about something inside the answer you just heard. If a counterpart says they have been travelling for three weeks, the follow-up is not "and how is the weather in Paris". It is "do you manage to keep your catch-ups with the regions?".

Two small expressions move the conversation without breaking it. By the way opens a new, light subject: "By the way, are you coming to the offsite in November?". Speaking of which connects to what was just said: "Speaking of which, how is the new process working on your side?". With those two, five minutes stop being a corridor and start being a conversation your counterpart remembers next month, when something difficult happens in their region and they decide who to call.

Stage 5: Grammar Tip -- Keeping a conversation alive

GRAMMAR

Four moves, and they repeat in every conversation you will have with a counterpart in another country.

FormWhat it doesExample
answer + returnone detail, then the question backBusy, in a good way. How about you?
acknowledgethree words that show you listenedThat sounds challenging.
follow-up questionask about something inside their answerDo you manage to keep your catch-ups?
open or connectadd a new subject, or link to theirsBy the way, ... / Speaking of which, ...
Watch outanswering correctly and stopping. In English the turn is given back with a question, and without it the silence belongs to you.

Before your next global call, choose one person and prepare two follow-up questions about what they told you last time.

Stage 8: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Real openings of global calls. Only one option is both correct and natural.

1. Your counterpart asks how things are on your side. Which answer keeps the conversation alive?
A Fine, thanks.
B Busy, in a good way. We closed the audit this week. How about you?
C Everything is normal here, nothing new.
D Fine. Shall we start the agenda?
2. He says he has been travelling for three weeks. What is a good follow-up?
A That sounds challenging. Do you manage to keep your catch-ups with the regions?
B I am sorry to hear that. Let us start the agenda.
C Travelling is always difficult for everybody.
D Why do you travel so much?
3. Which sentence connects to what the other person has just said?
A By the way, are you coming to the offsite?
B Speaking of which, how is that working on your side?
C Anyway, let us move to the agenda.
D Talking of that thing, how is it going there?
4. You want a short contact next month. What do you say?
A Let us touch base in October about the visit.
B Let us do a touch base in October about the visit.
C Let us touch the base in October about the visit.
D Let us make a catch up in October about the visit.
