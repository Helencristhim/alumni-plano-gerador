Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When you tell an investor what a regulator said, you are doing more than repeating words. You are telling them how firm the promise was, and whether it is still true. English does both jobs with two tools: the reporting verb and the tense.

The reporting verb measures certainty. If the director confirmed that the tariff review would begin in October, that is a decision. If she indicated or suggested that tariffs could fall, that is a signal. If she clarified something, she was correcting a misunderstanding, and if she acknowledged that operators had filed a rebalancing claim, she was admitting a problem.

The tense usually moves one step back: “The review will begin” becomes “She confirmed that the review would begin.” But when the fact is still true today, you can keep the present: “She confirmed that contracts signed before 2025 are protected by a grandfathering clause.”

Sometimes nobody wants to own the information. Then English reports it without a person: “The operator is said to be preparing a claim.” For anyone doing due diligence, that structure is a warning. And two Portuguese habits never work here: “she said me” and “she explained me.” Say she told me and she explained it to me.

Stage 1.6: Read and Understand

Reading

Read it once straight through, then read it again and answer. Every expression from Stage 1.1 is in here doing a job.

What the Regulator Did Not Say

Every regulated contract is read twice: once for what it promises, and once for what it leaves to somebody’s judgement. The second reading is the one that sets the price. A concession can be beautifully drafted and still be unfinanceable if too much of it rests on administrative discretion.

The instruments themselves are not mysterious. A tariff review sets out when and how the price the operator charges may be recalculated. A grandfathering clause protects contracts signed under the old rules when new ones arrive. A rebalancing claim is what the operator files when something outside its control has made the original arithmetic impossible. Force majeure covers the events nobody could have prevented, and it is a great deal narrower than most people assume.

Some of the machinery protects the state rather than the operator. A step-in right lets the authority take over operation if the concessionaire fails badly enough, and what counts as badly enough is defined by a material breach. Where the state ends the contract for its own reasons, the compensation is a termination payment, and the formula for it is negotiated years before anyone imagines using it.

Then there is what sits off the balance sheet. A contingent liability is an obligation that only becomes real if something specific happens, which is precisely why finance ministries like it, and why a fiscal framework written to be taken seriously puts a ceiling on how many of them a government may carry at once.

The process around all of this is slower than the sector would like. Environmental licensing takes as long as it takes. A public consultation may not change the outcome at all and still has to be done properly, because a consultation done badly is the commonest reason a licence is later set aside by a court.

Newer rules tend to arrive through a regulatory sandbox: a defined space in which a few operators may test something under relaxed conditions for a fixed period, so that the regulator learns before it legislates. None of this removes the work of due diligence. It only tells you where to point it.
