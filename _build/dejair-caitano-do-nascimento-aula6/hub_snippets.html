Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. You have read every one of these in a reviewer's report. This week you say them about somebody else's work.

Clear -- easy to understand, with nothing missing
"Her explanation was clearer than mine."
Complex -- made of many parts, and not easy to follow
"This study is more complex than the previous one."
Similar -- almost the same, but not exactly
"Our methods are similar, but our patients are different."
Effective -- it works, it does what it should do
"The treatment is effective, but it is slow."
Useful -- you can actually use it in your own work
"The most useful session was the smallest one."
Relevant -- connected to what you are doing right now
"That result is relevant to my own study."
Brief -- short, and finished quickly
"Keep your answer brief. You have two minutes."
Detailed -- with many small pieces of information
"Her poster was more detailed than the talk."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Two studies, one workshop. Study A is much bigger: four hundred patients against sixty. But study B followed each patient for two years, and A only for six months, so B is far more detailed. B also measured pain at home, which is more difficult but closer to real life, and its method is clearer -- step by step, not one paragraph. Sanjay says A is the better study, because bigger is safer. Dejair does not agree: his patients live far from the hospital, like the patients in B, so for him B is the more relevant one. In the end they say both: A is the better study, B is the most useful one for their own work.
Why is it "bigger" but "more detailed"?
A Because big is a short adjective and detailed is a long one
B Because bigger is about numbers and more detailed is about quality
C Because one is a comparison and the other is not
What does Dejair mean when he says B is more relevant for him?
A That B is a better study than A
B That B is closer to his own patients, even if A is bigger
C That A has no value at all

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Count the syllables. Short adjectives (one syllable, or two ending in -y) take -er and the -est. Longer adjectives take more and the most. Never both at once.

short adjectiveadjective + -er thanHer explanation was clearer than mine.
short adjective, topthe + adjective + -estIt was the smallest room in the building.
long adjectivemore + adjective + thanThis design is more complex than the other one.
long adjective, topthe most + adjectiveIt was the most useful session of the week.
irregulargood / bad / farbetter, the best -- worse, the worst -- further, the furthest

"More bigger" is wrong: English chooses one of the two forms, never both. And the second half of a comparison is than, not that.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz
The facilitator asks which study is better and why. Which answer shows criteria?
A "Study A. It is bigger."
B "A is the better study, but B is the more useful one for my patients, because they live far from the hospital."
C "Both are good studies."
Your partner says the bigger study is always the best one. You disagree. What do you say?
A "No, you are wrong."
B "I do not agree, and this is why. My patients live far from the hospital, like the ones in the small study."
C "Maybe. I do not know."
The moderator asks you to be shorter. Which answer is correct?
A "Sorry. Briefly: the second study is smaller but more detailed, and that is why I would use it."
B "Sorry, my English is slow."
C "The second study is more better and more detailed."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
The facilitator calls your name in front of twenty people. Record two minutes comparing the two studies from the handout: the numbers, one thing each one does better, which is more relevant to your own patients and why, and the difference between the better study and the one you would actually use. No script -- just speak.

Listening
"Which country has the best schools?" -- 6 Minute English, BBC Learning English

Six minutes of two presenters comparing countries, systems and numbers -- which is exactly the shape of the sentences you practised today. Comparatives and superlatives in almost every line.

Tip: listen once with your eyes closed. Then listen again and write down every comparative and superlative you hear. Sort them into two columns: the ones with -er and the ones with more. You will see the syllable rule work by itself.
