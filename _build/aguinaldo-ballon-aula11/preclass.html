Listening to the World -- Hearing What People Mean

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To imply -- to suggest something without saying it directly
"He did not say no, but he implied it."
To infer -- to understand something from what people say, even when they do not say it directly
"From her silence, I infer that she is not convinced."
A hint -- a small sign that helps you understand what somebody really means
"There was a hint of doubt in his voice."
Tone -- the way a voice sounds, which shows how the speaker feels
"Her words were polite, but her tone was cold."
An understatement -- a way of saying that something is less serious than it really is
"A small delay was an understatement: the project was a year late."
To read between the lines -- expression: to understand what somebody means but does not say
"If you read between the lines, they are not going to sign."
Sarcastic -- saying the opposite of what you mean, often to criticize
"Great, another delay, he said, in a sarcastic voice."
A filler -- a word like um, well or you know that fills a pause in speech
"Native speakers use a lot of fillers when they think."
Emphasis -- extra force on a word, to show that it is important
"Listen for the emphasis: it tells you the key word."
Connected speech -- the way words join together when people speak fast
"In connected speech, going to sounds like gonna."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

After a call with a fund in London, Aguinaldo listens to the recording again. The banker said the proposal was "interesting". His words were polite, but his tone was cold. It sounds like he did not like it.

The banker also said that the timeline was "a bit ambitious". That was an understatement: he was implying that the date was impossible. He seems to have a problem with the guarantee, too.

Aguinaldo does not invent a fact. He gives an impression and then checks it: "The fund appears to have doubts about the guarantee. Could you tell me what worries the committee?" Notice: sounds like + a sentence, seems + an adjective, seems to + a verb.

1. What did the banker really mean by "a bit ambitious"?
A That the date was impossible.
B That the team was very ambitious and he admired that a lot.
C That the proposal was too short and needed more pages.
2. Why does Aguinaldo say "It sounds like he did not like it"?
A Because the banker said very clearly that he did not like it.
B Because it is his interpretation.
C Because "sounds like" is the past form of "said" in English.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A "He seems having a problem with the guarantee."
B "He seems to have a problem."
C "He seems like to have a problem with the guarantee."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Seem, Appear and Sound Like

GRAMMAR

Use them to give an impression, not a fact. Sound like + sentence; sound or seem + adjective; seem or appear + to + verb.

JobFormExample
Interpret what you hearIt sounds like + subject + verbIt sounds like they are not happy.
An impression with an adjectivesound / seem + adjectiveThat sounds risky. He seems worried.
An impression with a verbseem / appear + to + verbShe seems to agree.
An impression about the pastseem / appear + to have + past participleThe regulator appears to have changed its mind.
Start with the impressionIt seems (that) + subject + verbIt seems that the deal is not ready.
The traplike goes before a sentence or a noun, never before an adjectiveNot It sounds like risky. Say It sounds risky.

Never: It sounds like risky · He seems agree · It sounds they do not want to sign · The regulator appears change its mind
