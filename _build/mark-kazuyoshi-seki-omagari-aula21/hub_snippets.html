Lesson 21 -- Pre-class

Once Upon a Time Online -- Narrative Tenses

Retell what you watched with all four past tenses working together: past simple for the chain of events, past continuous for the background, past perfect for the backstory and past perfect continuous for how long it had been going on. Key words: binge-watch, cliffhanger, spoiler, backstory, subplot, protagonist, character arc, build-up, red herring, lore, to set the scene, thread.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. These are the words people actually use when they talk about a series online.

Binge-watch -- to watch many episodes of a series one after another
"I binge-watched the whole season in a weekend and then regretted it."
Cliffhanger -- an ending that stops at the most tense moment, so you have to wait
"The episode ended on a cliffhanger and the next one comes out in a year."
Spoiler -- a piece of information that ruins a story for someone who has not seen it
"He dropped a spoiler in the group chat before anyone had finished the season."
Backstory -- the events that happened before the story you are watching begins
"Her backstory explains why she had been hiding from the family for years."
Subplot -- a smaller story that runs alongside the main one
"The subplot about the letters was the only part that made sense at the end."
Protagonist -- the character the story follows, and whose choices drive it
"The protagonist had been travelling with him since the first episode."
Character arc -- the way a character changes from the beginning of a story to the end
"His character arc goes from selfish kid to the one who stays behind."
Build-up -- the slow increase of tension before something big happens
"The build-up lasted six episodes, and the payoff lasted forty seconds."
Red herring -- a clue put there on purpose to send you the wrong way
"The neighbour was a red herring, and I had been watching him the whole time."
Lore -- the background history and rules of an invented world
"You need the lore to understand why that door had been closed for a thousand years."
To set the scene -- to describe where and when something happened before telling the events
"Let me set the scene: it was raining, and nobody was talking."
Thread -- a series of connected posts that tell one story online
"Somebody wrote a thread explaining the whole timeline, and it was better than the show."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the retelling and answer the questions. Watch how many different past tenses fit into one short paragraph.

Let me set the scene. Two of them were walking through an empty station, talking about nothing important, and the camera was following them from behind. Nothing happens for four minutes, and that is exactly the point, because the show had been building that moment for six episodes.

Then the lights went out. The protagonist stopped, turned around and realized that the person she had been travelling with was not there anymore. The backstory lands in one line: her brother had disappeared two years earlier, and the show had never told us why.

The second time I watched it, I noticed that the red herring had been sitting in the background of every single scene, and I had walked straight past it. That is what a good subplot does. And then it cuts to black, which is a cruel cliffhanger, because the main plot suddenly makes no sense at all.

1. Why is the first paragraph almost entirely in the past continuous?
A Because it sets the scene: it describes what was already going on, before any event in the chain arrives.
B Because the events in it lasted longer than the events in the other paragraphs.
C Because the writer is not sure whether those things really happened.
2. What does "the show had been building that moment for six episodes" tell you that "the show built that moment" would not?
A That the moment was more important than the other moments.
B How long it had been going on before that scene — and that is what makes the silence meaningful instead of empty.
C That the building finished before episode six started.
3. Why is "her brother had disappeared two years earlier" in the past perfect?
A Because it finished before the moment being described — it is one step further back, and that is where backstory lives.
B Because the writer is talking about something that is still happening now.
C Because two years is a long period, and long periods take the past perfect.
4. What is the job of the past simple in the second paragraph?
A To describe the background of the scene.
B To show that the writer is not certain about the order of events.
C To carry the chain of events, one after another: went out, stopped, turned, realized.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Narrative Tenses

GRAMMAR

Every sentence below is in the past, and every one of them is doing a different job. Choose the layer before you choose the words.

TenseFormIts job in the story
Past simplethe lights went outThe chain of events, in order. This is the spine of the story.
Past continuousthey were walkingThe background that was already running when the event arrived. It sets the scene, and it gets interrupted.
Past perfecthe had disappearedOne step further back: finished before the story started. This is where backstory lives.
Past perfect continuousshe had been lookingHow long that earlier thing had been going on, up to the moment of the story. It explains the reaction.
The pair to hearShe had waited for him tells you that it happened. She had been waiting for him tells you for how long — and that is why she reacted like that.
The connectorswhen + past simple cuts in; while + past continuous runs underneath; by the time and before pull you back into the past perfect.
The trapDuration up to a point in the past is had been running, never was running for twenty minutes. And English uses realize for noticing, never for making something.

Stage 2: Put the Five Steps in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the five steps of a good retelling in order.

?Step back with the past perfect to explain something that happened even earlier.
?Return to the past simple and carry the chain of events to the end.
?Set the scene with the past continuous: what was already going on.
?Use the past perfect continuous if the point is how long it had been going on.
?Drop in the first event with the past simple, interrupting the background.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

A friend asks what the characters were doing when the episode started. You:
A "They were walking through an empty station, and the camera was following them."
B "They walked through an empty station and the camera followed them."
C "They had walked through an empty station with the camera behind them."
You want to say that she was tired because of twenty minutes of running. You say:
A "She was exhausted because she was running for twenty minutes."
B "She was exhausted because she had been running for twenty minutes."
C "She was exhausted because she has run for twenty minutes."
Somebody in the group chat is about to describe the last scene, and you have not watched it. You say:
A "No spoilers, please. I have not finished it yet."
B "Please do not tell me the lore of this series."
C "That is a very good cliffhanger for me."
A clue was put in the story on purpose to make you suspect the wrong person. That is:
A a subplot.
B a red herring.
C a character arc.
You are explaining why a character reacts so strongly. The most useful sentence is:
A "She looked for her brother in the first episode."
B "She had been looking for her brother since the first episode."
C "She is looking for her brother since the first episode."
Your friend arrived after the important part. You say:
A "By the time you got here, the whole thing had already happened."
B "By the time you got here, the whole thing already happened."
C "When you got here, the whole thing was happening already before."

Lesson 21 — Once Upon a Time Online

Series
Stranger Things -- Season 1, Episode 1 ("The Vanishing of Will Byers")

The first episode is a masterclass in the layers you studied today. It sets the scene for almost four minutes before anything happens, it drops backstory in single lines instead of explaining it, and it ends on the cleanest cliffhanger on television.

Tip: watch the first ten minutes twice with English subtitles. The first time, just watch. The second time, pause whenever the story steps backwards in time and say the sentence out loud in the past perfect.

Podcast
Hidden Brain (NPR) -- Spoiler Alert!

Twenty-five minutes on why a plot twist works, with a cognitive scientist who studies them for a living. It explains exactly what a red herring does to your brain, and why knowing the ending in advance changes what you notice. The page has the audio and the full transcript side by side.

Tip: listen once without reading. Then open the transcript and find three sentences in the past perfect -- they are almost always the moment where the story steps backwards.

YouTube
TED-Ed -- What makes a hero? (Matthew Winkler)

Four minutes on the shape almost every story you like actually has, from Harry Potter to the games you play. It gives you the map behind the words character arc, protagonist and backstory, and the narration is slow enough to follow without subtitles.

Tip: after watching, take one character you know well and describe their arc in three sentences -- where they started, what had already happened to them, and who they became.
