What Would Dataside Look Like in Five Years? -- The Language of the Long View

A founder answered the five-year question three times, on three pages, and only then understood what she had been asked. Key words: a moat, headcount, to reinvest, runway, to spin off, a trade-off, to double down, dilution. Structure: the second conditional for hypothetical futures.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A moat -- an advantage a competitor cannot copy quickly, even with money
"Our moat is not the client list. It is the twelve engineers who built the platform."
Headcount -- the total number of people a company employs
"Headcount has doubled in three years, and margin has not moved."
To reinvest -- to put profit back into the business instead of taking it out
"For eleven years we reinvested everything, and nobody took a dividend."
Runway -- how many months a company can keep operating on the cash it already has
"Eighteen months of runway sounds comfortable until you try to raise in month sixteen."
To spin off -- to separate one part of a company into a new independent business
"If we spun off the analytics unit, it would need its own board."
A trade-off -- something you give up in order to get something else
"Every one of the three futures had the same trade-off: speed or control."
To double down -- to commit even more to a choice you have already made
"We doubled down on the vertical we already owned and refused every other market."
Dilution -- the fall in your share of ownership when new shares are issued
"The dilution would take me from seventy-one percent to something near forty."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An investor asks what your company would look like in five years. Nobody believes the forecast, including the person who wrote it, so the question is not really about the fifth year. It is a test of whether you have ever seriously imagined a company other than the one you already decided to build. Answering it needs a sentence shape that describes something untrue: if we opened an office in New York, we would need a different kind of leader.

The shape is if plus the past simple, then would plus the bare verb. Nothing in that sentence is about the past. The past form is not marking time here — it is marking distance: this has not happened, and it may never happen. That is the whole difference from the shape you already know. If we open in New York, we will need a leader there is a plan somebody is going to decide. If we opened in New York, we would need a leader there is a picture nobody has agreed to yet. Same office, completely different meeting.

Two details finish the structure. With the verb be, this shape takes were for every person, including I and it: if I were you, if the market were stronger. And when the result is a maybe rather than a certainty, could or might replaces would: if we spun it off, we might lose the platform team.

Two traps, both expensive. The first: would never follows if. Portuguese carries this idea with a dedicated verb form in the first half and a conditional in the second, and the direct translation puts would in both halves — a mistake that marks a speaker as translating rather than thinking. Say if we opened, not if we would open. The second is the permanent target turned inside out: after would, the verb takes no ending at all. Not he would needs. He would need.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Second Conditional for Hypothetical Futures

GRAMMAR

The past form is not time. It is distance from the truth.

JobFormExample
A future you are imagining, not planningIf + past simple, would + bare verbIf we opened in New York, we would need a leader there.
Advice, without giving an orderIf I were you, I would …If I were you, I would wait one more year.
The verb be, at this registerwere for every personIf the market were stronger, we would raise now.
A result you are less sure aboutcould or might for wouldIf we spun it off, we might lose the platform team.
A real plan, not a pictureif + present, will (lesson 4)If we open in New York, we will need a leader there.

Order

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

An investor asks what the company would look like in five years. You describe a version you have not decided on. You say:
A If we would open an office in New York, we would need a different leader.
B If we opened an office in New York, we would need a different kind of leader.
C If we opened an office in New York, we will need a different leader.
A younger founder asks whether he should raise money now. You think it is too early. You say:
A If I was you, I would wait one more year.
B If I were you, I would wait one more year.
C If I would be you, I waited one more year.
The board has actually decided to open the office. You are describing what happens next. You say:
A If we open in September, we will need a leader there by August.
B If we opened in September, we would need a leader there by August.
C If we will open in September, we need a leader there by August.
You want to say the result is possible but not certain. You say:
A If we spun it off, we would maybe lose the platform team, possibly.
B If we spun it off, we might lose the platform team.
C If we would spin it off, we can lose the platform team.

Reflection
