Chapter 3: The Code

The Problem, Without the Person

Who did it is not always the point

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The clause was drafted before the merger."

"This point was raised on the last call."

"It is understood that both sides sign this week."

"The deadline has been moved to the tenth by finance."

In three of them, nobody is named. In one, somebody is. What is the difference?

FormRuleExample
be + past
participle		The fact becomes the subject. The person who did it disappears.The clause was drafted before the merger.
Why you use itBecause on a call with three countries, naming who wrote it turns a technical point into an accusation. Same information, no defensive answer.
It is understood /
it was agreed		The standard opening of professional English when the source is the room, not one person.It is understood that both sides sign this week.
Tensesis/are done · was/were done · will be done · has/have been done. Only be moves; the participle never changes.
+ byAdd by somebody only when the person is the point — an owner, a deadline, an action item. Never hide the owner of a task in a passive.

The test: is the point what happened, or who did it?

Practice

Put It in the Passive

Say it first, then click to check

"The clause ___was drafted before the merger." (draft, past)
"It ___is understood that the timeline cannot move." (understand, present)
"This point ___was raised on the last call, and it is still outstanding." (raise, past)
"The minutes ___will be sent this afternoon." (send, future)
"Nothing ___has been agreed on the penalty yet." (agree, present perfect)
The Habit

Four Movements

Every feedback session is the same four moves, in the same order.

1. Open

The agenda and the time.
"Three points, about fifteen minutes."

2. Walk through

The findings, in order of priority, one at a time.

3. Handle

The interruption, the question out of order, the manager who disagrees.

4. Close

The recap, the takeaway, and what stays outstanding — with an owner and a date.

Whoever opens with the agenda owns the call. Start with the first finding instead, and you lose it at the first interruption.

Chapter 4: The Full Call

Fifteen Minutes, Three Points

Open, walk through, handle, close

Dialogue

The Whole Session

A
Before we start: three points on the agenda today, about fifteen minutes. The indemnity clause, the timeline, and one question for finance.
D
Good. The indemnity is my priority too. Go ahead.
A
Then let me do a short walkthrough. The clause was drafted before the merger, so it still names the old entity. It should be amended before signature.
D
Sorry, quick one — is the payment schedule a blocker for us?
A
Let me finish this one and I will come back to that. On the indemnity: nothing has been agreed with the counterparty yet, so the point is still open.
D
Understood. So where does that leave us? Give me the rundown.
A
Quick recap. One: the indemnity has to be amended — that is the takeaway. Two: the timeline has alignment. Three: the payment schedule stays outstanding. Finance answers by Friday and I will send you the minutes today.
Comprehension

About David

1. Which point does David say is his priority?
The indemnity clause — the first point on the agenda.
2. What does he interrupt with, and is it on the same point?
He asks whether the payment schedule is a blocker. No — that is point three, and Ana is still on point one.
3. How does he react when Ana asks to finish first?
He accepts it ("Understood") and asks for the rundown at the end instead.
Listening

The First Twenty Seconds

A manager on what a well-run call sounds like from the other side. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. How long does he need to know if a call will go well?
The first twenty seconds.
2. What does he say it has nothing to do with?
The lawyer's English — accent and grammar.
3. What one sentence changes the whole room?
How many points there are and how long it will take: "Three points, about fifteen minutes."
4. Why do people interrupt when there is no agenda?
They do not know when the call ends or whether their question will be covered, so they ask it immediately. He says they are not being rude.
Listening 2

The Apology That Costs You the Room

A lawyer on the sentence she stopped saying. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. How did she use to open every call?
With an apology for her English.
2. What did the apology do to the people listening?
They started checking her conclusions — she had told them the next fifteen minutes might not be reliable, and they believed her.
3. What did a colleague tell her to do instead?
Stop apologising and just give the agenda.
4. What does she do with silence now?
She lets it sit while she thinks. A pause reads as somebody thinking; an apology reads as somebody who is not sure.

The Real Thing

Find the Problem in These Minutes

Meeting minutes
Master Services Agreement — contract review

Date: 14 October  ·  Participants: A. Valdivia (legal), D. Whitfield (contracts), finance — not present

Points discussed

1. Indemnity clause — drafted before the merger, still names the old entity.
2. Timeline — end of the quarter, no objection raised.
3. Payment schedule — agreed.

Action items

1. Amend the indemnity clause — A. Valdivia, by 21 October.
2. The payment schedule will be confirmed.
3. The minutes are to be circulated to all stakeholders.

1. Which action items cannot be executed, and why?
Items 2 and 3. Both are passive with no owner and no date: "will be confirmed" and "are to be circulated" by whom, by when? Nobody in the room has to do anything.
2. Which point is classified wrongly?
Point 3. It is marked "agreed", but finance was not present and has not answered. It is still outstanding.
3. How do you close the call out loud so this cannot happen?
With the recap: name each point, say what has alignment and what stays outstanding, and give every action an owner and a date. "I will amend the clause by the twenty-first. Finance answers on the payment schedule by Friday."
