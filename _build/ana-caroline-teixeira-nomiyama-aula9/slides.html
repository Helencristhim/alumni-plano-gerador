Chapter 3: The Code

Although... However... Therefore...

Linking words for a contrast and for a result

Grammar Discovery

Contrast or Result?

"Although the defendant was served, he did not answer the complaint."

"Despite the deadline, the defendant did not file any documents."

"The defendant has assets in Florida. However, he is moving the money to other accounts."

"The money can disappear soon. Therefore, we request an urgent hearing."

Look at the red words: the second idea is a surprise, the opposite of what you expect. Now the blue word: the second idea is a result, a consequence of the first. Then look at what comes after each one: a full sentence, only a noun, or a comma at the start of a new sentence?

JobFormExample
Although + sentencecontrast inside one sentence: subject + verb after itAlthough he was served, he did not answer.
Despite + noun / -ingcontrast, but NO subject + verb after itDespite the deadline / Despite receiving the complaint, he did not answer.
However,contrast between two sentences: period, However, commaHe has assets. However, he is hiding them.
Therefore,result between two sentences, formal: perfect for a motion or a briefThe money can disappear. Therefore, we request a hearing.
soresult inside one sentence, less formal: good for meetingsHe did not answer, so we filed a motion.
The trapdespite never takes of, and although never takes butNot Despite of the deadline or Although he owes, but he.... Say Despite the deadline / Although he owes, he...

In one line: contrast: although + sentence, despite + noun, However, + new sentence; result: so (spoken), Therefore, (written).

Dialogue

Preparing the Motion with Miami

D
Ana, we have a problem with the Coastal Imports case. The defendant never answered the complaint.
A
Although they were served in August? That was more than two months ago.
D
Exactly. And despite the deadline, they are moving money out of their Florida account.
A
Then we need to act fast. I think we should file a motion to freeze the account.
D
I agree. However, the judge will want a strong argument. Can you help me with the brief?
A
Sure. First the facts, then the risk. The money can disappear; therefore, we request an urgent hearing.
D
Perfect. And if the judge doesn’t grant it, we can appeal the ruling.
A
Great. I’ll send you my notes on the argument tomorrow morning.

Listening 1 · A London Barrister

How to Write a Good Brief

A British barrister explains how a judge reads a written argument. Sound first — no text.

1. How much time does a judge usually have for each brief, according to him?
Very little time: often only a few minutes.
2. What should come first in a brief?
The request: what you want the judge to do.
3. Why does he like words like however and therefore?
They show the judge the logic of the argument: where the contrast is and where the result is.
Listening 2 · A Court Clerk

Why Judges Grant Motions

A former law clerk from India, now working in New York, talks about what she saw in court. Sound first — no text.

1. What did she use to do as a law clerk?
She used to read motions and prepare summaries for the judge.
2. Why did the judge deny many motions?
They did not explain the risk: why the decision was urgent.
3. What three things does a good motion show, according to her?
The facts, the risk, and a clear request.
Listening 3 · An In-house Lawyer

Win, Lose or Appeal?

An in-house lawyer at a Dutch bank explains how his team decides to appeal a ruling. Sound first — no text.

1. What does his bank usually do after it loses a case?
It does not appeal automatically; the team studies the ruling first.
2. Why do they sometimes decide not to appeal?
Because an appeal takes time and money, and sometimes the debtor has no assets.
3. What happened in the case he describes?
They appealed, and the higher court changed the ruling, so the bank recovered most of the money.
