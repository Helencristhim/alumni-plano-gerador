Chapter 4: The Sentence That Holds It

A Sentence Inside a Sentence

who, which, that, whose — and the two commas that change everything

Grammar Discovery

What Do the Commas Change?

"A condition that lasts for years does not present a single moment to be diagnosed."

"Arthur Kleinman, whose work is read far outside medicine, made the point differently."

"A patient whose testimony is dismissed stops offering one."

"Narrative medicine, which grew out of clinical practice, is not literary theory."

Delete the red part and the sentence stops making sense. Delete the blue part and the sentence still stands. That difference is the whole rule.

TypeFormExample
Defining — says WHICH oneno commas; that / which / who / whoseA condition that lasts for years...
Non-defining — adds extratwo commas; which / who / whose, never thatKleinman, whose work is read widely, ...
People / thingswho for people, which for thingsthe clinician who listened; a field which grew
whosepossession, for people AND thingsa patient whose testimony is dismissed
Dropping the pronounonly when it is the object, and only if definingthe story (that) the patient lives
The trapno extra pronoun at the endNot the book that I told you about it.

In one line: no commas means you need it; commas mean you could live without it.

Detail

Find the Spine

"Narrative medicine, a field which grew out of clinical practice rather than out of literary theory, argues that the second story is not decoration."

The spine is four words long: narrative medicine argues that. Everything between the commas can wait.

Spine of: "A patient whose testimony is dismissed stops offering one." ___A patient stops offering one
Spine of: "Testimony, in this view, is not a courtesy extended to the suffering." ___Testimony is not a courtesy
Spine of: "A condition that lasts for years presents a life that has been reorganized around it." ___A condition presents a life

Listening 1 · A Physician

Forty Minutes Not Treating Anybody

A doctor explaining why the least clinical hour of her week is the most useful. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she do in those forty minutes, and what does she never do?
She sits with someone who has been ill for a long time and asks them to tell her how it started. She never interrupts.
2. What comes back instead of a list of symptoms?
A story: a job that had to be given up, a marriage reorganized around appointments, a mother who moved in.
3. How does she answer the colleagues who say this is not medicine?
That she has changed treatment plans because of those forty minutes, and never once because of a tidier list of symptoms. The story is where the information about adherence lives.
Listening 2 · A Caregiver

Eleven Years in the Corridor

The person nobody in the building has ever called by his role. Sound first — no text.

1. What three things does he say he is, in that hospital?
The person who remembers the medication schedule, the person who notices the day before a bad week starts, and the person standing in the corridor while the decision is made behind a door.
2. What is the one thing he asks clinicians to do, and why is it not about politeness?
To ask him what he has seen. He has eleven years of observation that is not in the file, and he is not asking to be included out of courtesy.
3. How long does he say it takes, and what does it change?
Ninety seconds, and it changes what they decide.
