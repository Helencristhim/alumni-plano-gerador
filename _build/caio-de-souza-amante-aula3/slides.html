Chapter 3: Still Running

Three Ways to Talk About the Same Six Weeks

The shape a buyer uses for everything that has not finished

Grammar Discovery

Which One Is About the Process?

"We started due diligence in March."

"We have completed three due diligence processes."

"We have been running this one for six weeks."

"Our pipeline has been growing since March, and we have signed four contracts."

The red one names the month and closes. The green one counts a result: three, four. The blue one is not counting anything — it is telling you the activity started, has not stopped, and how long that has been true.

JobFormExample
How long an activity has been going, and it has not stoppedhas / have been + verb-ing + for or sinceWe have been running it for six weeks.
The result, countedhas / have + past participleWe have completed three processes.
Finished, and the time is namedpast simple + a closed timeWe started in March.
The test that decidescan you answer how many, or only how long?How many → have completed. How long → have been running.
Verbs that refuse the -ingbe, have (own), know, need, want, meanNot I have been knowing him. Say I have known him for ten years.
The trapa named time kills both perfect shapesNot Since March we have been growing 11% as a total. Say Last quarter we grew 11%.
The permanent targetthe company and the pipeline are it, so hasNot the pipeline have been growing. Say the pipeline has been growing.

In one line: how many is a result. How long is a process. English uses a different shape for each.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Man Whose Job Is To Check

One call, two recordings, and the list he sends afterwards

Dialogue

Forty Minutes with Corporate Development

J
Caio, thanks for making the time. I have read the one-pager, so I will skip the introductions. How long has the forecasting engine been in production?
C
Two years. We have been building it since 2022, and it has been live with clients for the last fourteen months.
J
And it is proprietary? Nothing licensed underneath it?
C
Entirely ours. That is the part of the business we would not sell separately.
J
Right. Now, the growth. Your pipeline — has it been growing evenly, or did you have one very good quarter that is doing all the work?
C
It has been growing every quarter since March. No single quarter carries it, and no single client is more than eight percent.
J
That is the answer I was hoping for. I should say, our due diligence is fairly unforgiving, so if anything is going to look awkward in the data room, I would rather hear it from you now than find it in week three.
C
Sorry, could you say that last part again, a little slower? I want to be sure I understood you correctly.
J
Of course. If there is bad news in the numbers, tell me today. Not in week three.
C
Understood. There is one thing, and I would rather you heard it from me. And you — what does your side need to see before you put a valuation on the table?
Comprehension

About James

1. What does James say he has already done, and what does he skip because of it?
He has read the one-pager, so he skips the introductions.
2. What is he testing when he asks whether the pipeline has been growing evenly?
Whether one very good quarter is doing all the work, instead of steady growth.
3. What does he ask Caio to do before due diligence starts, and what reason does he give?
To tell him now if anything is going to look awkward in the data room, because he would rather hear it from Caio than find it in week three.
Listening 1 · An M&A Lawyer

The Three Things Founders Get Wrong

An M&A lawyer on what founders do in the first month of diligence. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the first mistake she names?
Answering a question the founder has not checked, because the correction always costs more than the delay.
2. What does she say founders do with bad news, and what does she recommend instead?
They wait, hoping it will look smaller later. She says it never does, and that the buyer should hear it from the founder in week one.
3. What is the third mistake, and why does she say it is the most expensive?
Letting somebody else answer for you. She says the buyer starts pricing the company as if the founder were already gone.
Listening 2 · A CTO

What Survives the Data Room

The CTO of an IT services company on what a buyer actually values. Sound first — no text.

1. How long has his company been acquiring firms, and how many has it bought?
Eleven years, and nine companies.
2. What does he say does not survive the data room?
The story about culture and about being different, because every company says it and nothing in the documents supports it.
3. What does he say he pays a premium for?
Something proprietary that has been running in production long enough to prove it works, and a founder who has been answering the same questions consistently since the first call.
