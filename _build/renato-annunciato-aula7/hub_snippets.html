The Cross-Functional Meeting -- Proposing and Deciding

Ten minutes on the agenda with medical and marketing, and a decision at the end. Key words: agenda item, trade-off, to align, rationale, constraint, to push back, buy-in, contingency, consensus, to table, deadline, workaround. Structure: the first conditional, for promising something that depends on a condition.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words of the room where nobody reports to you.

Agenda item -- one topic on the list for a meeting
"The coverage plan is the third agenda item today."
Trade-off -- what you lose when you choose one thing over another
"The trade-off is fewer visits in the south for six months."
To align -- to make two teams agree on the same plan
"I need to align with medical before the meeting."
Rationale -- the reason behind a decision, in one clear sentence
"The rationale is simple: half of the interior is not covered."
Constraint -- something that limits what you are able to do
"Our only constraint is the training budget."
To push back -- to say no politely, and give a reason for it
"Marketing pushed back on the date, not on the plan."
Buy-in -- real agreement from the people who have to make it work
"Without buy-in from medical, the plan stays on paper."
Contingency -- a second plan, for when the first one does not work
"The contingency is to start with two clinics instead of ten."
Consensus -- a decision that everyone in the room can live with
"We reached consensus on the date, not on the budget."
To table -- to leave a topic for a later meeting
"Let us table the pricing question until October."
Deadline -- the date something has to be finished by
"The deadline for the medical review is the fifteenth."
Workaround -- a temporary way around a problem, without fixing it
"The workaround is to use the old form until the system is ready."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the proposal and answer the questions. Notice which half of each sentence takes will and which half does not.

Renato has ten minutes on the agenda item list, between medical and marketing. He opens with the rationale, in one sentence: half of the interior is not covered today. Then he makes the request. If medical approves the training material by the fifteenth, the team will start in October. He does not say "if medical will approve", because nothing after if ever takes will.

Nadia names her constraint immediately: her team has three submissions that month, so she may push back on the date. Renato is ready for that. If the fifteenth is not possible, the contingency is to start with two clinics instead of ten, and the workaround for the paperwork is the old form. He says it before anyone asks, because a plan with no visible cost sounds like a plan that is hiding one.

Then he names the trade-off himself: fewer visits in the south for six months. That is what buys him the buy-in. They reach consensus on the date and table the budget question until October. Renato will send the numbers as soon as he has them, and the deadline goes into the decision log. Unless something changes, the two teams will align again in four weeks.

1. Why is it "if medical approves" and not "if medical will approve"?
A Because the half of the sentence that begins with if uses the present simple, even though it is about the future.
B Because "approve" is an irregular verb.
C Because medical is a department and departments take the present simple.
2. "Renato will send the numbers as soon as he has them." Why not "as soon as he will have them"?
A Because "as soon as" is informal and informal English drops will.
B Because as soon as, when, before, after and until follow the same rule as if.
C Because the sentence is about the past.
3. "Unless something changes, the two teams will align again in four weeks." What does unless mean here?
A Because something changes.
B If something does not change.
C After something changes.
4. Why does Renato name the trade-off before anyone asks about it?
A Because the meeting rules require it.
B Because it makes the plan look cheaper than it is.
C Because a plan with no visible cost sounds like a plan that is hiding one — and naming it is what buys the buy-in.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The First Conditional

GRAMMAR

One question decides it: is that half the condition, or the result? The condition never takes will.

PartShapeExample
The conditionif + present simpleIf medical approves the material...
The resultwill / will not + verb...we will start in October.
The orderEither half can come first. Comma only when the if half comes first. "If we align, we will start." / "We will start if we align."
Same rule, other wordswhen · as soon as · before · after · until · unless — all present simple, never will.
Unlessunless = if notUnless we align this week, the plan will not be ready.
Less certain resultSwap will for may · might · canIf they push back, we may lose two weeks.
Two trapsNever If we will approve it. And never swap in would — would is for the imaginary; a proposal on the table is real, so it takes will.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

You have ten minutes on the agenda and the chair says "start with the reason". You say:
A "Let me give you some background on the market first."
B "The rationale is simple: half of the interior is not covered today."
C "I have twelve slides, so I will go quickly."
You want to promise October, but it depends on their approval. You say:
A "If you will approve the material, we will start in October."
B "If you approve the material, we will start in October."
C "If you approve the material, we would start in October."
The plan costs the south six months of fewer visits. In the meeting you:
A Name the trade-off yourself, before anyone asks about it.
B Leave it out, because nobody has noticed it yet.
C Mention it at the end, if there is time left.
Medical says the fifteenth is impossible. The strongest answer is:
A "But we really need the fifteenth, it is very important for us."
B "Then the contingency is to start with two clinics instead of ten."
C "If you will not approve it, we cannot do anything."
The budget question needs another meeting. You say:
A "Let us forget the budget question."
B "Let us table the budget question until October."
C "The budget question is not an agenda item for anybody."
The meeting is ending and nobody has said a date. You:
A Ask for one: a decision with no deadline is not a decision.
B Say nothing, because everyone seemed to agree.
C Send an email next month to ask what was decided.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

Take a decision you actually need from another area in the next few weeks. Make the proposal in ninety seconds, in three parts: the rationale in one sentence, the trade-off named before anyone asks, and the ask with a real date. Use at least two first conditionals, and offer one contingency.

Lesson 7 — The Cross-Functional Meeting -- Proposing and Deciding

Series
The Diplomat (A Diplomata) -- Season 1, Episode 1

Every episode is a room full of people from different areas who need each other and do not report to each other. That is exactly your cross-functional meeting, with higher stakes and better lighting. Listen for how the characters ask for something without ever sounding like they are begging for it.

Tip: watch with English subtitles. Every time somebody makes a conditional promise, write it down: "if you do this, I will do that". You will collect six or seven in one episode.

Podcast
Think Fast, Talk Smart -- Communicating Through Conflict: How to Get Along with Anyone

A free episode from the Stanford business school on the moment somebody in the room says no. The argument is that disagreement is not the problem, and that most meetings go wrong before the conflict, not during it. Around thirty minutes, clear American English, transcript on the page.

Tip: listen once without the transcript, on the way to work. Then pick one phrase for pushing back politely and say it out loud, in English, three times.

Listen on Stanford GSB ↗
YouTube
Celeste Headlee -- 10 Ways to Have a Better Conversation (TED)

Eleven minutes from a radio interviewer on the habits that quietly ruin a conversation, and almost all of them show up in meetings: finishing other people's sentences, waiting to speak instead of listening, and answering a question that nobody asked. She speaks fast and clearly, which is useful training on its own.

Tip: watch it once with subtitles and once without. Then choose the single habit you recognise in your own meetings and try dropping it for one week.
