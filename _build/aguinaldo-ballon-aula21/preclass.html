Lesson 21 -- Pre-class

The Language of Persuasion -- Cleft Sentences and Fronting

A foreign fund is skeptical about a port concession in Brazil. Key words: compelling, to win over, buy-in, a selling point, to make a case for, to appeal to, skeptical, credibility, to back up, a deal-breaker. Structure: cleft sentences (What... is, It is... that) and fronting for emphasis.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Compelling -- so strong and clear that people believe it or want to act
"The numbers make a compelling case for the port."
To win over -- phrasal verb: to persuade someone to support you or agree with you
"We need to win over the two partners in London."
Buy-in -- agreement and support from the people involved in a decision
"Without buy-in from the board, the deal will not move."
A selling point -- a feature that makes something attractive to buyers or investors
"The location is the main selling point of the port."
To make a case for -- expression: to give reasons why something is a good idea
"Let me make a case for this project."
To appeal to -- to try to interest someone by speaking to what they want or need
"A good pitch appeals to the investor's real concerns."
Skeptical -- not easily convinced; having doubts
"The committee is skeptical about new markets."
Credibility -- the quality of being believed and trusted
"Ten years of stable rules give the project credibility."
To back up -- phrasal verb: to support a claim with facts or evidence
"You need to back up every claim with data."
A deal-breaker -- a problem so serious that it makes you reject an agreement
"For this fund, a change in the tariff rules is a deal-breaker."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A fund from Canada is looking at a port concession in the south of Brazil, but its committee is skeptical. Aguinaldo has twenty minutes to win them over. He knows one thing: what the committee needs is one clear idea, not ten arguments.

So he starts with their concern. "It is the rules that worry you, not the port," he says. "The currency risk, you can manage. The political risk, we can explain." Then he backs up every point with a number from the memo.

At the end, he makes one request. "All I need is your buy-in before Thursday." Notice how each sentence puts the focus on one idea: What... is, It is... that, and the object first for contrast.

1. What worries the committee, according to Aguinaldo?
A The location of the port and the cost of the export contracts in the south of Brazil.
B The rules.
C The currency, because the revenues of the port are in reais and not in dollars.
2. What does Aguinaldo think the committee needs?
A One clear idea.
B Ten strong arguments, so that every member of the committee has a reason to say yes.
C A long presentation with all the numbers from the memo and every possible risk of the project.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A "Is the rules that worry you, not the port or the currency or the location of the project."
B "What the committee needs it is one clear idea, and not ten arguments about the project."
C "It is the rules that worry you."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences: What... Is, It Is... That

GRAMMAR

To put the focus on your key idea, start with What + subject + verb + is, or It is + X + that. To contrast two things, put the object first.

JobFormExample
Point to the key ideaWhat + subject + verb + is ...What investors need is a stable rule.
Point to a person or a thingIt is / It was + X + that / who ...It is the regulator that decides.
Name the reason or the problemThe reason why ... is · The thing that ... isThe reason why we are here is the port.
Only one thingAll + subject + verb + is ...All we need is one more signature.
Contrast two thingsfronting: object + subject + verbThe currency risk, we can manage.
The trapnever drop the it, never add an extra itNot Is the regulator that decides · not What we need it is.

Never: Is the regulator that decides · What we need it is · The reason because · All what we need

Stage 2: Put the Pitch in Order

Order

You speak to a skeptical investment committee. Put your sentences in a logical order.

?"Thank you for the time. Let me make a case for the South Port."
?"I know it is the rules that worry you most."
?"What makes this port different is ten years of stable rules."
?"The currency risk, we can manage with export contracts in dollars."
?"A change in the tariff formula is protected by a clause in the contract."
?"All we need now is your buy-in before Thursday."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want the committee to focus on stable rules. You say:
A "What investors need it is a stable rule, and this port has had one for ten years."
B "What investors need is a stable rule."
C "Is a stable rule what the investors need it, and this port has had one for ten years."
Someone thinks the government decides the tariff. You correct them:
A "It is the regulator that decides the tariff."
B "Is the regulator that decides the tariff, not the government, and it is independent by law."
C "The regulator it is who decides the tariff, not the government, and it is independent."
You want to contrast two risks. You say:
A "We can manage the currency risk and the political risk, because both of them are small."
B "The currency risk, we can manage. The political risk, we cannot."
C "The currency risk we can manage it, and the political risk we cannot manage it at all."
The fund will never accept a change in the tariff formula. For them, it is:
A "A selling point, because it makes the project more attractive to the investors in London."
B "Buy-in, because the partners in London need to agree with the change before Thursday."
C "A deal-breaker."
