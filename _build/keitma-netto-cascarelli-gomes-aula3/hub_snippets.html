Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A breakdown -- the same number, shown in the parts it is made of
"Here is the breakdown: three contracts, and one of them is new."
To drill down -- to open a number and look at what is inside it
"Let me drill down into that line before I answer you."
A line item -- one single row of a report, a budget or an invoice
"Only one line item moved, and it moved a lot."
An assumption -- something you accept as true in order to build a number on it
"The forecast is only as good as the assumption behind it."
To trace something back to -- to follow a number backwards to where it started
"I traced the whole difference back to one invoice from March."
Immaterial -- too small to change the conclusion
"The difference is immaterial, so we are not reopening the period."
A one-off -- something that happened once and will not happen again
"That cost is a one-off, so the trend has not changed."
To offset -- when one number cancels part of another one out
"The higher cost is offset by a credit we received in June."
The rationale -- the reasoning behind a number, said in words
"They did not question the number. They wanted the rationale."
To double-check -- to check a second time before you send it
"Give me ten minutes to double-check it, and then I will confirm."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nobody in a review call is asking for a number. The number is already on the screen, in front of everybody, and it has been there since before the meeting started. What the room is waiting for is the half of the sentence that comes after it — the part that says why. A person who gives only the figure has answered a question nobody asked.

English marks that second half with a small set of words, and the first thing to notice is that they do not all sit in the same place. Because, since and as are followed by the reason, and the reason has to be a full sentence with its own verb: the cost went up because we signed two new contracts. So is the mirror image — it is followed by the result: we signed two new contracts, so the cost went up. Therefore does the same job as so in a more formal register, which is why it belongs in an e-mail and rarely in speech. And which is why attaches a result to the whole sentence that came before it: the item is a one-off, which is why the forecast does not change.

Two traps catch almost everybody. The first is due to and because of. They also introduce a reason, but they demand a noun, not a sentence: due to the late invoice, never due to the invoice arrived late. The second trap is doubling. Many languages allow both halves to be marked at once, and English does not: a sentence takes because or so, and choosing one of them is not a stylistic preference, it is the grammar. Notice what this does to an explanation under pressure: once the connector is chosen, the rest of the sentence has only one place to go.

1. According to the text, what is the room actually waiting for in a review call?
A The number, because nobody has seen it yet.
B The half of the sentence that says why — the number is already on the screen.
C A written report to be sent after the meeting.
2. What is the difference between because and so, in the text?
A Because is followed by the reason; so is followed by the result.
B Because is formal and so is informal, but they take the same half.
C There is no difference; both introduce the reason.
3. Why is due to the invoice arrived late wrong?
A Because due to is too formal for spoken English.
B Because the reason has to come after the result.
C Because due to demands a noun, not a sentence with a verb in it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cause and Result

GRAMMAR

Six words. Each one decides which half of your sentence comes next.

WordWhat follows itExample
because · since · asthe reason, as a full sentenceThe cost went up because we signed two contracts.
sothe result, as a full sentenceWe signed two contracts, so the cost went up.
thereforethe result, in a formal register — e-mail and reportsThe item is immaterial. Therefore, we did not reopen the period.
which is whythe result, attached to the whole sentence before itIt is a one-off, which is why the forecast does not change.
due to · because ofthe reason, as a nounDue to the late invoice, the cost sits in this month.
the first trapdue to and because of never take a verb after themNot due to the invoice arrived late. Say due to the late invoice.
the second trapone sentence takes because or so, never bothNot because it is a one-off, so I kept the forecast.
the one that travels with ityou explain something to a person, and a credit lands on a line, in JuneI explained the rationale to the controller on Thursday.

Never: due to the invoice arrived late · because it is a one-off, so I kept the forecast · explain the rationale for the controller · the credit is at the same line item

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to give the reason as a noun, not as a sentence. You say:
A Due to the invoice arrived late, the cost sits in June.
B Due to the late invoice, the cost sits in June.
C Due to because the invoice was late, the cost sits in June.
A director asks whether the increase will come back next quarter. You say:
A No. It is a one-off, which is why I am not changing the forecast.
B No, because it is a one-off, so the forecast is the same.
C No, is one time only thing, the forecast no changes.
You do not have the figure in front of you and the director wants it now. You say:
A I think it is around six hundred thousand, more or less.
B What I can confirm is the total. Give me ten minutes to double-check the split, and I will come back before the review.
C I will check and come back to you.
You are writing this in an e-mail, not saying it out loud. The best connector is:
A The item is immaterial, so we did not reopen the period.
B The item is immaterial. Therefore, we did not reopen the period.
C The item is immaterial, because we did not reopen the period.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Take one real number from your last close and defend it, from the raw data to the conclusion. Say what the number is, what caused the movement, what offsets it, and whether it happens again. Finish with one question back.
