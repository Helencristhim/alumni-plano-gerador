Lesson 13 -- Pre-class

Third Conditional -- The Decision You Would Make Differently

Answering the hardest question in a senior interview: what you would have done differently, and what you changed because of it. Key words: hindsight, assumption, blind spot, to flag, to underestimate, fallout, to second-guess, to course-correct. Structure: third conditional -- if plus had plus a past participle for the condition, would have plus a past participle for the imagined result, with could have and might have for a softer version.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Hindsight -- the understanding of a situation that you only have after it has happened
"In hindsight, that was the assumption nobody tested."
Assumption -- something you believe is true without checking it
"The whole plan rested on one assumption about the vendor."
Blind spot -- a risk or a detail that you fail to notice, because of where you are standing
"Cost was our blind spot; everyone was watching the schedule."
To flag -- to point out a possible problem early, so that other people notice it
"One engineer flagged the cost risk in week two."
To underestimate -- to think something is smaller, easier or cheaper than it really is
"We underestimated the migration by six weeks."
Fallout -- the bad results that follow a decision or an event
"The fallout lasted two quarters."
To second-guess -- to doubt or question a decision after it has already been made
"I do not second-guess a call I made with the information I had."
To course-correct -- to change direction while the work is still running
"We course-corrected in month four, and the numbers recovered."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

In March the group chose a full rollout instead of a small test. The vendor had promised six weeks, and that promise became the assumption nobody checked. The migration ran twelve weeks, and the fallout lasted two quarters. In hindsight, the review is short: if the group had run a small test first, it would have found the gap in week two. Notice the shape of that sentence. The if half takes had plus a past participle, and the other half takes would have plus a past participle. Nothing in it happened -- it is the version of March that never existed. There was one more signal. An engineer flagged the cost risk in February, and her email received no answer, because everyone was watching the schedule. Cost was the blind spot. If the director had taken that email seriously, the team would have course-corrected three months earlier, and the group would not have lost the quarter. The team underestimated the work, and that is a fact in the past simple. Whether they should still second-guess the call is a different question: the decision was made with the information available at the time, and the process is what changed.

1. Why does the text say "if the group had run a small test first" and not "if the group ran a small test first"?
A Because the test is still possible today.
B Because the condition is in the past and it did not happen, so the if half takes "had" plus a past participle.
C Because "run" has no past simple form.
2. In "the team would have course-corrected three months earlier", did the team course-correct?
A No. "Would have" describes a result that never happened.
B Yes, three months before the deadline.
C Yes, but only after the email was answered.
3. Why is "the team underestimated the work" in the past simple?
A Because it is a condition, and conditions always take the past simple.
B Because the sentence is short.
C Because it really happened. Only the imagined version needs "had" and "would have".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Third Conditional

GRAMMAR

How English talks about a past that never happened.

RuleFormExample
The conditionif + had + past participle"if we had run a smaller test"
The resultwould have + past participle"we would have caught it in week two"
Negativeif + had not (hadn't) + participle
would not (wouldn't) have + participle		"If we hadn't underestimated it..."
"The fallout wouldn't have been so big."
Softer resultcould have / might have + participle"We might have saved the quarter."
Word order and the one big trapEither half can open the sentence, and the comma appears only when the if half comes first. The word would never enters the if half: "If we would have flagged it" is not English — it is "If we had flagged it, we would have fixed it."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each real executive moment.

You want to say that a smaller test would have found the problem. Which is correct?
A "If we would have run a smaller test, we would have found it."
B "If we had run a smaller test, we would have found it."
C "If we had run a smaller test, we would find it."
You are imagining a different decision today, not in the past. Which is correct?
A "If we had that data today, I would make a different call."
B "If we had had that data today, I would have made a different call."
C "If we would have that data today, I made a different call."
A recruiter asks about a decision you would make differently. Which opening is strongest?
A "Honestly, there is nothing I would change."
B "I am so sorry about that project, it was really my fault."
C "We chose a full rollout instead of a small test. If we had run the test first, we would have found the gap in week two."
You want to soften the imagined result, because you are not certain. Which is best?
A "If we had flagged it, we will have saved the quarter."
B "If we had flagged it, we might have saved the quarter."
C "If we had flagged it, we would save the quarter for sure."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Think of one decision in your career that you would make differently today. Answer in three parts: first say what happened and what the assumption was, in the past simple; then say what would have happened if you had chosen differently, using "if" plus "had" plus a past participle and "would have" plus a past participle at least twice; and finish in the present with what you do differently now. Take your time.
