Lesson 13 · Looking Back

"If We Had Known..."

Every senior interview asks about a decision you would make differently. Tonight you learn the sentence that answers it.

Chapter 1: Looking Back

From What Is Running to What Never Happened

Last time you reported the work still in progress: "We have been piloting the process since March." Tonight you do the opposite. You take a decision that is finished, and you describe the version of it that never happened: "If we had run a smaller pilot, we would have caught it in week two." That sentence is how a senior candidate talks about a mistake without apologizing for it.

The Document

The Decision Review

Decision Review
Platform Rollout · Q1
Prepared for: L. Serra, Director · Retrospective, week 1

Decision. In March the group chose a full rollout instead of a small test.

Assumption. The vendor promised six weeks. Nobody checked that promise.

Signal missed. The cost risk was flagged on February 14. It received no written answer.

Result. The migration ran twelve weeks. Fallout: one lost quarter.

Conclusion. If we had run a small test first, we would have found the gap in week two.

1. What was the assumption behind the decision?
That the vendor's promise of six weeks was real. Nobody checked it.
2. According to the conclusion, what would have happened if the group had run a small test first?
They would have found the gap in week two.
The Big Idea

Three Sentences, Three Times

What happened

past simple: "We chose a full rollout."

What never happened

tonight's grammar: "If we had tested it, we would have found the gap."

What you do now

present: "Every rollout starts with a test."

Stop at the first sentence and you sound careless. Stop at the second and you sound sorry. Say all three and you sound like someone who learns.

Chapter 3: The Past That Never Happened

"We Would Have Caught It"

The grammar of the other version of the story

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If we had run a smaller pilot, we would have caught the problem in week two."

"If the team had flagged the cost risk earlier, the fallout would have been much smaller."

"We would not have lost the quarter if somebody had questioned that assumption."

"If I had known then what I know now, I might have course-corrected in month two."

None of these things happened. The blue part is the condition, the orange part is the result. What is the pattern in each color?

RuleFormExample
The conditionif + had + past participle"if we had run a smaller pilot"
The resultwould have + past participle"we would have caught it in week two"
Negativeif + had not (hadn't) + participle
would not (wouldn't) have + participle		"If we hadn't underestimated it..."
"The fallout wouldn't have been so big."
Softer resultcould have / might have + participle"We might have saved the quarter."
Word orderEither half can come first. The comma appears only when the if half opens the sentence: "If we had flagged it, we would have fixed it." / "We would have fixed it if we had flagged it."

This is the only structure in English for a past that did NOT happen. The word would never enters the if half — that half always takes had.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If we would have flagged it, the fallout would have been smaller."
"If we had flagged it, the fallout would have been smaller."
"If we had run a pilot, we would fix the problem in week two."
"If we had run a pilot, we would have fixed the problem in week two."
"If I had knew the numbers, I would have said no."
"If I had known the numbers, I would have said no."

The if half takes had and never would. The other half needs the full would have — drop the have and you are talking about today, not about March.

Practice

Build the Other Version

Detail

Second or Third Conditional?

Imagined today → second. Imagined in the past → third.

"If we had that data today, I ___would make a different call right now." (make)
"If we had had that data in March, I ___would have made a different call." (make)
"If I were you, I ___would flag it at the next review." (flag)
"If the vendor had been honest about the timeline, we ___would not have signed the contract." (not sign)
Chapter 4: The Interview Question

Mark Asks About the Mistake

A senior recruiter for a VP of AI Safety role

Dialogue

The Decision You Would Make Differently

M
Last question, Lucia. Tell me about a decision you would make differently today.
L
The Q1 rollout. We chose a full rollout instead of a small test, because the vendor promised six weeks. That was the assumption, and nobody checked it.
M
And what would the small test have told you?
L
Everything. If we had run a smaller test first, we would have found the gap in week two. Instead we underestimated the migration by six weeks, and the fallout lasted two quarters.
M
Did anyone see it coming?
L
One engineer flagged the cost risk in February. I read her email and moved on, because we were all watching the schedule. Cost was our blind spot. If I had taken that email seriously, we would have course-corrected three months earlier.
M
That is a hard thing to say out loud. Do you still second-guess it?
L
No. In hindsight the call was wrong, but I made it with the information I had. What changed is the process: every rollout now starts with a test, and every flagged risk gets a written answer within a week.
Comprehension

About Mark

1. At what point in the interview does Mark ask this question?
At the very end — he introduces it as his last question.
2. After Lucia names the decision, what does Mark want to know?
What the small test would have told her.
3. What does Mark say before his final question, and what does he ask?
He says it is a hard thing to say out loud, and he asks whether she still second-guesses the decision.
Listening

Opening the Retrospective

A director opens the review of a rollout that went wrong. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What ground rule does he set before the review starts?
This is a retrospective, not a trial: nobody will be blamed for a decision that looked reasonable in March.
2. Why did the group choose the bigger rollout?
Because the vendor promised a six-week timeline, and they believed that promise without checking it.
3. What two changes does he announce at the end?
Every rollout starts with a small test, and every flagged risk gets a written answer within a week.
Listening 2

How to Answer It Without Apologizing

An interview coach on the question that ends most senior interviews. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What are the two bad ways candidates answer the question?
They get defensive and say there is nothing they would change, or they apologize for four minutes.
2. What are the three sentences she recommends?
Name the decision and the assumption in the past simple, say what would have happened with a third conditional, and say what you changed since, in the present.
3. Which grammar mistake does she warn about?
Putting "would" inside the if half: "If we would have flagged it" instead of "If we had flagged it".

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can use "if" plus "had" plus a past participle for a past that did not happen.
I can use "would have", "could have" and "might have" for the imagined result.
I can keep "would" out of the "if" half, which is the mistake most candidates make.
I can answer "Tell me about a decision you would make differently" in three sentences, without apologizing.
I can review a decision with hindsight, assumption, blind spot, to flag, to underestimate, fallout, to second-guess and to course-correct.
