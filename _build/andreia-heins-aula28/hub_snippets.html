Defending Your Position -- Argumentation

Boston wants to cut the simulator training in half, and you defend your view. Key words: claim, counterargument, valid, outweigh, stand by, persuade, common ground, drawback, compelling, debate. Structure: accept, then defend -- although, even though, while, despite.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Claim -- something you say is true, often before you show proof
"Start with a clear claim in one sentence."
Counterargument -- a reason you give against another person's idea
"Prepare a counterargument for each of his points."
Valid -- reasonable and based on good reasons
"That is a valid question, and I have the data."
Outweigh -- to be more important or bigger than something else
"For patients, the benefits outweigh the risks."
Stand by -- to keep your opinion, even when other people disagree
"I stand by what I said in the last meeting."
Persuade -- to make someone agree with you or do something
"Real cases can persuade people more than numbers."
Common ground -- ideas or interests that both sides share
"Patient safety is the common ground of every team."
Drawback -- a problem or a negative part of a plan
"Every new protocol has at least one drawback."
Compelling -- so strong and clear that people believe it
"The numbers from last year were compelling."
Debate -- a discussion where people give different opinions
"The debate about training hours took an hour."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Paul, the director of the training program in Boston, wants to cut the simulator training for new nurses from forty hours to twenty. Andreia does not agree, so there is a debate at the Thursday meeting. Her claim is simple: new nurses need forty hours.

First, she accepts the part of his view that is valid. "While I understand your concern, safety comes first." "Although the training takes time, it reduces errors." Then she gives her counterargument with compelling data: last year, nurses with forty hours were faster and more confident. Notice what comes after the linkers: although the training takes -- a full sentence; despite the smaller budget -- only a noun.

She is honest about the drawback: the training takes time. But despite the smaller budget, the benefits outweigh the costs. She stands by her position, and she proposes some common ground: forty hours for new nurses and twenty for experienced ones. Will she persuade Paul? He says he is open to a real debate.

1. What does Paul want to do?
A Buy a second simulator for the Boston team and train all the experienced nurses again.
B Cut the simulator training to twenty hours.
C Cancel the Thursday meeting and decide about the training hours by email next month.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "Despite the training takes a lot of time, it reduces errors in the operating room."
B "Although the training takes time, but it reduces errors in the operating room."
C "Although the training takes time, it reduces errors."
3. What common ground does Andreia propose?
A Forty hours for new nurses, twenty for experienced ones.
B Twenty hours for everyone, and a bigger budget for the simulator next year.
C Forty hours for everyone, and no training at all for the nurses in Boston.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Accept, Then Defend

GRAMMAR

The linker accepts the other side. The other part of the sentence defends your position.

LinkerWhat comes nextExample
althoughsubject + verbAlthough the course is expensive, we need it.
even thoughsubject + verb (stronger)Even though it looks cheaper, it costs more.
whilesubject + verb (polite)While I understand your concern, safety comes first.
despitenoun or -ingDespite the cost, ... / Despite being busy, ...

Remember: no but in the same sentence (Although it is long, the nurses learn a lot), and no of after despite (despite the cost, not despite of the cost).

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best sentence for each moment of the debate.

Paul says the training is too expensive. You accept that, but you still want forty hours. You say:
A "Despite the training is expensive, we need forty hours for every new nurse in the team."
B "It is not expensive. You are wrong."
C "Although it is expensive, we need forty hours."
A colleague asks why your argument is strong. You say:
A "Because I spoke first and I spoke louder than everybody else in the meeting room."
B "Because the data from last year is compelling."
C "Because I did not mention any drawback, so nobody could ask me difficult questions."
Paul still does not agree after ten minutes. You want to keep your view, but also move forward. You say:
A "I stand by my position, but let's find some common ground."
B "OK, you win. Twenty hours is fine, and we can forget about my proposal."
C "Despite of your concern, I stand by my position and I will not change anything."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Think of a real decision at work where you have a strong opinion (training, staff, equipment, schedules). Record 30 to 45 seconds: make your claim, accept one point of the other side with although, even though, while or despite, give one piece of evidence, and propose some common ground.
