Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

What If Everything Goes Right? -- The Plan and the Picture

Two futures in the same year, and an English that changes shape depending on whether you believe in the future you are describing. Key words: a scenario, realistic, feasibility, an outcome, likelihood, to envision, a contingency, in the event that, to pull something off, it all depends on. Structure: first and second conditionals contrasted.

A scenario -- one possible version of how things could go
"Neither scenario is the sad one."
Realistic -- describes a plan that could actually happen, not one that only sounds good
"Saying it out loud only means I have looked at it, and I can tell you whether it is realistic."
Feasibility -- whether a thing can actually be done with the time and the money you have
"She spent a month on the feasibility question before anything else."
An outcome -- the result, once the thing has finished happening
"If I only prepare for one outcome, I am refusing to look at the likelihood."
Likelihood -- how probable something is, from almost never to almost certainly
"Eighty thousand candidates and four hundred places is a likelihood, not an opinion."
To envision -- to see a future clearly enough in your head to describe it out loud
"Not what would be wonderful, but what she could envision herself doing for six years."
A contingency -- a second real plan, prepared in case the first one does not happen
"A contingency is not a lesser thing you would settle for."
In the event that -- a formal way of saying if, used in writing and in official notices
"In the event that I do not get the place, I will go, and I will also be happy."
To pull something off -- to succeed at something difficult that other people doubted
"That is what let me pull it off."
It all depends on -- introduces the one thing that decides everything else
"It all depends on what you think I was buying."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of talking about a future that has not happened, and they are not levels of politeness. They are levels of belief. The first one is for a future you actually expect: if plus the present simple, then will. If I get the place, I will stay. If I do not get it, I will go. Both halves are about January, both are possible, and nothing in the sentence says which is more likely.

The second one is for a future you are only looking at. Here the verb after if steps back one, into the past, and will becomes would. If I weren’t studying for this exam, I would probably be training every day. The step back is not about time — the sentence is about now. It is a signal, and what it signals is: this is not happening. That is also why the verb to be takes were for every person in this shape. If I were you, not if I was you. You will hear was in speech, and you will never see it in an exam.

Two traps, and both come straight from Portuguese. The first is putting the future inside the if half: If I will pass. English does not allow it — the if half stays in the present, always. The second is the mirror image: If I would have more time. The would belongs in the other half. Say If I had more time, I would sleep more. One last thing, small and constant: in English something depends on something, never depends of.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- First and Second Conditionals

GRAMMAR

Step the verb back one and will becomes would. That is the difference between a plan and a picture.

JobFormExample
Real, and likelyif + present simple … will + bare verbIf I get the place, I will stay.
Real, but less certain… may · might · could + bare verbIf I pass, I might move to another city.
Imagined, and not happening nowif + past simple … would + bare verbIf I weren’t studying, I would be working.
Imagined, with the verb to beif + were, for every personIf I were you, I would apply to both.
Formal, in writing onlyin the event that + present simpleIn the event that the result is negative, the file stays open.
Word orderthe if half can come second, and then no commaI will stay if I get the place.
The trapnever will inside the if halfNot If I will pass. Say If I pass.

Never: If I will pass · If I would have more time · If I was you (in writing) · It depends of the result
