Chapter 1: The Quarter You Have to Explain

The Decision You Would Take Back

One decision from this year that you would make differently today. Thirty seconds. Do not apologise for it — explain it.

Where Credibility Is Lost

Three Ways to Lose a Post-Mortem

The defence

nothing was anybody's fault and the market simply moved — nobody believes it, and nothing changes

The confession

ten minutes of what you should have done — sounds like courage, reads as a person who has lost the plot

The judgment

what was known, what was decided, what it cost, and what changes on Monday

Only the third one survives the second question. In English it is carried almost entirely by seven small structures, and choosing between them is the whole skill.

Chapter 3: The Code

What Are You Actually Claiming?

Modal perfects — regret, deduction, option and counterfactual

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"We should have tested that reading in June, and we did not."

"The pressure must have been building since March."

"We could have re-priced the two largest accounts in July."

"A monthly review might have saved two of the four points."

Four sentences, one June. Three of them describe something that never happened. One describes something that did. The shape is identical — so what is carrying the difference?

FormWhat you are claimingExample
should have + past participleit was available, we did not do it, and I accept that. Powerful once, fatal five timesWe should have tested that reading in June.
must have + past participlededuction: the only reading that fits what we know. Not proof, and not a guessThe pressure must have been building since March.
cannot have + past participlededuction the other way — the explanation you are ruling out for the roomIt cannot have been the pricing, because pricing was flat all year.
could have + past participlethe option that existed — without yet saying it was the right oneWe could have re-priced the two largest accounts in July.
might / may have + past participlea possible cause or a possible outcome you are not asserting. The honest size of a counterfactualIt might have saved two of the four points.
would have + past participlethe result of the road not taken, usually after if + past perfectIf we had re-priced in July, it would have covered half of the gap.
The form never movesmodal + HAVE + past participle. Never should had. Never must have to be — must have to is obligation, a different structure. And in speech it is should've, must've, could've, never spelled out and never written should of.
The modal is the claimSame fact, four sentences, four different positions in the room. Choosing the modal is not grammar — it is what you are prepared to defend.

The Executive Point

One Quarter, Four Different Claims

should have — I accept it

"We should have tested that reading in June."

Buys credibility once. Said five times, the room stops hearing accountability and starts hearing distress.

must have — I deduce it

"The pressure must have been building since March."

You are reasoning in public. The next question will be about evidence, which is a question you want.

could have — the option existed

"We could have re-priced in July."

You name the road not taken without conceding it was the better one. That argument comes next, on your terms.

might have — I am not asserting

"It might have saved two of the four points."

The honest size of the counterfactual. Claiming it would have saved all four is the fastest way to lose the room.

One sentence of should have, early and in the past. Everything after it in must, could, might — and in October.

Chapter 4: The Board Review

Viktor, Non-Executive Director

He is not judging the quarter. He is judging how you think about it.

Dialogue

The Quarter That Came in Below Plan

V
Denise, before the slides. In twenty years I have sat through two kinds of post-mortem: one that explains a quarter and one that apologises for it. Which am I about to hear?
D
The first. Three things: what we missed, why we missed it, and what changes in October.
V
Then start with the warning sign. Was there one, or are we discussing something that nobody in this building could have seen?
D
There was one, and we saw it. Submissions in the industrial book fell in June. We read it as a near miss repeating itself, and we should have tested that reading instead of explaining it. In retrospect, the pressure must have been building since March.
V
Careful with should have. Once, it is accountability. Five times, it is a confession, and this room stops listening at three. Tell me instead what you could have done and chose not to.
D
We could have re-priced the two largest accounts in July. We decided not to, in a room, with the numbers in front of us. That was a judgment call, not an oversight, and I would like the board to hear the difference.
V
So would I. Most reviews treat the two identically, and they are not the same failure at all. What was the blind spot?
D
Assuming the renewal cycle would behave as it did last year. That is the root cause, not the June number. Nobody owned that assumption, which is precisely why nobody tested it. The corrective action is a monthly re-pricing review from October, with my name against it. It might have saved two of the four points we lost. I cannot claim more than that.
V
That I can take to the board. A room forgives a quarter far faster than it forgives ten minutes of apologising for one.

Listening

The Review That Starts in the Wrong Place

A former investigations lead on why most post-mortems fail in the first five minutes. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Where does he say the bad reviews fail?
They start with who instead of what. Once a name is on the table, everybody is defending something and the analysis is over.
2. What do the good reviews do first?
They rebuild the timeline — what was known on a given date, and by whom — before anybody is allowed to say what should have happened.
3. What does he ask when somebody says the market must have shifted?
What would be true if it had. If they cannot answer, they are not deducing, they are decorating.
4. What does he say about a review that ends with no date and no name?
It has not ended — it has been postponed.

Chapter 5: Practice

The Sentences You Will Actually Say

Spot the Error

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "I should had known that the renewal cycle would change."
"I should have known that the renewal cycle would change." (modal + HAVE + past participle — the have never becomes had, in any modal perfect)
2. "The pressure must have to be building since March."
"The pressure must have been building since March." (must have + past participle is deduction; must have to is obligation, which is a different sentence entirely)
3. "We lost the deadline for the July re-pricing."
"We missed the deadline for the July re-pricing." (you miss a deadline; to lose is to no longer have something you had)
4. "I assume the mistake: it was my call."
"I own the mistake: it was my call." (or "I admit it". To assume means to suppose — so that sentence tells the board you are only guessing that there was a mistake)
The Real Thing

The One-Page Post-Mortem

Quarterly post-mortem — for the board, Tuesday
Q3 — four points below plan, industrial book
D. Gadelha Rocha
One page only

What happened

Revenue closed four points below plan. The shortfall sits almost entirely in the industrial book; the corporate book was in line.

Warning sign, and when

June: submissions fell for the second month. It was read as a near miss repeating itself. We should have tested that reading; the pressure must have been building since March.

Root cause

An assumption that the renewal cycle would repeat last year's shape. It was never written down, so it was never owned and never tested.

Judgment call, not oversight

We could have re-priced the two largest accounts in July and decided against it, with the numbers in front of us. The call was mine.

Corrective action — owner and date

Write your own. One change, one name, one month. A review without those has not ended.

1. Why does the sheet separate the judgment call from the oversight, in its own box?
Because a board acts on them differently. An oversight is a process that failed and can be fixed with a control; a judgment call is a person who weighed it and chose — which is defended with reasoning, not with a new procedure.
2. In "we should have tested that reading", what would change if it said "we could have tested that reading"?
Could have names an option that existed without accepting that it was the right one. Should have accepts it. In this box the sentence is doing accountability, so should have is the honest choice — once, and only here.
