Lesson 11 -- The Board-Ready Mind

Series
The Crown -- Season 2, Episode 5 (Marionettes)

A speech lands badly, the press turns, and the whole episode becomes one long post-mortem inside a very old institution. Watch how the people around the Queen present the failure to her: the ones who survive the scene name what was known, when it was known, and what they propose to change. The one who apologises repeatedly does not.

Tip: write down every modal perfect you hear in the advisers' scenes. Note which of them is a deduction (must have, cannot have) and which is an admission (should have) — and count how often each speaker uses the second one.

Podcast
WorkLife with Adam Grant -- the episodes on failure, blame and learning from mistakes

Grant's central argument is the one behind today's grammar: organisations that hunt for a person learn nothing, and organisations that rebuild the sequence learn everything. Listen to how his guests describe their own bad calls out loud. Almost none of them retreat into apology; they say what they knew at the time, what they now deduce was happening, and what they changed afterwards.

Tip: choose one episode and write the guest's worst decision as a four-line post-mortem in English — what happened, the warning sign, the root cause, the corrective action. Bring it to class and we will compare it with your own quarter.

YouTube
Celeste Headlee -- 10 Ways to Have a Better Conversation (TED)

Two of her ten rules are exactly what a post-mortem needs and what most executives fail at under pressure: say plainly when you do not know, and do not repeat yourself. Her line about repetition is the same point Viktor makes in today's dialogue — saying the admission five times does not make it five times more honest, it only makes the room stop listening.

Tip: watch the last four minutes twice and note her exact phrasing for admitting the limits of what she knows. Two of those sentences will work, unchanged, in the next review where somebody asks you a question you cannot answer yet.
