Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

The Board-Ready Mind -- Modal Perfects for Strategic Retrospection and Executive Judgment

How to explain a quarter that went wrong without spending your credibility on it. Key words: post-mortem, hindsight, blind spot, root cause, warning sign, near miss, oversight, judgment call, to own, to second-guess, in retrospect, corrective action. Structure: modal perfects -- should have, must have, could have, might have, would have.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words a board uses when a quarter has to be explained.

Post-mortem -- the structured review held after something has finished, to establish what happened and why
"This is a post-mortem, not a confession. There is a difference and the board can hear it."
Hindsight -- the clarity you only have once the outcome is known, which the person deciding did not have
"In hindsight the signal looks enormous. At the time it was one line in a report of forty."
Blind spot -- the area you were in a position to see and did not, because of where you were standing
"The blind spot was assuming the renewal cycle would behave as it did last year."
Root cause -- the thing that, if it had gone differently, changes everything downstream of it
"The June signal was not the root cause. It was the first place the root cause became visible."
Warning sign -- an early signal that a problem was forming, visible before the loss appears
"The warning sign was there in June. We read it and we explained it away."
Near miss -- the loss that almost happened and did not, which is exactly why nobody studies it
"We treated it as a near miss repeating itself. It was not repeating. It was building."
Oversight -- something you failed to do without ever noticing that you had not done it
"That was a judgment call, not an oversight, and I would like the committee to hear the difference."
Judgment call -- a decision the data could not settle, taken by a person who then carries it
"We had the numbers in front of us and we decided not to act. That was a judgment call."
To own -- to accept publicly that a decision was yours, in one sentence, without softening it
"It was my call and it cost us four points. I own it, and the review has my name on it."
To second-guess -- to question a decision after it has been taken, again and again, in front of other people
"Reviewing a decision once is analysis. Second-guessing it three times in one meeting is anxiety."
In retrospect -- said at the start of a sentence: looking back at it now, with what we know today
"In retrospect, we explained the signal instead of testing it."
Corrective action -- the specific change you commit to, with a name against it and a date
"The corrective action is a monthly re-pricing review, from October, and it is mine."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Pay attention to which modal comes before each have, and to what each one is claiming.

The board did not ask Denise to apologise for the quarter. It asked her to explain it, which is a different task and a harder one. She opened with the number — four points below plan, almost all of it in the industrial book — and then went straight to the warning sign. Submissions had fallen in June. The team had read it as a near miss repeating itself. "We should have tested that reading instead of explaining it," she said, once, and did not say it again.

Then she moved from regret to reasoning. In retrospect, the pressure must have been building since March; two competitors had re-priced and the market had absorbed it quietly. It cannot have been the pricing on the corporate side, because that book was flat all year and closed in line with plan. The root cause was an assumption — that the renewal cycle would repeat last year's shape — which was never written down anywhere, and therefore never owned and never tested. That was the blind spot, and in hindsight it is obvious, which is precisely what hindsight does to a signal that was one line in a report of forty.

She had one more distinction to make. They could have re-priced the two largest accounts in July and had decided against it, in a room, with the numbers in front of them. That was a judgment call, not an oversight — a board treats the two very differently, and she wanted the right one on the record. The corrective action is a monthly re-pricing review from October, with her name against it. It might have saved two of the four points, she said; she was not going to claim more than that. The one thing she refused to do was second-guess the July decision a second time in the same meeting.

1. "The pressure must have been building since March." What is she doing with that sentence?
A Deducing — stating the reading that best fits what is known, without claiming to have proof.
B Regretting — saying that the team failed to act in March.
C Obliging — saying that somebody was required to act in March.
2. Why does she say the July decision was a judgment call and not an oversight?
A Because the outcome was bad, and a bad outcome makes any decision a judgment call.
B Because the option was seen, discussed and rejected with the numbers in front of them — an oversight is something nobody noticed at all.
C Because judgment call is the softer of the two terms and protects the team.
3. She says "should have" once and does not repeat it. Why does that matter to the board?
A Once it reads as accountability; repeated, it reads as distress, and the room stops assessing the reasoning and starts assessing the person.
B Because should have is grammatically weak and should be avoided in formal English.
C Because admitting a failure more than once is legally risky in a board meeting.
4. She says the review "might have saved two of the four points" rather than "would have saved four". Why?
A Because she is not sure the review will be approved.
B Because might have is more polite than would have in a formal register.
C Because might have sizes the counterfactual honestly. Claiming the fix would have recovered everything is the fastest way to lose a room that has just watched a plan miss.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Perfects for Retrospective Judgment

GRAMMAR

The form never changes: modal + HAVE + past participle. What changes is the modal — and the modal is the claim you are making.

FormWhat you are claimingExample
should have + past participleit was available, we did not do it, and I accept that. Powerful once; fatal five timesWe should have tested that reading in June.
should not have + past participleit was done, and it was the wrong thing to doWe should not have carried the same limits into the second half.
must have + past participlededuction: the only reading that fits what we know. Not proof, and not a guessThe pressure must have been building since March.
cannot have + past participlededuction the other way — the explanation you are ruling out for the roomIt cannot have been the pricing, because pricing was flat all year.
could have + past participlethe option that existed, named without conceding it was the right oneWe could have re-priced the two largest accounts in July.
might / may have + past participlea possible cause, or the honest size of a counterfactual you are not assertingIt might have saved two of the four points.
would have + past participlethe result of the road not taken, usually after if + past perfectIf we had re-priced in July, it would have covered half of the gap.
The two trapsThe have never becomes had: never I should had known. And must have to is obligation, not deduction: must have to be building is a different sentence, and not the one you want.
How it soundsIn speech it reduces to should've, must've, could've, might've. Say it that way. Never write should of, which is what that sound tempts people to spell.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

You are opening the post-mortem of a quarter that closed below plan. The strongest first move is:
A The number and where it sits, then the warning sign and what you deduce from it.
B A full list of everything you should have done differently, so that nobody else has to say it.
C An explanation of how much the market moved, before any internal detail.
You want to say that the corporate book was not the problem, and you are certain of it. You say:
A "It should not have been the corporate book."
B "It cannot have been the corporate book — that book was flat all year."
C "It must not have to be the corporate book."
The option to re-price existed in July, you discussed it and decided against it. On the record, that is:
A An oversight — the outcome was bad, so something was missed.
B A judgment call — it was seen, weighed and decided, and it is defended with reasoning rather than with a new control.
C A near miss — the loss almost did not happen.
A director says: "Nobody could have seen this coming." The answer that protects your credibility is:
A "There was a signal in June and we read it wrong. In retrospect, the pressure must have been building since March."
B "That is right — the market simply moved and there was nothing in front of us."
C "I should have seen it, I should have tested it, and I should have escalated it in June."
You are estimating what the fix would have been worth. The honest sentence is:
A "It would have recovered the whole four points."
B "It might have saved two of the four points. I cannot claim more than that."
C "It is impossible to say what any of it would have been worth."
You have already reviewed the July decision once in this meeting. Returning to it for the third time is:
A Thorough — the room will read it as rigour.
B Second-guessing — and the room reads it as a person negotiating with the past in front of an audience.
C A corrective action — revisiting a decision is itself a change of process.
