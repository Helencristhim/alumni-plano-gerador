Phrasal Verbs at Work and in Life -- Where the Object Goes

The two-word verbs natives use in conversation instead of the formal word. Key words: to put off, to take up, to give up, to come up with, to figure out, to run into, to turn down, to set up, to carry on, to get round to. Structure: separable and inseparable phrasal verbs.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To put something off -- to decide to do something later instead of now
"I keep putting off the dentist, and it is now a year."
To take something up -- to start a new activity or hobby
"She took up running at forty and now does half marathons."
To give something up -- to stop doing or having something for good
"I gave up sugar in my coffee, and I do not miss it."
To come up with something -- to think of an idea, a plan or an answer
"He came up with a solution nobody else had seen."
To figure something out -- to finally understand something after thinking about it
"It took me a week to figure out the new tax rule."
To run into somebody -- to meet somebody by chance
"I ran into my old manager at the airport last month."
To turn something down -- to say no to an offer or an invitation
"She turned down the job because of the commute."
To set something up -- to organize or prepare something so it can start
"Can you set up a call with Chicago for Thursday?"
To carry on -- to continue doing something
"Carry on with the report; I will check it later."
To get round to something -- to finally do something you have wanted to do for a long time
"I finally got round to reading the book you lent me."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Most English verbs have two versions. One is long and comes from Latin: postpone, decline, establish. The other is made of two small words: put off, turn down, set up. Both are correct. The long one belongs in a report; the short one is what people actually say, and it is the one you will hear from a colleague in Chicago at dinner.

The difficult part is not the meaning. It is the object. Many phrasal verbs open: the object can go in the middle or at the end. She turned the offer down and she turned down the offer are both correct. But when the object is a pronoun — it, them, him — it has to go in the middle: she turned it down. She turned down it is not English.

Other phrasal verbs never open. I ran into her at the airport is correct; I ran her into is not. Verbs with three words, like come up with and get round to, never open either, and all three words have to stay: he came up with a plan, not he came up a plan.

If you are not sure whether a verb opens, there is a safe way out: use a noun and put it at the end. Can you set up the call? works whether or not you remember the rule. And when you speak, put the stress on the small word: put it OFF, turn it DOWN. That stress is what makes a phrasal verb sound natural.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Where the Object Goes

GRAMMAR

A pronoun goes in the middle when the verb can open.

TypeWhere the object goesExample
opensmiddle or endturn the offer down · turn down the offer
opens + pronounalways in the middleturn it down · put it off
never opensafter the whole verbrun into her
three wordsnever openscome up with a plan · get round to it
no objectnothing to placecarry on · she gave up

Never: turn down it · put off it · ran her into · came up a plan
