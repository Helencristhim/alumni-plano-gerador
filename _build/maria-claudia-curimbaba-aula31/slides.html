Chapter 3 -- The Code

Questions That Ask for a Yes

would you consider... wouldn't it make sense... don't you think...

Discovery

Four Questions, One Goal

Maria Claudia wants Tom to sponsor the exhibition. Are these real questions -- or something else?

"Would you consider sponsoring one evening?"

"Wouldn't it make sense to meet the clients in person?"

"Don't you think our clients should know us better?"

"Isn't it worth trying for one quarter?"

JobFormExample
a soft proposalWould you consider + verb-ing?"Would you consider sponsoring one evening?"
a proposal they can say yes toWouldn't it make sense + to + verb?"Wouldn't it make sense to meet them in person?"
invite them to share your opinionDon't you think (that) + full sentence?"Don't you think our clients should know us better?"
invite them to see the valueIsn't it worth + verb-ing?"Isn't it worth trying for one quarter?"
the answer you hope forYes = I agree"Yes, it would." / "Yes, I do."

Key idea: a negative question is not negative -- it expects a yes and invites the other person to think with you. Three traps: after consider and worth comes verb-ing ("consider to sponsor" → "consider sponsoring"); after make sense comes to + verb; and after don't you think comes a full sentence with its own subject ("don't you think is" → "don't you think it is").

The Structure

Three Jobs in the Pitch

The same questions, doing three different jobs.

1 · Invite them to think with you

"Don't you think our clients know our prices better than they know us?"

They answer yes -- and the idea starts to be partly theirs.

2 · Name the objection, then reframe it

"I understand the objection. But wouldn't it make sense to meet forty clients in one evening?"

You say their doubt before they do, and you move it to common ground.

3 · Ask for a small yes

"Would you consider sponsoring one evening instead of the whole week?"

A small yes today is worth more than a big no.

Notice what is missing: "you should" and "you are wrong". Neither wins anyone over.

The Question They Can Say Yes To

Sound first -- no text on screen.

0:000:00
1. According to her, when are people won over?
When they feel the idea is partly theirs -- not because of the number of your arguments.
2. What does she start with, instead of reasons?
Questions -- for example: wouldn't it make sense to look at what this could bring in before we look at what it costs?
3. Why does she name the objection before the other person does?
Because it is reassuring: people relax when they hear that you have already thought about their doubt.
4. What does she ask for, instead of everything at once?
A small yes -- for example, would you consider trying it for one quarter?
Artefact

The Persuasive Memo

MEMO -- ONE EVENING OF BRAZILIAN ART IN HOUSTON

From: Maria Claudia Curimbaba · To: Tom Reilly · Joint venture

The idea

A Houston gallery is opening "New Ground: Contemporary Brazil" in March, with five artists from Sao Paulo. The full sponsorship is one week: USD 18,000.

Why it matters to us

Three hundred guests at the opening, forty of them our own clients. Don't you think they know our prices better than they know us?

The obvious objection

We are a supply business, not a gallery. True -- but our common ground with these clients is long-term trust, and trust is built in person.

What I am asking for

One evening, not the week: USD 6,000. If it works, we make the case for next year with real numbers.

Last line of the memo: "Would you consider sponsoring the opening night?"

Dialogue

The Pitch to Houston

T

"I read your memo. With respect, we are a supply business, not a gallery. Why should the joint venture pay for an art show?"

M

"I understand the objection, and I would have asked the same. But don't you think our clients in Houston know our prices better than they know us?"

T

"Maybe. But eighteen thousand dollars is a lot of money for a party."

M

"It is not a party. It is three hundred guests, and forty of them are clients we want to keep. Wouldn't it make sense to meet them somewhere they will remember?"

T

"I can see the upside. I am still hesitant about the cost, though."

M

"That is fair. Would you consider sponsoring one evening instead of the whole week? If it works, I will make the case for next year with real numbers."

T

"One evening. That I could sell to the partners. What do you need from me?"

M

"Your name on the invitation and ten minutes to welcome the guests. Our common ground is the clients. The art is just the way in."

Dialogue Review

About Tom

1. What is Tom's first objection?
That the joint venture is a supply business, not a gallery -- so why pay for an art show?
2. How does Tom describe the event at first?
As a party -- and eighteen thousand dollars is a lot of money for it.
3. What is Tom still hesitant about?
The cost -- even though he can see the upside.
4. Why does Tom accept one evening?
Because one evening is something he could sell to the partners.
