Lesson 31 -- Pre-class

Would You Consider? -- The Art of Persuasion

Win people over with questions they want to say yes to: persuasive questions ("Would you consider + -ing", "Wouldn't it make sense to", "Don't you think", "Isn't it worth + -ing"). New vocabulary: win over, make the case, sway, hesitant, objection, common ground, appeal to, reassure, persuasive, resonate.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Win over -- to persuade someone to support you or agree with you, often after they were against the idea
"It took two meetings to win over the CFO."
Make the case -- to give clear reasons why something is right or why it should happen
"Let me make the case for a small pilot first."
Sway -- to influence what someone thinks or decides, so that they change their position
"One good story can sway a hesitant board."
Hesitant -- not sure about something, and slow to agree or to act
"The partners are still hesitant about the cost."
Objection -- a reason someone gives for not agreeing with an idea or a plan
"The main objection is the cost, so let me start there."
Common ground -- the interests or opinions that two sides share, even when they disagree on other things
"Our common ground is the clients we both want to keep."
Appeal to -- to try to persuade someone by speaking to something they care about or value
"Appeal to what they value, not to what you value."
Reassure -- to say or do something that makes another person feel less worried
"Let me reassure you: the risk is limited to one evening."
Persuasive -- good at making people agree with you or do what you suggest
"Her memo was short, but very persuasive."
Resonate -- to feel meaningful or important to someone, because it connects with their own experience
"The idea will resonate with clients who love art."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Maria Claudia has an idea: a Houston gallery is opening an exhibition of five artists from Sao Paulo, and she wants the joint venture to sponsor it. She knows Tom will be hesitant. His first objection will be simple: "We are a supply business, not a gallery." So she will not argue. She will not try to win him over with ten reasons. She will make the case in his language, and she will start with a question: "Don't you think our clients know our prices better than they know us?" Their common ground is the clients, not the art. Then she will appeal to what Houston values: "Wouldn't it make sense to meet forty clients somewhere they will remember?" She also wants to reassure him about the cost, so her last question asks for a small yes: "Would you consider sponsoring one evening instead of the whole week?" An idea is persuasive when it resonates with the listener -- and a good question can sway a hesitant CFO more than a good catalog.

1. Why does she start with "Don't you think our clients know our prices better than they know us?"
A Because she really does not know the answer and needs Tom to tell her.
B To invite Tom to agree with her before she makes the proposal.
C Because she wants to deflect his objection and change the subject to prices.
2. What is their common ground?
A The art and the five artists from Sao Paulo, which both of them admire.
B The clients.
C The gallery in Houston.
3. Why does her last question ask for one evening?
A Because the gallery only has one evening free in March.
B Because one evening is cheaper for the gallery and easier for the artists to organize.
C To reassure him about the cost with a small yes.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Persuasive Questions

GRAMMAR

When you want someone to agree, a question often works better than an order. Negative questions ("Wouldn't it...?", "Don't you think...?", "Isn't it...?") are not negative at all: they expect the answer yes, and they invite the other person to think with you. "Would you consider + -ing" asks for a decision softly, without pushing.

JobFormExample
a soft proposalWould you consider + verb-ing?Would you consider sponsoring one evening?
a proposal they can say yes toWouldn't it make sense + to + verb?Wouldn't it make sense to meet them in person?
a shared opinionDon't you think (that) + full sentence?Don't you think our clients should know us better?
the value of tryingIsn't it worth + verb-ing?Isn't it worth trying for one quarter?
the answer you hope forYes = I agree"Yes, it would." / "Yes, I do."

Key point: three traps. First, "consider to sponsor" → "consider sponsoring". Second, "make sense meet" → "make sense to meet". Third, "don't you think is a good idea" → "don't you think it is a good idea". Say negative questions with falling intonation: they sound like an invitation, not a doubt.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best sentence for each situation.

Tom says: "We are a supply business, not a gallery." Which answer is best?
A "You are wrong. Art is good for every business, and everybody knows it."
B "I understand the objection. But don't you think our clients should know us better?"
C "OK, then forget it. We can talk about the budget instead, if you prefer."
The partners are hesitant about the full cost. Which question is best?
A "Would you consider sponsoring one evening instead?"
B "Would you consider to sponsor one evening instead of the whole week?"
C "You should sponsor the whole week, because the price is fair."
You want to suggest meeting the clients in person. Which sentence is correct?
A "Wouldn't it make sense meet them in person this time, at the gallery?"
B "Don't you think is better to meet them in person this time?"
C "Wouldn't it make sense to meet them in person?"
