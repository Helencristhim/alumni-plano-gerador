Saying What You Mean -- The Verbs That Decide the Next Verb

One small word can turn a decision into a doubt: stop to think and stop thinking are not the same promise. Key words: a priority, to postpone, to intend, to refuse, to insist on, upfront, bluntly, a commitment. Structure: verbs followed by the infinitive or by the -ing form.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A priority -- the thing that you decide to do first, before everything else
“Our priority is to close the deal before December.”
To postpone -- to move an event or a decision to a later date
“We postponed signing until the audit was finished.”
To intend -- to have a clear plan to do something
“The buyer intends to keep the brand for at least two years.”
To refuse -- to say clearly that you will not do something
“We refused to sign the exclusivity clause in its current form.”
To insist on -- to say firmly that something must happen
“They insisted on keeping me as CEO until the end of the transition.”
Upfront -- paid or agreed at the beginning, before the rest
“We want sixty percent of the price upfront, at closing.”
Bluntly -- in a very direct way, without making it softer
“To put it bluntly, we will not accept less than that number.”
A commitment -- a promise that people expect you to keep
“Staying for two years is a commitment, not a hope.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A buyer asked a founder whether he would stay after the sale. He answered, I stopped to think about it. He meant the opposite: he had stopped thinking about it, because he had already decided. The buyer wrote down that the founder was still unsure, and the next three weeks of negotiation started from that note. One small word changed what he meant.

In English, the first verb decides the form of the verb that follows it. Some verbs take the infinitive with to: we intend to keep the brand, they refused to sign, we agreed to postpone the closing. These are verbs that look forward, toward something that has not happened yet. Other verbs take the -ing form: we avoid promising dates, they suggested waiting, we postponed signing. And after a preposition, it is always -ing: they insisted on keeping me, I look forward to meeting you. In that last one, to is a preposition, not part of the verb, and that is why the verb after it takes -ing.

Then there are the verbs that change meaning. Stop doing something means you no longer do it; stop to do something means you pause in order to do it. Remember to send the contract is a job for later; I remember sending the contract is a memory. Try to cut costs means make an effort; try cutting costs means test the idea and see what happens.

The trap for a Portuguese speaker is the sentence that sounds natural in your head: I suggest to wait, I look forward to meet you, they want that we sign. None of them is English. Say I suggest waiting, I look forward to meeting you, they want us to sign. A buyer will understand all three, but the correct version is the one that sounds like a person who has done this before.

1. What did the founder actually mean when he said I stopped to think about it?
A That he paused the meeting in order to think about staying, and wanted more time before he gave the buyer an answer.
B That he wanted the buyer to stop asking the question.
C That he had already decided, so he was no longer thinking about it.
2. According to the text, which verbs take the infinitive with to?
A Every verb that comes after a preposition.
B Verbs that look forward, toward something that has not happened yet.
C Verbs about a habit that you had in the past and no longer have, like a routine that stopped years ago.
3. Why is I look forward to meet you wrong?
A Because to is a preposition here, and after a preposition the verb takes -ing.
B Because look forward is too formal for an email to a buyer, and a shorter phrase sounds more natural.
C Because meet needs a person after it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Verb + to or Verb + -ing

GRAMMAR

Verbs that look forward take to. Activities, and anything after a preposition, take -ing.

JobFormExample
A plan or a decisionverb + to + verb (want, plan, intend, agree, refuse, decide, expect)We intend to keep the brand for two years.
An activityverb + -ing (avoid, suggest, consider, postpone, keep, finish)We postponed signing until the audit was finished.
After a prepositionpreposition + -ingThey insisted on keeping me for two years.
Asking someone else to actverb + person + to + verbThey want us to sign before December.
Stopstop doing = no longer do it · stop to do = pause in order to do itI stopped checking every invoice. I stopped to check one invoice.
Rememberremember to do = a job for later · remember doing = a memoryRemember to send the contract. I remember sending it.
Trytry to do = make an effort · try doing = test itTry to cut costs. Try cutting the meeting to twenty minutes.
The trapto as a preposition takes -ingI look forward to meeting you. Not to meet.

Never: I suggest to wait · they want that we sign · I look forward to meet you · we avoid to promise
