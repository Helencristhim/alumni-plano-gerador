Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A percentage point -- the difference between two percentages, said as a unit of its own
"From four to six is two percentage points, and fifty per cent."
A ratio -- one number expressed against another, such as two to one
"That line is a ratio, and both numbers in it changed."
A margin -- what is left of the price once the cost of it has been taken out
"The margin is where it should be, so the problem is elsewhere."
Net -- the number that is left after everything has been taken out
"The net movement is smaller than the gross figure suggests."
To round -- to make a number simpler by cutting the small end off it
"It was rounded to one decimal place in 2019, when the numbers were smaller."
A benchmark -- the outside number you compare yours against
"We were comparing ourselves against a benchmark built on a different basis."
To break even -- to make exactly enough to cover the costs, and nothing more
"That line was supposed to show whether the region would break even."
A figure -- any single number in a report, said out loud
"Every figure in the pack has to make sense when you say it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A rate goes from four per cent to six per cent. How big is that? The honest answer is that there are three correct ways to say it, and they sound so different from each other that the choice can decide whether a room approves something or refuses it. It went up by two percentage points. It is fifty per cent higher than it was. It went from four per cent to six. All three are true. Only the last one is impossible to misread.

The trap has a name and it is worth learning it once, properly. A movement between two percentages is measured in percentage points, never in per cent. Saying it went up by two per cent makes a fifty per cent increase sound like almost nothing — and the person listening will make a decision on that. The rest of the machinery is smaller. Comparatives take than, never that: higher than last year. Multiples take as: twice as high as, half as expensive as. And the words that soften or sharpen a comparison — slightly, roughly, significantly — go in front of it, not after the number: slightly above budget, not above budget slightly.

What all of this is really protecting is not accuracy. The figures were accurate before. It is protecting the listener, who has about four seconds to build a picture and will build one whether or not you helped. Giving both numbers first, from and to, hands them the picture instead of a conclusion. It costs one extra second, and after it nobody in the room can quietly arrive at a different number from yours.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparing Numbers

GRAMMAR

Both numbers first, from and to. Then the movement, and which kind it is.

ShapeWhat it saysExample
from X to Ythe safest — both numbers, no interpretationIt went from four per cent to six per cent.
up / down bythe size of the movement itselfIt went up by two percentage points.
X per cent higher thanthe movement as a proportion of where it startedIt is fifty per cent higher than it was.
twice · half · three timesmultiples, always with as ... asIt is twice as high as last year.
slightly · roughly · significantlythese go before the comparisonIt is slightly above budget.
the first trapa movement between two percentages is in percentage pointsFour to six is two percentage points, never two per cent.
the second trapcomparatives take than, never that or ofNot higher that May. Say higher than May.
the one that travels with itin line takes with, and you send a pack to somebody, on a dayIt is in line with budget. I sent it to Mark on Monday.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A rate went from four per cent to six. The safest way to say it is:
A It went up two per cent.
B It went from four per cent to six, so up by two percentage points.
C It grew a little bit, around two.
You want to compare this quarter with the same quarter last year. You say:
A This quarter is higher that the same quarter last year.
B This quarter is significantly higher than the same quarter last year.
C This quarter is more high of the same quarter last year.
The figure is very close to what you expected. You say:
A It is slightly above budget, and roughly in line with last year.
B It is above budget slightly, and in line of last year roughly.
C It is a little more than the budget, more or less like the last year.
Somebody outside finance asks whether the margin is good. The strongest answer is:
A It is thirty-two per cent, up from thirty.
B What that means in practice is that we cover our costs by day sixteen, which is two days earlier than last month.
C Yes, it is good, the ratio improved.

Reflection
