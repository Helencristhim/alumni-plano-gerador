I Used to Be Somebody Else -- Twelve Years, Told as a Change

Your own story in the form English keeps for what stopped being true. Key expressions: to pick something up, to fall out of the habit of, back then, to be thrown in at the deep end, to get the hang of something, a stepping stone, to look back on, set in your ways, to grow out of something, a phase. Structure: used to, and the near-identical be used to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To pick something up -- to learn something informally, without being taught
"I picked up most of my English on calls, not in a classroom."
To fall out of the habit of -- to stop doing something you used to do regularly
"I fell out of the habit of studying, and it took eight years to start again."
Back then -- at that time in the past
"Back then nobody expected me to speak English at work at all."
To be thrown in at the deep end -- to be given a difficult job with no preparation at all
"My first week was a call with the German office. I was thrown in at the deep end."
To get the hang of something -- to learn how to do something after some practice
"It took me about six weeks to get the hang of the accent."
A stepping stone -- a job or stage that leads you to a better one
"Audit was a stepping stone, and I did not know that at the time."
To look back on -- to think about something in the past, from where you are now
"I look back on that year and I have no idea how I did it."
Set in your ways -- unwilling to change how you do things
"I am more set in my ways than I was at twenty-five, and I know it."
To grow out of something -- to stop liking or doing something as you get older
"I grew out of romance novels at about sixteen and never went back."
A phase -- a period of time that does not last
"I told myself it was a phase. It lasted four years."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences about the same six years. I worked in audit for six years. I used to work in audit. The first is a fact and tells you nothing about today. The second says something the first does not: it stopped. That is the entire job of used to, and it is why a career told without it sounds like a list instead of a story.

The form is small and has one detail that catches everybody. After did, the verb loses its D: I did not use to present to senior people, Did you use to travel a lot? Never didn’t used to. And used to is only ever about the past — for a habit you still have, English uses usually: I usually read before bed, not I use to read before bed.

Now the part that costs Portuguese speakers most, because the two structures are two words apart and mean opposite things. I used to fly at night means you did and you do not any more. I am used to flying at night means you still do it and it no longer bothers you. One closes a door; the other says you have stopped noticing. Watch the ending too: used to takes the bare verb, and be used to takes the -ing form. The near relative get used to describes the process: it took six weeks to get used to the accent.

Why it is worth the trouble: almost nobody can describe their own change without it. You picked up a skill you were never taught, you were thrown in at the deep end, you eventually got the hang of it, and you probably fell out of the habit of something along the way. All of those are before-and-after sentences, and used to is the form that carries the before.

1. What does "I used to work in audit" say that "I worked in audit" does not?

2. Which of these is correct?

Quiz

Somebody asks about your first job in English. The fullest answer is:
A It was difficult.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Tell the last twelve years of your working life as a change, not as a list: what you used to do, what you did not use to be able to do, what you are used to now. Finish with the line that has not happened yet.

Talk
Dan Gilbert — “The psychology of your future self” (TED)

Six minutes, and it is the exact study from the second recording of this lesson, told by the researcher who ran it. Nineteen thousand people, all convinced they had just finished changing. Short, funny and hard to argue with.

Tip: after watching, write three sentences about what you used to be certain of and are not certain of now. Then predict one thing that will change by 2028 and keep the note.
