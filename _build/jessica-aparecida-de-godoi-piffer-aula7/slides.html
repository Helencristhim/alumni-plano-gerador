Chapter 3: What Stopped Being True

Used To, and the Other One

Two words apart, and not the same sentence at all

Grammar Discovery

Which of These Is Still True?

"I used to work in audit."

"I worked in audit for six years."

"I did not use to present to senior people."

"I am used to presenting to senior people."

Three of these are about the past. The green one is about today, and it is two words away from the red ones.

JobFormExample
a habit or state that endedused to + bare verbI used to work in audit.

just a past factpast simpleI worked there for six years.

becoming accustomedget used to + verb-ingI got used to the accent.
the trapI used to = it stopped. I am used to = it is normal for me now. Different sentences.

In one line: used to closes a door; am used to says you no longer notice it.

Six Months Later, On a Call

J
Slowly. I used to do an hour a week and I have made it two. Back then I could not order a coffee, so two hours feels like progress.
G
You told me in March you were going to do that and I did not believe you. Most people fall out of the habit of it by February.
J
I did, once. I studied English every night for a year when I was twenty-two, and then I stopped for eight years. I look back on that and it still annoys me.
G
And now? Do you still find the calls hard, or are you used to them?
J
I am used to them now. I did not use to sleep the night before a call with the German office. That was my first job in English — I was thrown in at the deep end.
G
Everybody is, and nobody says so afterwards. How long did it take you to get the hang of it?
J
Longer than I would like to admit. And you? What did you use to be afraid of that you are not afraid of now?
Comprehension

About Greg

1. What did Greg not believe in March, and why?
That she would really increase her Spanish hours — because most people fall out of the habit by February.
2. What does Greg ask about the calls?
Whether she still finds them hard, or whether she is used to them now.
3. How does he respond when she says she was thrown in at the deep end?
He normalises it: he says everybody is, and nobody admits it afterwards — and then asks how long it took her.
Listening 1 · A Training Lead

Nobody Learns This in a Classroom

A training lead on where professional skill actually comes from. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say the first six months of a new job look like?
People are thrown in at the deep end, they pick most of it up from watching other people, and almost nobody is formally taught the part that matters.
2. What does she say people get wrong when they look back?
They remember the difficulty and forget that they got the hang of it, so they assume the next hard thing will not go the same way.
3. What does she ask people to write down, and why?
Three things they could not do two years ago and can do now — because nobody keeps that list, and without it every new difficulty feels like the first one.
Listening 2 · Science Radio

Why You Think You Have Stopped Changing

A short piece on a mistake almost everybody makes about their own future. Sound first — no text.

1. What two questions did the researchers ask the nineteen thousand people?
How much they had changed in the last ten years, and how much they expected to change in the next ten.
2. What was the pattern in the answers?
At every age, people reported large change behind them and predicted very little change ahead — and a forty-year-old's prediction was contradicted by what the fifty-year-olds actually reported.
3. What explanation does the piece offer?
Remembering is easy and imagining is hard, so people mistake the difficulty of imagining change for evidence that it will not happen.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I use "used to" for something that stopped, and the past simple for a fact with no contrast in it.
I say "did not use to" and "did you use to", with no D after did.
I know that "I used to" and "I am used to" are different sentences, and I can say both.
I can tell twelve years of my career with a before and an after in every sentence.
I know the expressions: to pick something up, to fall out of the habit of, back then, to be thrown in at the deep end, to get the hang of something, a stepping stone, to look back on, set in your ways, to grow out of something, a phase.
