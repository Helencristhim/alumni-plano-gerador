Chapter 3: The Code

Already Decided, Not Yet Done

Three words in front of every verb

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I am going to submit the last two documents on Friday."

"They are going to call the candidates in November."

"I am not going to apply for a scholarship."

"Are you going to renew your passport first?"

The same three red words sit in front of every verb, and every verb after them is bare. The blue part does the no and the question — and it is never do.

JobFormExample
A plan you decided before nowam / is / are + going to + bare verbI am going to submit it on Friday.
Negativeam / is / are + not + going toI am not going to apply for a scholarship.
QuestionAm / Is / Are + subject + going to — never with doAre you going to renew your passport?
Something you can already see comingsame form, from the evidence in front of youLook at the queue — the freight is going to get expensive.
A decision made this secondwill + bare verb"We need one more document." — "I'll send it today."
When it happensnext week, in November, before the deadline, soonWe are going to meet in October.
The trapthe present simple is not a future in EnglishNot next month I travel to Miami. Say I am going to travel.

In one line: going to for what you decided before the conversation, will for what you decide during it.

The Fork

Decided Before, or Decided Now?

One question decides it: when did you decide?

1. You booked the flight last week. Somebody asks about November.
"I'm going to travel to Amsterdam in November." — decided before
2. The client says: "We need one more document today."
"I'll send it this afternoon." — decided this second
3. You have had the plan since March: the thesis is about delivery clauses.
"I'm going to write about delivery clauses." — decided before
4. Your colleague says the deadline moved to Monday and you had no idea.
"Then I'll finish it over the weekend." — decided this second
5. Two hundred ships are already waiting outside the port. You look at the queue.
"Freight is going to get expensive." — you can see it coming
6. Somebody offers you a place for January, and you want it in September.
"I'm going to turn it down." — if you had already decided. "I'll think about it." — if you are deciding now.

About Claire’s Year

1. What does she say about the interview: how many people, when, and how long?
They are going to call about sixty people in November, and the interview is thirty minutes, in English.
2. Why is she going to be away in March, and for how long?

Listening 1 · After You Apply

What Is Going to Happen Next

A recorded message from an admissions team. Sound first — no text.

1. What is going to arrive in two weeks, and what happens if a document is missing?
An email confirming that the file is complete. If something is missing, the email says which one, and the candidate has ten days to send it.
2. What does she say about the interview: how many candidates, how long, and what it is not?
About sixty candidates, thirty minutes, in English — and it is not a test of the accent. They want to hear how the candidate thinks about a problem.
3. How are they going to read the motivation letter?
Not as literature. They read it for one thing: why this program, and why now.
Listening 2 · Market Briefing

Everybody Is Looking at the Same Calendar

Eight weeks before the harvest starts. Sound first — no text.

1. What is going to happen in about eight weeks, and what does it move?
The harvest in Mato Grosso is going to start, and roughly forty million tons are going to move toward three ports in ninety days.
2. What is the difference between the trader who books in June and the one who waits?
They pay different prices, and the second one is going to blame the market instead of the calendar.
3. What two things does he ask every client in April?
What they are going to do in September, and who else in the office knows about it. Without an answer there is no plan, only hope.

What I Can Do Now

I can describe my next twelve months with dates, without stopping to build each sentence.
I never use the present simple as a future. Not "next month I travel".
I choose will only when I decide during the conversation, not before it.
I ask with Are you going to...? and When are you going to...?, never with do.
I know the words: a goal, a thesis, a scholarship, an entry requirement, an offer, an academic calendar, to arrange, to book, to renew, to turn down.
