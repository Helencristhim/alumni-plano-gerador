Chapter 3: The Code

The Audit That Did Not Happen

Third and mixed conditionals

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If the audit had happened in the second year, it would have cost a conversation."

"If the control had been tested, we would not be here now."

"If he were a more careful reader, he would not have signed it."

"Had they disclosed earlier, the penalty would have been smaller."

All four are about something that did not happen. But two of them are entirely in the past, one reaches into the present, and one has no if at all. Which is which?

FormWhat it doesExample
third conditionalIf + had + past participle, then would have + past participle. Both halves are finished and neither happened. This is the tense of every review of a failure."If the audit had happened in year two, it would have cost a conversation."
mixed: past → presentThe cause is in the past; the consequence is NOW. So the if-half stays in the past perfect and the main half loses the have."If the control had been tested, we would not be here now."
mixed: present → pastA permanent fact about somebody explains something they did. The if-half is the second conditional; the main half is the third."If he were a more careful reader, he would not have signed it."
would have, never would ofIn speech would have reduces to would’ve, which sounds exactly like would of. It is not of, ever — and it is the most common written error native speakers make."we would have seen it" · "we would’ve seen it"
never "would of"
inversion, no ifIn formal and legal register, drop the if and invert: Had they disclosed.... Same meaning, one register higher, and it is everywhere in the documents you read."Had they disclosed earlier, the penalty would have been smaller."

This is the longest verb form in English and it is the one that collapses under pressure. Portuguese gets there in fewer syllables, so the instinct is to shorten the English — and what falls out is usually the had. Say the whole thing slowly once; speed comes later and on its own.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If they would have run the audit, it would have been caught."
"If they had run the audit, it would have been caught."
"If they had disclosed early, the fine would of been smaller."
"If they had disclosed early, the fine would have been smaller."
"If we had known about the red flag, we will not have signed."
"If we had known about the red flag, we would not have signed."

The first puts would in the if-half again — the same rule as the second conditional, one tense further back. The second is would of, which comes from hearing would’ve and writing what you heard. The third leaves will in a sentence about something that already did not happen: for a finished past it is always would have.

Listening 1

Nobody Hid Anything

An investigator on what he finds when he opens the files. Sound first — no text.

1. How often has he found a conspiracy?
Perhaps twice in fifteen years.
2. What does he find the rest of the time?
An email read on a Friday, a control written down and never tested, and an audit postponed because the quarter was busy.
3. Why is that not comforting to tell a board?
Because a conspiracy is somebody else's fault, and a postponed audit is theirs.
Listening 2

The Lesson Nobody Wants to Sit Through

A risk officer on why near misses are wasted. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she call the cheapest lesson an organisation gets?
The one where nothing happened — a near miss that gets stopped and is over in a morning.
2. What does aviation do differently?
It writes near misses up whether or not anybody was hurt, and sends the write-up to people who were not involved.
3. What is the consequence of investigating in proportion to the harm?
The only lessons that get learned are the expensive ones.
