Compliance and the Cost of Getting It Wrong

A feature on what actually goes wrong before a compliance failure, and which of the things that did not happen would have changed the outcome. Key expressions: a fine, a settlement, a regulator, wrongdoing, an audit, a red flag, to conceal, to disclose, negligence, a penalty, reputational damage, an internal control, accountability, a near miss. Structure: third and mixed conditionals — would have, the mixed forms, would’ve against would of, and the inversion that drops the if.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen appear in the text you will read in class, and in every enforcement report published in English.

A fine -- money a company is ordered to pay as a punishment
"The fine was the largest that regulator had ever imposed."
A settlement -- an agreement that ends a case without a decision on the substance
"The settlement cost less than three more years of litigation."
A regulator -- the public body that supervises an industry
"The regulator had warned them twice, in writing."
Wrongdoing -- behaviour that is unlawful or seriously improper
"Nobody admitted wrongdoing, which is normal in a settlement."
An audit -- an official inspection of accounts or of a process
"An audit in the second year would have caught the whole thing."
A red flag -- an early sign that something is going wrong
"The red flag sat in a queue for eighteen months."
To conceal -- to hide something deliberately
"It is far cheaper to disclose early than to conceal and explain later."
To disclose -- to make information known officially
"They chose to disclose four days before the newspaper had it."
Negligence -- failure to take the care that was required of you
"The claim was negligence rather than fraud, and that distinction mattered."
A penalty -- a punishment imposed for breaking a rule
"The penalty was calculated on revenue and not on profit."
Reputational damage -- harm to the way an organisation is seen
"The fine was survivable; the reputational damage was not."
An internal control -- a process designed to stop an error before it happens
"The internal control existed on paper and nowhere else."
Accountability -- being answerable for what happened, and to whom
"Accountability stopped two levels below the people who decided."
A near miss -- something that almost went wrong and did not
"Every near miss is a free lesson that nobody wants to sit through."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The third conditional is the tense of the review meeting. It talks about a past that did not happen and a consequence that therefore did not either: if the audit had happened in the second year, it would have cost a conversation. Both halves are finished. Nothing in the sentence is still open. It is the form in which every post-mortem, every regulator report and every honest explanation of a failure is written.

The mixed conditional is the one almost nobody is taught, and it is the more useful of the two at work. There, the cause is in the past and the consequence is NOW, so the main half loses its have: if the internal control had been tested, we would not be here now. The other direction exists too: a permanent fact about somebody explaining something they did — if he were a more careful reader, he would not have signed it.

Two traps. The first is structural and it is the same one as the second conditional, one step further back: would never appears in the if-half. If they had run the audit, never if they would have run the audit. The second is a trap of the ear. In speech would have reduces to would’ve, which sounds exactly like would of. It is never of. That error is so common among native speakers that seeing it will not warn you.

One last form, and it is worth having because you already read it every week. In formal register the if disappears and the sentence inverts: Had they disclosed earlier, the penalty would have been smaller. Same meaning, higher register, and it appears in judgments, memoranda and enforcement notices constantly. Being able to produce it once, deliberately, is worth more than being able to recognise it a hundred times.

1. What does the third conditional describe?
A Something that might still happen.
B A past that did not happen, and a consequence that therefore did not either.
C A permanent fact about somebody.
2. In a mixed conditional with a past cause and a present result, what happens to the main clause?
A It keeps would have + past participle.
B It loses the have: would + base verb.
C It becomes the present simple.
3. Which is correct?
A "If they would have run the audit, it would have been caught."
B "If they had run the audit, it would have been caught."
C "If they had run the audit, it will have been caught."
4. Why is "would of" so common in writing?
A Because it is acceptable in informal English.
B Because "would've" sounds exactly like it in speech.
C Because "of" is a modal verb.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Third and Mixed Conditionals

GRAMMAR

This is the longest verb form in English and it is the one that collapses under pressure. Portuguese reaches the same meaning in fewer syllables, so the instinct is to shorten — and what usually falls out is the had. Say the whole thing slowly once; the speed arrives on its own later.

FormWhat it doesExample
third conditionalIf + had + past participle, then would have + past participle. Both halves are finished and neither happened. This is the tense of every review of a failure."If the audit had happened in year two, it would have cost a conversation."
mixed: past → presentThe cause is in the past; the consequence is NOW. So the if-half stays in the past perfect and the main half loses the have."If the control had been tested, we would not be here now."
mixed: present → pastA permanent fact about somebody explains something they did. The if-half is the second conditional; the main half is the third."If he were a more careful reader, he would not have signed it."
would have, never would ofIn speech would have reduces to would’ve, which sounds exactly like would of. It is not of, ever — and it is the most common written error native speakers make."we would have seen it" · "we would’ve seen it"
never "would of"
inversion, no ifIn formal and legal register, drop the if and invert: Had they disclosed.... Same meaning, one register higher, and it is everywhere in the documents you read."Had they disclosed earlier, the penalty would have been smaller."

One habit for this week: after anything that goes wrong, however small, say one sentence out loud in the third conditional about it. A late reply, a missed train, a document sent to the wrong person. The form only becomes automatic on things that do not matter.

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to how a compliance failure actually unfolds, then put the steps in order.

?A rule exists, and a process is written down to apply it.
?The audit that would have tested that process is scheduled and then postponed twice.
?A red flag arrives and sits in the queue of somebody with four hundred other things to read.
?Eighteen months later the regulator finds it, and nothing had been concealed at any point.
?A fine is paid, nobody admits wrongdoing, and the reputational damage has no number attached to it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are explaining a failure to people who were not involved. Choose the version that says what you mean.

You want to say the audit did not happen and would have caught it:
A "If they would have run the audit, it would have been caught."
B "If they had run the audit, it would have been caught."
C "If they ran the audit, it will have been caught."
The cause is in the past but the problem is happening right now:
A "If the control had been tested, we would not have been here now."
B "If the control had been tested, we would not be here now."
C "If the control was tested, we will not be here now."
You are writing a memorandum and want the formal register:
A "If they would disclose earlier, the penalty would be smaller."
B "Had they disclosed earlier, the penalty would have been smaller."
C "Have they disclosed earlier, the penalty would be smaller."
You want to be honest about what actually happened:
A "Everything was hidden from us."
B "Nothing was concealed here. Nobody looked."
C "There was a conspiracy at two levels."
A board member asks whose fault it was:
A "Nobody's, these things happen."
B "The question that changes anything is which of the things that did not happen would have changed the outcome."
C "It was the compliance team, clearly."
