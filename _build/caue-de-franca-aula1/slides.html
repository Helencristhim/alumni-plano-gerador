Lesson 1 · A Dinner in Toronto

Reacting Like You Mean It

You understand much more than you say. In a real conversation, that gap shows in the small moments: someone tells a story and you need to react, fast and naturally. Today you learn the small words that do that job.

Chapter 1: First Words

The Small Moments

In a conversation, most of what we say is not information. It is reaction: really?, me too, fair enough, so did I. In Portuguese you do it without thinking. In English, these are the words that make you sound like yourself.

Think of a conversation in English where you understood everything but did not know how to react. What happened?

Chapter 3: The Code

So Do I, Neither Have I

Small words, big difference

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Answers Have in Common?

"I love winter." — "So do I!"

"I’ve never been to Quebec." — "Neither have I."

"I can’t skate." — "Really? I can!"

"I grew up near a lake." — "Did you? So did I."

Look at the blue words: do, have, can, did. Where do they come from? Look back at the first sentence of each pair. The answer never repeats the main verb; it borrows the auxiliary. Now look at the red words: so for a positive sentence, neither for a negative one.

JobFormExample
Agree with a positiveSo + auxiliary + I“I love winter.” — “So do I.”
Agree with a negativeNeither + auxiliary + I“I haven’t been there.” — “Neither have I.”
The informal wayI do too / I don’t either / Me too / Me neither“I don’t eat meat.” — “I don’t either.”
Disagreesubject + the opposite auxiliary“I can’t skate.” — “I can!”
Show interestan echo question with the same auxiliary“I grew up in Toronto.” — “Did you?”
The trapthe auxiliary follows the tense of the first sentenceNot “I went there.” — “So do I.” Say So did I.

In one line: react with the auxiliary of the first sentence: so for yes, neither for no, the opposite auxiliary to disagree.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Conversation, at Real Speed

One dinner, three recordings, one invitation

Dialogue

Dinner at Emily’s

E
So, Cauê, this is your first winter trip to Canada, right? I hope you brought a real coat.
C
I did, but to be honest, I don’t think it’s warm enough. I’m from São Paulo.
E
Fair enough. I’ve never been to Brazil, but I’d love to go.
C
You should! And I’ve never seen real snow. Tomorrow will be my first time.
E
Haven’t you? Wow. I grew up with it, and I still love it.
C
So do I, and I haven’t even touched it yet! Your brother told me you have a lot in common with me: old movies.
E
He’s right. I watch everything with subtitles, even in English. Most people think that’s strange.
C
Not necessarily. I do the same thing, so I see your point. And honestly, it makes sense.
Comprehension

About Emily

1. Has Emily ever been to Brazil?
No, she hasn’t, but she would love to go.
2. How does Emily feel about snow?
She grew up with it and she still loves it.
3. What does Emily do that most people think is strange?
She watches everything with subtitles, even in English.

How Small Talk Works Here

A woman from Toronto explains small talk to a group of newcomers. Sound first — no text.

The weather and hockey.

Listening 2 · A Podcast About Words

When Yes Means No

A British podcast host talks about polite expressions that do not always mean what they say. Sound first — no text.

1. What can I see your point really mean, according to him?
I understand you, but I do not agree.
2. What does he say about fair enough?
It means you accept what the other person said, even if it was not your first choice.
3. What is his advice for learners?
Listen to the tone of voice, not just the words.
Listening 3 · Making Friends Abroad

How I Broke the Ice

An Australian man talks about the first months after he moved to a new country. Sound first — no text.

1. Why was the first month difficult for him?

2. What changed everything?
He started reacting with short answers: really?, so did I, neither have I.
3. What did he and his neighbor have in common?
They both loved fishing.
