Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Reacting Like You Mean It -- So Do I, Neither Have I

A dinner with Canadian friends of the family and the small words that keep a conversation going. Key words: fair enough, I see your point, that makes sense, I couldn’t agree more, not necessarily, to be honest, small talk, to break the ice, to get along with someone, to catch up, to have a lot in common, to change the subject. Structure: short responses with auxiliaries.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Fair enough -- used to say that what someone said is reasonable, even if it is not your first choice
"You want to leave early? Fair enough, it is a long drive."
I see your point -- I understand your opinion, even if I do not fully agree
"I see your point, but I still prefer the original version."
That makes sense -- that is logical and easy to understand
"You moved to be closer to your family? That makes sense."
I couldn’t agree more -- I agree completely
"Winter here is too long. I couldn’t agree more."
Not necessarily -- not always true; it depends on the situation
"Expensive restaurants are better? Not necessarily."
To be honest -- used before you say what you really think
"To be honest, I prefer summer to winter."
Small talk -- light, polite conversation about things that are not important
"Canadians usually start with small talk about the weather."
To break the ice -- to say or do something that makes people feel relaxed when they first meet
"A joke about the cold helped to break the ice."
To get along with someone -- to have a friendly relationship with someone
"I get along with my brother-in-law, even without Portuguese."
To catch up -- to talk with someone you have not seen for a while and hear their news
"We had dinner to catch up after two years."
To have a lot in common -- to share many interests, opinions or experiences
"We have a lot in common: we both love old movies."
To change the subject -- to start talking about something different
"When the topic got political, she changed the subject."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At a dinner in Toronto, Cauê meets Emily, a friend of the family. They start with small talk about the cold. “I’ve never seen real snow,” he says. “Haven’t you?” she answers. “I grew up with it.” A joke about his coat helps to break the ice.

Soon they discover they have a lot in common. “I love old movies,” Emily says. “So do I!” he answers. “I don’t like horror movies.” “Neither do I.” They get along very well.

When her cousin says subtitles ruin a movie, Cauê answers: “I see your point, but not necessarily.” The cousin laughs: “Fair enough.” Then someone changes the subject to hockey.

1. Why does Cauê say “Neither do I” and not “So do I” to the sentence about horror movies?
A Because the sentence about horror movies is in the past, and the past always takes neither.
B Because Emily’s sentence is negative.
C Because neither is more polite than so in Canada.
2. What does Emily mean with “Haven’t you?”
A She is surprised and interested.
B She does not believe him and wants to change the subject to something else.
C She did not hear what he said, so she asks him to repeat it.
3. Which answer is correct? “I went to Toronto last year.”
A “So do I.”
B “So did I.”
C “So I did go there too, last year.”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Short Responses -- So Do I, Neither Have I

GRAMMAR

To react, English does not repeat the main verb. It borrows the auxiliary from the first sentence: do, does, did, have, can, will, am, is, are.

JobFormExample
Agree with a positiveSo + auxiliary + I“I love winter.” — “So do I.”
Agree with a negativeNeither + auxiliary + I“I haven’t been there.” — “Neither have I.”
The informal wayI do too / I don’t either / Me too / Me neither“I don’t eat meat.” — “I don’t either.”
Disagreesubject + the opposite auxiliary“I can’t skate.” — “I can!”
Show interestan echo question with the same auxiliary“I grew up in Toronto.” — “Did you?”
The trapthe auxiliary follows the tense of the first sentenceNot “I went there.” — “So do I.” Say So did I.

Never: I went there. -- So do I. · Neither I have · I don’t like it. -- Me too. · I get along my cousin

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Someone says: “I don’t like cold weather.” You feel the same. You say:
A "Me too, I also don’t like cold weather very much."
B "Neither do I."
C "So do I, I don’t like it."
A new friend says: “I grew up near the ocean.” You want to show interest. You say:
A "Did you?"
B "Do you grow up near the ocean? Tell me more about that."
C "Are you? I am interested in it."
You partly disagree with someone’s opinion. You say:
A "You are wrong and I don’t agree with anything you said."
B "I see your point, but I’m not sure."
C "I couldn’t agree more, but you are not right about this."
You meet an old friend after two years. You say:
A "Let’s have coffee and break the ice of our friendship again."
B "Let’s have coffee and catch up."
C "Let’s have coffee and change the subject of our lives."
