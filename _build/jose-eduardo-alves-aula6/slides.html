Real Document

Who Pays for the Extra Day?

LOADING STATEMENT
m/v Northern Star · Santos · sugar · matter reviewed by E. Alves
Cargo agreed60,000 tons
Cargo actually loaded58,400 tons
Days allowed for loading11
Days actually used13
Price of one extra dayUSD 24,000
Export quota still available140,000 tons
Condition: the seller pays for the extra time only if the delay was caused by the terminal. Rain does not count as a delay under this contract.
1. How many extra days did the loading take, and how much do they cost in total?
Two extra days, at twenty-four thousand dollars each, so forty-eight thousand dollars in total.
2. How much cargo did they load, and how much less is that than the agreed amount?
Fifty-eight thousand four hundred tons, which is one thousand six hundred tons less than the sixty thousand agreed.
3. It rained for two days. Who pays, and why?
Not the seller. The condition at the bottom says rain does not count as a delay under this contract.
Chapter 4: The Code

What You Count, and What You Measure

One noun, one question word

Grammar Discovery

Why Do These Words Split in Two?

"There are a few difficult calls a month."

"There is not much drama in this job."

"How many credits does the program have?"

"How much tuition does it cost?"

The red words sit in front of things you can count one by one. The blue ones sit in front of things you can only measure.

JobYou can count itYou can only measure it
The nounsa credit, a condition, an option, a quota, a daytuition, information, cargo, volume, time, money
AskingHow many credits?How much tuition?
A small quantitya few callsa little time
Saying it is not enoughnot many optionsnot much information
A large quantitya lot of works for both: a lot of options, a lot of cargo
Positive and questionsome in a positive sentence, any in a question or a negative: "we need some information" · "are there any conditions?"
The trapno plural and no a on the measured ones: not an information, not two informations, not many cargos

In one line: if you can put a number in front of it, it is many. If you cannot, it is much.

Listening 1 · Operations Note

Four Numbers, and I Am Done

A Monday morning note from a trading desk. Sound first — no text.

1. How much cargo is on the vessel, and how many days do they have to load it?
Sixty thousand tons, and eleven days.
2. What happened to the export quota, and what is she doing about it?
It changed on Friday. A few contracts still carry the old figure, and she is correcting them today.
3. What does she say about the price of the extra time?
It is already in the contract. Nobody negotiates it when the problem happens — they only pay it.
Listening 2 · Before You Sign

Four Questions Nobody Asks in Time

An academic adviser on the arithmetic of a program. Sound first — no text.

1. What are his first two questions?
How many credits the degree has and how many of them are compulsory; and how much the tuition is and how many payments you can make.
2. What kind of conditions does he say to look for in the second year?
Things like a minimum grade or a language certificate.
3. What does he point out about all four questions, and where is the answer usually hidden?
Not one of them is about the ranking. Most of that information is on a page that almost nobody opens.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I choose between how much and how many without stopping to think about it.
I never write "an information" or "informations". I say some information.
I use a few for what I count and a little for what I measure.
I ask "are there any conditions?" and I say "we need some information".
I know the words: an amount, a quantity, cargo, volume, a quota, tuition, a credit, a condition, information, an option.
