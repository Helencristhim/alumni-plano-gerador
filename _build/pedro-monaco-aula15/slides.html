Chapter 3: The Code

The Background, the Event and the Back-Story

Narrative tenses: past continuous and past perfect

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I was reviewing the contract when the client called."

"By the time we noticed, the other side had already signed."

"We had been negotiating for three months when the deal collapsed."

"While the partners were arguing, the deadline passed."

All four sentences are about the past, and in all four something else happened at a different moment. In each one, which action was already in progress, which one came first, and which one interrupted?

FormWhat it doesExample
past simpleThe MAIN events, in the order they happened. This is the spine of the story: everything else hangs on it."I noticed the clause, I called my boss, and we read it together."
past continuous: was / were + -ingThe BACKGROUND: something already in progress when the main event happened. It sets the scene and makes the listener see the moment."I was reviewing the contract when the client called."
past perfect: had + participleThe BACK-STORY: something that happened EARLIER than the moment you are telling. It lets you jump back without saying a date."By the time we found it, the contract had already renewed."
past perfect continuous: had been + -ingSomething that had been going on for a while UP TO that moment. It tells the listener how long, and usually how tired everybody was."We had been negotiating for three months when the deal collapsed."
when · while · by the time · alreadywhile + continuous for the background, when + simple for the interruption, by the time + past perfect for what was already finished."While the partners were arguing, the deadline passed."

None of these forms is new to you. What is new is choosing them on purpose, so that you can start the story at its best moment and still keep the listener sure of the order.

Chapter 4: The Story

Tell Me About a Case That Went Wrong

Megan Hart, a partner in New York, and the question nobody prepares for

Dialogue

The Clause Nobody Had Flagged

M
So, Pedro, tell me about a case that did not go to plan. Everybody has one.
P
Mine came out of the blue. I was reviewing a supplier renewal on a Friday evening when I noticed a clause that nobody had flagged.
M
Let me guess. It had renewed automatically.
P
Exactly. By the time I found it, the contract had already rolled over for three years, and the supplier had been sending invoices at the new price for a month.
M
That would catch anybody off guard. What did you do?
P
I spent the weekend trying to piece together the paper trail. The turning point was an old email: their own manager had promised us ninety days of notice.
M
So it backfired on them. I had a case like that in my second year. It dragged on for eight months because we were arguing about the wrong clause.
P
Ours came to a head in one meeting. In hindsight, the email was the whole case. We just had not read it.
Comprehension

About Megan

1. What does Megan guess about the clause?
That it had renewed automatically.
2. How does she react when Pedro explains the renewal?
She says it would catch anybody off guard and asks what he did.
3. When did she have a similar case?
In her second year.
4. Why did her case drag on for eight months?
Because they were arguing about the wrong clause.
Listening 1

A Story Is Not a List

A trial lawyer on how she prepares a witness to tell what happened. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she tell every witness a story is not?
A list.
2. What mistake does she say most people make?
They tell everything in the order it happened, and the listener gets lost by the third date.
3. Where does she tell them to start?
At the moment things changed, and then go back.
Listening 2

The Worst Week of My Career

An in-house lawyer on the email nobody had seen. Sound first — no text.

1. Where was he, and what was he doing, when his phone rang?
In a hotel in Chicago, preparing for a completely different meeting.
2. Why had nobody in the office seen the termination email?
It had gone to an address that nobody had checked since the merger.
3. What does he say the real problem was, in hindsight?
Not the customer, but the notice clause they had written themselves.
