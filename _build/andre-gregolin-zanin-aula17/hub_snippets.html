Cause, Effect, and Consequence -- Which Way the Arrow Points

Where the rust in your English came from, and the language that explains why things happen. Key words: a root cause, a factor, a consequence, a knock-on effect, a vicious circle, to stem from, to bring about, to backfire, unintended, in turn. Structure: cause-and-effect language (lead to / result from / because of / which in turn).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A root cause -- the deepest reason a problem exists, not the obvious one
"My mistakes were not the root cause. The real problem was two years without practice."
A factor -- one of several things that influence a result
"Sleep is one factor in my scores, but it is not the only one."
A consequence -- a result of an action, often an important or a bad one
"Moving to Franca had one consequence I did not expect: I stopped using English."
A knock-on effect -- an indirect effect that one change has on other things
"Less sleep had a knock-on effect on my focus in the afternoon."
A vicious circle -- a situation where one problem causes another that makes the first one worse
"I speak less because I feel rusty, and I feel rusty because I speak less. It is a vicious circle."
To stem from -- to be the result of something; to have its origin in it
"Most of my stress stems from the exam calendar, not from the work itself."
To bring about -- to make something happen, especially a change
"Two years of basketball brought about a big change in my discipline."
To backfire -- to have the opposite result of the one you wanted
"Studying until two in the morning backfired. I forgot everything the next day."
Unintended -- not planned or expected
"The unintended effect of the move was that my English got rusty."
In turn -- as a result of the thing just mentioned, as the next step in a chain
"Better sleep improved my focus, which in turn improved my scores."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When you explain why something happened, every cause-and-effect verb has a direction, like an arrow. Some verbs start with the cause and point forward: The move led to a new routine. Two years without practice resulted in a drop in my fluency. Others start with the effect and point back: The new routine resulted from the move. My stress stems from the exam calendar. Result in and result from look almost the same, so draw the arrow before you speak.

The small word after the connector matters, too. Because of and due to are followed by a noun: because of the schedule. Because is followed by a full clause: because I moved. And when a cause acts on a person, English says The schedule caused me to stop, never caused that I stopped.

Real explanations are rarely one straight line. They are chains: Less sleep hurt my focus, which in turn hurt my scores. Which in turn adds the next link, and a careful speaker often says one factor instead of the reason.

1. According to the text, what is the difference between lead to and result from?
A They mean exactly the same thing, so you can always use one in place of the other in any sentence.
B They point in opposite directions: one starts with the cause, the other with the effect.
C Lead to is formal and result from is informal.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A I was late because of the traffic.
B I was late because of I missed the bus that goes to the cursinho in the morning.
C I was late due to I missed the bus.
3. What does which in turn do in a sentence?
A It introduces the first cause of a problem, before the effects and the consequences are explained.
B It shows a contrast between two ideas.
C It adds the next link in a chain of effects.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cause and Effect

GRAMMAR

Decide where the arrow starts, then choose the verb.

DirectionFormExample
Cause → effectlead to · result in · bring about · causeThe move led to a new routine.
Effect ← causeresult from · stem from · be caused by · be due toThe routine resulted from the move.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A friend asks why you stopped studying English at sixteen. You say:
A The move caused that I stopped my classes, and after that I never had time to go back to them.
B I stopped because of the move to Franca.
C I stopped due to I moved to Franca.
Your teacher asks where your stress comes from. You say:
A Most of it stems from the exam calendar.
B Most of my stress results in the exam calendar and in all the pressure that comes with it.
C The exam calendar stems from my stress.
You tried to save time by skipping lunch, and you were too tired to study in the afternoon. You say:
A My plan brought about exactly the result that I wanted, so I am going to do it again tomorrow.
B My plan was a root cause.
C My plan backfired.
You want to sound careful, not absolute, about the reason for a problem. You say:
A Sleep is the only reason, and there is absolutely nothing else that could explain the problem.
B Sleep is one factor, but probably not the only one.
C Sleep is the vicious circle.
