Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read it in context. These are the words critics use to describe how movements begin, break with the past and gain or lose reputations.

En plein air -- painted outdoors, directly in front of the subject
"Working en plein air, the painters chased changing light."
Brushwork -- the visible way a painter applies paint with a brush
"Seen up close, the brushwork looks almost careless."
Fleeting -- lasting for a very short time
"Monet wanted to capture a fleeting effect of light."
Ephemeral -- lasting a very short time and then disappearing
"Fog, reflections and smoke are ephemeral subjects."
Spontaneity -- the quality of seeming natural, unplanned and immediate
"The spontaneity of the canvas was carefully constructed."
Dissolve -- to seem to melt or disappear into something else
"The boats dissolve into the morning haze."
Flatness -- the quality of looking two-dimensional rather than deep
"Critics later praised the flatness they had once mocked."
Avant-garde -- artists or ideas that are new, experimental and ahead of their time
"The avant-garde defined itself against the official Salon."
Rupture -- a sudden, complete break with what came before
"Some historians describe Impressionism as a rupture; others as an evolution."
Deride -- to mock or laugh at something as worthless
"Critics derided the canvases as unfinished sketches."
Precursor -- someone or something that comes before and prepares the way for another
"Manet is often seen as a precursor of modern painting."
Lineage -- a line of descent or influence from one generation to the next
"Critics trace a lineage from Manet to Picasso."
Canon -- the group of works considered the most important in a tradition
"Paintings once rejected are now at the center of the canon."
Audacious -- showing bold willingness to take risks
"Exhibiting outside the Salon was an audacious decision."
Reappraisal -- a new assessment of the value or meaning of something
"The twentieth century brought a reappraisal of the movement."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Readers who find critical prose slow are often not struggling with vocabulary. They are struggling with compression. Academic writers routinely remove words that a reader is expected to supply, and a reader who does not recognize the missing pieces has to reread every sentence twice.

The most common form of compression is the participle clause. Painted outdoors and at speed, the canvases looked unfinished. The full version would be because they had been painted outdoors and at speed, the canvases looked unfinished, but the reader can reconstruct the subject and the relationship without help. A past participle, as in painted or rejected by the jury, usually carries a passive meaning. A present participle, as in working en plein air, carries an active one and often suggests that two actions happen at the same time. A perfect participle, as in having been derided, places one action clearly before another.

All three depend on a single condition: the participle must share the subject of the main clause. When it does not, the sentence acquires an unintended meaning. Walking into the gallery, the paintings seemed brighter tells the reader, absurdly, that the paintings were walking. Grammarians call this a dangling participle, and even experienced writers produce it when they revise in a hurry.

A second form of compression is ellipsis, the omission of words that have already appeared. In Monet painted the harbor; Morisot, the garden, the verb painted is understood in the second clause, and a comma marks the gap. Ellipsis works best in parallel structures, where the reader can easily see what has been removed.

Recognizing these patterns is the difference between reading critical prose and decoding it. Producing them sparingly, and correctly, is the difference between writing that explains and writing that moves.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Participle Clauses and Ellipsis

GRAMMAR

Participle clauses compress a subordinate clause when it shares the subject of the main clause. Ellipsis removes words a reader can recover from a parallel structure.

FormWhat it doesExample
past participle clausepassive meaning, same subjectPainted outdoors, the canvases looked unfinished.
present participle clauseactive meaning, often simultaneousWorking en plein air, Monet chased the light.
having + past participlean earlier completed actionHaving been rejected, the group exhibited alone.
ellipsis (gapping)a repeated verb is omitted and a comma marks the gapMonet painted the harbor; Morisot, the garden.
The C2 trapthe dangling participle. If the subject of the main clause cannot perform the action of the participle, rewrite the sentence.

Check every participle clause by asking: who is doing this? The answer must be the subject that follows the comma.
