Chapter 4: The Style Code

Saying More with Less

Participle clauses and ellipsis: the grammar of compression

Discover the Style

Where Did the Subject and Verb Go?

"Painted outdoors and at speed, the canvases looked unfinished to the Salon."

"Having been rejected by the jury, the group organized its own exhibition."

"Seen up close, the brushwork dissolves into separate strokes."

"Monet painted the harbor at dawn; Morisot, the garden at noon."

Expand each colored part into a full clause. What was removed, and why does the sentence still make sense?

FormWhat it doesExample
past participle clausepassive meaning, same subject as the main clausePainted outdoors, the canvases looked unfinished.
present participle clauseactive meaning, often simultaneousWorking en plein air, Monet chased the light.
having + past participlean action completed before the main oneHaving been rejected, the group exhibited alone.
ellipsis (gapping)omits a verb already stated in a parallel clauseMonet painted the harbor; Morisot, the garden.
The C2 trapthe dangling participle. The participle must share the subject of the main clause: "Walking into the gallery, the paintings seemed brighter" says the paintings were walking.

Chapter 5: Voices in the Galleries

The Movement, Out Loud

A museum educator, an exhibition guide and a lecture on modernism

Dialogue

Julian and the Postcard Problem

J
Confession: I find this whole room a little boring. Water lilies, picnics, pretty light. It looks like the gift shop.
S
That is exactly what the critics in 1874 would have hated to hear. They thought these paintings were ugly.
J
Ugly? Show me one ugly thing on this wall.
S
Seen with their eyes, all of it. The brushwork looked unfinished, and the subjects were far too ordinary for a serious painting.
J
So the scandal was not the content but the handling.
S
Mostly, yes. Having learned to see them, we find them pretty. Having never seen anything like them, the critics derided them.
J
Nice use of the participle. You realize you are arguing that we have been trained to like them?
S
Arguably, yes. Which is why the gift shop is not evidence against them. It is evidence that they won.
J
I used to tell visitors that Impressionism was the last polite movement. I may need a new line.
S
Try this one: the most audacious paintings in the museum are the ones printed on umbrellas.
Comprehension

About Julian

1. What does Julian confess about the Impressionist room at the start?
That he finds it a little boring, because it looks like the gift shop: water lilies, picnics and pretty light.
2. How does Julian summarize Stella's point about the scandal?
That the scandal was not the content but the handling.
3. What does Julian say he used to tell visitors, and what does he conclude?
That Impressionism was the last polite movement. He concludes he may need a new line.
Listening 1

How an Insult Became a Name

An exhibition guide speaks in front of a harbor painting. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. Where and when was the painting first shown, according to the guide?
In 1874, at an independent exhibition organized in a photographer's former studio in Paris.
2. How did a critic use the title of the painting?
He mocked it, suggesting that an impression was all the painter had managed, and called the whole group Impressionists.
3. What does the guide invite visitors to do before moving on?
To step back several paces and watch the loose strokes turn back into a harbor, boats and a rising sun.
Listening 2

After the Impression

A professor lectures on the road from Impressionism to modernism. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. Why does the lecturer say the story of modern art is often told too neatly?
Because it is presented as a straight line of heroes, each one leading to the next, when in fact many paths were tried and abandoned.
2. What does she say later painters took from the Impressionists, and what did they reject?
They took the freedom of the visible brushstroke and ordinary subjects, and rejected the idea that painting should record a passing moment of light.
3. What does she mean by calling flatness the quiet revolution?
That painters gradually stopped pretending the canvas was a window and accepted it as a flat surface covered with paint.
