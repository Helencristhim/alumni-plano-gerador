Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

Dinner With Eight People -- Hosting and Talking at the Table

Eight chairs, two languages, and one host who has to keep all of it moving. Key words: to host, to break the ice, to make somebody feel at home, to help yourself, a second helping, an acquired taste, to talk shop, a lull, to draw somebody out, to steer the conversation, to hit it off, a toast. Structure: question tags and interaction signals.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To host -- to be the person who invites guests and looks after them
"We host about once a month, never more than eight people."
To break the ice -- to say or do something that makes strangers feel relaxed at the start
"The wine question broke the ice before anybody had sat down."
To make somebody feel at home -- to make a guest feel relaxed, welcome and comfortable
"She made everybody feel at home in about five minutes."
To help yourself -- to take food or drink yourself, without waiting to be served
"Please help yourself. Nothing on this table is decorative."
A second helping -- a second portion of the same food
"Nobody refuses a second helping of my mother's moqueca."
An acquired taste -- something most people only learn to like after trying it a few times
"Cachaça is an acquired taste, so start with a small glass."
To talk shop -- to talk about your work in a social situation, often boring for the others
"The two lawyers talked shop until dessert, and nobody else could join in."
A lull -- a short, quiet moment when a conversation stops
"There is always a lull just after the main course arrives."
To draw somebody out -- to help a quiet person talk more, by asking the right questions
"It took two questions to draw him out, and then he told the best story of the night."
To steer the conversation -- to move a conversation gently toward a different subject
"When it got political, she steered the conversation back to the food."
To hit it off -- to like each other immediately, from the first conversation
"I seated them together and they hit it off before the first course."
A toast -- a few words said before everybody drinks, to honor a person or a moment
"Keep the toast short: one sentence about the guest and one about the evening."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Rodrigo hosts eight people on a Saturday night, and the last guest to arrive is Claire, who knows nobody. "You found the building all right, didn't you?" he asks at the door. It is not really a question. It is a way to break the ice, and it works: "He does take his job seriously, doesn't he?" she says about the doorman, and they are already talking.

He offers her a glass of wine from the south of Brazil. "It is an acquired taste, isn't it?" she says after the first sip, smiling. Then he tells her to help herself to anything on the table, and warns her that there will be a second helping whether she wants one or not. By then she feels at home.

The difficult part comes later. Paulo starts to talk shop, and there is a lull at the other end of the table. Rodrigo steers the conversation toward Marina, who has not said a word: "You have just come back from Japan, haven't you?" His voice goes down at the end, because he is not asking, he is inviting. It is enough to draw her out. "Have you been?" she asks. "Have I? Twice," he says. By dessert, Marina and Claire have hit it off, and Rodrigo stands up for a short toast.

1. "You found the building all right, didn't you?" Why didn't and not did?
A Because the question is about the past.
B Because a positive sentence takes a negative tag, with the same auxiliary.
C Because didn't is more polite than did.
2. "You have just come back from Japan, haven't you?" Why does his voice go down at the end?
A Because he already knows the answer and is inviting her to talk, not asking for information.
B Because he is not sure she has been to Japan.
C Because tags always go down in American English.
3. When Marina asks "Have you been?", Rodrigo answers "Have I? Twice." What is Have I? doing?
A It shows he did not hear the question.
B It asks her to repeat the question more slowly.
C It is a short reply that shows interest and keeps the conversation moving.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Question Tags and Interaction Signals

GRAMMAR

Copy the auxiliary, flip the sign, and let the tune decide whether you are asking or inviting.

JobFormExample
Same auxiliaryhave → haven't · is → isn't · can → can'tYou have met Paulo, haven't you?
Opposite signpositive → negative tag · negative → positive tagYou don't eat meat, do you?
No auxiliarydo / does / didShe lives in Rome, doesn't she?
Two special onesI am → aren't I · Let's → shall weLet's sit down, shall we?
Voice goes downyou expect agreementLovely evening, isn't it?
Voice goes upyou really do not knowThe dinner is at eight, isn't it?
The listener's tagauxiliary + subject, to show interestI grew up in Salvador. Did you?

Never: isn't it? after every sentence · You don't drink wine, don't you? · She lives in Rome, doesn't it? · Let's sit down, don't we? · a direct question where a tag would invite

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A guest tells you she has never tried Brazilian wine. You answer:
A "You have never tried it, isn't it?"
B "You have never tried it, have you? Some of it is an acquired taste, so start with this one."
C "You have never tried it, haven't you?"
Two lawyers at your table have been talking shop for ten minutes. You say:
A "Please stop talking about work."
B "That sounds exhausting, doesn't it? Speaking of long days, Tom, you ran a marathon last month, didn't you?"
C "Why are you talking about work at dinner?"
A guest tells you: I lived in Chicago for ten years. You show interest with:
A "Did you? Then you know the winters, don't you?"
B "Why did you live there?"
C "Chicago is in the United States."
You want everybody to sit down for dinner. You say:
A "Let's sit down, don't we?"
B "Sit down now, please."
C "Let's sit down, shall we? The food is ready."
