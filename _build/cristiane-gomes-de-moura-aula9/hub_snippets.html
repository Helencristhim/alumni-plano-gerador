What Should I Do? -- Giving Advice Without Giving an Order

Describing something is one skill. Telling somebody what to do about it is another one, and it needs three small words. Key words: an option, a risk, to worry, to give up, worth it, to warn somebody, a mistake, honestly, it depends, to think it over. Structure: should and shouldn’t for advice, could for an option, might for what is only possible — and never a to after any of them.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An option -- one of the things you are allowed to pick
"You have two options, and neither of them is wrong."
A risk -- something that could go wrong, and you know it before you start
"It was a risk, you took it with your eyes open, and it did not work."
To worry -- to keep thinking about a problem that has not happened yet
"That is exactly the part I keep worrying about."
To give up -- to stop trying, before the thing is finished
"You should think it over, but you shouldn't give up on it."
Worth it -- good enough to pay for with your money, your time or your patience
"Go for one month first and see whether it is worth it."
To warn somebody -- to tell somebody about a problem before they walk into it
"I think you should go. But I am going to warn you about one thing first."
A mistake -- something you did that you would do differently now
"If the answer is yes, it was not a mistake. It was a risk."
Honestly -- the word you put in front of an opinion the other person may not want
"Honestly? I think you should go."
It depends -- the answer when the right answer changes with the situation
"Everybody gave me a different answer, and every one of them said it depends."
To think it over -- to take time before you answer, instead of answering now
"Maybe I should think it over for another week."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Until now you have been describing: what you do, what you did, what was going on. Advice is different, because it is not a fact — it is your opinion about somebody else’s life, and English gives you three small words to say exactly how heavy you want that opinion to be. The heaviest is should: "I think you should go." It pushes. It says this is what I would do in your place, and I am not hiding it. Its negative is shouldn’t: "You shouldn’t decide this on a Sunday night."

The middle one is could. It does not push — it opens a door and lets the other person walk through it or not: "You could go for one month first and see." That is why could is the safest word in a difficult conversation: you are giving an option, not an instruction. And the lightest of the three is might, which only admits that something is possible: "You might not know a single person there for a year." No opinion at all — just a risk put on the table so nobody can say later that nobody warned them.

All three behave the same way, and it is the easy part. The verb after them never changes: no -s, no -ed, and the same form for every person — I should, he should, they should. Never he shoulds. To ask for advice, the small word jumps in front: "What should I do?" "Should I tell her?" — and the short answer is just the small word again: "Yes, you should." Now the trap, and it is the one Portuguese sets for you: never put a to after them. Portuguese says deveria ir, with a word in the middle, so the hand writes should to go. In English there is nothing in the middle. Nothing at all.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Should, Could, Might

GRAMMAR

Should pushes. Could offers. Might only says it is possible. None of the three ever takes to.

Stage 2: Put the Advice in Order

Order

This is how good advice is built. Put it in a logical order.

?"So they offered you the job. Tell me what the two options are."
?"Honestly? I think you should take it."
?"But I am going to warn you about one thing first."
?"You might not know a single person there for a year."
?"You could go for one month and decide after that."
?"And you? What frightens you more, the city or the job?"

Quiz
