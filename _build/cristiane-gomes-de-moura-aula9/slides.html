Chapter 3: The Code

Three Small Words That Change the Weight

One pushes, one offers, one only admits it is possible

Grammar Discovery

One Pushes. One Offers. One Only Says Maybe.

"Honestly? I think you should go."

"You could go for one month first and see."

"You might not know a single person there for a year."

"You shouldn't decide this on a Sunday night."

The blue word carries all the weight, and the red verb never changes: no -s, no -ed, and never a to in front of it. Line them up by strength: should pushes, could offers, might only admits it is possible.

JobFormExample
Advice — what you think is rightshould + plain verbI think you should go.
Advice againstshouldn't + plain verbYou shouldn't decide this tonight.
An option — one way out of severalcould + plain verbYou could go for one month first.
Possible — maybe yes, maybe nomight + plain verbYou might like it. You might not know anybody.
Asking for adviceWhat should I + verb? · Should I + verb?What should I do? · Should I take it?
Short answerYes, you should. / No, you shouldn't.Should I tell her? — Yes, you should.
Softening a strong opinionI think you should · Maybe you should · Honestly, I wouldMaybe you should think it over first.
The three never changeno -s, no -ed, same for every personI / you / he / she / we / they should. Never he shoulds.
The trapnever a to after themNot you should to go, not you could to wait. Portuguese puts a word there. English does not.

In one line: should pushes, could offers, might only admits it is possible — and none of the three ever takes to, -s or -ed.

Comprehension

About Daniel

1. What was he offered, and what are the three things he says about it?
A job in Curitiba: more money, a better team, and he has to move in March.
2. How long had he been putting off the question, and what does he keep worrying about?
All week. He keeps worrying about the part she warned him about — not knowing a single person there.
3. What does he say he might do instead of answering now?
He says maybe he should think it over for another week.
Listening 1 · Stop Asking

The Best Advice She Ever Got, and Ignored

A woman on four years of asking the wrong question. Sound first — no text.

1. What did the woman tell her, and what did she do about it?
That she should stop asking people whether she was ready, because nobody was ever going to say yes and it was the wrong question. She said thank you and carried on asking, for four years.
2. Who did she ask, and what did every answer have in common?
Her sister, her old boss, and a man on a plane who had no idea what she was talking about. Everybody gave a different answer, and every one of them started with the same two words: it depends.
3. What does she say now when somebody asks her whether they should do it?
She does not say yes and she does not say no. She says: you should stop asking. If you are still asking after two months, you already know, and you are looking for somebody to blame if it goes wrong.
Listening 2 · In Defense of It Depends

The Phrase Everybody Hates

A man arguing that the vaguest answer is the most useful one. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say people think when they hear it depends, and what does he think?
People think it sounds like somebody who will not commit. He says it is usually the opposite.
2. Give two of his examples of what the answer actually depends on.
Taking the job depends on whether you can afford six months of being bad at it. Telling your boss depends on what she did the last time somebody told her something she did not want to hear. Moving depends on how many people you know there and how quickly you make friends.
3. What does he say about people who answer with just a yes or just a no?
They are either not listening, or they have stopped being careful.
Listening 3 · A Risk Is Not a Mistake

Was I Right, or Would I Do It Again?

A short talk on the question that is actually worth asking. Sound first — no text.

1. How does she define a mistake, and how does she define a bad decision?
A mistake is when you had the information and you did not use it. A bad decision is when you did not have the information, you chose anyway, and it went badly.
2. What happens if you only call something a mistake after you see how it ended?
Every decision becomes a coin, and you spend the rest of your life frightened of choosing.
3. What does she say is the useful question, and what follows if the answer is yes?
Not was I right, but: with what I knew that Tuesday, is that still the choice I would make? If yes, it was not a mistake — it was a risk taken with your eyes open that did not work.

1. Which two lines make the offer look good, and which one makes it reversible?
The salary, at twenty-two per cent more, and the team going from nine people to twenty-one. The trial period makes it reversible: ninety days, and either side can stop.
2. How much time does he have between the answer and the start?
He answers by the nineteenth of December and starts on the second of March, so about ten weeks.
3. Give him three sentences of advice, using should, could and might once each.
You should ask who else is on the team of twenty-one. You could go for the trial period and decide in March. You might not know anybody there for a year, so I would look at that first.

No keywords, no notes, two minutes. Nobody will interrupt you.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can say what somebody should and shouldn't do, without sounding like an order.
I can offer an option with could, instead of pushing only one answer.
I use might for what is only possible, and I do not confuse it with should.
I never put to after should, could or might, and I never add an -s to them.
I know the words: an option, a risk, to worry, to give up, worth it, to warn somebody, a mistake, honestly, it depends, to think it over.
