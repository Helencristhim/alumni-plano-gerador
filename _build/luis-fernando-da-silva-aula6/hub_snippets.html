What You Can and What You Must

The same refusal, said two ways: to a family farm and to a distributor with a legal department. Key words: an obligation, a procedure, permission, a regulation, liability, to authorize, a restriction, collateral, to sign off on, by the book, that is not up to me, a request. Structure: modal verbs for obligation, permission and advice.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An obligation -- something you have to do, whether you want to or not
"Every file has an obligation on both sides. Ours is to deliver, his is to pay after the harvest."
A procedure -- the agreed set of steps a company follows to get something done
"The procedure is ours. We wrote it, and we can change it if it stops making sense."
Permission -- being officially allowed to do something
"Being asked for permission to break a rule is normal. Giving it is not."
A regulation -- a rule written by a government or an official body, from outside the company
"A regulation comes from outside. We do not get to argue with it, only to plan around it."
Liability -- legal responsibility for a loss, if something goes wrong
"If you are ever unsure whether the liability sits with us or with the dealer, stop and ask."
To authorize -- to give somebody official approval to do something
"I can authorize up to a limit. Above it, the answer is not mine to give."
A restriction -- a limit on what somebody is allowed to do
"There is a restriction on new customers in their first season, and it exists for a reason."
Collateral -- something valuable a customer offers, in case they cannot pay
"Most large files carry collateral. Nobody ever wants to use it, and that is the point."
To sign off on -- to give final approval to a document or a decision, usually in writing
"Before I sign off on a new limit, I have to see the compliance record."
By the book -- exactly according to the rules, with nothing improvised
"On a first season we do everything by the book. After ten years, there is room to talk."
That is not up to me -- a way of saying the decision belongs to somebody else, not to you
"That is not up to me, but I can take it to the finance director this afternoon."
A request -- a written or spoken ask, made politely and on the record
"His request came in writing, which tells me the legal department has already seen it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A customer asks for something you cannot give him, and English decides most of the answer with about eight small words. I must see the compliance record before I sign off on a new limit is an obligation that comes from the speaker. Above that figure, the request has to go to headquarters is an obligation that comes from the rules. Both are real, but they do not feel the same to the person hearing them: must sounds like you, have to sounds like the procedure. And there is a reason that matters. A written limit is the only thing that makes a refusal impersonal.

Then comes the pair that catches almost everybody. You do not have to decide today and you must not decide today are not the same sentence in two strengths. They are opposites. The first says it is optional; the second says it is forbidden. With a customer who owes you money, choosing the wrong one is not a grammar mistake, it is a commercial one. Beside them sit the softer forms: should and ought to give advice rather than orders, and can, could and may move up a scale of formality when you ask for permission.

The last part is register, and it is not politeness. To a family farm you say it plainly, on the telephone, and you say what you can do instead. To a large distributor you write it down, name the restriction, and use the flat institutional forms on purpose, because the person reading has to forward it upwards. I am not allowed to authorize that at this level and we are not able to authorize an increase at this stage refuse exactly the same thing at two different heights. Add that is not up to me when the decision genuinely sits elsewhere, do everything by the book in a first season, and remember that liability and collateral are the two words that decide who carries the loss when a regulation, an obligation or a season goes against you. One warning: after must and should there is never a to. Not I must to see it, but I must see it.

1. What is the difference between "I must see the record" and "the request has to go to headquarters"?
A "Must" is an obligation that comes from the speaker; "have to" comes from the rules or the situation.
B "Must" is present and "have to" is future.
C There is no difference. They are interchangeable in every situation.
2. "You do not have to decide today" and "you must not decide today" are:
A The same idea, one stronger than the other.
B Opposites. The first says it is optional, the second says it is forbidden.
C Both incorrect. English uses "cannot" in both cases.
3. According to the text, why does a large distributor get the written, formal version of the refusal?
A Because large companies deserve more respect than small ones.
B Because the formal register is always safer in business.
C Because the person reading it has to forward it upwards, and a warm, vague message is useless for that.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Must, Have To, Should, May

GRAMMAR

Do not have to is a door that is open. Must not is a door that is locked.

JobFormExample
Obligation from me, the speakermust + base verbI must see the compliance record first.
Obligation from the rules or the situationhave to / has to + base verbThe request has to go to headquarters.
NOT an obligation at alldo not have to = it is optionalYou do not have to decide today.
The opposite of thatmust not = it is forbiddenYou must not release the product.

Permission, going up the formal scalecan < could < mayCan I ask · Could I ask · May I ask?
The trapno to after must, should, can, mayNot I must to see it. Say I must see it.

Never: you must not decide today (when you mean it is optional) · I must to see the record · you should to talk to him · can I to ask? · he have to send it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to say that sending the documents this week is optional. You say:
A "You must not send the documents this week."
B "You do not have to send the documents this week."
C "You cannot need to send the documents this week."
A farmer you have known for twenty years asks for more room. The best first move is:
A "Pursuant to our internal policy, we are not able to authorize the requested increase."
B "I am not allowed to approve that on my own. What I can do is release part of the order this week."
C "Sorry, sorry, I really am very sorry, it is not possible, sorry."
The same refusal, now by email to a distributor whose legal department is copied. You write:
A "We are not able to authorize an increase at this stage. The request has to be signed off by the finance director."
B "Hi, no chance this week I am afraid, let us talk after the harvest."
C "The increase is impossible because the rules of the company do not permit for it."
You want to give advice without giving an order. You say:
A "You must to talk to the dealer before the harvest."
B "You should to talk to the dealer before the harvest."
C "I think you should talk to the dealer before the harvest."
