Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Say That Again -- The Call That Fails and the Polite Repair

The line breaks up, two people speak at once, and you need a sentence ready. Key words: patchy, to break up, to follow, a heads-up, a rundown, to run through something, to loop somebody in, to talk over someone, to drop off, to chime in, to get back to someone, to be on mute. Structure: indirect questions and polite requests.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Patchy -- coming and going, never steady -- said of a line or a signal
"Sorry, your line is a little patchy. Could you say that again?"
To break up -- when a call becomes impossible to hear because the connection fails
"You are breaking up a bit. I got the first half."
To follow -- to understand what somebody is saying while they are still saying it
"I am not sure I followed the last point. Could you take me through it again?"
A heads-up -- a warning given in advance, so that nobody is caught by surprise
"Thanks for the heads-up. I will have the draft ready before the call."
A rundown -- a short summary of the main points, given quickly
"Could you give me a quick rundown of what I missed?"
To run through something -- to go over the points of something quickly, one by one
"Let me run through the agenda before we start."
To loop somebody in -- to add a person to a conversation they should be part of
"I will loop our tax partner in before we answer that."
To talk over someone -- to keep speaking while another person is still speaking
"Sorry, I talked over you. Please go ahead."
To drop off -- to lose the connection and leave a call without meaning to
"I dropped off for a second. What did I miss?"
To chime in -- to add a short comment to a conversation that is already running
"May I chime in here for a second?"
To get back to someone -- to reply later, once you have found the answer
"I do not have that figure here. Can I get back to you this afternoon?"
To be on mute -- speaking with the microphone turned off, so nobody hears you
"I think you are on mute. We cannot hear you."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nine in the morning in São Paulo, one in the afternoon in London. The organizer opens the call and says she will run through the three points first, because she has a hard stop at half past. The line from her side is patchy from the first minute, and a warning arrives with it: if it breaks up, say so immediately.

She says a revised clause went out on Tuesday. He did not see it, and he does not say "I did not receive it". He says: "Could you tell me when exactly you sent that?" — a question with no inversion inside, and no accusation either. Then: "Would you mind copying our tax partner?", because he wants to loop the partner in before anybody answers on the indemnity. He was in a hearing all Tuesday, so he will check and get back to her before lunch.

Somebody on the New York line has been silent for ten minutes, and it turns out he is on mute. Another participant talks over the organizer twice, and then drops off completely for a minute. When he returns he asks for a quick rundown, because he could not follow the last exchange at all. At the end Rodrigo closes the way he always closes now: "So, to confirm: have I got that right?" Nobody has ever been annoyed by that question. It is the cheapest sentence on the call.

1. "Could you tell me when exactly you sent that?" Why is it not "when exactly did you send that"?
A Because the sentence is about the past.
B Because the question is now inside another sentence, so the word order goes back to subject before verb and the auxiliary disappears.
C Because could you tell me cannot be followed by a question word.
2. "Would you mind copying our tax partner?" Why copying and not copy?
A Because would you mind is always followed by the -ing form.
B Because the action is happening right now.
C Because the tax partner is a person and not a document.
3. He asks when the clause was sent instead of saying he never received it. What does the indirect question buy him?
A Nothing: the two sentences are identical in effect.
B It hides the fact that he did not read his email.
C It gets the same information without putting blame on anybody, which keeps the call moving instead of turning it into a defence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions and Polite Requests

GRAMMAR

The moment a question goes inside another sentence, it stops looking like a question.

JobFormExample
Direct questionverb before subjectWhen did they send it?
Indirect questionnormal order, no auxiliaryCould you tell me when they sent it?
Yes or no insideif / whetherI was wondering whether the clause is still there.
The openersCould you tell me / Do you know / I was wondering / Do you happen to knowall take normal order after them
Would you mind+ verb-ingWould you mind repeating that?
Softer requestCould / Would rather than CanCould you send it by Friday?
PunctuationI was wondering... is a statementI was wondering whether it arrived.

Never: Could you tell me when did they send it · Do you know where does it is · Would you mind to repeat · I was wondering if is the clause there? · Repeat, please (as your only repair)

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You missed half a sentence on a bad line. You say:
A "I do not understand your English."
B "Sorry, you are breaking up a bit. Could you say that last part again?"
C "Repeat."
You want to know when the draft was sent, without accusing anybody. You say:
A "Could you tell me when did you send the draft?"
B "Could you tell me when exactly you sent the draft?"
C "You never sent the draft."
Somebody has been speaking for four minutes and you need to come in. You say:
A "Sorry to interrupt. May I chime in before we move on?"
B "Stop talking, please."
C Nothing. You wait and send an email afterwards.
The call is ending and three things were agreed. You say:
A "Okay. Bye."
B "I hope I understood everything."
C "So, to confirm: I send the mark-up today and you loop in your tax partner. Have I got that right?"
