Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

The Building You Cannot Photograph

Describing a place so well that nobody needs the photograph. Key words: facade, archway, column, dome, mosaic, ornate, weathered, shade. Structure: the zero conditional for general truths -- if you arrive early, you avoid the crowds.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Facade -- the front wall of a building, the side you see from the street
"If you stand at the end of the street, the facades look flat and grey."
Archway -- a curved opening in a wall that people walk through
"If you stand under the archway and just wait, something else happens."
Column -- a tall round stone support that holds a roof or an arch above it
"The columns are Roman, taken from an older temple."
Dome -- a round roof, shaped like the top half of a ball
"If you look up from the middle of the square, you see the dome first."
Mosaic -- a picture made of many small coloured pieces of stone or glass
"The light lands on the mosaic above the altar at ten in the morning."
Ornate -- covered in decoration, with almost no empty space left
"Everything in here is ornate except the ceiling, which is completely plain."
Weathered -- changed in colour and surface by many years of sun, rain and wind
"The stone is weathered on one side only, where the wind comes from the sea."
Shade -- the cool dark area beside a wall, where the sun does not reach
"The shade is cold, the noise changes, and you start hearing the water."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is no good photograph of this street, and I always tell people the same thing. If you stand at the end of it, the facades look flat and grey, because a phone cannot see the texture of the stone. If you walk two more minutes and stop under the archway instead, everything changes. The shade there is cold even in August, the noise of the street disappears, and you hear water.

The four columns in front of you do not match, because somebody took them from an older building. The stone turns almost orange if the sun hits it at six in the evening. And high above the door there is one small mosaic, gold and blue and completely ornate, which almost nobody has photographed. It is the kind of place where nobody looks up. If nobody looks up, they never see it.

1. The text says "If you stand at the end of it, the facades look flat" -- not "will look". Why?
A Because the writer is describing one particular day in the past.
B Because it is always true. Every time somebody stands there, that is what they see.
C Because "will" is never correct in English after a comma.
2. Look at "The stone turns almost orange if the sun hits it at six". What does this tell you about the order of the two halves?
A The "if" half must always come first in a sentence.
B Either half can come first, and the meaning does not change.
C When the "if" half comes second, the verb changes to the past.
3. In "If nobody looks up, they never see it", why is there an "s" on "looks" but not on "see"?
A Because "nobody" behaves like he or she, and "they" does not take the "s".
B Because the verb after "if" always takes an "s" in this structure.
C Because "see" is an irregular verb and never takes an "s".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Zero Conditional

GRAMMAR

One sentence shape for everything that is always true about a place.

PartFormExample
The "if" partif + present simpleIf you arrive early...
The other partpresent simple again -- no "will"...you avoid the crowds.
What it meansA general truth: every time this happens, that happens. Not a plan and not a prediction.
The orderEither half can come first. With "if" first, use a comma: If the sun hits it, the stone turns orange · The stone turns orange if the sun hits it.
Third personThe "s" stays in both halves: If the sun hits the facade, the stone turns orange · If nobody looks up, they never see it.
Against "will"If you arrive early tomorrow, you will avoid the crowds is a prediction about one day. Take the "will" out and the sentence becomes a law of the place. That one word is the whole difference.

This is how a guide talks about a building, and how you explain a city to somebody who has never been there. Two useful openers for the same job: "It is the kind of place where..." and "What I noticed right away was..."

Quiz

You are describing a place to somebody who has never been there. Choose the best thing to say.

You want to say that this is always what happens when the sun is low, at any time of year. You say:
A "If the sun will be low, the stone will turn orange."
B "If the sun is low, the stone turns almost orange."
C "If the sun is being low, the stone is turning orange."
A friend asks what the front of the building looks like. The precise word is:
A "The dome is plain and grey."
B "The facade is plain and grey."
C "The shade is plain and grey."
The stone has clearly been standing there for six hundred years, in the sun and the rain. You say it is:
A "ornate"
B "weathered"
C "available"
You want to open a description by naming the first thing that caught your eye. You start with:

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Choose one building or one square you have never stopped thinking about. Take about ninety seconds and describe it to somebody who has never seen it and never will. Say what you notice first, what a photograph would miss, and what is always true about it at a certain hour. Use at least four words from this lesson and two sentences with "if". You cannot use the words beautiful, nice or incredible. Do not stop to correct yourself.
