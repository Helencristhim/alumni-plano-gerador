Chapter 4: The Code

It Must Have... It Can’t Have...

How sure are you about the past?

Grammar Discovery

How Sure Are You?

"The rain must have got into the sand."

"The night shift can’t have been responsible."

"The new operators might have made a mistake."

"The parts could have been damaged during transport."

All four sentences talk about the past, and nobody saw what happened. The speaker is deducing from evidence. The modal shows how sure the speaker is: almost certain, only possible, or impossible.

CertaintyFormExample
Almost certain (yes)must have + past participleThe rain must have got into the sand.
Possiblemight have / may have / could have + past participleThe operators might have made a mistake.
Almost certain (no)can’t have / couldn’t have + past participleThe night shift can’t have been responsible.
Passivemodal + have been + past participleThe parts could have been damaged in transport.
In progressmodal + have been + verb-ingThe furnace must have been running too cold.
The trapthe negative of must have is can’t have, not mustn’t haveNot “It mustn’t have been the machine.” Say It can’t have been the machine.

In one line: sure = must have; possible = might / may / could have; impossible = can’t / couldn’t have.

Grammar Practice

Choose the Modal

Say it first, then click to check

"The records show that the batch was cast on Tuesday, so the night shift ___can't have made it." (impossible, make)
"All the cracked parts have porosity. Moisture ___must have caused the problem." (almost certain, cause)
"I’m not sure. The supplier ___might have changed the material." (possible, change)
"The trucks were fine, so the parts ___can't have been damaged during transport." (impossible, passive, damage)
"The first parts of the week were cold. The furnace ___must have been running at a low temperature." (almost certain, in progress, run)

Listening 1 · A German Quality Engineer

Only on Monday Mornings

A quality engineer at a German car plant remembers a strange problem with brake parts from a supplier. Sound first — no text.

1. When were the parts failing the tests?
Only on Monday mornings.
2. Why can’t the transport have been the problem?
They checked the trucks, and they were fine.
3. What must have happened to the first parts of the week?
They must have been cast while the metal was still too cold.
Listening 2 · A Supplier Quality Manager in India

It’s Just the Weather

A supplier quality manager in Pune tells how he found the cause of small holes in engine parts. Sound first — no text.

1. What problem did every part have?
Small holes inside the metal.
2. What did he see when he visited the supplier?
It was the monsoon, and the sand storage area had no roof.
3. What question does he always ask first now?
What changed?
Listening 3 · A British Consultant

Leave the Names Out of It

A British consultant explains what he does first when he arrives at a factory after a problem. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the first thing he usually hears?
A name: someone to blame, for example the night shift.
2. What questions does he ask instead?
Which batch, when it was made, which machine, which material and which operator.
3. What was the real cause in his example?
A change in the material that nobody had noticed.
