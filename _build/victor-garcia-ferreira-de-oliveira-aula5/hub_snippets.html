Staying in the Room -- What to Say While You Are Still Thinking

Everybody needs a second to think. The difference is whether the room hears silence or hears you working. Key words: nerve-racking, to rehearse, composure, a follow-up question, concise, to elaborate, to go blank, to buy yourself time, to think out loud, to get back on track. Structure: discourse markers for buying time and holding the floor.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Nerve-racking -- making you nervous in a way you can feel in your body
"The first ten minutes were nerve-racking, and after that I forgot to be nervous."
To rehearse -- to practise something out loud before the real thing happens
"I rehearsed the first thirty seconds and improvised everything after that."
Composure -- the calm you keep, or lose, while people are watching you
"I lost my composure for about four seconds, and those four seconds felt like a week."
A follow-up question -- the question that comes after your answer, to test whether you meant it
"Anybody can survive the first question. The follow-up question is the real one."
Concise -- short and complete at the same time, which is harder than either one alone
"Two minutes is not a long time. A concise answer still needs a beginning and an end."
To elaborate -- to say more about something you have already said, when somebody asks for it
"Could you elaborate on that? means keep going, not say it again."
To go blank -- to lose everything you were about to say, for a few seconds
"I went blank on the second question and I still got the place, so it is survivable."
To buy yourself time -- to give yourself a few seconds without letting the conversation stop
"That is a good question is not politeness. It is three seconds, and it is legal."
To think out loud -- to let people hear you working the answer out, instead of hiding it
"Let me think out loud for a second is a full sentence, and it buys you the whole pause."
To get back on track -- to return to the point after you have drifted away from it
"Anyway, to get back on track, the real answer is the hours, not the money."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Something happens after about two seconds of silence, and it has nothing to do with your English. The listener stops thinking about your topic and starts guessing why you stopped, and the guess is almost never the true reason. You were looking for a word; they have decided you do not know the answer. That is the whole cost of a pause, and in a two-minute answer three of them are enough to change what somebody thinks of you.

The fix is not speaking faster, because speed was never the problem. The fix is a set of sentences that carry no content at all and do a job anyway. That is a good question buys you three seconds. There are two things here promises a shape you have not planned yet, and you invent the second thing while you are saying the first. What I mean is repairs a bad sentence without an apology. Anyway, coming back to your question pulls you out of a story you should not have started. And so, in short lands the ending, instead of letting it fade into nothing.

Three habits are worth losing, and all three are direct translations. How to say is not a question in English: say How can I put this? Let me explain better sounds like an accusation of the listener: say Let me put it another way. And answering your question should be to answer your question. One last rule, and it is the one people ignore: never apologise for pausing. Nobody had noticed until you said sorry, and from that second on, everyone is waiting for the next one.

1. According to the text, what does a listener do after about two seconds of silence?
A They wait patiently and keep thinking about the topic.
B They stop thinking about the topic and start guessing why you stopped.
C They decide your grammar is weak.
2. Why does the text say "There are two things here" is useful even before you know the two things?
A Because it promises a shape, and you invent the second thing while you are saying the first.
B Because it makes the answer sound longer than it is.
C Because interviewers always ask for exactly two reasons.
3. What does the text say about apologising for a pause?
A It is polite and it repairs the silence.
B It is necessary if the pause was longer than three seconds.
C Nobody had noticed until you said sorry, and after that everyone is waiting for the next pause.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Discourse Markers That Hold the Floor

GRAMMAR

You are not filling silence. You are showing the room that you are still driving.

JobWhat you sayWhat it buys you
Buy two or three secondsThat is a good question · Let me think about that for a secondTime, without silence
Announce the shapeThere are two things here · First of all... · and the second thing is...A plan you invent while you speak
Repair without apologisingWhat I mean is... · Or rather... · Let me put it another wayA second attempt that sounds deliberate
Come back after driftingAnyway, coming back to your question... · To answer your question...Control of a long turn
Add without starting againOn top of that... · which brings me to...A second minute connected to the first
Land the endingSo, in short... · That is really the whole answerA finish, instead of a fade
Ask for a momentGive me one moment to think · How can I put this?A pause that sounds chosen

Never: how to say... · let me explain better · answering your question · I need one moment for think · sorry for the pause.

Stage 2: Put the Answer in Order

Order

You have just been asked a hard question you have never seen. Put the two minutes in the order that keeps the room with you.

?"That is a good question, and the honest answer is that it depends."
?"There are two things here. First of all, the hours."
?"What I mean is that a classroom always waits, and a team does not."
?"On top of that, nobody there ever waited for me to translate."
?"Anyway, coming back to your question, I would still do it again."
?"So, in short, the hours were the cost and the English was the return."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You have been asked something hard and you need three seconds. You say:
A Nothing, and you look at the ceiling until the answer arrives.
B "That is a good question, and the honest answer is that it depends."
C "Sorry, one moment, I need to think about this."
Your last sentence came out badly and you want to try again. You say:
A "Let me explain better."
B "What I mean is that a classroom always waits, and a team does not."
C "How to say... it is different, no?"
You have been talking for a minute about the wrong thing. You say:
A "Anyway, coming back to your question, I would still do it again."
B "Answering your question, I would do it again."
C "Sorry, I forgot the question, can you repeat it?"
Two minutes are nearly up and you want to finish properly. You say:
A "And... yes. That is it. I think."
B "I do not have more things to say about this."
C "So, in short, the hours were the cost and the English was the return."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Here is a question you have not seen before: should a university care what you did outside the classroom at all? Start speaking within two seconds, even before you know your answer. Announce your shape, repair anything that comes out badly, come back if you drift, and land the ending on purpose. Two minutes. Then listen to your own recording and count the silences over three seconds.
