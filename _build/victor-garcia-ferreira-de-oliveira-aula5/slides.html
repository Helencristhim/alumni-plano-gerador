Lesson 5 · Staying in the Room

What to Say While You Are Still Thinking

Everybody needs a second to think. The difference is whether the room hears silence or hears you working.

Warm-Up

Sixty Seconds on Your Application Year

What will you do this year, and what would you do if the money were not part of it? One minute, both halves, no notes.

I am counting two things: how long you last, and how many times you stop. I will tell you both numbers before we go on.

Today’s Goal

Nobody Thinks Instantly

1. The Words

to go blank, to buy yourself time, composure, concise, to elaborate...

2. One Structure

the sentences that hold your place while your head catches up

3. Two Minutes

the same answer as this morning, with the silences filled

In a two-minute answer, three long silences cost roughly a quarter of what an examiner or an interviewer thinks of you. The fix is not thinking faster. The fix is a set of sentences that keep the room with you while you think at normal speed.

Start answering within two seconds, even if you have no idea yet. Whatever comes out of your mouth first is the thing we are working on today.

Chapter 3: The Code

Sentences That Say Nothing, and Do Everything

Four jobs, four openings

Grammar Discovery

What Are These Four Doing?

"That is a good question, and the honest answer is that it depends."

"There are two things here. First of all, the hours."

"What I mean is that the season taught me something a classroom could not."

"Anyway, coming back to your question, I would still do it again."

Delete every red part and each sentence still means exactly the same thing. And yet nobody fluent speaks without them. Why?

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Other Side of the Table

Dialogue

Twenty Minutes With an Interviewer

C
Let me start with something open. Should a university care what you did outside the classroom at all?
V
That is a good question, and the honest answer is that it depends on what you learn there. There are two things here. First of all, the hours it costs.
C
And the second thing?
V
The second thing is what you cannot get anywhere else. What I mean is that nobody on my team waited for me to translate, and a classroom always waits.
C
Could you elaborate on that? Plenty of people would say a classroom is exactly where you should be waited for.
V
Let me think about that for a second, because I want to be fair to your point. You are right for the first month. After that, being waited for is what stops you.
C
That is the clearest version of that argument I have heard today, and I have heard it eleven times.
V
Anyway, coming back to your question, so, in short: yes, but only if the applicant can tell you what changed. And you, what do you hear when somebody goes quiet in here?
Comprehension

About Claire

1. How does Claire open the interview, and what does she call her own question?
She opens with something she calls open: whether a university should care what you did outside the classroom at all.
2. What objection does Claire raise, and whose side does she put it on?
She says plenty of people would argue that a classroom is exactly where you should be waited for. She puts the objection in other people’s mouths rather than her own.
3. What does Claire say at the end about the argument she has just heard?
That it is the clearest version of that argument she has heard today, and that she has heard it eleven times.
Listening 1 · The Coach

What the Room Does While You Are Quiet

A communication coach on the mechanics of a pause. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say a listener starts doing after about two seconds of silence?
They stop listening to the topic and start guessing why you stopped, and the guess is almost never the true reason.
2. What is her argument about speaking faster, and what does she recommend instead?
She says speaking faster does not help, because the problem is not speed, it is the gap. She recommends filling the gap out loud with a sentence that says what you are doing.
3. What does she say about people who apologise for pausing?
That the apology costs more than the pause did. She says nobody had noticed until they said sorry, and after the apology everybody is watching for the next one.
Listening 2 · The One Who Froze

Four Seconds That Felt Like a Week

A candidate on going blank, and what he does now. Sound first — no text.

1. What question made him go blank, and why was that surprising?
A very easy one, about why he had chosen that course. It was surprising because he had rehearsed the hard questions and never practised the obvious one.
2. What does he say he does now, in the first two seconds of every answer?
He says one sentence out loud before he has decided anything, usually that it is a good question or that there are two things there, and he says the rest arrives while he is talking.
3. What does he say about rehearsing, and what does he say he no longer prepares?
He rehearses the first thirty seconds of an answer and nothing after that. He no longer prepares whole answers, because he says a memorised answer collapses the moment the follow-up question arrives.

1. Two lines on this form describe the same forty seconds in opposite ways. Which two, and why is that not a contradiction?
Silences over three seconds, marked in red, and Recovery, marked as good. It is not a contradiction because the form counts the pause and the interviewer judges what happened next.
2. The form says the longest turn was ended by the candidate. Why would an interviewer bother writing that down?
Because it shows the candidate finished on purpose instead of running out of things to say. An answer that fades and an answer that lands sound completely different from the other side of the table.
3. According to the comment, what does the interviewer actually remember on Friday?
The candidates who come back into a sentence without apologising first. She says almost nobody does that, which is why she remembers the ones who do.
