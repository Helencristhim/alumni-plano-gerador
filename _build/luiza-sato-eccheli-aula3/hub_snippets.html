Say It Once, Clearly -- Reporting What Somebody Said

A classmate missed the seminar. Summarize what the professor said, in three blocks, without stumbling. Key words: To sum up, A key point, To point out, To argue, To claim, To acknowledge, A counterargument, To back up, Evidence, To get your point across, To stumble over, To rephrase, To trail off. Structure: reported speech with backshift and reporting verbs.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To sum up -- to give the most important ideas of something in a few words
"To sum up, the professor thinks precedents should guide judges, not control them."
A key point -- one of the most important ideas in an argument or a talk
"Her key point was that courts need to be predictable."
To point out -- to tell somebody a fact they may not have noticed
"He pointed out that the treaty had never been ratified."
To argue -- to give reasons to show that an idea is true or right
"The author argues that sovereignty is changing."
To claim -- to say that something is true, often without giving proof
"The article claims that most treaties are ignored."
To acknowledge -- to accept or admit that something is true or exists
"She acknowledged that the other side had a fair point."
A counterargument -- a reason that is given against another argument
"The strongest counterargument came from a student in the back row."
To back up -- to support an idea with facts, numbers or examples
"You need a real case to back up that claim."
Evidence -- facts or information that show whether something is true
"The professor asked for evidence, not opinions."
To get your point across -- to make other people understand your idea clearly
"I knew the answer, but I could not get my point across."
To stumble over -- to make a mistake or stop when you are saying a word or a sentence
"I always stumble over long words when I am nervous."
To rephrase -- to say the same thing again in different words
"Let me rephrase that, because it came out wrong."
To trail off -- to speak more and more quietly until you stop, without finishing
"He started a strong sentence and then trailed off."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The day after a seminar, the most common question in any law school is simple: what did the professor say? Answering it well is harder than it looks, because you are not repeating the words. You are reporting them, and English changes the sentence when it moves from somebody’s mouth into your summary.

The first change is the verb. If the professor said “Precedents guide judges,” your report is “She said that precedents guided judges.” The tense moves one step back: is becomes was, have read becomes had read, and will becomes would. When the idea is still true, or when you report it right away, the change is optional.

The second change is who heard it. Say does not take a person directly, so “she said me” is always wrong. Use told: “She told me that the reading was short.” Questions become statements: “Is it binding?” turns into “He asked whether it was binding.”

Finally, a good summary does not use said for everything. A professor who gives reasons argues; one who shows a fact you missed points out; a classmate who admits the other side is right acknowledges it. The verb tells your listener what kind of statement it was, and a short summary with the right verbs gets your point across faster than a long one that trails off.

2. Why is “she said me that” wrong?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech

GRAMMAR

Choose a verb that shows the kind of statement, and move the tense one step back.

JobFormExample
A statementsaid (that) + clauseShe said that it was short.
Say who heard ittold + person + (that)He told me that he had read it.
Tense moves backis → was · has → had · will → wouldShe said she would come.
A yes/no questionasked whether / if + statement orderHe asked whether it was binding.
A wh- questionasked + wh- word + statement orderShe asked where the room was.
The kind of statementargued / pointed out / claimed / acknowledged thatHe pointed out that the date had changed.

Never: she said me that · he asked if was the treaty binding · she said she will come tomorrow · he argued me that

Stage 2: Put the Summary in Order

Order

A classmate missed the seminar. Put your voice message in a logical order.

?"You missed a good one: Professor Reeves talked about precedent."
?"Her key point was that precedents should guide judges, not control them."
?"She backed it up with two cases where the court changed its mind."
?"Marcus claimed that a court without fixed rules was dangerous."
?"She acknowledged that he had a point, but she did not change her view."
?"She told us the reading for Friday was short, so do not skip it."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Your classmate asks what the professor said. You answer:
A "She said me that precedents guide judges."
B "She told me that precedents guided judges."
C "She told that precedents guide judges to me."
You want to report a question a classmate asked. You say:
A "He asked whether the treaty was binding."
B "He asked if was the treaty binding."
C "He asked is the treaty binding."
The professor admitted that the other side was partly right. You say:
A "She claimed that he had a point."
B "She pointed him that he had a point."
C "She acknowledged that he had a point."
You stumble over a long word in the middle of your summary. You say:
A "Sorry, I forgot the word, I cannot continue."
