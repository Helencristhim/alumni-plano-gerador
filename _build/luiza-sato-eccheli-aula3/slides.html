The Target

A Summary in Three Blocks

1. Who said it, and the main point, in one sentence.

2. One piece of evidence they used to back it up.

3. Your reaction: do you agree, and why, in one sentence.

Diagnostic

You Missed the Class?

An exchange student in your Global Law course missed the seminar and texts you: “What did the professor say today? Anything important?”

Answer out loud for forty-five seconds, as if you were sending a voice message. Say what the professor said, not what you think.

Chapter 3: The Code

What She Said, Told Later

Four sentences about one seminar, and the verbs that move back in time

Grammar Discovery

Why Did Is Become Was?

"She said that precedents guided judges."

"He told me that he had read the case."

"She pointed out that we would discuss it on Friday."

"He asked whether the treaty was binding."

The exact words in the seminar were: “Precedents guide judges”, “I have read the case”, “We will discuss it on Friday” and “Is the treaty binding?” Compare them with the red and blue words. What happened to the verbs? And why told me, pointed out and asked whether, instead of only said?

JobFormExample
Report a statementsaid (that) + clauseShe said that precedents guided judges.
Say who heard ittold + person + (that)He told me that he had read the case.
Move the time backpresent → past, past → had + participle, will → would“I will” → she said she would

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Voice Message After the Seminar

One conversation, two recordings, one rubric

Dialogue

Ethan Missed the Class

E
Luiza, hey. I overslept and missed the whole seminar. Please tell me Professor Reeves did not say anything important.
L
Sorry, she did. Her key point was that precedents should guide judges, not control them.
E
Okay, and how did she back that up? She always asks for evidence.
L
She used two old cases. She pointed out that the court had changed its mind twice in ten years, and she argued that nobody had noticed.
E
Did anyone disagree? That sounds like the kind of thing Marcus would fight about.
L
He did. He claimed that a court without fixed rules was dangerous. It was a good counterargument, and she acknowledged that he had a point.
E
And what did you say? You were talking about this all week.
L
I started well, but I stumbled over the word jurisprudence and trailed off. So I stopped, rephrased it, and I think I finally got my point across.
E
Good enough for me. Can you sum up the reading for Friday too, or is that too much to ask?
L
It is too much to ask. But she told us the reading was short, so you have no excuse. Did you at least see the new deadline?

Listening 1 · A Podcast

Thirty Seconds, No Stumbling

A speaking coach on summarizing out loud. Sound first — no text.

1. According to the coach, what is usually the real problem when students stumble?
Not vocabulary: they try to say everything in one long sentence, so they run out of breath and trail off.
2. What are the three blocks of a spoken summary?
Who said it and the main point; one piece of evidence; your reaction.
3. What should you do when a word does not come, and why?
Rephrase and keep going, because the listener cannot see the word you are looking for; they only hear the silence.
Listening 2 · A Lecture

Should the Past Decide?

Part of a law lecture on precedent. Sound first — no text.

1. What simple question does the lecturer say has divided lawyers?
Whether the next court should be forced to decide a case the same way as an earlier court.
2. What does precedent give the system, and what example does he use?
Predictability. A company that signs a contract today wants to know how a judge will read it in five years.
3. What cost of precedent does he mention, and what is the real debate?
Societies change faster than courts, so an old rule can produce an unfair result. The real debate is how much weight the past should carry, and who decides when to let it go.
