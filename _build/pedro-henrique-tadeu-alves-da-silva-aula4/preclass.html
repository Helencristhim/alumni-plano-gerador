Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Inside a Lyric

A song has three minutes and no room to explain anything, so it shows instead. Key words: a verse, a line, an image, a twist, to hint at something, plain, to set a scene, a narrator, bittersweet, to unfold. Structure: the past continuous is the room, and the past simple is what happens in it.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A verse -- one section of a song that carries the story, with new words each time
"The second verse says the same thing as the first, and that is the problem."
A line -- one row of words in a song or a poem
"The best opening line I read last year was six words long."
An image -- a picture the words put in your head, without explaining it
"The car was still running outside. That image does all the work."
A twist -- a moment where the meaning of everything before it suddenly changes
"A surprise takes something away from you. A twist gives you something back."
To hint at something -- to suggest it without ever saying it
"Nobody said afraid, and nobody said leaving. The line only hinted at both."
Plain -- simple and ordinary, with no decoration at all
"She put the keys on the table is a plain line, and that is why it works."
To set a scene -- to give the place, the time and the weather, so the listener can stand there
"Two lines set the scene: the rain was falling and she was standing in the kitchen."
A narrator -- the voice that tells the story, which is not always the writer
"The narrator can know less than you do, or lie. That is a freedom, not a mistake."
Bittersweet -- happy and sad at the same time, and neither one wins
"The ending is bittersweet, which is where most memorable songs live."
To unfold -- to happen gradually, with each step revealing the next
"The twist is the reason the song unfolds instead of simply ending."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A song has about three minutes and no room to explain anything, so the first lines do not report events — they set a scene. The rain was falling. She was standing in the kitchen with her coat still on. Both of those were already running when you arrived, and the form that says so is was or were plus -ing. It opens a window on something in progress, and the listener walks in halfway.

Then the events arrive, and they arrive in the past form, one after another, like beats. She put the keys on the table. He asked where she had been. She did not answer. Each of those is finished, and finished is what moves a story forward. The two forms do different jobs, and a good verse uses both in one breath: while the radio was playing in the other room, he said the one sentence he could not take back. The background keeps running; the event lands on top of it.

The third tool is what the words refuse to do. A plain line such as she put the keys on the table hints at everything and states nothing, and that absence is the craft: the listener supplies the feeling. The best lyrics are full of images and almost empty of explanation. The narrator is not the writer either, and can know less than you do. And near the end something turns: the coat was never taken off, because she was never staying. That is the twist, the reason the song unfolds instead of ending, and it usually leaves the listener somewhere bittersweet. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: the background needs the -ing form. Not the rain fell and she stood in the kitchen as an opening.

1. Why is it "the rain was falling" and not "the rain fell" at the start of a song?
A Because "fall" is an irregular verb and needs the longer form.
B Because it was already running when the listener arrived, and the -ing form is what says so.
C Because the past simple cannot be used for the weather.
2. "While the radio was playing, he said the one sentence he could not take back." What does each half do?
A The first half is the background that keeps running; the second half is the event that lands on top of it.
B Both halves report finished events, one after the other.
C The first half is more polite than the second.
3. Why does the text say a plain line works better than one that names the feeling?
A Because short lines are easier to sing.
B Because the listener supplies the feeling, and a feeling you supplied yourself is stronger.
C Because adjectives are not allowed in songs.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Simple and Past Continuous

GRAMMAR

One decision: is this the room, or is this what happened in it?

JobFormExample
Already running, the backgroundwas / were + verb-ingThe rain was falling. She was standing in the kitchen.
Happened and finished, the eventthe past formShe put the keys down. He asked. She left.
Both in one sentenceWhile + continuous, then the past formWhile the radio was playing, the phone rang.
Two backgrounds at oncecontinuous + and + continuousThe rain was falling and she was standing in the doorway.
Events in a rowpast form, past form, past formShe put the keys down, sat and said nothing.
Negative and questiondid not + base verb · was not + -ingShe did not answer. She was not listening. What was she doing?
The trapthe background needs the continuousNot the rain fell and she stood in the kitchen as an opening.

Never: the rain fell and she stood in the kitchen (as an opening) · she was putting the keys on the table and was leaving · she did not answered him · while the radio played, he was saying it

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You are describing the opening of a song in a seminar. You say:
A "The rain fell and she stood in the kitchen, and after that she put the keys."
B "The rain was falling and she was standing in the kitchen. Then she put the keys on the table."
C "It is raining and she is in the kitchen, and she puts the keys on the table."
Somebody asks what the writer does instead of naming the feeling. You say:
A "The lines are plain: they hint at everything and state nothing, so the listener supplies the feeling."
B "The writer does not have vocabulary for the feelings, so he writes simple."
C "It is more poetic because it is short and the words are beautiful."
You want to explain the last line of the song. You say:
A "In the end there is a surprise and nobody can expect this."
B "There is a twist: she never took the coat off, so every line before it means something else."
C "The end is very sad and I liked it a lot."
Somebody asks who is speaking in the song. You say:
A "It is the writer, obviously, because he wrote the words."
B "The narrator is not the writer, and by the end he knows less than we do."
C "Nobody is speaking, it is only a description of a kitchen."
