Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Executive Email and Written Authority -- Communicating Decisions, Requests and Updates with Precision

How an executive email in English carries authority: the ask and the date first, the context second, the warmth last. Key words: subject line, action item, sign-off, thread, turnaround, bandwidth, loop in, acknowledge, chase, heads-up, boilerplate, keep someone posted. Structure: future perfect and future continuous -- the deadline you close and the arrangement you state.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words your counterparts use in writing every day and never stop to explain.

Subject line -- the one line that decides whether an email is opened today or on Friday
"The subject line has to carry the ask and the date, not the topic."
Action item -- a task with a named owner and a date, taken out of the discussion
"Three action items, three owners, three dates. Nothing else goes in the table."
Sign-off -- the final approval that lets something go out
"I need your sign-off on two clauses by Wednesday evening."
Thread -- the chain of replies that carries one subject from beginning to end
"The thread below has both clauses. Nothing else has changed."
Turnaround -- the time between a request going out and the answer coming back
"Our turnaround on wording questions is the same day."
Bandwidth -- the capacity someone has left to take on more work
"Nobody in Milan has the bandwidth to read three paragraphs of context."
To loop in -- to add someone to a thread so that they can follow it from there on
"I have looped in Mexico, because the same wording will be going to them in April."
To acknowledge -- to confirm you have received something, before you are able to answer it
"I will acknowledge the same hour, even if the answer takes until the evening."
To chase -- to ask again for something that is late, without accusing anyone
"I will chase once and then proceed as drafted."
Heads-up -- an early warning given so that nobody is surprised later
"Milan gets a heads-up now, not an explanation in April."
Boilerplate -- standard wording reused unchanged in every document
"The rest is the boilerplate we have used since March."
To keep someone posted -- to send updates as things change, without being asked for them
"I will keep you posted either way, and you will not hear it from the broker first."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Pay attention to the two futures in bold: one closes a deadline, the other states an arrangement.

Lars found the email at seven in the morning, under about forty others, and he almost did not open it. The subject line said "Colombia, update", and an update asks for nothing. It was a good email underneath: careful, complete and completely unreadable at that hour, because the request sat in the last paragraph, after three lines of courtesy and a page of context. Nobody in Milan has the bandwidth for that.

Denise rewrote it the same morning, and the new version began where the old one had ended. "I need your sign-off on clauses 4 and 11 by Wednesday, 18:00. By Thursday we will have closed the Colombian renewal." Then the evidence: the thread below carried both clauses, and the rest was the boilerplate the team had used since March. Then the copy list, with a reason attached to it: Mexico had been looped in as a heads-up, because the same wording will be going to them in April.

She closed with two things most people leave out. Her own turnaround: she would acknowledge within the hour, even if the full answer took until the evening. And the consequence: if she had not heard from him by Wednesday evening, she would chase once and then proceed as drafted, and she would keep him posted either way. Three action items, three owners, three dates. Eighty words. Lars answered before lunch.

1. "By Thursday we will have closed the Colombian renewal." At what point is the renewal closed?
A Now — the sentence describes something already finished.
B At some point between now and Thursday, so that on Thursday it is already done.
C On Thursday itself, and not a moment before.
2. "The same wording will be going to them in April." What does this form tell Lars?
A That it is an arrangement already in place, stated as a fact rather than promised or requested.
B That she is asking his permission to send it in April.
C That the wording is being written at this moment.
3. Why did the first version fail, even though nothing in it was wrong?
A Because the English was too formal for a message to a colleague.
B Because the request arrived last, after the courtesy and the context, and the subject line named a topic instead of an ask and a date.
C Because it was sent at seven in the morning, when nobody reads carefully.
4. Why does she state what she will do if Wednesday passes in silence?
A Because it gives him the choice while there is still time to make one, and it names her own next step rather than his failure.
B Because a deadline only works if there is a penalty attached to it.
C Because she wants a written record she can show the committee later.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Future Perfect and Future Continuous

GRAMMAR

Two futures, two effects. One closes a commitment against a date. The other states an arrangement and asks without ordering.

FormWhat it does in an emailExample
will have + past participle (future perfect)stands at a future moment and looks back: at that point, this is doneBy Thursday we will have closed the Colombian renewal.
will have been + past participle (passive)same deadline, but the doer does not matter — the register of governanceBy the time you land, the wording will have been signed off.
will be + -ing (future continuous)states an arrangement already in place. No promise extracted, no favor askedNext week I will be presenting the figures in Milan.
Will you be + -ing?asks without giving an order. "Send me the numbers" commands; this one enquires about a planWill you be sending the Mexican numbers before Friday?
The deadline anchorthe future perfect almost always travels with one of theseby Wednesday evening · by the end of the quarter · by the time you land
Trap 1 — by, never untilby Thursday = at any point up to Thursday, and then it is done. until Thursday = the whole time continuing up to Thursday and stopping there. A deadline is always by.
Trap 2 — no will in the time clause"By the time you land..." / "When the broker replies..." — after when, by the time and as soon as, the verb stays in the present and the will lives in the other half.
Why it matters here"We will close it on Thursday" is a plan. "By Thursday we will have closed it" is a commitment with a date attached, and it is the difference between a note and an instruction the reader can schedule around.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

You need the Group COO to approve two clauses before Thursday. The strongest subject line is:
A "Colombia renewal — sign-off needed by Wed 18:00"
B "Colombia — update"
C "Quick question when you have a moment"
You want to commit to a deadline in writing. You say:
A "Until Thursday we will have closed the renewal."
B "By Thursday we will have closed the renewal."
C "We are closing the renewal until Thursday."
You need the Mexican numbers before Friday, and you do not want to sound as if you are giving an order. You write:
A "Send me the Mexican numbers before Friday."
B "Will you be sending the Mexican numbers before Friday?"
C "Sorry to bother you, but if it is not too much trouble, could you possibly send the Mexican numbers?"
A long email arrives at six in the evening and you cannot answer it properly until tomorrow. The best thing to send now is:
A One line to acknowledge it, saying when the full answer will arrive.
B Nothing — a partial answer is worse than silence.
C A long apology for the delay that has not happened yet.
You are copying the Mexican team on a decision that does not require anything from them yet. In English that is:
A An action item — a task with an owner and a date.
B A heads-up — an early warning so that nobody is surprised in April.
C A sign-off — the approval that lets it go out.
Wednesday evening arrives and there is still no answer from Milan. The most professional next step is:
A Wait, because chasing a Group officer looks impatient.
B Escalate to the head of the region the following morning.
C Chase once, exactly as the original email said you would, and then proceed as drafted.
