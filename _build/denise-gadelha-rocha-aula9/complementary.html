Lesson 9 -- Executive Email and Written Authority

Series
The Crown -- Season 1, the red boxes

An institution that governs almost entirely in writing. Watch what arrives in the red dispatch boxes and how the letters, telegrams and private notes are worded: the decision is at the top, the reasoning underneath, and the courtesy is a single line that never softens the instruction. It is the oldest version of what you practised today, and it is why the writing survives the people who wrote it.

Tip: find one written message in the season and note where the request sits — first line or last. Then say what it would have cost the sender to put it in the other place.

Podcast
Think Fast, Talk Smart -- episodes on writing, concision and getting to the point

Matt Abrahams keeps returning to the question behind this lesson: how much can you take out before the message stops working, and what does a busy reader do with what is left. The episodes on written communication are the ones to choose. Listen for how consistently the guests treat structure, and not vocabulary, as the thing that decides whether a message is acted on.

Tip: for each episode, write the one sentence that would have been its subject line. If you cannot find it in under a minute, that is exactly what happens to your reader.

YouTube
Celeste Headlee -- 10 Ways to Have a Better Conversation (TED)

You watched this in an earlier lesson for listening. Watch it again for the last rule, which is the shortest and the one everybody ignores: be brief. Her argument against padding, repeating yourself and explaining what the other person already knows is the spoken version of the email you rewrote today. Nothing she cuts is information; everything she cuts is reassurance for the speaker.

Tip: take the longest email you sent last week and cut it in half without losing a single fact. Whatever disappears was written for you, not for the reader.
