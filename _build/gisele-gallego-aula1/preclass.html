Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Who I Am and Why I Am Here -- The Conversation on the Sidewalk

A neighbor stops you with the dog and asks how long you have been here. Key words: to settle in, a newcomer, to be transferred, a relocation, to keep to yourself, next door, the block, to run into someone, small talk, to catch up, to get your bearings, to be swamped. Structure: the present perfect with for and since.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To settle in -- to get used to a new home or a new place, until it starts to feel normal
"It has taken us two months to settle in, and we are not done."
A newcomer -- a person who has just arrived in a place and does not know it yet
"Everybody on this street knows I am the newcomer."
To be transferred -- to be moved by your company to a job in another city or another country
"My husband was transferred, and the four of us came with him."
A relocation -- the whole move of a family or an employee to another city or country
"The relocation happened fast. We had six weeks."
To keep to yourself -- to stay quiet and private, and not talk much to the people around you
"I have kept to myself since we arrived, and I want to stop."
Next door -- in the house or the apartment right beside yours
"The family next door is from Singapore."
The block -- the street you live on and the houses around it, as one small area
"There are four languages on my block."
To run into someone -- to meet somebody by accident, without planning it
"I ran into my neighbor at the store this morning."
Small talk -- short, easy conversation about nothing important, just to be friendly
"Small talk is the part I find hardest, and it is the part that opens the door."
To catch up -- to talk with somebody and tell each other the news since you last met
"We should get a coffee and catch up."
To get your bearings -- to work out where you are and how things work in a new place
"Give yourself three months to get your bearings."
To be swamped -- to have far too much to do, with no free time at all
"Sorry, I have been swamped all week."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The Gallego family has been in Washington for about two months. Gisele has lived on this block since February, and she is still the newcomer: everybody else knows which day the trash goes out. Her husband was transferred by his company, and the whole relocation happened in six weeks.

She has not met the family next door yet. She has kept to herself since the day the boxes arrived, partly because she has been swamped and partly because small talk is the hardest part of a new language. Greg, the man across the street, has lived here since 1998, and this morning he stopped her on the sidewalk.

He asked how long they had been here and how she was settling in. She told him the truth: slowly, and she is still getting her bearings. He told her to stop and talk any time she runs into him, because nobody on that street should keep to themselves. It was two minutes of conversation, and it was the first one she has had on this block.

1. "She has lived on this block since February." Why since and not for?
A Because February is in the past, and for is only used for the future.
B Because February is the point in time when it started, not a length of time.
C Because since is the polite form and for is informal.
2. "The family has been in Washington for about two months." Are they still there?
A Yes. It started two months ago and it is still true today.
B No. They were there for two months and then they left.
C The sentence does not say anything about now.
3. "She has not met the family next door yet." What does yet add here?
A That she does not want to meet them.
B That she met them a long time ago.
C That it has not happened up to now, and it probably still will.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect With For and Since

GRAMMAR

One structure for everything that started before and is still true.

UseFormExample
Affirmativehave / has + past participleWe have been here two months.
for+ a length of timefor two months, for a week, for twenty years
since+ a point in timesince February, since 1998, since we arrived
QuestionHow long + have / has + subject + participleHow long have you lived here?
Negativehave / has not + participle (+ yet)I have not met them yet.
Short answerYes, I have. / No, I have not.Have you been to the new store? Not yet.

Never: I am here since February · We are here two months · I have lived here since two months · How long are you living here? · I have knew Greg for a week.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

A neighbor asks how long you have been in Washington. You answer:
A "I am here since two months."
B "We have been here for about two months."
C "We are here two months ago."
You want to know how long the other person has been on the street. You ask:
A "How long time you live here?"
B "How long are you living here?"
C "How long have you lived here?"
Somebody invites you for coffee and this week is impossible. You say:
A "I would love to, but I have been swamped this week. Can we catch up next week?"
B "No, I am very busy."
C "I am swamped since this week, sorry."
You still have not spoken to the people in the house beside yours. You say:
A "I did not meet the family next door yet."
B "I have not met the family next door yet."
C "I am not meeting the family next door since two months."
