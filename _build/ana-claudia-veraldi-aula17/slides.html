Chapter 3: The Code

A Rule, a Choice or a Good Idea?

have to · don’t have to · mustn’t · should · had better

Grammar Discovery

Necessary, Not Necessary, Forbidden, Wise

"In Norway, you have to take your shoes off at the door."

"It is a small dinner. You don't have to bring a gift."

"You mustn't smoke inside the house."

"It is minus ten tonight. You'd better bring warm socks."

Which sentence is a rule? Which one is a choice? Which one is forbidden? Which one is a warning? And look at don’t have to and mustn’t: do they mean the same thing?

FormWhat it doesExample
have to / must + verbNecessary. Have to = a rule from outside. Must = the speaker feels it.You have to take your shoes off.
don’t have toNOT necessary. You can choose.You don’t have to bring a gift.
mustn’tForbidden. Do not do it.You mustn’t smoke inside.
should / ought toAdvice: it is a good idea.You should try the brown cheese.
had better (’d better)Strong advice with a warning: or something bad happens.You’d better bring warm socks.
past of must and have toMust has no past. Use had to.Last winter I had to buy new boots.

In one line: have to = necessary, don’t have to = you choose, mustn’t = forbidden, should = a good idea, had better = or else.

Chapter 4: Many Englishes

A Norwegian and an Indian Give Advice

Norwegian and Indian English — and why the same words can be a rule or a hug

Dialogue

Packing for Oslo

L
So, Ana, you are really coming to Oslo in January? You are a brave woman.
A
I am! Tell me the truth. What should I pack?
L
Well, you might want to bring wool socks. Actually, you'd better bring two pairs.
A
Two pairs? Is that a Norwegian rule of thumb?
L
Sort of. And at my parents' house you have to take your shoes off at the door. It is not optional.
A
Do I have to bring a gift? In Brazil, you never arrive with empty hands.
L
You don't have to bring anything big. Flowers or chocolate is perfect. But you mustn't be late. Dinner at six means six.
A
Dinner at six? In Brazil we eat at nine! But okay, when in Rome. I will be there at five to six.
Comprehension

About Lars

1. What does Lars tell Ana to bring for the cold?
Wool socks — actually two pairs.
2. What is not optional at Lars’s parents’ house?
Taking your shoes off at the door.
3. What does Lars say about the gift and about the time?
She doesn’t have to bring anything big — flowers or chocolate is perfect. But she mustn’t be late: dinner at six means six.

The Shoes at the Door

A Norwegian woman on the unwritten rules of a home in Bergen. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What do you have to do at the door of a Norwegian home?
Take your shoes off. Nobody will tell you, but everybody will look at your feet.
2. Why don’t you have to bring a big present?
A small thing is perfect, and something too expensive can make people uncomfortable.
3. What happened to Ingrid’s friend Camila, and why?
She was cold, because Ingrid’s advice about a warmer coat sounded soft, like a small suggestion. It was minus twelve.
Listening 2 · India

You Must Eat More

An Indian man on advice that sounds like an order — and an order that sounds like advice. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. How does Arjun’s aunt give advice, and what does it mean in his family?
Very directly: you must eat more, you should call your mother. In his family, it means love.
2. What did Arjun’s landlady in Manchester say in his first week?
You might want to put the bins out on Thursday night.
3. What did Arjun learn about soft advice in England?
That it can be a rule. Now he always follows it.
