Chapter 4: The Code

The Joint That Holds a Long Sentence

Four words, and a comma that changes the meaning

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"We work with manufacturers that run their operations on SAP."

"Harbrook, which was founded in 2009, is based in Utrecht."

"The person who signs the contract is rarely the person who uses the product."

"Their biggest market is Germany, where they opened a second office last year."

Cover the blue part with your finger. Two sentences survive and still mean something. Two collapse. The commas tell you which is which.

After a commaNEVER thatNot Harbrook, that was founded...
You can drop the wordonly with no commas, and only when it is the objectthe vendor (that) we chose

In one line: no commas, the words say WHICH one. Two commas, the words are extra — and never use that.

PROFESSIONAL PROFILE

NameMarta Oliveira Lund
RolePartner Manager, Harbrook Systems
Based inUtrecht, Netherlands
In the role since2021
Before thatEight years in ERP implementation
LanguagesDutch, English, Portuguese
Marta runs the partner programme at Harbrook, which means she decides who the company connects to and who it does not. She spent eight years implementing ERP systems before she moved to the commercial side, so she is the rare partner manager who has actually installed the thing. She grew up in Lisbon, where her family still lives, and she reads Portuguese well enough to be sent Brazilian documents by mistake.
1. The paragraph says she is "the rare partner manager who has actually installed the thing". What does that tell you about how to talk to her?
That she will understand the technical side, so a vague answer about the platform will not survive. She can ask about the integration itself.
2. Which of the relative clauses in the paragraph could be removed without changing which person we are talking about?
"where her family still lives" is extra information about Lisbon and can be removed. "who has actually installed the thing" says which kind of partner manager she is, so removing it changes the point.
3. She reads Portuguese. Is that an advantage or a risk for you on the call?
Both. She may accept a document in Portuguese, but the call and the contract will still be in English, and speaking Portuguese with her would hand the relationship back to somebody else.
Chapter 6: Your Turn

Two Recordings, Then You

Listening 1 · Ten Minutes of Homework

What She Reads Before She Dials

A salesperson on the ten minutes before a first call. Sound first — no text.

1. What are the three things she says she looks for, and in what order?
Who they sell to, what changed in the last year, and who the person on the call is. In that order.
2. What does she say she stopped reading, and why?
The product pages. She says every product page in the industry says the same thing, so they tell her nothing about that particular company.
3. What does she say she never does with what she found?
She never recites it. She turns one fact into a question instead, so it does not sound like she is reading a file back to them.
Listening 2 · The Page and the Person

What the Page Says, and What He Says

An executive on the distance between his own About page and his own mouth. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say about the sentence at the top of his own company page?
That nobody has ever said it out loud, including him, and that a lawyer and a marketing agency wrote it together.
2. How does he describe the same company when he is speaking?
He names the customer and the problem: factories that cannot tell on Friday what they produced on Monday.
3. What advice does he give about reading a company page before a call?
Read it for the facts, never for the words. Take the numbers and the names, and throw the adjectives away.
