Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Reading the Other Side -- The Partner’s Company Page

Ten minutes before the call you open their company page. Everything you need is on it, in long sentences held together by four small words. Key words: a vendor, headquarters, to specialize in, a subsidiary, revenue, an industry, to serve, to launch, a footprint, an account. Structure: defining and non-defining relative clauses.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A vendor -- a company that sells a product or a service to another company
"They work with four vendors, and they are replacing two of them."
Headquarters -- the main office of a company, where the leadership works
"Their headquarters is in Utrecht, but the engineers are in Porto."
To specialize in -- to work in one area only, and to do it better than anything else
"They specialize in ERP for food manufacturers. Nothing else."
A subsidiary -- a company that a bigger company owns
"The Brazilian office is a subsidiary, so the budget is decided abroad."
Revenue -- the money a company receives from what it sells, before costs
"Revenue grew, but the margin did not."
An industry -- all the companies that make or sell the same kind of thing
"Food and drink is the industry where they win most of their deals."
To serve -- to sell to a particular group of customers and look after them
"We serve manufacturers. We do not serve retail."
To launch -- to make a product available for the first time
"They launched the platform in 2019 and rebuilt it twice since then."
A footprint -- all the places where a company operates, seen together
"Their European footprint is three offices and one partner in Brazil."
An account -- a customer company that one salesperson is responsible for
"That account is mine. I have had it since the first meeting."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ten minutes before a first call, the only page worth opening is the one the company wrote about itself. It is written in long sentences on purpose, and almost every one of them is held together by a small word: that, which, who or where.

Harbrook Systems is a vendor that sells software to manufacturers. It specializes in one industry and refuses everything else. Its headquarters is in Utrecht, where ninety of its people work, and its second office is in Munich. Harbrook, which was founded in 2009, launched its current platform in 2019 and rebuilt it twice after that. Germany is the market where it earns most of its revenue, and its Brazilian operation is not an office at all: it is a subsidiary of a local partner.

Notice the difference the commas make. The clause in "a vendor that sells software to manufacturers" has no commas, and you cannot remove it: without it, Harbrook is just a vendor, and the sentence stops saying which kind. The clause in "Harbrook, which was founded in 2009," sits between two commas, and you can remove it: there is only one Harbrook, so the year is extra information.

The last thing to read is not the company at all. It is the person who will be on the call. If she is the partner manager, she decides which vendors the company connects to, and the account is hers. If she is not, you are being screened, and the person who decides has not joined yet.

1. "A vendor that sells software to manufacturers." Why is there no comma before that?
A Because the sentence is short.
B Because the clause says which kind of vendor. Remove it and the sentence stops identifying the company.
C Because that never takes a comma in any sentence.
2. "Harbrook, which was founded in 2009, launched its current platform in 2019." Why which and not that?
A Because the clause sits between commas and adds extra information, and after a comma English never uses that.
B Because Harbrook is a company and companies always take which.
C Because the sentence already has that later on.
3. "Germany is the market where it earns most of its revenue." Why where?
A Because Germany is a country, and countries always take where.
B Because the writer wanted to avoid using that twice in one paragraph.
C Because the clause says in which place the revenue is earned, and where replaces in which.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

One decision, made by the commas: does the clause say WHICH one, or is it extra?

JobFormExample
Says WHICH one (cannot be removed)no commas — that, which, who, wheremanufacturers that run on SAP
Adds extra information (can be removed)two commas — which, who, whereHarbrook, which was founded in 2009, is in Utrecht
Peoplewhothe person who signs the contract
Things and companieswhich, or that when there is no commathe platform that they launched in 2019
Placeswhere (= in which)Germany, where they opened a second office
After a commanever thatNot Harbrook, that was founded...
Dropping the wordonly with no commas, and only when it is the objectthe vendor (that) we chose
Never repeat the objectthe clause already contains itNot the city where they work there

Never: Harbrook, that was founded in 2009 · manufacturers, that run on SAP · the person which signs the contract · the city that they work there · the company who sells software

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to open the call by showing that you read their page. You say:
A "I read your website. You have ninety employees and your headquarters is in Utrecht and you launched in 2019."
B "I saw that you specialize in manufacturers that run on SAP. Is that still where most of the work comes from?"
C "I did not have time to look at your website, sorry."
You are describing a company and the information is extra, not identifying. You write:
A "Harbrook that was founded in 2009 is based in Utrecht."
B "Harbrook, which was founded in 2009, is based in Utrecht."
C "Harbrook, that was founded in 2009, is based in Utrecht."
You want to say that you only sell to one kind of company. You say:
A "We serve manufacturers that handle thousands of invoices a month."
B "We serve manufacturers, that handle thousands of invoices a month."
C "We serve manufacturers who handling thousands of invoices a month."
You want to ask who really makes the decision. You say:
A "Who is the person which decides about the vendors?"
B "Who decides? Tell me now, please."
C "Who is the person who decides which vendors you work with?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Your CEO asks whether a company you have just read about is worth a call. In one minute, say what they do, who they serve, where they operate, and one thing that makes them a good or a bad fit. Use that, which, who or where in at least three of your sentences, and make every claim something you actually read.
