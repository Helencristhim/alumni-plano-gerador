Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

What If You Could Live Anywhere? -- Plans, Not Daydreams

The conversation about leaving, and the sentence shape it needs. Key words: an opportunity, an application, a neighborhood, a commute, to afford, to settle down, a lifestyle, to look into, to make a living, to fit in. Structure: the first conditional — if plus the plain present, then will plus the bare verb, for the plans you could really start this year.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An opportunity -- a chance to do something that will not come back on its own
"The opportunity came from somebody I met at a party, not from a website."
An application -- the form and the documents you send when you want a job, a visa or a school place
"My application took three weeks to write and four months to be answered."
A neighborhood -- the small part of a city you actually live in: your street, your market, your walk
"I want a neighborhood where I can walk to everything."
A commute -- the trip between your home and your work, every day, in both directions
"My commute is forty minutes each way, and I have stopped noticing it."
To afford -- to have enough money for something without hurting the rest of your life
"I could afford the rent there, but not the rent and a life."
To settle down -- to stop moving around and build a steady life in one place
"We are not ready to settle down anywhere yet."
A lifestyle -- the way you spend your days: your hours, your money, your weekends
"I do not want a bigger salary. I want a different lifestyle."
To look into -- to start finding out about something seriously, before you decide
"I have been looking into three cities and one of them surprised me."
To make a living -- to earn enough from your work to pay for your life
"You can make a living there, but you will work for it."
To fit in -- to feel that you belong in a place, and to be treated that way
"It took me a year to fit in, and then a week to feel at home."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Here is a sentence you will say out loud within a year: "If I get the opportunity, I will take it." Two halves, one comma, and a rule so small that almost nobody notices it until they get it wrong in front of somebody.

The half that starts with if talks about the future, but it does not wear a future verb. "If I get the job" — not if I will get. The other half is where will lives: "I will move." So the shape is: if + the plain present, then will + the bare verb. "If the rent is too high, we will look into another neighborhood."

You can turn the sentence around and nothing changes except the comma. "We will look into another neighborhood if the rent is too high." Same two halves, same rule, and no comma when the if comes second. Americans do this constantly in speech, and they swallow the whole first half into one sound: if-I-get-a-job-there.

And this one is for real plans, not daydreams. "If I move to Austin, I will find a job" is something you believe can happen this year. The daydream version — "if I lived in New York, I would walk everywhere" — is a different shape, and it is next week. Today, keep it real: if it is a plan you could actually start on Monday, it takes if + present, then will.

1. Why is it "If I get the job" and not "If I will get the job"?
A Because the half that starts with if uses the plain present, even when it is about the future.
B Because "get" is an irregular verb.
C Because "will" can only be used in questions.
2. What happens to the comma when the sentence starts with the other half?
A It moves to the end of the sentence.
B It disappears — there is no comma when the if comes second.
C Nothing changes; the comma stays in the middle.
3. Which of these is the shape for a plan you could really start this year?
A If I lived in New York, I would walk everywhere.
B If I will move to Austin, I find a job.
C If I move to Austin, I will find a job.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- If + Present, Then Will

GRAMMAR

Two halves and one comma. The if half never wears will. Turn the sentence around and the comma disappears.

JobFormExample
A real plan, in two halvesif + present, ... will + bare verbIf I get the offer, I will take it.
The same sentence, turned around... will + bare verb + if + present (no comma)I will take it if I get the offer.
Negative in the if halfif + do not / does not + bare verbIf we do not find a neighborhood we like, we will wait.
Negative in the will halfwill not + bare verbIf the rent is that high, we will not afford it.
QuestionWhat will + subject + bare verb + if + present?What will you do if the visa takes two years?
Softer than willmight / may + bare verbIf I go alone first, I might stay three months.
The trapnever will inside the if halfNot if I will move. Say if I move.
The other shape (next lesson)a daydream, not a planIf I lived in New York, I would walk everywhere.

Never: If I will move, I take the job · If I will get the visa, I will go · If the rent will be high, we will look into another neighborhood · If I move to Austin I would find a job

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

You are calling the one person you know who already moved abroad. Put your side of the call in a logical order.

?"Hi -- do you have ten minutes? I am looking into moving, and you are the only person I know who did it."
?"First question: how long did the application take, from the first form to the answer?"
?"And the neighborhood -- how did you choose, when you had never been there?"
?"Could you afford the rent on the first salary, or did that take a while?"
?"What is the one thing you would do differently if you started again tomorrow?"
?"Last one. Do you feel like you fit in now, or is that still coming?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A friend asks what you will do if the company says yes. You answer:
A "If they will say yes, I go."
B "If they say yes, I will go."
C "If they said yes, I would go."
You are describing a real plan for this year, not a daydream. You say:
A "If I move to Austin, I will find a job in operations."
B "If I moved to Austin, I will find a job in operations."
C "If I will move to Austin, I find a job in operations."
You want to put the if half at the end of the sentence. You say:
A "I will take it, if I will get the offer."
B "I will take it if I get the offer."
C "I will take it, if I got the offer."
You are not sure, so you want something softer than "will". You say:
A "If I go alone first, I might stay three months."
B "If I will go alone first, I stay three months."
C "If I go alone first, I will maybe might stay three months."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds. Somebody asks you the question straight: what would have to happen this year for you to actually go? Answer in real plans, not daydreams — the money, the dogs, the house in Barueri, the job. Use if and will at least four times.
