Warm-Up

The Outlier Now Has to Explain Himself

Last time the class profile made twenty-two years in one firm look unusual. That is exactly what the next question in the interview is about.

In two sentences: name the two moments in those years that changed what your job actually was.

Today’s Goal

Twenty Years, Three Moves

1. The Words

a stint, a predecessor, a misstep, a milestone...

2. One Structure

what had already happened before each decision

3. Your Answer

three moves, in order, with the reason inside each

The question is not asking for a list of job titles. It is asking for a sequence of decisions, and a decision only makes sense once the listener knows what had already happened when you took it.

One Question

What Had Already Happened by Then?

Take the year you took over the direction of the company. Say it out loud in one sentence.

Now the harder one: what had you already done, and what had already changed in the company, before that year arrived?

Chapter 3: The Code

The Past Before the Past

One tense you have read a thousand times and probably never said out loud

Grammar Discovery

Which of These Happened First?

"When my father died, I had already taken on the commercial side."

"I took over the direction in 2011."

"By then the company had completed thirty buildings."

"We diversified in 2016, and it carried us through the next year."

Two of the verbs are plain past. Two of them carry had in front. Put the four events on a line and you will see what the had is doing: it is not making the event older, it is telling you the event was already finished when the other one happened.

JobFormExample
The moment in the storypast simpleI took over the direction in 2011.
Already finished by thenhad + past participleI had already taken on the commercial side.
A count up to that pointby then / by 2011 + had + participleBy then we had completed thirty buildings.
Two steps, same orderpast simple + past simpleWe diversified, and it carried us through.
Negativehad not + participle (+ yet)Nobody had written a succession plan yet.
In speechhad contracts to 'dBy then I'd already taken it on.
The trapword order does not carry the sequenceNot when he died, I already took it on.

In one line: the past simple is the camera; had plus the participle is everything that was already true when the camera arrived.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Career, at Full Speed

One interview, three recordings, one timeline

Dialogue

The Second Interview, Ten Minutes In

A
So, walk me through it. Not the titles — the moments where the job changed underneath you.
F
There are three. The first was a four-year stint on site, straight out of university, which nobody would call a career move and which taught me how the company actually works.
A
And the second?
F
2011. My father got ill and I was thrown in at the deep end. But by then I had already taken on the commercial side, so it was less of a jump than it looks on paper.
A
What had changed in the company by then?
F
A great deal. We had completed around thirty buildings, we had lost two of our oldest clients, and nobody had ever written a succession plan. I inherited all three of those on the same afternoon.
A
And the third moment? Take your time.
F
2016. We consolidated instead of growing, which felt like a misstep at the time. In hindsight it was the two years that laid the groundwork for everything since. What made you ask about the moments rather than the roles?
Comprehension

About Alison

1. What does Alison ask for, and what does she say she does not want?
She asks him to walk her through it, and says she does not want the titles: she wants the moments where the job changed underneath him.
2. How does Alison keep the story moving, and what does she offer him before the last answer?
With very short questions: "And the second?" and "And the third moment?". Before the last one she tells him to take his time.
3. One of Alison's questions forces a particular verb tense. Which question, and which tense?
"What had changed in the company by then?" It forces the past perfect, because it asks about what was already true at that moment.
Listening 1 · A Director

The Year the Job Changed Underneath Me

A director telling the turn in his own career, at speaking speed. Sound first — no text.

1. What happened in the year he describes, and what had already happened before it?
The two founders left within four months of each other. Before that he had already been running the operation for two years without the title.
2. What does he say he inherited, and what had nobody done?
A supplier contract nobody had read in six years, and a team that had lost two managers. Nobody had written anything down.
3. What does he say about that period in hindsight?
That it looked like a promotion and was really a rescue, and that the two quiet years before it were the ones that laid the groundwork.
Listening 2 · A Career Coach

Why Most People Tell It Badly

A coach on the shape of a career answer that works. Sound first — no text.

1. How many moves does she say an answer should have, and what do most people do instead?
Three. Most people give thirteen, one for every job, and the listener stops keeping track after the fourth.
2. What does she say has to be inside each move?
The reason. Not what the job was, but what had happened that made the move necessary or possible.
3. What does she say about admitting a misstep?
That every strong answer has one, said without drama, and that candidates who present a career with no wrong turns are the ones nobody believes.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Story We Tell About Ourselves

A radio report on what people leave out when they narrate a career. Sound first — no text.

1. What did the researchers ask people to do, and what did they find?
To tell their career story twice, a year apart. The second version had fewer people in it and more decisions, and the luck had mostly disappeared.
2. What does the report say about the parts people leave out?
That the missing parts are almost always the ones somebody else set up: a predecessor's groundwork, a market that turned, a person who handed them something.
3. What does the interviewer at the end say she listens for?
Whether the candidate can name one thing that was not their doing, because a career with no external force in it has been edited.

A timeline shows what happened and in which order. It cannot show what had already happened when each decision was taken, and that is the only column the interview can fill in. Candidates who read their own timeline aloud in the interview are answering a question nobody asked.
1. Look at 2009 and 2011. Say in one sentence what had already happened when the second date arrived.
By 2011 he had already taken on the commercial side, which he had been given two years earlier.
2. What is the one thing the timeline cannot show, according to the note?
What had already happened when each decision was taken. It shows order, not cause.
3. What mistake does the note say candidates make with their own timeline?
Reading it aloud in the interview, which answers a question nobody asked.
