Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

Walk Me Through Your Career -- The Story in Two Past Tenses

Twenty years, ten minutes, and one tense you have probably never used out loud. Key words: a stint, a lateral move, to inherit, a predecessor, to be thrown in at the deep end, a misstep, to consolidate, to be handed something, to lay the groundwork, a milestone, to phase something out, in hindsight. Structure: past simple and past perfect in narrative.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A stint -- a period of time spent doing one particular job, seen as a block
"After a four-year stint on site, I moved into the office."
A lateral move -- a change of job at the same level, taken for what it teaches rather than for the title
"It was a lateral move on paper, and it taught me more than any promotion."
To inherit -- to receive something from the person who had it before, whether you asked for it or not
"I inherited a supplier contract that nobody had read in six years."
A predecessor -- the person who had the job before you did
"My predecessor was my father, which makes the comparison a little harder."
To be thrown in at the deep end -- to be given a difficult job with no preparation and no warning
"I was thrown in at the deep end. There was nobody left to hand it to."
A misstep -- a wrong move that cost something, described with less drama than a mistake
"Taking that fifth project in the same year was a misstep, and an expensive one."
To consolidate -- to make what you already have solid before adding anything new to it
"We spent two years consolidating instead of growing, and it saved the company."
To be handed something -- to receive a responsibility because somebody else decided to give it to you
"I was handed the commercial side on a Friday afternoon, with no conversation."
To lay the groundwork -- to do the unseen preparation that makes a later step possible
"Nobody noticed those two years. They were the groundwork for everything after."
A milestone -- a point in a project you can name and date, and that everybody agrees on
"The fortieth building was a milestone, and we noticed it two months late."
To phase something out -- to end something gradually, over time, instead of stopping it all at once
"We phased out the smaller jobs over three years, so nobody lost work overnight."
In hindsight -- looking back, with information you did not have at the time
"In hindsight I should have brought somebody in two years earlier."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A career answer is a story with two clocks in it. One is the moment the story is standing in, and English marks that with the past simple: I took over the direction in 2011. The other is everything that was already true when that moment arrived, and English marks that with had plus a past participle: by then I had already taken on the commercial side. The second clock is the one Portuguese speakers usually leave out, because in Portuguese the order of the sentences already carries the sequence. In English it does not. The tense carries it.

Look at what the second clock does to a misstep. "I took a fifth project and it cost us money" is a confession. "By the time I took the fifth project, we had already been two engineers short for a year" is an explanation, and it is not an excuse, because the outcome has not changed. It is the same admission with the context restored, and in an interview that difference is the whole difference.

The same structure carries the parts of the story that were not yours. My predecessor had laid the groundwork for thirty years before I arrived. Nobody had written a succession plan. Two of our oldest clients had gone before I sat down in the chair. Each of those is a fact about the past of the past, and each one makes a decision you took afterwards look reasonable instead of lucky. In hindsight is the phrase that opens that door out loud, and in speech the had almost always contracts: by then I'd already taken it on.

1. According to the text, what does "had" plus the participle mark?
A Something that happened a very long time ago.
B Everything that was already true when the moment of the story arrived.
C An action that is still going on today.
2. Why does the text say Portuguese speakers leave the second clock out?
A Because in Portuguese the order of the sentences already carries the sequence, and in English the tense does.
B Because Portuguese has no past tense of its own.

3. What does the second clock do to the admission of a misstep?
A It removes the responsibility from the speaker.
B It changes the outcome of the decision.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past Before the Past

GRAMMAR

The past simple is the camera. Had plus the participle is everything already true when it arrived.

JobFormExample
The moment in the storypast simpleI took over the direction in 2011.
Already finished by thenhad + past participleI had already taken on the commercial side.
A count up to that pointby then / by 2011 + had + participleBy then we had completed thirty buildings.
Two steps, same orderpast simple + past simpleWe diversified, and it carried us through.

Never: when he died, I already took it on · the company had complete thirty buildings · I had taken over in 2011 · before I took over, nobody wrote a plan · by then I have already taken it on.

Stage 2: Put the Career in Order

Order

An interviewer has asked you to walk her through the moments, not the titles. Put the answer in a logical order.

?"There are three moments, and the first is a four-year stint on site."
?"By 2009 I had been handed the commercial side as well."
?"So when my father died in 2011, I was thrown in at the deep end but not unprepared."
?"Nobody had written a succession plan, and I inherited that on the same afternoon."
?"The third moment is 2016, when we consolidated instead of growing."
?"What made you ask about the moments rather than the roles?"
