Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Company Behind the Name -- Ownership, Succession and Growth

Why so few family firms reach the third generation, and how to tell fifty years of company history in English. Key words: to found, to take over, succession, a downturn, to weather something, to diversify, a stake, governance, to bring somebody in, a legacy, to scale, a blind spot. Structure: past simple and present perfect.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To found -- to start a company and be the person its history begins with
"My father founded the company in 1978, with two employees and one truck."
To take over -- to become the person in charge of something somebody else was running
"I took over the direction of the company after my father passed away."
Succession -- the passing of control of a company from one person or generation to the next
"Nobody in the family had ever written a succession plan, and that cost us two years."
A downturn -- a period when business slows down across a whole market, not only in your company
"The company has survived three downturns since 1978."
To weather something -- to get through a difficult period without being destroyed by it
"We weathered 2015 because we had no debt. Half of our competitors did not."
To diversify -- to add new kinds of work so that the company does not depend on one market
"We diversified into renovation work in 2016, and it carried us for two years."
A stake -- the share of a company that somebody owns
"My uncle and I hold equal stakes, so neither of us can decide alone."
Governance -- the rules that decide who makes which decision inside a company
"Good governance is boring on a good year and priceless on a bad one."
To bring somebody in -- to hire somebody from outside to do what the family used to do
"We brought in a finance director in 2019, and it was the best decision of the decade."
A legacy -- what a founder leaves behind, which the next generation inherits and has to carry
"The legacy is forty buildings and a name that opens doors in this city."
To scale -- to grow in a way the structure can actually support, instead of just taking more work
"Taking a fifth project is not scaling. Scaling is being able to take the sixth."
A blind spot -- something you cannot see about your own business because you are too close to it
"Every owner has a blind spot. Mine is that I still price the way my father priced."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A construction company in São Paulo. His father founded it in 1978, with two employees and one truck. That sentence names a year, and the year is closed, so the verb stays in the past. Nobody can go back and change 1978.

Then there is the other half of the story. The firm has completed forty buildings since then, and the counting is not finished: it could be forty-one next month. It has weathered three downturns, and it has never taken outside capital. None of those sentences names a year, because none of them is closed. When the line runs from a point in the past all the way to today, English switches to have or has plus the past participle.

The two tenses live side by side in the same paragraph. He took over the direction with his uncle after his father died; today they hold equal stakes, and the governance has been shared ever since. They diversified into renovation work in 2016, and they brought in a finance director in 2019 — two closed years, two past verbs. But ask what the company has done with that decision and the tense changes again, because the answer has not stopped happening.

1. "His father founded it in 1978." Why the past simple and not "has founded"?
A Because the company no longer exists.
B Because the sentence names a finished year, and a finished year never takes the perfect.
C Because "found" is an irregular verb.
2. Why is it "the firm has completed forty buildings since then"?
A Because the period runs to today and the number could still go up.
B Because forty is a large number.
C Because the buildings are still under construction.
3. The text says both tenses live in the same paragraph. What decides which one you use?
A How long ago the event happened.
B Whether the sentence is positive or negative.
C Whether the sentence names a closed moment or describes a line that reaches today.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Simple and Present Perfect

GRAMMAR

One history, two tenses: the year that closed, and the line that is still open.

JobFormExample
A closed momentpast simple, with in 1978 / last year / agoHe founded it in 1978.
A period still openhave / has + past participle, with since / forWe have completed forty buildings since then.
Experience, no datehave / has + participle, with ever / neverThe firm has never had an outside partner.
Recent newshave / has + participle, with just / already / yetWe have just brought in a finance director.
Negativedid not + base form / have not + participleHe did not take over in 2011.
QuestionDid you...? vs Have you ever...?Did you diversify in 2016? / Have you ever hired outside?
One story, both tensesopen with the perfect, then give the datesWe have weathered three downturns. The worst was in 2015.

Never: he has founded it in 1978 · we build forty buildings since then · last year we have brought in a director · he did not took over · I have taken over three years ago.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A buyer asks how old the company is. You answer:
A "My father has founded the company in 1978."
B "My father founded it in 1978, and it has been in the same family since."
C "The company is founding since 1978."
You want to say the company has delivered forty buildings up to today. You say:
A "We build forty buildings since 1978."
B "We have completed forty buildings since 1978."
C "We completed forty buildings until now."
Somebody asks whether you have ever hired a director from outside the family. You answer:
A "Not for the direction. We have brought in specialists, but the decisions have stayed inside the family."
B "No, never in 2019."
C "We are not bringing anybody since always."
You want to explain how the two partners decide. You say:
A "We have the same part of the company, so we are the same boss."
B "We hold equal stakes, and the governance has been shared since I took over."
C "Nobody decides. It is a family company."
