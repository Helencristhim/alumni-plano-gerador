Chapter 4: The Code

A Year That Closed, and a Line Still Open

Past simple and present perfect — the same history, two jobs

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"My father founded the company in 1978."

"We have completed forty buildings since then."

"He took over the direction three years ago."

"The firm has never had an outside partner."

Two of the blue parts name a moment that is closed and cannot change. Two of them describe a line that runs all the way to today. Look at what happens to the red verb in each case.

JobFormExample
A closed momentpast simple, with in 1978 / last year / agoHe founded it in 1978.
A period still openhave / has + past participle, with since / forWe have completed forty buildings since then.
Experience, no datehave / has + participle, with ever / neverThe firm has never had an outside partner.
Recent newshave / has + participle, with just / already / yetWe have just brought in a finance director.
Negative and questiondid not + base form / have not + participleHe did not take over. / Have you ever hired outside?
One story, both tensesopen with the perfect, then give the datesWe have weathered three downturns. The worst was in 2015.
The trapa finished date never takes the perfectNot he has founded it in 1978.

In one line: if you name the year, use the past. If the line runs to today, use have.

The company was founded in 1978 and has stayed in the same family since. It diversified into renovation work in 2016 and brought in a finance director in 2019. Governance is shared between two partners, and no decision above a fixed value has been taken by one of them alone since that year.
1. Two lines on this page use a closed year and two describe a line running to today. Give one of each, and say why.
Closed: "Founded 1978" and "diversified in 2016" — the year cannot change. Open: "Delivered since 1978" and "has stayed in the same family since" — both run to today and could change tomorrow.
2. What does the page say about ownership and about outside capital?
There are two family stakes, held equally, and the company has taken no outside capital to date.
3. According to the paragraph, what changed in 2019, and what rule came with it?
A finance director was brought in. Since that year no decision above a fixed value has been taken by one partner alone.
Listening 1 · Third Generation

The Year Nobody Wanted to Talk About It

A third-generation owner on the succession that nearly did not happen. Sound first — no text.

1. When was the company founded, and what does she say has not changed since?
Her grandmother founded it in 1961. The building has not changed since, and neither has the recipe.
2. What happened in 2014, and why does she call it the expensive year?
Her father got sick and there was no succession plan. Three people thought they were in charge, and it cost the company eighteen months and a big client.
3. What does she say she has done since, and what is her advice?
She has written everything down and brought in two people from outside the family. Her advice is to have the conversation while it is still boring, because it is never boring later.
Listening 2 · An Advisor

What I See in the First Two Meetings

A family-business advisor on the blind spot every owner has. Sound first — no text.

1. What question does he ask in the first meeting, and what answer worries him?
He asks who decides. If two people answer at the same time and give different names, he knows where the year is going to go.
2. What does he say the common blind spot is, and how does he describe its cost?
Owners confuse ownership with authority. It is not that they disagree about the decision, it is that nobody has ever written down who gets to make it, and that costs more than any bad decision.
3. What does he say about hiring from outside the family?
That the companies which have survived three generations brought somebody in early, and that they never regret it, they only regret the years they waited.
