Lesson 13 -- Pre-class

If I Had More Time -- The Tense for Things That Are Not Real

How to talk about what you would do in a situation that is not true, and why English uses a past tense to do it. Key expressions: to take up, to swap, to be tied up, otherwise, to wind down, a shame, to look forward to, to have second thoughts, sooner or later, to be in two minds. Structure: the second conditional.

To take up -- to start a new hobby or activity
"If I had more time I would take up sailing."
To swap -- to exchange one thing for another
"I would swap two hours of meetings for one hour of silence."
To be tied up -- to be too busy to do anything else
"I am tied up until about seven every day."
Otherwise -- if not, or in the other case
"Tuesday works. Otherwise it would have to be next month."
To wind down -- to relax slowly after a period of effort
"It takes me two hours to wind down after a bad day."
A shame -- a situation that is disappointing
"It is a shame, because the idea was a good one."
To look forward to -- to feel happy about something that is going to happen
"I am looking forward to the first week of August."
To have second thoughts -- to start doubting a decision you have already made
"He signed it and then he had second thoughts."
Sooner or later -- at some point in the future, certainly
"Sooner or later somebody will ask me that in English."
To be in two minds -- to be unable to decide between two options
"I am in two minds about the whole thing."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a question that turns up at every dinner table in every language, usually after the plates are gone. If you had a free year, what would you do with it? Nobody is offering anybody a free year. That is exactly why people answer it honestly: nothing is at stake, nobody has to commit, and for two minutes the conversation is about a person instead of a job.

Janderson's answer has been the same for about ten years. If he had the time, he would take up sailing and learn it properly, not badly on somebody else's boat. He is tied up until seven most days, so it stays where it has always been, in the imaginary half of the sentence, and he says it with a laugh that means he has said it before.

His wife has pointed out the obvious more than once. If he waited for a free year he would wait for ever. One Saturday a month would already be enough, and by the summer he would be sailing badly, which is how everybody starts. He has second thoughts every time, he is in two minds, and it is a shame, because the maths is not difficult.

One grammatical detail is worth noticing here, because it explains a whole tense. Every sentence in the paragraph above uses a past form — if he had, if he waited — and none of them is about the past. English borrows the past tense to mark a sentence as not real. Sooner or later he will start, and on that day the tense changes back.

1. "If he had the time, he would take up sailing." Is this sentence about the past?
A Yes, it describes something he did years ago.
B No -- the past form marks the sentence as imaginary, and it is about now.
C Yes, because had is a past verb.
2. Why does the text say people answer the free-year question honestly?
A Because nothing is at stake and nobody has to commit.
B Because it is a work question.
C Because it is easy to answer in a second language.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A If I would have more time, I would learn.
B If I had more time, I would learn.
C If I have more time, I would learn.
4. What does the text say his wife pointed out?
A That sailing is too expensive.
B That if he waited for a free year he would wait for ever, and one Saturday a month is enough.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Second Conditional

GRAMMAR

One structure for everything that is imaginary, unlikely or simply not true today. The only strange part is that English uses a past tense to say it.

FormWhat it saysExample
if + past simple, would + verbThe situation is not real. The sentence is about now, not about the past."If I had more time, I would take up sailing."
The past means distance from realityNot distance in time. That single idea explains the whole tense."If I spoke better English, I would say more." (today)
if I were, not if I wasThis structure keeps were for every subject. It is the standard form in advice."If I were you..."
"If he were here..."
could and mightThe result can be uncertain too."If we started earlier, we could finish by six."
First or second conditionalThe tense is the whole difference, and people hear it."If I have time, I will call." (real)
"If I had time, I would call." (not real)
The questionIt inverts like any other question with would."What would you do?"
NOT "What you would do"

There is a professional use for this beyond dinner. If I were in your position, I would is the safest way in English to give a strong opinion to somebody senior or to a client, because it leaves the decision with them while saying exactly what you think.
