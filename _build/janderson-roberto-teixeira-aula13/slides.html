Chapter 3: The Code

If I Had, I Would

A past tense doing a job that has nothing to do with the past

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If I had more time, I would take up sailing."

"If I were you, I would wait two weeks."

"What would you do with a free year?"

"If I have time this week, I will call him."

Three of these are imaginary and one of them is a real plan for this week. The tenses tell you which is which, before the meaning does. How?

FormWhat it saysExample
if + past simple, would + verbThe situation is not real: imaginary, unlikely, or simply not true today."If I had more time, I would take up sailing."
The past is not the pastThe past tense is a signal of distance from reality, not distance in time. The sentence is about now."If I spoke better English, I would say more in meetings." (today)
if I were, not if I wasWith I, he and she, this structure keeps the old form were. It is the standard in writing and in advice."If I were you..."
"If he were here, he would agree."
could and mightThe result does not have to be certain, exactly as in the first conditional."If we started earlier, we could finish by six."
First or secondThe tense is the whole difference. Present + will = it may really happen. Past + would = it probably will not."If I have time, I will call." (real)
"If I had time, I would call." (not real)

Nothing in this structure is about the past. English borrows a past tense to mark the whole sentence as imaginary, and once you hear it that way it stops being confusing for ever.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If I would have more time, I would take up sailing."
"If I had more time, I would take up sailing."
"If I was you, I would wait two weeks."
"If I were you, I would wait two weeks."
"What you would do with a free year?"
"What would you do with a free year?"

The half that starts with if never takes would: the would lives in the other half, exactly as will did in Lesson 12. In this structure English keeps were for every subject, so it is if I were you. And a question still inverts: what would you do, never what you would do. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Chapter 4: Dinner on a Thursday

Dinner on a Thursday

The part of the evening where nobody talks about work any more

Dialogue

What Would You Do?

B
Right, no more work talk. If somebody gave you a free year, what would you do with it?
J
Honestly? If I had a free year I would take up sailing, and I would learn it properly.
B
Sailing. That is not the answer I expected from you.
J
Nobody expects it. I am tied up until seven every day, so it stays imaginary.
B
It is a shame. If you started now, one Saturday a month, you would be sailing by the summer.
J
You sound like my wife. I am in two minds about it, and I have been for about ten years.
B
Then sooner or later you will do it. What would you do first if you had the boat tomorrow?
J
I would go nowhere. I would sit on it and wind down for three hours. What about you?
Comprehension

About Beth

1. What does Beth ask him at the start?
What he would do with a free year.
2. How does she react to his answer?
She says sailing is not the answer she expected from him.
3. What does she suggest he could do now?
Start with one Saturday a month, and he would be sailing by the summer.
4. What is her last question?
What he would do first if he had the boat tomorrow.
Listening

The Question at Every Dinner Table

A short radio segment. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does the speaker say happens when you ask somebody what they would do?
They answer honestly, because nothing is at stake and nobody is being asked to commit to anything.
2. Why does she say the question works with strangers?
Because it asks about a person without asking anything private.
3. What does she say about the answers people give?
That the same answer comes back for years, which usually means it is not imaginary at all.
Listening 2

Ten Years of Two Minds

A short personal story from a podcast. Sound first -- no text.

1. How long had he been in two minds about it?
About ten years.
2. What did his wife say to him?
That if he waited for a free year he would wait for ever, and that one Saturday a month was already enough.
3. What does he say he does on the boat?
Mostly nothing -- he sits on it and winds down, and he says that is the point.

The Habit

One Imaginary Sentence, Out Loud

Before the next class, say one imaginary sentence out loud each day: if I had something, I would do something. Real desire, imaginary condition, past tense in the first half.

This is the tense of every dinner conversation you will ever have in English. It costs one sentence a day.
