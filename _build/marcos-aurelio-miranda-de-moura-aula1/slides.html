Chapter 3: The Code

What I Do, and What I Do Not

One verb, three shapes

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I look after pricing and trade marketing."

"We sell to a distributor, and we visit the workshops."

"We do not sell to the driver."

"Do you look after pricing as well?"

Every red verb is exactly the same shape, in all four sentences. Now look at the blue words: they only turn up when the sentence says no, or asks a question.

JobFormExample
Something true every dayI / you / we + bare verbI look after pricing.
Negativedo not + bare verbWe do not sell to the driver.
QuestionDo + subject + bare verbDo you look after pricing?
Question word in frontWhat / Who / How often + do + subject + bare verbWho do you report to?
Short answerYes, I do. / No, we do not."Do you set the prices?" — "Yes, I do."
How oftenevery quarter, twice a year, usually, neverI present to this board every quarter.
The trapno to be in front of the verbNot I am look after pricing. Say I look after pricing.

In one line: the verb never changes shape, and do carries the no and the question for it.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Board Meeting, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one agenda

Dialogue

Five Minutes Before the Board Starts

D
I do not think we have met. Diane Whitaker, regional marketing. Which part of the business do you look after?
M
Marcos Moura. I am the marketing director in Brazil, and I look after five areas.
D
Five? That is a lot for one director. Which ones?
M
Pricing, trade marketing, market intelligence, and brand and communication. Business strategy is the fifth one.
D
And who do you report to?
M
I report to the general manager here, and I present to this board every quarter. My team is small. There are eleven of us.
D
Good. Then I hear from you today. What is on your agenda?
M
One agenda item: the price list for next year. And you — how many markets do you cover from your side?
Comprehension

About Diane

1. How does Diane open the conversation, and what does she say about the two of them?
She gives her name and her job, regional marketing, and says she does not think they have met.
2. What is her reaction when she hears how many areas he looks after?
She is surprised. She says five is a lot for one director, and then she asks which ones they are.
3. What does Diane tell him about today, and what does she want to know at the end?
She tells him she hears from him today, which means he speaks in the meeting, and she asks what is on his agenda.
Listening 1 · A Director

Two Minutes at the Start of the Board

A marketing director introducing herself and her team. Sound first — no text.

1. Which areas does she say she looks after?
Pricing, brand and communication, and she also works with trade marketing, market intelligence and business strategy.
2. What does she say about the size of her team, and about her own boss?
There are nine of them and six work on the brand side. She reports to the group commercial vice president.
3. How many items does she bring to the meeting, and what does she promise about it?
One item, the price list for next year. She says she knows it is the item nobody enjoys, so she keeps it short.
Listening 2 · A Podcast

What a Good Introduction Sounds Like

A leadership coach on introducing yourself in a senior meeting. Sound first — no text.

1. What three things does he say a good introduction contains?
Your name, what you are responsible for in one line, and who you report to if the room does not already know. Then you stop.
2. What mistake does he say he hears every week?
The person who explains the whole history of the department before anybody asks. He says nobody in the room is listening to the history.
3. What does he say to people who work in a second language?
That nobody in a board meeting cares about their grammar. They care whether they understand what that person owns.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Market Nobody Thinks About

A short business radio report on the lubricants market. Sound first — no text.

1. Who does the report say these companies sell to, and why do we almost never see their advertising?
They sell to distributors, to workshops and to industry, not to the driver. That is why their advertising is almost invisible to the public.
2. What does the report say makes marketing a strange job in this sector?
The product does not change very often, the price moves with the price of oil, and the customer is usually another company.
3. What two questions does the report say fill the marketing director’s week?
What do we charge, and what does the workshop on the corner say about us when the customer asks.
