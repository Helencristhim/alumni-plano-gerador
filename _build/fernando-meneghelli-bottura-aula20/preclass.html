Lesson 20 -- Pre-class

The Final Calibration -- Sounding Like Yourself

Your English is correct; now it has to sound like a conversation, not a report. Short answers, fewer repeated words, one fact, and a hand back. Key words: rapport, small talk, stilted, long-winded, to ramble, to come across, off the cuff, self-assured, understated, to strike a balance, a back-and-forth, to over-explain. Structure: ellipsis and substitution (I have. I think so. I hope not. So did I.).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words for how an answer sounds: the length, the tone, and the impression it leaves.

Rapport -- a friendly connection that makes people trust and understand each other
“Five minutes of real conversation built more rapport than my whole presentation.”
Small talk -- light, polite conversation about easy topics before the real business
“The CEO opened with small talk about the traffic, and I answered in one line.”
Stilted -- too formal and stiff, so it does not sound natural
“My first answers were correct but stilted, like a report read aloud.”
Long-winded -- using far more words than you need, so people lose interest
“A long-winded answer loses the room before it reaches the number.”
To ramble -- to talk for too long without a clear direction
“When I am nervous, I ramble; when I am prepared, I stop.”
To come across -- to give a particular impression to other people
“I want to come across as calm, not as rehearsed.”
Off the cuff -- said without preparing it first
“Her best question was off the cuff, and so was my best answer.”
Self-assured -- calm and confident about your own ability
“A self-assured candidate can say I do not know without apologizing.”
Understated -- quiet and modest, without trying to impress
“His answer was understated: one number, one reason, and a smile.”
To strike a balance -- to find the right middle point between two things
“You have to strike a balance between detail and brevity.”
A back-and-forth -- a conversation in which both people speak and answer in turn
“The best interviews feel like a back-and-forth, not an exam.”
To over-explain -- to give more detail than the listener needs or asked for
“If you over-explain a decision, it starts to sound like an excuse.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

After round one, the headhunter called with one note: my answers were right, but they were long-winded. When the panel asked if I had ever led a sale, I repeated the whole question back and answered at the end.

In round two I tried something different. The chief executive asked if I had worked with private equity. I said, I have, twice, and gave one fact. She asked if I thought the market would recover. I think so, I said, but not before the autumn. Was the reporting line going to change? I hope not.

At the end she said she preferred direct reporting lines, and so did the chair. It was the first time the interview felt like a back-and-forth. I had not prepared less. I had just stopped trying to over-explain.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Say Less, Mean the Same

GRAMMAR

In conversation, native speakers do not repeat what everybody already knows. They keep the auxiliary (I have, I did), or replace a whole clause with so or not.

FormMeaningExample
subject + auxiliaryA short answer, no repetitionHave you led a sale? I have. Did you sign it? I did.
think / hope / expect + soYes, replacing the whole clauseWill they approve it? I think so.
hope / be afraid + notNo, replacing the whole clauseWill it change? I hope not. I am afraid not.
so / neither + auxiliary + subjectThe same is true for somebody elseI left in 2017, and so did my controller. I have not signed, and neither has she.
to / oneKeep the infinitive; replace a nounWould you like to meet them? I would like to. The first plan was ambitious; the second one was realistic.
The trapNever I think yes or I hope no. With think, the negative is I don’t think so.

In an interview, the short form sounds self-assured. Repeating the whole question sounds rehearsed.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen, then record yourself. In a short answer the stress falls on the last word: I have. I think so. I hope not. So would most CFOs.

Have you ever led an acquisition? I have, twice.
Will the board approve it? I think so, but not before June.
Is the role going to change? I hope not.
I would rather report to you directly, and so would most CFOs.
Do you think the team is strong enough? I think so, from what I have seen.
I can give you the numbers, if that would help.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The person asking is the chief executive, in the second interview. Choose the answer that works for her.

She asks: Have you ever worked with private equity? You start with:
A Yes, I have worked with private equity before, in my previous company, and in the one before that one.
B I have, twice. The second time was a sale.
C Yes, I worked.
She asks: Will the board approve your plan? You think it will. You say:
A I think so.
B I think yes, because the plan is good and the board has already seen the numbers twice.
C I think it.
She asks: Is the role going to change? You do not want it to. You say:
A I don’t hope.
B I hope no, because I like the role exactly as it was described to me in round one.
C I hope not.
You say you prefer reporting directly to the CEO. You want to add that most CFOs prefer the same. You say:
A ...and so would most CFOs.
B ...and most CFOs would also prefer to report directly to the CEO, in my experience of the market.
C ...and so most CFOs would.
She asks a yes or no question, and you have a lot to say. The strongest move is:
A Say everything you prepared, so that she has the full context before she asks anything else.
B Answer short, give one fact, and offer more.
C Answer yes or no and say nothing else.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the questions below. Nobody is correcting this one, and there is no score.

Imagine the second interview has just started. Answer these five questions out loud, one after the other: Have you ever reported to a foreign parent company? Do you think the market will recover this year? Would you relocate? Is your notice period longer than three months? Have you ever lost a client? Start every answer with the short form (I have, I think so, I would, I hope not), add one fact, and stop. Then listen once and count how many answers repeated the question.
