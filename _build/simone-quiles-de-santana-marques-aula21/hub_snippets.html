M&A Vocabulary -- The Words of a Deal

Carry a whole transaction in English: what the buyer does, what the seller keeps, and what the lawyers check in between. New vocabulary: acquire, merge, target, due diligence, valuation, stake, bid, synergy, divest, letter of intent. Grammar: the three future forms of a deal timeline -- present continuous for a fixed date, "be going to" for a decision already taken, and "will" for a prediction or a promise.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Acquire -- to buy another company and take control of it
"Our client is going to acquire a logistics company in Campinas."
Merge -- to join two companies so that they become one
"The two banks decided to merge and share one board."
Target -- the company that another company plans to buy
"The target is smaller than the buyer, so this is an acquisition."
Due diligence -- the detailed check of a company that a buyer runs before signing
"Due diligence will take six weeks and will cover every contract."
Valuation -- the price that a bank or an expert puts on a company
"The valuation is already with the bank; we only review the legal side."
Stake -- the part of a company that a person or a firm owns
"The seller is going to keep a minority stake of fifteen percent."
Bid -- the price a buyer formally offers for a company
"The buyer will make a formal bid once due diligence is finished."
Synergy -- the extra value two companies create when they work as one
"The board wants a note on synergy before it approves the price."
Divest -- to sell a part of a business that you no longer want to own
"They are going to divest the warehouse arm before completion."
Letter of intent -- a short document that sets out the price and the timetable before the contract
"We are signing the letter of intent next Friday."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Simone is the lawyer on Project Aurora. Her client is going to acquire a logistics company in Campinas. The target is smaller than the buyer, so this is an acquisition and not a decision to merge. The bank has already delivered the valuation, and the legal team does not comment on the price. "We are signing the letter of intent on Friday at ten," she tells the client, because the date and the room are already booked. Due diligence will take about six weeks, and honestly it will probably take longer, because there are three companies inside the target. The seller is going to keep a minority stake of fifteen percent for two years, and is going to divest the warehouse arm before completion. After the report, the buyer is going to make a formal bid, and the board wants a note on synergy before it approves the price.

1. Why does Simone use "we are signing" and not "we will sign"?
A The date and the room are already booked, so it is a fixed arrangement.
B Because the signing is only a prediction.
C Because the client has not decided yet.
2. Which form does the text use for the seller's decision about the stake?
A "will keep", because it is a promise made now.
B "is going to keep", because the decision was taken before this conversation.
C "is keeping", because it is in the diary with a time.
3. Why is this an acquisition and not a merger?
A Because the two companies are the same size.
B Because the bank delivered the valuation.
C Because the target is smaller and the buyer takes control of it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Future Forms on a Deal Timeline

GRAMMAR

A transaction is a calendar, and English carries three different levels of certainty about it. Ask yourself when the decision was made. If the date is already in the diary, with a time and a room, use the present continuous. If the plan was decided before this conversation, use be going to + the base verb. If you are predicting, or promising something right now in the room, use will + the base verb. All three are correct English; the wrong one only makes the client hear the wrong level of certainty.

FormUseExample
Present continuousa fixed date, with a time and a place"We are signing on Friday at ten."
Be going to + base verba plan decided before you speak"They are going to divest the warehouse arm."
Will + base verba prediction about the future"Due diligence will take six weeks."
Will + base verba promise you make in the room"I will send you the report on the tenth."
Negativesame difference, same meaning"They are not going to divest it early."

Key point: two traps to watch. After will and after going to the verb is always the base form ("will to take" → "will take"; "going to divesting" → "going to divest"). And a date already booked never takes will: a client who hears "we are signing on Friday" writes it in the calendar.

Order
