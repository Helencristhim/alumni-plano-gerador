Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

The Question Behind the Question -- Asking Well

A studio visit where you ask the questions, and the grammar of asking politely and following up. Key words: a follow-up question, to probe, a leading question, open-ended, to rephrase, to clarify, to elaborate, to dodge, rapport, candid, to tease out, to put someone on the spot. Structure: indirect questions and follow-up questions.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A follow-up question -- a second question that builds on the answer you have just heard
"The follow-up question is where the real answer usually starts."
To probe -- to keep asking, carefully, until you reach the detail underneath
"She probed gently until he admitted the show had changed at the last minute."
A leading question -- a question that pushes the other person toward the answer you want
"Would you say it is about loss? That is a leading question."
Open-ended -- impossible to answer with a simple yes or no
"Start with one open-ended question and then stay quiet."
To rephrase -- to ask or say the same thing again, in different words
"If he does not understand, rephrase it once, more plainly."
To clarify -- to make something clearer, or to ask for it to be made clearer
"Could you clarify what you mean by the second phase?"
To elaborate -- to add more detail or explanation to what you have said
"Could you elaborate on why you chose those six artists?"
To dodge -- to avoid answering a question, often in a clever way
"He dodged the question about the budget twice."
Rapport -- a relationship of trust and ease between two people
"Ten minutes in the studio built more rapport than an hour of email."
Candid -- honest and direct, even when it is uncomfortable
"That is a candid answer, and I did not expect it."
To tease out -- to draw out information or meaning slowly and carefully
"Good interviewers tease out what the artist has never said in public."
To put someone on the spot -- to force someone to answer something difficult, in front of others
"I do not want to put you on the spot, but was the jury divided?"

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before the studio visit, Liliane wrote her questions on one card. She did not write what inspired you, because that question is so open-ended that it invites a prepared answer. She wrote: Could you tell me how you chose the six artists?

The curator gave a short, polished reply, so she asked a follow-up question made of his own words: When you say still changing, what do you mean exactly? Then, carefully, she probed a little further: I was wondering whether you ever choose somebody you do not fully understand.

He paused, then gave a candid answer: yes, two of the six. Later he dodged a question about money, so she rephrased it once: Do you mind if I ask what the budget was? This time he answered. By the end there was real rapport in the room.

1. "Could you tell me how you chose the six artists?" Why is there no did?
A Because the question is about the past, and the past never uses did.
B Because after a polite opener the rest keeps the order of a normal statement.
C Because the curator is a professional.
2. Why did she ask "When you say still changing, what do you mean?"
A To follow up with his own words and get past the polished first answer.
B To correct his English.
C Because she had not heard him.
3. He dodged the question about money. What did she do?
A She accused him of hiding something.
B She changed the subject completely.
C She rephrased it once, gently, with a polite opener.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions and Follow-up Questions

GRAMMAR

How to ask politely, and how to ask for the second answer.

RuleFormExample
Opener + question word + statement orderno do, does or did after the openerCould you tell me how you chose the artists?
Yes/no inside: if or whetheropener + whether + subject + verbI was wondering whether you work with sound.
The verb comes after its subjectnot what was the budgetDo you mind if I ask what the budget was?
Punctuation follows the openera statement opener takes a periodI was wondering whether you are free on Friday.
Follow up with their wordWhen you say X, what do you mean?When you say risk, what do you mean exactly?
Explain to someoneexplain + to + personCould you explain to me why the show closed?

Never: Could you tell me how did you choose · I was wondering if do you · what was the budget (inside another question) · explain me why.

Stage 2: Put the Interview in Order

Order

Six moments of a good interview with a curator. Put them in the order they happen.

?"Thank you for making time to meet me in the studio."
?"Could you tell me how the show began?"
?"When you say began, do you mean the idea or the funding?"
?"I was wondering whether any of the six artists surprised you."
?"Could you elaborate on that?"
?"Is there a question you expected me to ask, and I did not?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the sentence you would actually say.

You want to know how a curator chose the artists. You ask:
A "Could you tell me how did you choose the artists?"
B "Could you tell me how you chose the artists?"
C "Tell me how chose you the artists."
The curator says the show is about a different kind of risk. You follow up:
A "When you say a different kind of risk, what do you mean exactly?"
B "Why?"
C "Risk is interesting."
A doctor dodges your question about the night shift. You say:
A "You are hiding the answer."
B "Let me ask it another way: what did the night shift look like at three in the morning?"
C "OK, next question."
You want to ask about money without being rude. You say:
A "How much money you have?"
B "Explain me the budget."
C "Do you mind if I ask what the budget is?"
