Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

What Does This Actually Do? -- The Label on the Back

The supermarket aisle, the small type on the back of the box, and how to tell two products apart. Key words: a label, an ingredient, fragrance-free, to dissolve, grease, a stain, gentle on, heavy-duty, a scoop, shelf life, value pack, to be worth it. Structure: comparatives and superlatives.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A label -- the printed text on a package that says what is inside and how to use it
"Everything that matters is on the label, on the back."
An ingredient -- one of the things that a product is made of
"The ingredient list is always the smallest type on the box."
Fragrance-free -- with no perfume and no added smell at all
"I only buy fragrance-free now, because of my hands."
To dissolve -- to break down and disappear into a liquid
"It dissolves in cold water, so you do not need a hot cycle."
Grease -- thick oily dirt from cooking, that water alone does not remove
"This one breaks down grease on an oven tray in one wash."
A stain -- a mark on cloth that does not come out with water alone
"There was a coffee stain on a white shirt since the day before."
Gentle on -- soft enough not to damage skin, color or fabric
"It is gentle on color, so I use it on the girls’ clothes."
Heavy-duty -- made strong, for the hardest and dirtiest jobs
"Keep the heavy-duty one under the sink for the oven."
A scoop -- the small measuring cup that comes inside the box
"One scoop is enough for a normal load."
Shelf life -- how long a product is still good before you throw it away
"The shelf life is printed on the bottom of the box."
Value pack -- a bigger package that costs less for each wash
"The value pack holds twice as much for six dollars more."
To be worth it -- good enough to justify what you pay for it
"It costs eleven cents more per load, and it is worth it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The two boxes on that shelf look the same, and they are not. The heavy-duty one is stronger on grease than the gentle one, and it is also cheaper per load, especially in the value pack. The gentle one is fragrance-free, it is gentler on skin and on color, and it dissolves faster in cold water. On paper, the heavy-duty one wins. In a normal week, it does not.

The reason is the one thing the label never prints: how often you use it. You take the heavy-duty box down twice a month; you take the gentle one down every day. The product you use every day has to be the kindest thing in the cupboard, not the most powerful one. That is why the best choice here is not the strongest product.

There is one more trap, and it is the biggest box in the aisle. The value pack costs less per load, but the shelf life is only nine months. If you wash three times a week, the big box is cheaper. If you wash once a week, it is the most expensive thing you can put in your cart, because half of it goes in the bin before you open it. That is the whole question: not what is strong, but what is worth it for you.

1. Why does the text say "cheaper" in one place and "the most expensive" in another, about the same value pack?
A Because the price of the box changes during the year.
B Because it depends on how often you wash: cheaper if you use it fast, the worst deal if you do not.
C Because cheaper and the most expensive mean the same thing in English.
2. "It is gentler on skin" and "it is more powerful". Why does one take -er and the other take more?
A Because gentle is a short adjective and powerful is a long one.
B Because one is positive and the other one is negative.
C Because the first one compares two things and the second one compares three.
3. "The best choice here is not the strongest product." What is the writer saying?
A That the strong product does not really clean anything.
B That you should buy both boxes every month.
C That for something you use every day, being kind to your hands matters more than power.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Two products take than. All the products in the aisle take the.

AdjectiveTwo things (+ than)All of them (the ...)
short: cheap, strong, gentlecheaper thanthe cheapest
ending in -y: dirty, easydirtier thanthe dirtiest
one vowel + one consonant: bigbigger thanthe biggest
long: expensive, effectivemore expensive thanthe most expensive
good / badbetter than / worse thanthe best / the worst
the sameas gentle as this onenot as strong as that one

Never: more cheaper · the most big · gentler then this one · the more expensive of all of them · more better
