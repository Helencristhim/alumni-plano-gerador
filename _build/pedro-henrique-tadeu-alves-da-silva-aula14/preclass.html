Lesson 14 -- Pre-class

How Much Is Too Much

Ninety-six tracks, one chorus, and the courage to delete: how to say what is too much, what is enough and what to keep. Key words: overproduced, a layer, a plug-in, clutter, to strip something back, sparse, restraint, overkill, to get carried away, to leave room for something. Structure: quantifiers and determiners -- many / much, a few / a little, too many / too much, enough, hardly any, with countable and uncountable nouns.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Overproduced -- having too many effects and extra parts, so it loses its natural feeling
"The demo was raw, but the album version sounds overproduced."
A layer -- one of several sounds or recordings placed on top of each other
"There are twelve layers of vocals in the chorus."
A plug-in -- a small piece of software that adds an effect to a sound
"He used only one plug-in on the lead vocal."
Clutter -- a lot of things in one place, so it is hard to see or hear what matters
"There is so much clutter in the chorus that you cannot find the voice."
To strip something back -- to remove extra parts so only the important ones stay
"He stripped the chorus back to four tracks."
Sparse -- with very few parts or sounds, often on purpose
"The verse is sparse: just a voice and a piano."
Restraint -- the decision not to do too much, even when you can
"Restraint is a skill, not a lack of ideas."
Overkill -- much more than is necessary
"Six reverbs on one vocal is overkill."
To get carried away -- to do too much because you are excited and stop thinking clearly
"I got carried away and added a few more harmonies."
To leave room for something -- to keep some space free so another thing can fit
"A good arrangement leaves room for the voice."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Pedro opens a session from a new artist. It has ninety-six tracks. There are too many layers in the chorus, a lot of synths, and so much reverb that the voice sounds far away. The song is overproduced: the artist got carried away. Look at the words before each noun. They tell you the amount, and they change with the noun.

First question: can you count it? Layers, tracks and plug-ins you can count, so you use many, a few and too many: there are too many tracks, and Pedro keeps only a few of them. Reverb, space, time and clutter you cannot count, so you use much, a little and too much: he adds a little reverb, not much. Questions follow the same rule: How many tracks are there? How much time is left?

Some words work with both: a lot of, enough, hardly any and no. There is hardly any space for the voice, and there are no backing vocals in the verse. Be careful with two small pairs. A few and a little are positive: a small amount, and that is OK. Few and little without a are negative: almost none, and that is a problem. And enough comes before the noun: enough energy, never energy enough.

So Pedro strips the chorus back. The new version is sparse in the verse and full in the chorus, and it leaves room for the singer. Six reverbs were overkill; one is enough. That is restraint: not fewer ideas, but better choices.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Quantifiers and Determiners

GRAMMAR

Two questions: can you count it? Is the amount OK, or is it a problem?

Remember: enough goes before the noun (enough space), and information, advice and feedback are not counted (a lot of feedback, not many feedbacks).
