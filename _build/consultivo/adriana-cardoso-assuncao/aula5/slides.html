5 · Check, then answer

Renata does not answer straight away

Greg asks Renata to break the cost down. She does not give him a number. What does she do instead, and what would have gone wrong if she had just answered?

Greg: “Can you break that down for me?”
Renata: “Just so I answer the right question, do you mean the total, or how we get to the total?”
Greg: “How you get to it.”
Name the two meanings, and let them choose “Break that down” can mean “tell me the total, in its parts” or “tell me how you got to that number”. Renata says both meanings out loud and lets Greg choose. Her answer is then the one he asked for, and she has a few seconds to think.
“Just so I answer the right question, do you mean… or…?” — two meanings, and he chooses.
“Are you asking about… or about…?” — the same check, shorter.
“Let me think about that for a second.” — when the question is clear and the answer is not ready.
6 · Say the check

A question that can mean two things, and your check

Your teacher asks you a short question about your work. Before you answer, check what they mean: say the two meanings you hear, and let them choose. Listen to the five examples first: you can use them when you check your teacher’s questions.

Sentence 1“Just so I answer the right question, do you mean the total, or how we get to it?”
Sentence 2“Are you asking about this quarter, or about the whole year?”
Sentence 3“Compared with what: last year, or the market?”
Sentence 4“Sorry, was that thirteen or thirty?”
Sentence 5“Let me think about that for a second.”
