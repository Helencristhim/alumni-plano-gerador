3 · Read it once

Read it once, for the story

Read the article once. What happened, and what has the company not published or not decided?

Longer routes, longer shifts: a shipping detour reaches the warehouse

Sample article.

For eleven weeks, container ships that used to take one of the shortest routes between Asia and the Atlantic have been going the long way around, after the route was closed to commercial traffic. The detour adds nine to twelve days to each trip, according to two of the largest carriers.

In Brazil, the effect is showing up far from the ports. At Norvale Logistics, which employs 1,312 people at three distribution centers in São Paulo state, overtime hours rose 18.4% between April and June, the company says. Ships now arrive in groups instead of on a regular schedule, and warehouse teams work long shifts when they do.

Company figures show that voluntary turnover at Norvale’s largest site went from 9.2% in the first quarter to 14.6% in the second. Its HR director, Claudia Reis, said exit interviews point to “schedules nobody can plan a life around.”

Norvale has not published what the departures cost. Brightline Advisory, an industry consultancy, estimates that replacing one warehouse worker costs about $4,870, including recruiting, training and lost productivity. On that estimate, the 48 people who left the site between April and June would represent roughly $233,800.

The carriers expect the route to reopen by the end of the year. Norvale says it is reviewing its shift model and has not decided whether to hire temporary staff.

5 · The same change, three ways

The same change, and one sentence says something else

Turnover went from 9.2% to 14.6%. Two of these sentences say what the article says. Which one says something different, and what number does your listener hear?

“Turnover rose from 9.2 to 14.6 percent.”
“Turnover rose 5.4 percent.”
“Turnover rose 5.4 percentage points.”
Percent of what? 14.6 minus 9.2 is 5.4. That gap between two percentages is measured in percentage points. “Rose 5.4 percent” means it grew by 5.4% of itself: from 9.2 to about 9.7. The real change was much bigger — turnover went up by more than half.
“from 9.2 to 14.6 percent” — both figures. The listener can check it.
“5.4 percentage points” — the gap between the two percentages.
“up by more than half” — the change, compared with where it started.
6 · Say the figures

The figures, with where they come from

Listen to each sentence and say it yourself. Then give your teacher the three figures a director would ask about first, each with its source.

“According to the company, overtime hours rose 18.4% between April and June.”
“Company figures show turnover went from 9.2% to 14.6%.”
“That is a rise of 5.4 percentage points.”
“A consultancy estimates about $4,870 to replace one worker.”
“That would be roughly $233,800, but it is an estimate, not a company figure.”

A model briefing

It is an article about warehouse jobs, and the numbers come from three different places. According to the company, overtime rose 18.4% between April and June, and turnover at its largest site went from 9.2% to 14.6% — that is 5.4 percentage points. The cost is not a company figure: a consultancy estimates about $4,870 to replace one worker, so the 48 people who left would represent roughly $233,800. The carriers expect the route to reopen by the end of the year, and the company has not decided about temporary staff. My reading is that the schedules are what is pushing people out, but the article only has the exit interviews on that.
