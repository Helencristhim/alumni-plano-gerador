Lesson 03

The Shape of
a Talk

Same facts. Same ask.
Only one of them can be followed.

1 · What they asked for

Three minutes, and they have not read it

From: the chair of the review board

Sample message, written for this lesson. No real product, no real board.

“None of us will have read the memo before Thursday. Give us three minutes on it at the top of the meeting — where we are, what the problem is, and what you are asking for. We will decide in that meeting, so whatever is not in your three minutes will not be in the decision.”

What do they need from you, and what will they do with it?

2 · Before you read

Two versions, and the room only follows one

When a room loses the thread of a good argument, what did the speaker fail to do?
Does adding signposting make a talk longer, or only different?
Which is harder to hear without a signal — a fact, or a concession?
3 · Read them once

The same argument, written twice

Version A

Sample text, written for this lesson. Invented product, invented numbers.

We tested the model on twelve months of data. Accuracy is ninety-four percent. The team wants to ship in October. The model cannot explain why it declines a case. Regulators in three markets now ask for that. We would have to rebuild the pipeline. Six weeks. I would hold the launch.

Version B

Sample text, written for this lesson. The same facts, in the same order.

Let me start with where we are. We tested the model on twelve months of data, and accuracy is ninety-four percent — which is why the team wants to ship in October.

Now, here is the problem. The model cannot explain why it declines a case, and regulators in three markets now ask for exactly that. What this means is that shipping in October buys us a launch and costs us the license.

Having said that, the fix is not enormous — rebuilding the pipeline is six weeks. So where does that leave us? My recommendation is that we hold the launch until December.

6 · Say the move out loud

Announce it, then say it

Your teacher gives you the bare fact. You give it back with the move in front of it.

Let me start with …
Now, here is the problem. …
What this means is …
Having said that, …
So where does that leave us?  ·  My recommendation is …
7 · Your three minutes

Take the room, and hold it

Your teacher is the review board.
They have not read the memo, and they decide in this meeting.

Where are we, what is the problem, and what are you asking for?

Open by saying where things stand — one sentence, signalled.
Announce the difficulty before you describe it.
Draw the consequence out loud, so nobody has to.
Concede the strongest thing against you, and keep your point.
Close with the ask, and with the question neither version answers.
Let me start with…  ·  Now, here is the problem.  ·  What this means is…
Having said that…  ·  So where does that leave us?  ·  My recommendation is…
8 · Ninety seconds

Again, to a board that disagrees

They have now read the memo — and they want to ship in October.

Same argument. Ninety seconds. What goes first?

What changes when they already disagree
You no longer have to establish the facts. You do have to make the concession early, because a room that thinks you have not heard the objection stops listening for it — and that concession is now the whole ninety seconds.
