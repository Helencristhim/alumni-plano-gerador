Lesson 09

Two Accounts of
the Same Night

A witness and a police officer describe the same incident.
Neither of them is lying, and the two texts still do not say the same thing.

When did you last read two versions of the same episode, and have to tell someone what the difference changes?

1 · What she asked for

A colleague asks you for the difference

Claire Whitfield is a lawyer from another jurisdiction. She has the two files on the same incident, and she is about to talk to you.

From: Claire Whitfield — visiting colleague

Sample message.

“I have both files but I will not have time to read them before we talk. Can you talk me through where the two accounts stop agreeing? I need to know which parts are in the documents and which parts are your reading — I have to repeat this to someone else afterwards.”

What does Claire need from you, and what will she do with it?

2 · Before you read

Two honest people, two different nights

In a moment you will read two texts about the same incident: an account written by the woman who was there, and an incident report written by the officer who arrived afterwards.

Before you open them, say what you expect.

Which of the two is more likely to be exact about the hour — the person who was there, or the record written afterwards?
Which of the two is more likely to know who spoke first?
If the two disagree, does that make one of them false?
3 · Read them once

First, read each one on its own

Read the account, then the report. Do not compare them yet.

For each text, be ready to say: who wrote it, what for, and what it does not set out to say.

Witness account — Ms. Ferraz

Sample document.

I got home a little before eleven. There were four of them on the corner, by the gate.

One of them called out to me first. I did not speak until he did. I answered, and then I went in.

I did not see anyone leave. I was not there long enough to say what happened after.

Incident report — officer on duty

Sample document.

Attendance recorded at 23:40. Three individuals present at the location on arrival.

The complainant states that she addressed the group. No further exchange reported.

No mention of any individual leaving the scene. This report records what was found on arrival and what was stated at the time.

6 · Say where it came from

Attach the source, while you say it

Your teacher points at one of the six. You say it as a full sentence, with the source inside it.

Four people were on the corner.
The hour recorded is 23:40.
Nobody is recorded as leaving.
She was frightened by the group.
She addressed the group first.
Someone in the group spoke first.
According to the account, …
The report states …
She claims that … — the report says the opposite.
Both of them …  ·  Neither of them mentions …
As far as I can tell, … — that is my reading, not theirs.
7 · Your briefing

Brief her, and hold it

Your teacher is Claire.
She has read neither account, and she has another call after this one.

What does she need to know, and what does she have to decide?

Open with what each text is, in one sentence.
Give the divergences, and say which text each one comes from.
Say where the two agree — it counts.
Mark the part that is your reading, not the text’s.
Close with what neither text says, and what you would ask for.
According to…  ·  The report states…  ·  She claims…
Both accounts…  ·  Neither of them mentions…
As far as I can tell…  ·  What I would want to ask is…
8 · What just happened

What worked, and once more

Let’s put down what worked and one thing to build on.

Again, for someone who read one of them

Claire has now read the report, but not the account. What goes first?

What changes when she has read one of them
You no longer have to explain the report. You do have to say where the account differs from what she already read — and that is now the whole briefing.
