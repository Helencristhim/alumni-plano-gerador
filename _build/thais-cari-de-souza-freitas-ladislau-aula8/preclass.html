Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A requirement -- something that has to be true before you can go on
"Six months of income is a requirement for that limit."
An exception -- a case where the rule does not apply
"Every exception has a name and a date, or it is not an exception."
To comply -- to do what the rule says
"The team has to comply, and I have to be able to prove it."
A threshold -- the number where the rule changes
"Above the threshold, a human has to look at the case."
An audit -- an official check that the rules were followed
"In an audit, a decision without a written reason does not exist."
A waiver -- official permission to skip a rule once
"Only two people in the bank can sign a waiver, and I am one of them."
Mandatory -- something you have to do, with no choice
"The written reason is mandatory, and it always has been."
Optional -- something you can choose to do or not
"The second review is optional below the threshold."
To enforce -- to make sure a rule is actually followed
"A rule nobody enforces is a guideline with a serious name."
A guideline -- advice on how to apply a rule, not the rule itself
"That line is a guideline. You do not have to follow it, but you should."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A policy is a list of small words that decide what a whole team does. To say something is necessary, English uses have to or must: "A human has to review every case above the threshold." "Every waiver must carry a date." The difference is small and useful: have to points at the rule, and must points at the person speaking. In a document, both work; in conversation, have to is far more common.

Whatever the modal, the verb after it goes bare. Never must to carry, never can to sign, and never an -s on the modal itself: it is he must, not he musts. That is the whole shape, and it never changes with the subject.

Now the part that costs money in your work. Two negatives look similar and mean opposite things. Cannot and must not mean the door is locked: "Nobody can change a limit without a written reason." Do not have to means the door is open and you simply do not need to walk through it: "You do not have to send income proof under two thousand." If you write you must not send income proof when you meant that it is not necessary, you have just forbidden something your policy allows. Two more, and you have the whole document: can for permission ("Only the Director can sign a waiver"), and should for a guideline ("You should read it first, but it is optional").

1. Why is "every waiver must to carry a date" wrong?
A Because must is only used in questions.
B Because the verb after a modal is always bare, with no to in front of it.
C Because carry is an irregular verb.
2. Your policy allows something but does not require it. Which sentence says that?
A "You must not send income proof."
B "You do not have to send income proof."
C "You cannot send income proof."
3. The text says have to and must are not identical. What is the difference?
A Have to points at the rule; must points at the person speaking.
B Have to is for the past and must is for the present.
C Must is only used with he, she and it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Obligation and Permission

GRAMMAR

After every modal the verb goes bare. Must not locks the door; do not have to leaves it open.

MeaningFormExample
It is necessaryhave to / has to + bare verbA human has to review the case.
It is necessary, and I am saying somust + bare verbThe waiver must carry a date.
It is not allowedcannot · must not + bare verbNobody can change a limit without a reason.
It is not necessarydo not have to + bare verbYou do not have to send income proof.
It is allowedcan · may (formal) + bare verbOnly the Director can sign a waiver.
It is a good ideashould + bare verbYou should read the guideline first.
Neverto after a modal · -s on a modalNot must to carry, not he musts, not can to sign.
