Chapter 3: The Code

If, Unless, Provided That

Real offers, hypothetical offers, and conditions

Grammar Discovery

Real or Imagined?

"If you sign a long-term contract, we will keep the price."

"If steel prices fell, we would cancel the surcharge."

"Unless costs come down, we will need a price increase."

"We can offer a discount, provided that the volume goes up."

Sentences 1, 3 and 4 are real offers: they can happen. Sentence 2 imagines a situation that is not true now. In a negotiation, the second type is a soft way to talk about something you do not expect.

UseFormExample
First conditional: a real offerif + present, will / can + verbIf you sign, we will keep the price.
Second conditional: imagined or unlikelyif + past, would / could + verbIf prices fell, we would cancel the surcharge.
Unlessif... notUnless costs come down, we will need an increase.
Provided that / as long asonly if: a strong conditionWe can do it, provided that you pay in thirty days.
Polite pressuresecond conditional for a softer requestWould you accept it if we offered sixty days?
The trapno will after if, unless or provided thatNot “If you will sign...” Say If you sign...

In one line: real = if + present, will; imagined = if + past, would; unless = if not; provided that = only if.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Across the Table

A conversation, three voices and one offer

Dialogue

Anna Wants a Better Price

A
Julius, thank you for the quote. But six percent more is a lot. Our board won’t accept a price increase like that.
J
I understand, Anna. But steel is twenty percent more expensive this year. We can’t absorb the cost alone.
A
What if we increased the volume? We could order fifteen thousand parts instead of ten.
J
That helps. If you made a commitment to fifteen thousand, we would reduce the increase to four percent.
A
Four is still too high. Can we meet halfway at three?
J
Three is possible, provided that you sign a long-term contract for three years.
A
And the payment terms? We need ninety days. Sixty is a deal-breaker for us.
J
We can accept ninety days, as long as there’s a surcharge clause. Unless steel prices fall, we’ll review the price in six months.
Comprehension

About Anna

1. What does Anna say about the six percent increase?
It is a lot, and her board won’t accept it.
2. What does Anna offer to increase?
The volume: fifteen thousand parts instead of ten.
3. What is a deal-breaker for Anna?
Payment terms of sixty days; she needs ninety.
Listening 1 · An American Purchasing Manager

What a Buyer Really Wants

An American purchasing manager explains how she reacts when a supplier asks for a price increase. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she always ask a supplier who wants a higher price?
To show the numbers: which costs went up, and by how much.
2. What happens if the supplier shows the numbers?
She usually accepts part of the increase.
3. What would she do if a supplier surprised her with an increase?
She would start looking for another supplier.
Listening 2 · A French Negotiation Trainer

Never Give Without Getting

A French trainer explains the one rule he teaches in every negotiation course. Sound first — no text.

1. What is his one rule?
Never give something without getting something back.
2. What should you say instead of simply accepting a discount?
Yes, provided that... / We can do that if you...
3. Why does he like the word unless?
Because it sets a limit politely.
Listening 3 · An Italian Sales Director

The Coffee After the Meeting

An Italian sales director remembers a difficult price negotiation with a large customer in Germany. Sound first — no text.

1. Why was the negotiation difficult?
The price of aluminum had gone up, and the customer refused any increase.
2. Where did they finally find a solution?
Over a coffee after the meeting.
3. What was the final agreement?
Half of the increase, provided that the price was reviewed every six months.
