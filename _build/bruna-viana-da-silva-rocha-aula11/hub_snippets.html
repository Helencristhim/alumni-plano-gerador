Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To take up -- to start a new hobby or activity
"I took up pottery last year, and now my whole kitchen is full of bowls."
To give up -- to stop doing something you used to do, for good
"I gave up coffee for a month, and nobody in my house enjoyed it."
A habit -- something you do so often that you stop deciding to do it
"Checking my phone in bed is a habit I am trying to break."
Lately -- in the last few weeks, up to now
"Lately I have been cooking at home almost every night."
To be hooked on -- to enjoy something so much that you cannot stop
"I am hooked on a podcast about people who quit everything to travel."
To look forward to -- to feel happy about something that is coming
"I am really looking forward to seeing the mountains again."
To put off -- to decide to do something later, again and again
"I keep putting off booking the trip, and the prices keep going up."
Can't stand -- to hate something, strongly and honestly
"I can't stand waiting in line for a table."
To keep up with -- to stay at the same speed as something that keeps moving
"I can't keep up with all the shows my friends recommend."
To make time for -- to find space in a busy week for something that matters
"I make time for a long walk every Sunday, no matter what."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When an American friend asks what are you doing these days?, the answer is almost always two verbs in a row. "I enjoy cooking on Sundays." "I decided to take a real vacation." The first verb is easy. The second one is where everybody hesitates, because it can end in -ing or start with to.

Here is the secret: the first verb decides. Verbs about something you already live, like or hate — enjoy, keep, finish, mind, avoid, can't stand — take -ing. "I can't stand waiting in line." "I keep putting it off." Verbs about a plan or a decision — want, decide, plan, hope, need — look forward in time and take to. "I am planning to drive to Alaska again."

A few verbs accept both with the same meaning: like, love, hate, start, begin. "I started running" and "I started to run" are both fine. And after a preposition, it is always -ing — which is why the to in look forward to is a trap: "I am looking forward to seeing you," never to see you.

One verb changes its meaning. "I stopped checking my phone in bed" means you do not do it anymore. "I stopped to check my phone" means you paused what you were doing, in order to check it.

1. Which sentence is correct?
A I enjoy cooking for my friends.
B I enjoy to cook for my friends on Sundays.
C I enjoy cook for my friends on Sundays.
2. What decides whether the second verb takes -ing or to?
A The tense of the sentence, past or present.
B The first verb.
C Whether the sentence is formal or informal.
3. "I stopped checking my phone in bed." What does it mean?
A I paused something else so that I could check my phone.
B I check my phone in bed only on weekends now.
C I do not check it in bed anymore.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Verb + -ing or Verb + to

GRAMMAR

The first verb decides. Plans take to, experiences take -ing, and after a preposition it is always -ing.

JobFormExample
Something you live, like or hateenjoy · mind · avoid · keep · finish · miss · can't stand + -ingI enjoy cooking for my friends.
A plan or a decisionwant · decide · plan · hope · need · learn · would like + toI decided to take a real vacation.
Both work, same meaninglike · love · hate · start · begin + -ing or toI started running / I started to run.
After a prepositionlook forward to · think about · be good at + -ingI am looking forward to seeing you.
Stop changes the meaningstop + -ing = not anymore · stop + to = pause in order toI stopped checking my phone. / I stopped to check my phone.

Never: I enjoy to cook · I decided taking a break · I am looking forward to see you · I want start running · I can't stand to wait in line

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

An old friend from a trip calls you after a year. Put the conversation in a logical order.

?"Pottery! Are you still running in the mornings?"
?"I know that feeling. You have to make time for it, or it just does not happen."
?"It has been a whole year. So, what are you doing these days?"
?"I have been trying to learn Spanish lately, but I keep putting off the lessons."
?"Honestly? I took up pottery. I am terrible at it, and I love doing it anyway."
?"No, I gave up running in March. What about you?"

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen, then record yourself. In natural speech the to melts into the verb before it -- want to sounds like wanna, got to like gotta.

I gave up running, and I do not miss it at all.
I keep putting it off, and I need to stop.
I can't stand waiting, so I decided to book it today.
