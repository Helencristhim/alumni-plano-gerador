Reading the Program -- The Show Before It Starts

The playbill, the ticket, the break and the end of the night. Key words: a playbill, an usher, the mezzanine, an intermission, an understudy, a matinee, a curtain call, a standing ovation, a revival, sold out, to steal the show, break a leg. Structure: countable and uncountable nouns with many, much, a few and a little.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A playbill -- the small booklet you get at the door of a theater, with the cast and the show
"Read the playbill before the lights go down, not after."
An usher -- the person at the theater door who checks your ticket and shows you your seat
"The usher walked us to row C and gave us two playbills."
The mezzanine -- the first balcony in a theater, above the floor seats
"The mezzanine is a little cheaper, and you can see the whole stage."
An intermission -- the short break in the middle of a show
"There is one intermission of fifteen minutes, so order your drink early."
An understudy -- an actor who learns a role and plays it when the usual actor is out
"Tonight the understudy is playing the lead, and she is wonderful."
A matinee -- a performance in the afternoon, not in the evening
"We always go to the Wednesday matinee, because there are fewer tourists."
A curtain call -- the moment at the end when the actors come back to the stage to take a bow
"Do not leave during the curtain call. The actors are still on stage."
A standing ovation -- when the whole audience stands up and applauds at the end
"The understudy got a standing ovation on a Wednesday afternoon."
A revival -- a new production of an old show that people loved years ago
"It is a revival of a musical my mother saw in the nineteen eighties."
Sold out -- with no tickets left: every seat is already taken
"The Saturday show was sold out, so we bought tickets for the matinee."
To steal the show -- to be the best and most memorable person in a performance or event
"The little girl in the second act stole the show."
Break a leg -- what you say to an actor before a show: good luck
"Break a leg tonight! We will be in the mezzanine."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Our neighbors invited us to a show, and the first problem was the tickets. The Saturday evening performance was sold out, so I called the box office and asked how many seats were left for the Wednesday matinee. The man said there were only a few, all in the mezzanine, and that there was not much time to decide.

On Wednesday, an usher took our tickets and gave us a playbill. Inside it there was a little white slip: the lead actress was out, and an understudy was playing the part. I did not have much information about her, and I was a bit worried.

I should not have worried. The show was a revival of an old musical, and she stole the show. The intermission was short, so we had very little time for a drink. At the curtain call, a lot of people stood up, and in a few seconds it was a standing ovation.

1. "How many seats were left?" Why is it many and not much here?
A Because seats can be counted, and many goes with nouns you can count.
B Because the question is about the past, and much is only used in the present tense.
C Because many is more polite than much when you talk to someone at the box office.
2. "There was a little white slip" and "we had very little time." Do they mean the same?
A Yes. Both mean that there was a small quantity, and both are good news for the writer.
B No. In the first, little is the size of the slip; in the second, very little time means almost no time.
C No. The first is about time and the second is about the size of the paper.
3. "I did not have much information about her." Which sentence is also correct?
A I did not have many informations about her.
B I did not have an information about her before the show started.
C I did not have a lot of information about her.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Countable and Uncountable Nouns

GRAMMAR

First ask: can I count it? The answer chooses the word that comes before the noun.

FormUse and meaningExample
how many / many + plural nounnouns you can count (seats, tickets, people)How many tickets do you need?
how much / much + nounnouns you cannot count (time, money, information)There is not much time.
a few / a littlesome; positive, there is somethinga few seats · a little time
few / littlealmost none; negativevery few tickets left
a lot of / plenty ofa big quantity, with BOTH kinds of nouna lot of people · a lot of money
some / anysome in positive sentences and offers, any in negatives and questionsDo you have any information?

Never: How much tickets · a lot of peoples · an information · a few time.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

You call the box office for you, Glauber and the twins. You say:
A Hello. How much tickets do you have for the Saturday show, please? We are four people.
B Hello. Do you have four tickets for the Saturday show?
C Hello. I want four tickets. How many it costs for the Saturday show?
The man says there are some seats left, but only upstairs. He says:
A There are only a few seats left, in the mezzanine.
B There are only a little seats left, all of them upstairs in the mezzanine.
C There is only few seat left in the mezzanine.
A neighbor is in the show tonight. Before it starts, you text her:
A Good luck tonight! I hope that you do not break a leg on the stage.
B Steal the show tonight, and please break the leg!
C Break a leg tonight! We will be in the mezzanine.
The show ends and the actors come back to the stage. Your daughter stands up to leave. You say:
A Wait, it is the curtain call. The actors are still on stage.
B Wait, it is the intermission. We still have much minutes.
C Wait, it is the standing ovation, so everybody must sit down now and stay quiet.
