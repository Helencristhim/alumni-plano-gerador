Chapter 3: What You Stopped Doing

Two Forms for a Life You Left

One of them works everywhere. The other one has a limit.

Grammar Discovery

Which One Happened Only Once?

"I used to sign every contract myself."

"The company used to be six people in one room."

"Every Friday I would sit with the engineers until the build passed."

"In 2019 we hired our first commercial director."

The orange one happened once, on a date, so it takes the plain past. The other three repeated for years. Now swap used to and would around: the red ones accept both, and the green one accepts only one of the two.

JobFormExample
A habit you had and stoppedused to + bare verbI used to sign every contract myself.
A situation that is no longer trueused to only — would does not workThe company used to be six people. Not would be.
A repeated action you rememberwould + bare verb, with a time markerEvery Friday I would sit with the engineers.
One event, on one dateplain past simpleIn 2019 we hired our first commercial director.
Negative and questiondid not use to / did you use toNot did not used to. Say I did not use to delegate.
The trapbe used to + -ing is a different structureI am used to working alone = normal for me now. I used to work alone = not any more.
The permanent targetafter used to and would, no endingNot he used to signs. Say he used to sign.

In one line: used to works for everything you left behind. Would only works for things you did.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Advisor Who Sold His Own

One conversation, two recordings and one page of a real document

Dialogue

What You Did, and What You Stopped

D
Before we talk about buyers, I want to talk about you. When you started, what did a Monday look like?
C
Long. We bootstrapped the whole thing, so there was never anybody else to do the work. I used to write the proposals, sign the contracts and run the interviews, all in the same week.
D
That was me as well. I used to be the only salesperson in my company for four years. Every Friday I would drive two hours to a client and come back the same night.
C
When did that stop for you?
D
When I made a hire I did not want to make. A commercial director, in 2018. I used to think nobody could sell what I had built, and I was wrong within two months. I was also about two months from burnout and I did not know the word yet.
C
I have the same blind spot. I still read every proposal.
D
Then here is the question a buyer will ask, and it is not about proposals. Who runs this company on the Monday after you step back?
C
Honestly, three people each own a piece of it. There is no single successor.
D
Say that out loud in the room. It is a better answer than an invented name. But say the rest of it too: what you used to do and what you no longer do. That is the only proof that the company has outgrown you, which is the compliment nobody enjoys hearing.
C
I used to approve every hire. I stopped last year. And I did not use to delegate anything on the technical side, and now I delegate all of it.
D
That is the sentence. Keep it in exactly that shape, and put a date on each half.
Comprehension

About Duncan

1. What did Duncan use to do in his own company, and for how long?
He used to be the only salesperson for four years, and every Friday he would drive two hours to a client and come back the same night.
2. What changed it for him, and what had he been wrong about?
A commercial director he did not want to hire, in 2018. He used to think nobody could sell what he had built, and he was wrong within two months.
3. What question does he say a buyer will ask, and what does he tell Caio to add to the answer?
Who runs the company on the Monday after you step back. He says to give the honest answer and then add what you used to do and no longer do, with a date on each half.
Listening 1 · An Executive Coach

The Sentence Buyers Are Listening For

A coach who prepares founders for the first diligence meeting. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say founders do wrong when they describe their own company?
They describe everything they still do, because that is what they are proud of — and it tells the buyer the company cannot run without them.
2. What does she ask her clients to write, and how long does she give them?
Two lists in twenty minutes: what they did every week five years ago, and what they do every week now.
3. What does she say about founders who cannot fill the first list?
That there is always something on it — the problem is never that nothing changed, it is that nobody ever asked them to say it out loud.
Listening 2 · A Buyer

What I Check Before I Buy

Somebody who has bought eleven founder-led companies. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say he asks the founder, and what does he ask everybody else?
He asks the founder what he stopped doing, and then he asks four other people in the company the same question and compares the answers.
2. What worries him more than a founder who still does everything?
A founder who says he delegated something that the rest of the company still brings back to him.
3. What does he say about the founder who admits there is no single successor?
That it is the answer he trusts most, because inventing a name is easy and the invented name never survives the second meeting.

Proposals and the eight largest client relationships, because they are the only two still in the founder's name and they are the two that would leave with him.
