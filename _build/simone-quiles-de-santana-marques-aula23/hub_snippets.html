Regulatory Language -- Answering the Regulator

Write and speak to an authority in its own register: state the rule, state the fact, and evidence every line. New vocabulary: regulator, oversight, scrutiny, disclosure, sanction, whistleblower, filing, safeguard, code of conduct, audit trail. Grammar: the passive of obligation -- be required to and be permitted to with the base verb, and be prohibited from with the -ing form.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Regulator -- the public body that supervises an industry and enforces its rules
"A letter came in from the regulator this morning."
Oversight -- the continuous supervision of what a company does
"The audit trail is what oversight actually wants to see."
Scrutiny -- close, critical examination by an authority
"We are under scrutiny on two points this quarter."
Disclosure -- the act of making information public because the rules demand it
"A disclosure that cannot be evidenced is worse than none."
Sanction -- a punishment a regulator applies when a rule is broken
"If we get it wrong, the sanction is a fine."
Whistleblower -- an employee who reports wrongdoing inside their own company
"Someone used the whistleblower channel in March."
Filing -- a document a company sends to an authority by a fixed deadline
"The quarterly filing went out on day thirty-four."
Safeguard -- a measure put in place to prevent harm or to protect people and data
"We explain the safeguard we put in place so it cannot happen again."
Code of conduct -- the written rules of behavior a company gives its own staff
"Read the code of conduct before you write anything."
Audit trail -- the record that shows who did what, and when, step by step
"Every step of the review is logged, with a name and a date."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

A letter arrives from the regulator, and the company is under scrutiny on two points. The first is a late filing: the company is required to send the quarterly report within thirty days, and it went out on day thirty-four. Simone writes it in one sentence, and then describes the safeguard now in place, because arguing about four days in front of oversight is how a small problem becomes a large one. The second point is heavier. Somebody used the whistleblower channel in March, and the regulator wants the steps that followed, with dates. Two rules govern the week. The company is permitted to request one extension, but only in writing and only before the deadline. And all staff are prohibited from contacting the person who raised the concern. What saves the response is the audit trail: every line is provable, with a name and a date, because a disclosure that cannot be evidenced is worse than no disclosure at all. Simone reads the code of conduct before she drafts, so the answer uses the same language the company uses about itself. Done well, the worst outcome is a warning. Done badly, the sanction is a fine.

1. Why does Simone refuse to argue about the four days?
A Because arguing in front of oversight turns a small problem into a large one.
B Because the filing was actually on time.
C Because the regulator did not ask about it.
2. What exactly is the company permitted to do about the deadline?
A Take as much extra time as it needs.
B Request one extension, in writing, before the deadline.
C Ask the whistleblower for more time.
3. Why is the audit trail what saves the response?
A Because it replaces the quarterly filing.
B Because every line becomes provable, with a name and a date.
C Because it removes the need for a code of conduct.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Passive of Obligation

GRAMMAR

A regulator does not write "you must" and "you must not". It writes is required to, is permitted to and is prohibited from, and the company answers in the same words. The passive leaves out who requires it, because the rule stands on its own and not on a person. Watch the shape of each one: required to and permitted to take the base verb; prohibited from takes the -ing form. And "not required to" is not a prohibition: it only means there is no obligation.

FormUseExample
be required to + base verban obligation the rules impose"The company is required to file within thirty days."
be permitted to + base verba permission, usually with a limit"We are permitted to request one extension."
be prohibited from + -inga hard ban, with no exceptions"Staff are prohibited from contacting that person."
must be + past participlethe rule in its shortest passive form"The report must be signed by a director."
be not required to + base verbno obligation -- and not a ban"We are not required to disclose the name."

Key point: writing "we are not required to" where the rule says "we are prohibited from" is not a language mistake. It is a compliance one.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best sentence for each situation.

The regulator asks why the report arrived on day thirty-four. Which opening is best?
A "The company is required to file within thirty days. The report was filed on day thirty-four."
B "I think four days is not a serious delay."
C "The company is required to filing within thirty days."
A manager asks for the name of the person who raised the concern. Which answer is best?
A "We are not required to give the name, so I would prefer not to."
B "Staff are prohibited from contacting that person while the request is open."
C "Staff are prohibited to contact that person."
The deadline is tight and you need more time. Which sentence states the rule correctly?
A "We are permitted requesting an extension whenever we want."
B "We are permitted to request one extension, in writing, before the deadline."
C "We are prohibited from requesting an extension in writing."
