Lesson 08 -- Pre-class

Who, What, Where, When, How -- Asking the Right Questions

In the first seven lessons you learned to answer. In this lesson you learn to ask — and whoever asks runs the conversation. November is here: the Miami trip Sofia announced in lesson 6 happens in this lesson, and you go alone. Six airport words (gate, flight, departure, arrival, reservation, spell) and the five little words that open any door — who, what, where, when, how — with one rule only: after the question word comes the helper (is or does), never the subject. This Pre-class is only a first contact: come to class with “Where is the gate?” in your ear, not on the tip of your tongue.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Six words — and you have read all of them on the screens at the airport, without ever saying one of them out loud. Listen to each one and repeat it, alone. Two sound traps: flight is said FLAIT (the GH is silent, like night and light) and reservation is said re-zer-VEY-shon — every English -tion is said -shon, and that single rule gives you hundreds of words for free.

Gate -- the door at the airport where you board
"Where is the gate?" It is the door you board the plane through, and it always has a letter and a number: gate B7.
Flight -- a trip in a plane
"My flight is on Monday." Watch the sound: FLAIT. The GH is never pronounced.
Departure -- the moment a plane leaves
"The departure is at ten in the morning." On the airport screens, it is the column of the flights that leave.
Arrival -- the moment a plane lands
"The arrival in Miami is at six." It is the pair of departure: on the screens, they are the two columns.
Reservation -- a place kept for you, booked before
"I have a reservation. My name is Paula Morales." Say this sentence BEFORE they ask you anything — it settles the whole desk.
Spell -- to say a word letter by letter
"How do you spell your name?" Every desk in the world will ask for your last name: M — O — R — A — L — E — S. Slowly. Nobody finds that strange.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions. The words in bold are the grammar of this lesson — look at what comes right after each where, when, who, what and how.

November. The Zurich travel desk sends Paula the page for her Miami trip — and it is written as a list of questions.

"When is the flight? Your flight is on Monday. The departure is at ten in the morning, and the arrival in Miami is at six in the evening. Where is the gate? We don't know — the gate is on the screen at the airport, and it changes."

"Where do you sleep? You have a reservation at the hotel, for three nights. Who is your contact in Miami? Sofia. And one more: how do you spell your last name? They are going to ask you this at the hotel. The answer is M-O-R-A-L-E-S."

Paula reads it twice and thinks: where is the gate, when does the flight arrive? (Notice one thing: in English, after the question word comes the helper, always. Where is the gate? — never Where the gate is? That single turn is the whole lesson.)

1. Why does it say "Where is the gate?" and not "Where the gate is?"
A Because it is an important question.
B Because in English the helper (is) comes right after the question word — it jumps in front of the subject.
C Because gate is a short word.
2. Why does the page not say which gate it is?
A Because Paula forgot to ask.
B Because nobody knows yet — the gate goes on the airport screen, and it changes.
C Because the flight is only in December.
3. It says "Who is your contact?" and "how do you spell?". Why two different helpers?
A Because the first uses the verb to be (it asks for is) and the second has an ordinary verb, spell (it asks for do).
B Because one is about a person and the other is about a thing.
C Because is and do are the same — it makes no difference.
4. What will they ask Paula at the hotel?
A The flight number and the gate.
B To spell her last name — "how do you spell your last name?"
C To speak to Sofia first.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Question words: question + helper (is / does)

GRAMMAR

Five words open any question in English: who, what, where, when, how. And there is ONE rule for all of them — the same at the office and at the airport.

Question wordWhen you use itEnglish (question + helper + rest)
WhoquemWho is your manager?
Whatasks about a thingWhat is your flight number? · What do you do?
Whereasks about a placeWhere is the gate? · Where do you work?
Whenasks about a timeWhen is the meeting? · When does the flight arrive?
HowcomoHow do you spell your name?
What timeque horasWhat time is the departure?

The rule, in four words: question + helper + rest. And the helper has only two options. If the sentence already has the verb to be (“the gate is there”), the helper is is/are itself: Where is the gate? If the verb is an ordinary one (“the flight arrives”), the one that steps in is do/does: When does the flight arrive?

The most common mistake here: saying “Where the gate is?”. In your first language a question keeps the SAME order as a statement, and only the voice rises at the end. In English the question turns the sentence around: the helper jumps in front of the subject. It is not carelessness — it is your first language at work. Knowing where the mistake comes from is half of fixing it.

Only one S per question. When does comes in, the verb loses its S: “the flight arrives” becomes “does the flight arrive?”. Never “does the flight arrives?” — the S is already in does, and it does not show up twice. If you make that mistake, celebrate: it only happens to people who have already learned does.

Why this matters: whoever answers depends on whoever asks. In the first seven lessons you answered — and someone who only answers is left waiting. These five words take you out of the waiting: in an airport, “Where is the gate?” is four words, and somebody points. You do not need a beautiful sentence. You need a question.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen to each question, then record your voice. Nobody is grading you — it is only you and your microphone. Careful with two of them: flight is FLAIT and reservation is re-zer-VEY-shon. And listen to the melody: in a question, the voice rises at the end only when the answer is yes or no. With where, when and how, the voice falls at the end.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best option for each real situation — Miami airport, the hotel, the new office.

You are at the airport and you do not know where to go. You ask:
A "Where the gate is?"
B "Excuse me, where is the gate?"
C "The gate is where?"
You want to know what time the flight arrives in Miami. You ask:
A "When the flight arrives?"
B "When does the flight arrives?"
C "When does the flight arrive?"
The receptionist says a name and you do not understand it. You say:
A "Sorry, how do you spell that?"
B "Yes, yes." (pretending you understood)
C "How you spell that?"
You get to the hotel at eleven at night, tired. The first thing you say is:
A "Hello. Room, please."
B "Hi. I have a reservation. My name is Paula Morales."
C "I have a reservation? Maybe? Sorry, my English..."
First day at the Miami office. You want to know who runs the commercial team. You ask:
A "Who your manager is?"
B "Who is the manager here?"
C "The manager is who?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record your answer. Speak for 60 seconds, with no script. Do not redo it more than twice. This is exactly the exercise you will do in class — and the homework audio afterwards.

Imagine you have just landed alone at an international airport, at six in the evening, and nobody is going to translate anything for you. Ask six questions out loud: where the gate is, what time the departure of the return flight is, when the flight arrives, how to get to the hotel, and two more of your own. Use at least four different question words. For example: "Where is the gate? What time is the departure? How do I get to the hotel?" It does not have to be perfect — if you get the order wrong and say "Where the gate is?", keep going: anybody in that airport would understand and point. If you get stuck, use the rescue kit from lesson 1.
