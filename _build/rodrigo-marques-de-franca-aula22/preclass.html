Lesson 22 -- Pre-class

A Very British Email -- Register and Tact in Writing

How to read the polite code of a British business email, and how to write firmly without blaming anyone. Key words: to chase someone, a gentle reminder, at your earliest convenience, to bear something in mind, an oversight, regrettably, curt, tactful, to come across as, to tone down, with all due respect, to err on the side of caution. Structure: nominalization -- there has been a delay, upon receipt of, following our discussion, we would be grateful for + noun.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To chase someone -- to remind someone, more than once, to do something they have not done yet
"I had to chase them twice for the signed copy."
A gentle reminder -- a polite message about something that should already have been done
"Just a gentle reminder that the invoice is now due."
At your earliest convenience -- as soon as you can; a formal phrase that often means now
"Please send the documents at your earliest convenience."
To bear something in mind -- to remember a fact and think about it when you decide something
"Please bear in mind that the office closes at noon on Friday."
An oversight -- a mistake made because you did not notice something
"The missing page was an oversight on our part."
Regrettably -- a formal word for unfortunately, often used just before bad news
"Regrettably, we cannot accept the new date."
Curt -- so short and direct that it sounds rude
"His reply was only two words, and it felt curt."
Tactful -- careful not to upset or embarrass other people
"She found a tactful way to say no."
To come across as -- to give a particular impression to other people
"A one-line email can come across as rude."
To tone down -- to make something less strong, direct or extreme
"Could you tone down the second paragraph?"
With all due respect -- a polite phrase used just before you disagree with someone
"With all due respect, that is not what the contract says."
To err on the side of caution -- to choose the safer option when you are not sure
"When in doubt, err on the side of caution and add a greeting."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

On Monday morning, Rodrigo found an email from Harriet Lowe, a partner at a London firm. It began politely: "Following our call last week, many thanks for the revised draft." Then came a few small points to bear in mind. Rodrigo had learned to read this code. A few small points meant a new draft, and a gentle reminder about the board minutes meant that Harriet was annoyed.

His first reply was three lines long and, he realized, quite curt. So he decided to tone it down. Instead of "We forgot Schedule 3," he wrote, "There has been an oversight on our side: Schedule 3 will follow upon receipt of the final figures." Instead of "Sign it by Friday," he wrote, "We would be grateful for your confirmation by Friday."

The facts were the same, and so was the deadline. But the tone was tactful, nobody was blamed, and Harriet replied within the hour.

1. What did a few small points really mean?
A Harriet was happy with the draft and only wanted to correct two or three spelling mistakes before signing it.
B Harriet wanted a new draft.
C Harriet had no comments.
2. Why did Rodrigo change his first reply?
A It was too long.
B His boss told him that every email to London had to be checked by a senior partner first.
C It was so short that it sounded curt.
3. "There has been an oversight on our side." What does the noun oversight do in this sentence?
A It admits the mistake without naming a person.
B It names the junior lawyer who forgot the schedule, so the London firm knows exactly who to blame.
C It makes the sentence longer.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Nominalization in Formal Emails

GRAMMAR

Turn the action into a noun, and the person leaves the sentence. The fact and the deadline stay.

JobFormExample
Verb to noundeliver → delivery · receive → receiptthe delivery of the documents
AffirmativeThere has been + a / an + nounThere has been a delay in payment.
NegativeThere has been no + nounThere has been no reply to our letter.
QuestionHas there been any + noun?Has there been any progress on the minutes?
Formal linkersfollowing / upon / pending + nounUpon receipt of payment, we will sign.
A firm requestWe would be grateful for + nounWe would be grateful for your comments by Friday.
Who or whatnoun + of + objectyour confirmation of the date

Never: following we discussed · upon receive · there has been a delayed · grateful by your comments

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You missed a deadline and want to apologize to a London client without sounding dramatic. You write:
A "I am terribly, terribly sorry. It was completely my fault, and it will never, ever happen again, I promise."
B "Apologies for the delay on our side. The documents are attached."
C "Sorry. Late."
A British partner writes, "With all due respect, I'm not sure that works." What does she mean?
A She disagrees, quite strongly.
B She respects your work very much and is only asking for a little more time to think about it.
C She agrees.
You need to chase a client about an unpaid invoice for the first time. You write:
A "Pay now."
B "We have noticed that you have not paid, which is very disappointing, and we will take legal action tomorrow."
C "Just a gentle reminder that the May invoice is now due."
A friend's complaint email to a hotel is very angry. You suggest:
A "Send it as it is, because hotels only answer customers who shout, and a polite email is simply ignored."
B "Tone it down: say what happened and ask for a refund by a date."
C "Don't send anything."
