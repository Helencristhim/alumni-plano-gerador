Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

The Person Who Handles the Accounts -- Who Is Who

Relative clauses, for describing people precisely in a company of four countries. Key expressions: counterpart, point of contact, handover, backup, org chart, remit, to liaise with, direct report, dotted line, to cover for, onboarding, workstream. Structure: who, which, that, where and whose -- and the commas that change the meaning.

Counterpart -- the person who has the same job as you in another company or country
"My counterpart in Santiago is the person who handles the mining accounts."
Point of contact -- the one person somebody should call about a subject
"She has been the point of contact for that client for six years."
Handover -- the process of passing your work to somebody else
"A proper handover matters more than any system."
Backup -- the second person who can do the work if the first is not available
"My backup is the colleague who covers for me when I am travelling."
Org chart -- a diagram showing who reports to whom in a company
"The org chart is accurate and almost useless."
Remit -- the area of work that somebody is responsible for
"Pricing is not in my remit, but I know who decides it."
To liaise with -- to work with somebody and keep them informed
"I liaise with the operations team, but they do not report to me."
Direct report -- somebody whose manager you are
"She has four direct reports and two dotted lines."
Dotted line -- a reporting relationship that is not the main one
"I have a dotted line to the regional office and a solid one to Brazil."
To cover for -- to do somebody's work while they are away
"Ana covers for me whenever I am travelling."
Onboarding -- the process of introducing a new person to a company
"Onboarding gives you the org chart and none of the real map."
Workstream -- one defined part of a big project, with its own people and goals
"The pricing workstream is the one that decides everything else."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every large company hands a new employee an org chart, which is a diagram that shows who reports to whom. It is accurate, it is almost useless, and everybody pretends otherwise for about a year. Onboarding gives you that chart and none of the map you actually need.

The real map is a different list. It contains the colleague who answers an email in twenty minutes, the analyst who knows why the system does something strange, and the two people whose approval unblocks everything. It contains your backup, the person who covers for you when you are away, and your counterpart in each of the other three countries.

It also contains one name per client, and this is the part new people get wrong. The name on the contract belongs to the person whose signature the company needs. The name you actually want is the analyst who runs the account day to day, and the two are almost never the same person. That second name is your real point of contact.

None of this is published, and it is fragile. A colleague who has been the contact for six years retires, and a whole workstream discovers that nothing she knew was written down. That is why a proper handover matters more than any system: because the office where the team sits, the remit each person has and the dotted line that connects them are all easy to draw, and the thing that actually keeps a client is a list somebody kept by hand.

1. Why does the text say the org chart is "accurate and almost useless"?
A Because it is often out of date.
B Because it shows who reports to whom, which is not the information you need.
C Because new employees never read it.
2. "The analyst who runs the account day to day." Why is there no comma before who?
A Because the clause says which analyst -- remove it and you do not know who is meant.
B Because who never takes a comma.
C Because the sentence is short.
3. What is the difference between the name on the contract and the real point of contact?
A There is none -- they are the same person.
B The contract name signs; the point of contact is the one who runs the account day to day.
C The contract name answers faster.
4. What does the text say a proper handover protects?
A The org chart.
B The reporting lines.
C The informal list that keeps a client, which nobody wrote down.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

How to put two sentences about the same person into one. In a company with four countries and ninety colleagues, this is the difference between sounding organized and sounding lost.

FormWhat it saysExample
who · thatA person. Both are correct in everyday speech."the colleague who covers for me"
which · thatA thing. That is more common in speech, which more formal in writing."the route that connects the two capitals"
where · whosewhere for a place; whose for something that belongs to the person or thing."the office where the team is based"
"the client whose contract expires in March"
Defining: no commasThe clause says which one. Remove it and the sentence stops making sense."The colleague who covers for me is called Ana."
Non-defining: two commasExtra information about somebody already identified. Remove it and the sentence still works. Never that here."Ana, who joined in March, is my backup."

The commas carry meaning. The clients who pay late get a reminder means only some of them. The clients, who pay late, get a reminder means all of them pay late. And the relative word replaces the subject: never say the colleague who she covers for me.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are describing your team and your clients to a colleague who started on Monday. Choose the best thing to say.

1. He asks who does your work while you are away. You say:
A The colleague who she covers for me is called Ana.
B The colleague who covers for me when I travel is called Ana.
C The colleague which covers for me is called Ana.
2. You want to add extra information about Marcos, who he already knows by name. You say:
A Marcos that joined last year handles mining.
B Marcos, that joined last year, handles mining.
C Marcos, who joined the Santiago office last year, handles mining.
3. You want to identify a client by his contract date. You say:
A The client whose contract expires in March called me.
B The client who his contract expires in March called me.
C The client which contract expires in March called me.
4. You are talking about a route, not a person. You say:
A This is the route who connects the two capitals.
B This is the route that connects the two capitals.
C This is the route whose connects the two capitals.
5. You want to say where the regional team sits. You say:
A The office which the regional team is based opened in September.
B The office where the regional team is based opened in September.
C The office who the regional team is based opened in September.
