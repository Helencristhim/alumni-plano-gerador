Why One Conditional Is Not Enough

A Decision Has Two Lives

Past to past

"If I had studied, I would have passed." You already know this one.

Past to now

"If I had joined the club, I would be building things every weekend."

Always to past

"If I were less impatient, I would not have decided in ten seconds."

A bad decision does not stay in the past. It follows you into today, and the reason you made it was already in you before the day arrived. English has one shape for each of those, and today you get both.

Chapter 3: The Two Shapes

One Decision, Two Directions

Past into today, and always into the past

Grammar Discovery

Four Sentences, Two Time Zones

If I had joined the club, I would have learned a lot that year.

If I were braver, I would ask them today.

If I had joined the club, I would be building things every weekend.

If I were less impulsive, I would not have decided in ten seconds.

The first two you already know: both halves live in the same time. In the last two, the halves live in different times. Which half is the past in each one, and which half is now?

ShapeThe if halfThe result half
Third conditional (known)If I had joinedI would have learned — past condition, past result
Second conditional (known)If I were braverI would ask — unreal now, unreal now
Mixed 1: past → nowIf + had + past participlewould + bare verb. No "have", no "been". The decision is old; the result is today.
Mixed 2: always → pastIf + past simple (were / had / knew)would have + past participle. Who you are is permanent; the consequence sits in the past.
How to chooseAsk when twice, once for each half. Old cause + today's result = mixed 1. Permanent cause + old result = mixed 2.
The trapNever If I had joined, I would have be building. One "have" per sentence, and it goes on the side that is in the past.
Practice

Choose the Half

Say it first, then click to check

"If I ___had joined the robotics club, I would be building things every weekend." (join — the old condition)
"If she had stayed in the city, she ___would be a different person today." (be — the result is now)
"If I ___were less impulsive, I would not have decided in ten seconds." (be — permanently true about me)
"If he were the kind of person who asks for help, he ___would have asked in episode two." (ask — the result sits in the past)
"If they had not sent that message, nothing ___would be irreversible now." (be — the situation today)
"If I ___knew how much was at stake, I would have weighed it up properly." (know — permanent, not a single moment)
The Tool

Ask When Twice

Question 1 · the cause

Is the cause a moment in the past, or something permanently true about the person?

A moment: if + had + past participle. A permanent thing: if + past simple.

Question 2 · the result

Is the result a fact about today, or something that already happened?

Today: would + bare verb. Already happened: would have + past participle.

The check

Count the word "have". There is exactly one, and it sits on the side that is in the past.

Two "have" means you mixed the shapes. Zero means the sentence never left today.

This is why the two shapes exist: the choice happened once, and the person who made it did not.

The Club He Did Not Join

N
They opened the robotics club again. You are going this time, right?
M
I do not know. Two years ago it was on the same afternoon as football, and I chose football.
N
Everyone chose football. Nobody wanted to be the only one in a lab.
M
Exactly. And if I had joined the club then, I would be building things every weekend now instead of watching other people do it.
N
So you regret playing football. That is a trade-off, not a disaster.
M
I do not regret football. I regret the reason. If I were less afraid of being the odd one out, I would not have decided it in ten seconds.
N
Did anybody try to talk you out of it?
M
My brother did. He asked whether I could do both, and I never even checked. So I paid for it in the long run, and the thing at stake was smaller than I thought.
N
Then this is your second chance at the same choice. That is the only kind that counts.

Chapter 5: Practice

Building Both Halves

Spot the Error

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "If I had joined the club, I would have be building things every weekend."
"...I would be building things every weekend." (the result is TODAY, so no "have": one "have" per sentence, on the past side)
2. "If I were less impulsive, I would not decide it in ten seconds."
"...I would not have decided it in ten seconds." (the result already happened, so "would have" plus past participle)
3. "If I would have known how much was at stake, I would have weighed it up."
"If I had known how much was at stake..." (English never puts "would" inside the if half — Portuguese does, and that is where this comes from)
4. "I pretend to join the robotics club this year."
"I intend to join the robotics club this year." (in English "pretend" means to act as if something false were true)
The Real Thing

The Decision Sheet

Decision sheet — one choice, both shapes
Mark · robotics club or football, two years ago
same choice
two angles
What happenedBoth activities were on the same afternoon, and I had a week to choose. I chose football in about ten seconds.
At stakeTwo years of building things, against one afternoon a week with my friends. The trade-off was real, and smaller than I thought.
Past → nowIf I had joined the club, I would be building things every weekend today.
Always → pastIf I were less afraid of being the odd one out, I would not have decided it in ten seconds.
Now whatYour line. The club opened again. What do you do this time, and what question do you ask first?
1. Why does the sheet need both conditional lines instead of one?
Because one explains what today would look like, and the other explains why the choice was made at all. Only together do they describe the decision.
2. Why is the last line about the future and not about the past?
Because you cannot change the old decision. Analysing it is only useful if it changes the next one.
