Lesson 22 -- Pre-class

If I Had Known, Now I Know -- Mixed Conditionals

Analyse a decision with both mixed conditionals: an old condition with a result today (if I had joined the club, I would be building things every weekend), and a permanent condition with a result in the past (if I were less impulsive, I would not have decided in ten seconds). Key words: at stake, trade-off, impulsive, to second-guess, fatal flaw, redemption, in the long run, to talk someone out of, irreversible, to end up, to weigh up, payoff.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. These work for characters in a series and for your own choices.

At stake -- what you can win or lose if a decision goes wrong
"Nothing was at stake, so nobody in the room actually cared."
Trade-off -- what you give up in order to get something else
"The trade-off was real, but it was smaller than I thought."
Impulsive -- done quickly, without stopping to think about it
"I am not going to be impulsive about it twice."
To second-guess -- to keep questioning a decision after you have already made it
"I second-guessed that choice for two years, and it changed nothing."
Fatal flaw -- the weakness inside a character that causes their downfall
"His fatal flaw is that he cannot ask anyone for help."
Redemption -- the moment a character makes up for something they did wrong
"There is no redemption if the second choice costs nothing."
In the long run -- over a long period, after the immediate effect has passed
"The trade-off looked small, and I paid for it in the long run."
To talk someone out of -- to persuade somebody not to do something they were going to do
"My brother tried to talk me out of it, and I did not listen."
Irreversible -- impossible to undo once it has happened
"The message was sent, and that made the whole thing irreversible."
To end up -- to reach a situation or place you did not plan
"I ended up watching other people build things online."
To weigh up -- to compare the good and bad sides carefully before deciding
"A decision a character weighs up for three episodes is a plot point."
Payoff -- the reward that arrives at the end, after a long build-up
"Six episodes of build-up, and the payoff lasted forty seconds."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the analysis and answer the questions. Watch how the two halves of each sentence live in different times.

Two years ago the school offered a robotics club and a football team on the same afternoon. I chose football, because my friends were going and because I did not want to be the only one sitting in a lab. It took me about ten seconds. My brother tried to talk me out of it, and I did not listen.

Here is the part that annoys me. If I had joined the club, I would be building things every weekend instead of watching other people build them online. That sentence has an old condition and a result that is happening right now, which is why the second half has no "have" in it. The choice is finished. The consequence is not.

And here is the harder half. I do not regret playing football; I regret the reason. If I were less afraid of being the odd one out, I would not have decided it in ten seconds, and I would have asked whether I could do both. Being afraid of standing out is not something that happened to me on a Tuesday. It was already true, and it is still true, which is why that half is in the past simple while the consequence sits in the past.

The trade-off was real, and it was smaller than I thought. Nobody told me I could do both, and I never asked. This year the club opened again, and I am not going to be impulsive about it twice.

1. Why does "I would be building things every weekend" have no "have" in it?
A Because that half describes the situation today, not something that already happened.
B Because "build" is an irregular verb and does not take "have".
C Because the writer is not certain that it would have happened.
2. Why is "If I were less afraid" in the past simple and not "If I had been less afraid"?
A Because the past simple is shorter and sounds more natural in speech.
B Because being afraid of standing out is permanently true about him, not a single moment in the past.
C Because the sentence is about the future, not the past.
3. What exactly does the writer regret?
A Choosing football instead of the robotics club.
B The reason behind the choice — deciding in ten seconds out of fear of standing out.
C Not listening to his brother about the football team.
4. What does the last paragraph say about the trade-off?
A That it did not exist, because he could always have done both.
B That it was real but smaller than he believed, and he never asked whether both were possible.
C That his brother had already checked whether both were possible.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Mixed Conditionals

GRAMMAR

In lesson 14 both halves lived in the past. Here they live in different times. Ask "when" twice, once for each half.

ShapeThe if halfThe result half
Third conditional (known)If I had joined the clubI would have learned a lot that year — past condition, past result.
Second conditional (known)If I were braverI would ask them today — unreal now, unreal now.
Mixed 1: past → nowIf + had + past participlewould + bare verb. "If I had joined the club, I would be building things every weekend." No "have", no "been".
Mixed 2: always → pastIf + past simple (were, knew, had)would have + past participle. "If I were less impulsive, I would not have decided in ten seconds."
How to chooseAsk when for the cause and when for the result. Old cause plus today's result = mixed 1. Permanent cause plus old result = mixed 2.
The checkCount the word have. There is exactly one per sentence, and it sits on the side that is in the past. Two means you mixed the shapes; zero means the sentence never left today.
The trap from PortugueseNever If I would have known. English keeps would out of the if half, always: If I had known.

Stage 2: Put the Five Steps in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the five steps of a decision analysis in order.

?Turn it around: if plus the past simple, and would have plus the past participle.
?Name the decision itself, in the past simple, so we know what happened.
?Build the first mixed conditional: if plus had plus the past participle, then would plus the bare verb.
?Say what you would do now, because the point is the next decision.
?Say what was at stake, so we understand why the choice was difficult.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

Take one decision you actually made in the last two years -- a club, a subject, a friendship, a message you sent. Say what happened and what was at stake. Then say it twice: once with "if I had..., I would..." to describe your life today, and once with "if I were..., I would have..." to explain why you chose the way you did. Finish by saying what you would do if the same choice came back tomorrow.
