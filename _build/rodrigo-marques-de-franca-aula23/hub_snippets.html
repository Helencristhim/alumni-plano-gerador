Lesson 23 -- Pre-class

The Question Behind the Question -- Follow-Up Questions

How to find out what a client, a patient or a guest really wants to know: do not answer the first question, ask the second one. Key words: to probe, a follow-up question, an open-ended question, a leading question, to dig deeper, to get to the bottom of something, to elaborate, an underlying concern, a hidden agenda, to put somebody on the spot, to beat around the bush, to tease out. Structure: subject questions without did (who told you?, what happened?), object questions with did (who did you tell?), negative questions to check what you think you know (didn't they sign it?), and the preposition at the end (who did you sign it with?).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To probe -- to ask careful questions to find information that is not obvious
"Before you answer the client, probe a little."
A follow-up question -- a question you ask after an answer, to learn more about it
"The interview really started with her first follow-up question."
An open-ended question -- a question that cannot be answered with yes or no
"What else is worrying you? is an open-ended question."
A leading question -- a question that pushes the other person toward the answer you want
"You were angry, weren't you? is a leading question."
To dig deeper -- to look for more information below the first answer
"The numbers looked fine, so we decided to dig deeper."
To get to the bottom of something -- to find the real cause or the whole truth of something
"We need to get to the bottom of this before Friday."
To elaborate -- to give more details or explain something more fully
"Could you elaborate on that last point?"
An underlying concern -- a real worry that is under the surface and is not said directly
"Her underlying concern was the exclusivity clause."
A hidden agenda -- a secret purpose behind what someone says or does
"I don't think they have a hidden agenda. They just want a better price."
To put somebody on the spot -- to ask somebody a difficult question that they must answer immediately, often in front of others
"I didn't want to put him on the spot in front of his boss."
To beat around the bush -- to avoid talking about the main point
"Let's not beat around the bush. Is the deal still on?"
To tease out -- to get information slowly and carefully from a person or a situation
"It took three calls to tease out what the client really wanted."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Claire Dunn is the operations director of a kitchen equipment company in Chicago. On Monday, she called Rodrigo with what she said was a simple question: could her company end its contract with its Brazilian distributor next month? Rodrigo did not answer. Instead, he decided to probe a little. "What happened?" he asked. "Did they break the contract?"

They hadn't. Their sales were fine. So Rodrigo asked two short follow-up questions: "Who made that decision? And who else knows about it?" The answer changed everything. The new CEO had been talking to an online retailer in São Paulo for months. Rodrigo did not beat around the bush. He asked Claire if the real question was how to start selling online without a fight.

It was. Claire hadn't wanted to put him on the spot with the whole story. Then Rodrigo checked something he thought he already knew: "Didn't the distributor sign an exclusivity clause with you?" They had, in 2022, and that was Claire's underlying concern. Before the call ended, Rodrigo asked one last question: "Who did you sign it with on their side?" Now he had what he needed to get to the bottom of the problem.

1. "Who made that decision?" Why is there no did in this question?
A Because Rodrigo is asking about the past, and questions in the past simple about a decision never need an auxiliary verb.
B Because it is a negative question.
C Because who is the subject: the person who made the decision.
2. What was Claire's real question?
A How to start selling online without a fight.
B Whether the distributor had broken the contract, because its sales in Brazil had been very disappointing for months.
C Who the new CEO was.
3. "Didn't the distributor sign an exclusivity clause with you?" What does Rodrigo think?
A He is sure that they never signed one.
B He thinks they did, and he wants to check.
C He has no idea at all, so he asks a completely open question to let Claire tell the whole story again.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Follow-Up Questions

GRAMMAR

If who or what did the action, there is no did. If somebody did the action to who or what, did comes back.

QuestionFormExample
Who / What is the subjectwho / what + verb (no do, does, did)Who told you? What happened?
Who / What is the objectwho / what + aux + subject + verbWho did you tell? What did she say?
Negative questionDidn't / Isn't / Haven't + subject ... ? (you think yes, and you check)Didn't they sign an exclusivity clause?
Preposition at the endquestion word ... verb + prepositionWho did you sign it with? What is it for?
The shortest follow-upsquestion word + prepositionWhat for? Since when? Like what?

Never: Who did tell you? · What did happen? · Who you spoke to? · With who did you sign it with?

Stage 2: Put the Call in Order

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the question a real speaker would ask.

A client says: "We had a problem with a supplier last month." What is the best first follow-up question?
A "What did happen?"
B "Did you have a problem with the price, with the delivery, or with the quality of the products?"
C "What happened, exactly?"
After a meeting, a client called somebody at the other company. You want to know who that person was. You ask:
A "Who called you?"
B "Who did you call?"
C "Who you called at the other company after the meeting last week?"
You think the other side received your notice last week, and you want to check. You ask:
A "Didn't they receive the notice?"
B "Haven't they receive the notice that we sent them by email and by courier last week?"
C "Didn't they received the notice?"
At the start of a meeting, a new client asks a very simple question. What is the best first reply?
A "That is a leading question, so I'm afraid I can't answer it until our firm has studied every document."
B "Before I answer, can I ask a few follow-up questions?"
C "Yes, of course. I'll send you the answer tomorrow."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

A new client calls and asks: "Can we stop paying our landlord until the roof is repaired?" Record the first sixty seconds of the call. Do not give advice yet. Ask at least five short follow-up questions, and include one subject question (who or what + verb), one object question with did, and one negative question (didn't...?).
