Lesson 21 -- Pre-class

The Art of the Story -- Telling Travel Anecdotes with Impact

Tell a travel story people want to hear, and look forward from the past: was going to, was about to, was supposed to and would. New vocabulary: to set the scene, out of the blue, to cut a long story short, the punchline, a twist, to ramble, to embellish, a cliffhanger, to keep someone in suspense, to hang on someone's every word, to get to the point, to see the funny side.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To set the scene -- to say when, where and what was happening before the action starts
"Let me set the scene: it was raining, and we were lost."
Out of the blue -- suddenly, with no warning at all
"Out of the blue, a shepherd appeared on the path."
To cut a long story short -- to skip the details and go straight to the end
"To cut a long story short, we never reached the village."
The punchline -- the last line of a story, the one that makes people laugh or understand
"He forgot the punchline halfway through the story."
A twist -- a sudden change in a story that nobody expected
"Then came the twist: the hotel did not exist."
To ramble -- to talk for too long without a clear point
"Stop me if I start to ramble about the bike."
To embellish -- to add details that make a story better than the truth
"Every time he tells it, he embellishes it a little more."
A cliffhanger -- an ending that stops just before you find out what happens
"She ended the episode on a cliffhanger."
To keep someone in suspense -- to make someone wait, curious and nervous, for the end
"Do not keep us in suspense. Did you make it?"
To hang on someone's every word -- to listen with total attention
"The whole table was hanging on his every word."
To get to the point -- to say the important part without any delay
"Get to the point: what happened on the pass?"
To see the funny side -- to find something to laugh at in a bad situation
"It took me a year to see the funny side of that storm."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Marcelo is telling his favorite story from Albania. "Let me set the scene," he begins. "We were supposed to reach Theth by six, and we were going to cross the pass that afternoon. But at four o'clock the rain came in sideways, and I was about to turn back. Then, out of the blue, a shepherd came down the path with a mule." He stops for a second to keep everyone in suspense. "I had no idea that night would become the best memory of the trip." Notice the shapes. Each one stands at a moment in the past and looks forward. Was going to is a plan that changed. Was about to is something that almost happened. Was supposed to is what was expected but did not happen. And would is the future, seen from the past.

1. Did Marcelo reach Theth by six?
A No. He says they were supposed to, which tells us the plan did not work out.
B Yes, exactly on time.
C The text does not say anything at all about the time they were expected in Theth.
2. What does "I was about to turn back" tell the listener?
A That he turned back immediately and went home.
B That he almost turned back, but something happened first.
C That he was planning to turn back the next morning after a good night of rest in the village.
3. Why does Marcelo say "that night would become the best memory" and not "will become"?
A Because he is looking forward from a moment in the past.
B Because "would" is more polite.
C Because the night has not happened yet and he is still waiting for it to begin.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Future in the Past

GRAMMAR

Stand at a moment in the past and look forward.

FormYou use it forExample
was / were going toa plan in the past, often one that changedWe were going to cross the pass.
was / were about tosomething that almost happenedI was about to turn back.
was / were supposed towhat was expected, but did not happenWe were supposed to reach Theth by six.
wouldthe future, seen from the pastI knew it would rain.

Key point: after about to and supposed to, use the base verb: "about to turn", never "about to turning". And when you look forward from the past, will becomes would: "I knew the climb would be hard", never "I knew the climb will be hard".

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best response for each situation.

A friend asks about a trip where the plan changed. How do you start?
A "We were going to ride to the lake, but a storm came in."
B "We will go to the lake, but a storm comes in."
C "We are going to the lake yesterday, but a storm was coming in and we stayed in the hotel."
You almost missed the last train. How do you build suspense?
A "I miss the last train almost."
B "I was about to miss the last train when the doors opened again."
C "I was about missing the last train."
Your friend is telling a long story and you want the ending. What do you say, politely?
A "Finish now."
B "Sorry, can you get to the point? What happened in the end?"
C "Can you set the scene again, from the very beginning, with all the details about the bike?"
The guesthouse expected you at seven, but you arrived at midnight. How do you say it?
A "We were supposed to arrive at seven, but we got there at midnight."
B "We supposed arrive at seven."
C "We would arrive at seven, but at midnight we arrive, and they are waiting for us at the door."
