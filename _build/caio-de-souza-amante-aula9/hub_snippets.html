Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

What They Said at the Table -- The Language of the Debrief

Whoever writes the recap owns the deal, because what everybody remembers three weeks later comes out of that email and not out of the room. Key words: a rebuttal, to reiterate, a caveat, to paraphrase, a recap, to push back, to escalate, a takeaway. Structure: reported speech with backshifting.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A rebuttal -- an answer that argues back against an objection, point by point
"If it is a position and not a mistake, you will need a rebuttal by Friday."
To reiterate -- to say the same thing again on purpose, so that it holds
"Should I reiterate the June date in the same email?"
A caveat -- a condition or warning attached to what somebody has just agreed
"I said we could hold the price, with one caveat about the timing."
To paraphrase -- to say what somebody said, in your own words, without changing it
"I will paraphrase it in the recap and copy him, so nothing is invented later."
A recap -- a short written summary of what was said and decided
"The recap goes out with three lines and one takeaway."
To push back -- to disagree with something openly, and say why
"Their finance lead pushed back on the retention number."
To escalate -- to move a question up to somebody senior enough to answer it
"Then we escalate that one, in writing, today."
A takeaway -- the one thing somebody will actually do after the meeting
"The takeaway is theirs to confirm, not yours to announce."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In a negotiation, what was agreed is not what was said in the room. It is what was written afterwards. Somebody leaves the meeting, sends an email, and three weeks later everybody argues from that email and nobody remembers the call. So the person who can repeat other people accurately, in English, controls the record — and the person who cannot has handed that job to the other side.

The mechanism is one step backwards. Everything the person said moves one tense further into the past. We are ready becomes he said they were ready. The pool was agreed becomes she said the pool had been agreed, because it was already past when she spoke. The modals move with it: can becomes could, will becomes would, must becomes had to. The pronouns move too, and so does the time: tomorrow becomes the next day.

A reported question stops being a question. It loses the auxiliary, it loses the question order and it loses the question mark: can you hold the price? becomes he asked whether we could hold the price. Keeping the original order — he asked what did we want — is the single most common mistake in this structure, and it is audible immediately. The other one is smaller and just as visible in writing: tell needs a person after it and say does not. He told me. He said that. Never he said me.

There is one exception worth knowing, because it belongs to your world. When the thing reported is still true at the moment you are speaking, the step back becomes optional: he said the threshold is fourteen percent is correct if it still is. And the permanent target survives here too: in the present, the reporting verb keeps its ending. She says, not she say. Finally, one rule that is not grammar at all. Report the sentence, not your feeling about the sentence. If the buyer said the margin was too thin, say he said the margin was too thin. You are allowed to disagree in the next sentence; you are not allowed to edit his on the way home.

Stage 2: Put the Recap in Order

Order

You are dictating the recap email after the call. Put the lines in a logical order. Listen first, then put the lines in order.

?"Three lines, so that we are working from the same version."
?"You said you were ready to move in June, and we are working to that date."
?"You asked whether we could hold the price if closing moved to September."
?"I said we could, with one caveat: the pool would have to be funded before September."
?"Your finance lead said the pool had already been agreed at a lower number."
?"Could you confirm the pool figure you are working from? Everything else can wait for Thursday."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The buyer said "we are ready to move in June". You report it. You say:
A He said me they are ready to move in June.
B He said they were ready to move in June.
C He said me that they were ready to move in June.
The buyer asked "what do you want on the retention pool?" You report it. You say:
A He asked what did we want on the retention pool.
B He asked what we wanted on the retention pool.
C He asked what do we want on the retention pool?
She said "we will fund the pool before closing". You report it. You say:
A She said they would fund the pool before closing.
B She said they will fund the pool before closing.
C She told they would fund the pool before closing.
The buyer objected that the margin is too thin. You report it to your board. You say:
A He had a few questions about the numbers.
B He said the margin was too thin for that multiple, and I disagree, for three reasons.
C He said me the margin is a bit thin, more or less.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Dictate the recap email out loud, as if you were recording it for your lawyer: three lines reporting what was said in the meeting, and one takeaway phrased as a question for the other side. Do not soften the objection.

Video
Chris Voss — “The Mirroring Technique”

The former lead hostage negotiator at the FBI on the cheapest move in any negotiation: repeating the other person's own words back to them. It is the spoken version of what this lesson practises in writing, and he shows why it makes people keep talking.

Tip: try it on your next call in Portuguese first, then in English. Repeat the last three words the other person said and then stay silent, and count how long they keep going.

Hidden Brain — “Did That Really Happen? How Our Memories Betray Us”

The research behind the reason the recap email exists. Most people believe memory works like a camera; it does not, and the way a question is worded changes what a witness later remembers seeing. Half an hour, clear American English, full transcript on the page.

Tip: listen once without the transcript, then read it and mark every sentence where the host reports what a researcher said. That is the whole grammar of this lesson, used for real.
