Chapter 3: One Step Back

Every Verb Moves One Step

And the one case where it does not have to

Grammar Discovery

Which One Did Not Move?

"We are ready to move in June." → "He said they were ready to move in June."

"Can you hold the price?" → "He asked whether we could hold the price."

"The pool was agreed at a lower number." → "She said the pool had been agreed at a lower number."

"The threshold is fourteen percent." → "He said the threshold is fourteen percent, and it still is."

Every red verb has moved exactly one step further into the past, and the modals moved with them. The orange one did not move, and it did not have to: the fact is still true at the moment of speaking. Notice also what disappeared from the second line: the question order, and the question mark.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Twenty Minutes After the Call

One debrief, two recordings and one recap email

Dialogue

The Words, Not the Summary

N
Before I write anything down, I need that call back in your words. Not the summary. The words.
C
Alright. Werner opened by saying that they were ready to move in June.
N
Said, or implied? I ask because his colleague told me last week that nothing had been approved by their investment committee.
C
He said it. Then he asked whether we could hold the price if closing moved to September.
N
Good, and you reported that one correctly. What did you answer?
C
I said we could hold the price, with one caveat. I told him the retention pool would have to be funded before September, not after.
N
You gave something and you attached a condition to it. Keep doing exactly that. Did anybody push back?
C
Their finance lead did. She said the pool had already been agreed at a lower number, which is not what the term sheet says.
N
Then we escalate that one, in writing, today. I will paraphrase it in the recap and copy Werner. If it is a misunderstanding it dies quietly, and if it is a position, you will need a rebuttal ready by Friday.
C
Should I reiterate the June date in the same email?
N
No. One issue per email. The recap goes out with three lines and one takeaway, and the takeaway is theirs to confirm, not yours to announce.
Comprehension

About Nora

1. What does Nora ask for at the start, and what does she refuse?
She asks for the call back in his own words, before she writes anything down, and she refuses the summary.
2. What did her contact tell her last week, and why does it matter here?
That nothing had been approved by their investment committee — which is why she asks whether the June date was said or only implied.
3. What does she say she will do about the retention pool, and what does she want in the recap?
She will escalate it in writing that day and paraphrase it, copying Werner. The recap goes out with three lines and one takeaway, and the other side confirms it.
Listening 1 · A Mediator

Whoever Writes the Recap Owns the Deal

A commercial mediator on where most arguments actually begin. Sound first — no text.

1. Where does he say most of these arguments start?
Somebody left a meeting and told a colleague what had been agreed, and the version that got written down was not the version that was said.
2. What example does he give, and what does he say the difference is?
Somebody says they are open to a September closing, and it reaches the finance team as they committed to September — two verbs of difference, and now one side believes there is a commitment.
3. What is his rule, and what does he say it is not about?
Whoever writes the recap owns the deal. He says it is not about honesty, it is about mechanics — what people remember three weeks later comes out of the email, not the room.
Listening 2 · An Adviser

The Habit of Softening It

An adviser on what founders delete when they report an objection. Sound first — no text.

1. What habit does she try to break, and what does she say it really is?
Softening what the other side said. She says it is not a language problem but a confidence problem that shows up as one.
2. What is the example she gives, and what has the founder deleted?
The buyer says the margin is too thin for that multiple, and the founder tells his board the buyer had some questions about the numbers. He has deleted the objection.
3. What is she willing to allow, and what is she not?
Disagreeing with the sentence in the very next sentence is allowed. Editing it on the way home is not.
Real Document

The Recap Email, Line by Line

Subject: Recap — call of 14 May
From: Caio Amante · To: Werner · Cc: Nora

Werner,

Three lines, so that we are working from the same version.

1. You said you were ready to move in June. We are working to that date.

2. You asked whether we could hold the price if closing moved to September. I said we could, with one caveat: the retention pool would have to be funded before September, not after.

3. Your finance lead said the pool had already been agreed at a lower number. That is not what the current term sheet says, so I would like to close that gap this week rather than at signing.

Takeaway: could you confirm the pool figure you are working from? Everything else can wait for Thursday.

Caio

Nothing in this email is new. Every line repeats something somebody already said, which is exactly why it is difficult to argue with.
1. Which line reports a question, and how can you tell?
The second one. It uses asked whether, and the words after it are in normal order with no question mark.
2. Which line reports a disagreement, and what does it do immediately afterwards?
The third. It reports what the finance lead said, and then states that the term sheet says something else — the objection is repeated first, and answered second.
3. Why is there only one takeaway, and why is it a question?
Because a recap with four requests gets no answer at all. Making it a question puts the next move on the other side, in writing.
