Lesson 7 · Ten Years in the Chair

Patterns, Habits and Change

You have been running the group for ten years. Almost nothing about how you do it is the same as it was, and English has three different tools for saying so.

Chapter 1: First Words

From the Chain to the Person Running It

Last time the subject was a system breaking. Today it is the person in the middle of it, and how ten years changed the way he works — which is the question every serious conversation abroad eventually reaches.

Think of one thing you did every week in your first year as director and do not do at all now. Say what it was, and say what replaced it.

Today’s Goal

Three Missions

1

Twelve words for habit, change and the way you work.

2

Three ways to talk about what you used to do.

3

Tell ten years of change in four sentences.

The structure of the day is a set of three that look alike and are not: I used to go to the plant every day, I would go in every morning before six, and I am used to going in early. Three sentences, three different meanings, and one of them is not about the past at all.

One Question

What Did You Do Then That You Do Not Do Now?

Two sentences, out loud, right now.

One about a habit you had ten years ago and dropped. One about something that felt strange at first and is now completely normal for you. English marks those two things differently, and running them together is the most common mistake at your level.

Chapter 3: The Code

Three Ways to Say Used To

Two of them are the past. One of them is now.

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I used to go to the plant every single day."

"On Mondays I would drive the eighty kilometres before six."

"I am used to getting up at five now."

"It took a year to get used to not being in the building."

Three of these have the same three letters in the middle and only two of them are about the past. Look at what comes straight after used to in each one — in the blue pair it is not the same kind of word.

JobFormExample
A past habit or state, over nowused to + infinitiveI used to go to the plant every day.
A repeated past action you rememberwould + infinitiveOn Mondays I would drive out before six.
The limit of wouldnever for a past STATENot I would have two plants. Say I used to have.
Something is now normal for yoube used to + verb-ing / nounI am used to getting up at five.
Becoming normalget used to + verb-ingIt took a year to get used to not being there.
Question and negativedid you use to ...? / did not use toDid you use to go every day? I did not use to.
The trapafter be used to comes -ing, not the infinitiveNot I am used to get up early. Say I am used to getting up early.

In one line: used to plus infinitive is the past; be used to plus -ing is now.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Conversation, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one sheet

Dialogue

The Question Everybody Eventually Asks

R
Ten years in the chair. What do you do now that you did not do at the start?
F
Almost the opposite of everything. I used to be in one of the plants every single day. I was extremely hands-on, and for about three years that was the right answer.
R
And then it stopped being the right answer.
F
Then we opened the second plant. That was the turning point. On Mondays I would drive eighty kilometres before six, and I was still the slowest part of every decision.
R
What did stepping back actually cost you?
F
A year of things being done worse than I would have done them. Doing it myself had been a crutch — it was faster and it taught nobody anything.
R
And now? Be honest with me. What have you not changed?
F
Pricing. It is my blind spot and I know it, which does not seem to help. I am used to being out of the building, but I am not used to somebody else setting a price.
Comprehension

About Rachel

1. How does Rachel open the conversation, and what exactly does she ask for?
She notes that he has been ten years in the chair and asks what he does now that he did not do at the start.
2. What does Rachel say when he explains that being hands-on was right for three years?
She says, in her own words, that it then stopped being the right answer — she completes the thought rather than asking a new question.
3. What is the last thing Rachel asks, and how does she frame it?
She asks what he has not changed, and she frames it by telling him to be honest with her.
Listening 1 · The Owner

What I Used to Do Every Monday

A second-generation owner on the habits he kept far too long. Sound first — no text.

1. How did he run the company when he took over, and what does he say about that?
The way he had watched his father run it, which he says is what everybody does and almost nobody admits.
2. What was he signing every month, and why had the rule stopped making sense?
Three hundred orders over a value set in the nineteen nineties that had never been updated, so the amounts no longer mattered.
3. What did the new finance manager do, and what did he conclude about himself?
She asked within a fortnight what the number was for and why it had never changed. He concluded that he had been repeating a policy rather than deciding one.
Listening 2 · The Coach

Why the Habit Outlives the Reason

An executive coach on why founders keep doing what stopped working. Sound first — no text.

1. What pattern does she say she sees most often?
Not somebody refusing to change, but somebody who changed the company and forgot to change themselves at the same time.
2. Why is it hard to question the behaviour that got somebody here?
Because it is usually a real strength, and people do not question the thing that worked.
3. What exercise does she give, and what does it usually show?
List five things you do every week and, for each, say when you started and what problem it solved. About half turn out to solve a problem the company no longer has.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Founders Who Stepped Back

A business radio report on what happens after the founder stops running it. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the finding about companies whose founder steps back?
On average they perform slightly worse for about two years, and the line crosses somewhere in year three.
2. What do owners say the hard part was?
Not the decision, but the middle period, when they could still do every job faster than the person now doing it.
3. What do the owners who took a task back say, and what is the researchers’ observation?
They say they only meant to help for a week. The researchers observe that a week is exactly how long it takes for everybody to understand the job is still yours.

Two of the lines on this page are habits that were decided, and one is a habit that was simply never stopped. The distinction is the whole point of the exercise: a director who used to price every job kept doing it after the company outgrew the reason, and is now used to being out of the building but not used to somebody else setting a number.
1. What is the difference between the "Kept on purpose" line and the "Kept without deciding to" line?
One is a habit that was chosen and re-chosen; the other is a habit that was simply never stopped after the reason for it disappeared.
2. Which sentence in the note describes something that is normal now rather than something in the past?
"is now used to being out of the building" — be used to plus -ing describes what is normal now, not a past habit.
3. Why would a review sheet ask what you have kept as well as what you have dropped?
Because the habits nobody examines are the ones that outlive their reason, and only the kept column exposes them.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I use used to plus the infinitive for a habit that is over.
I use would for a repeated past action, and never for a past state.
I say I am used to getting up early, and never I am used to get up early.
I can ask the question form: did you use to, with no d on use.
I know the words: a turning point, hands-on, to step back, a blind spot, a crutch, to be set in your ways.
