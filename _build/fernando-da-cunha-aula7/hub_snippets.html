Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

Patterns, Habits and Change -- Ten Years in the Chair

Almost nothing about how you run the group is the same as it was ten years ago, and English has three different tools for saying so. Key words: a turning point, to take over, hands-on, to step back, a blind spot, to second-guess, a gut feeling, a rut, discipline, a crutch, to be set in your ways, to grow into something. Structure: used to, would and be used to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A turning point -- the moment after which things went differently, and you can name it
"The second plant was the turning point. Before that we were a workshop."
To take over -- to become the person in charge of something that already existed
"I took over the directorship ten years ago, almost to the month."
Hands-on -- involved directly in the practical work, rather than only deciding about it
"I was very hands-on for the first three years, and it did not scale."
To step back -- to stop being involved in the detail so that somebody else can run it
"Stepping back was harder than taking over."
A blind spot -- something about your own operation you cannot see, because you are too close to it
"Pricing is my blind spot. I still price the way we priced in 2016."
To second-guess -- to question a decision somebody else has made, usually after they have made it
"If you second-guess a manager twice, the third decision comes back to you."
A gut feeling -- a judgement you trust without being able to show the data behind it
"My gut feeling was right about that client, and I still cannot explain why."
A rut -- a way of working you keep repeating because it is familiar, not because it is right
"Every Monday meeting was in a rut and nobody wanted to say so."
Discipline -- doing the necessary thing whether or not you feel like doing it that day
"Discipline is the only reason the five o’clock alarm still works."
A crutch -- something you lean on that quietly stops you getting better
"Doing it myself was a crutch. It was faster and it taught nobody anything."
To be set in your ways -- expression: unwilling to change how you do things, because they have always worked
"The risk at fifty is not that you stop learning. It is that you get set in your ways."
To grow into something -- expression: to become able to do a role that was too big for you at the start
"Nobody is ready for the job. You grow into it or you do not."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A director looks back at ten years and the sentences he needs are not all the same shape. I used to go to the plant every single day. That is a habit that is over: used to plus the plain form of the verb, and the whole point of the structure is that it is no longer true. On Mondays I would drive the eighty kilometres before six. That is would, which does the same job for a repeated action you remember, and which comes with one hard limit: it never works for a state. You can say I used to have two plants. You cannot say I would have two plants.

Then the third one, which looks identical and is not about the past at all. I am used to getting up at five. Here used to is not a past marker: be used to means something is normal for you now, and what follows it is a verb with -ing or a noun, never the plain form. It took a year to get used to not being in the building is the same structure describing the process of becoming normal.

The mistake almost everybody at this level makes is I am used to get up early, because Portuguese does not force the choice. Two smaller ones are worth naming. In the question and the negative, the d disappears: did you use to sign every order, I did not use to. And in the affirmative it stays: I used to. Once the three are separate, ten years of change fits into four sentences: what you used to do, what you would do on a particular day, what you are used to now, and the one habit you kept without ever deciding to — which is usually the blind spot.

1. Why can you say "I used to have two plants" but not "I would have two plants"?
A Because "have" is irregular.
B Because "would" never works for a past state, only for a repeated action.
C Because two plants is not a habit.
2. What follows "be used to"?
A The plain form of the verb: I am used to get up early.
B A verb with -ing or a noun: I am used to getting up early.
C Nothing: be used to always ends the sentence.
3. What happens to "used" in the question form?
A It loses the d: did you use to sign every order?
B It keeps the d: did you used to sign every order?
C It becomes "were used to".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Used To, Would and Be Used To

GRAMMAR

Three structures that look alike. Two of them are the past, and one of them is now.

JobFormExample
A past habit or state, over nowused to + infinitiveI used to go to the plant every day.
A repeated past action you rememberwould + infinitiveOn Mondays I would drive out before six.
The limit of wouldnever for a past STATENot I would have two plants. Say I used to have.
Something is now normal for yoube used to + verb-ing / nounI am used to getting up at five.
Becoming normalget used to + verb-ingIt took a year to get used to not being there.
Question and negativedid you use to ...? / did not use toDid you use to go every day? I did not use to.
The trapafter be used to comes -ing, not the infinitiveNot I am used to get up early. Say I am used to getting up early.

Never: I am used to get up at five · I would have two plants at that time · did you used to sign every order · I use to go to the plant every day · it took a year to get used to not be in the building.

Lesson 7 — Patterns, Habits and Change

Talk
TED -- Carol Dweck, "The power of believing that you can improve" (10 min)

A psychologist on the difference between people who treat their own way of working as fixed and people who treat it as a draft. It is the argument under this whole lesson: the habit that stops being examined is the one that outlives its reason.

Tip: ten minutes, clear delivery. Watch it once, then say out loud one thing you used to believe about running the group and no longer do.

Podcast
How I Built This -- LinkedIn: Reid Hoffman

A founder describing, at length and without much vanity, the things he did in the early years that he had to stop doing. The past habit language runs through the entire interview, which makes it an unusually good listening exercise for this structure.

Tip: listen to any twenty minutes. Every time he says used to or would about a past habit, write the sentence down. You should reach ten quickly.
