The figure, the comparison, and the word that goes in front of it. Key words: a rate, an average, a share, a gap, roughly, to exceed, to fall short, a range, a sample, a baseline. Structure: quantifiers with countable and uncountable nouns, and saying figures out loud.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A rate -- a number that shows how often something happens, out of a hundred
"The default rate is four point two per cent this quarter."
An average -- the middle number of a group, when you add and divide
"The average limit is eight hundred reais, and almost nobody uses all of it."
A share -- the part of a whole that belongs to one thing
"New customers are a small share of the portfolio and most of the risk."
A gap -- the difference between two numbers
"There is a gap of one point one between the target and the result."
Roughly -- not exactly, but close
"Roughly a third of the base is in the pilot by now."
To exceed -- to go above a number
"Revenue exceeded the target by eleven per cent."
To fall short -- to stay below what was expected
"We fell short of the target by half a point, and I said so first."
A range -- the distance between the smallest and the largest number
"The limits are in a range from three hundred to five thousand."
A sample -- a small part of a group, used to learn about the whole
"The sample was too small, so the number moved every week."
A baseline -- the number you compare everything else with
"Without a baseline, every result looks good or looks terrible."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nobody trips on the number itself. People trip on the word in front of it. English sorts nouns into two families. If you can put three in front of it, it is countable: three customers, three limits, three samples. If you cannot, it is uncountable: revenue, data, risk, information. The families take different words. Countable takes many and a few: "Many customers never use the whole limit." Uncountable takes much and a little: "We do not have much data on that cohort yet."

Two words work with both families and save you every time: a lot of and most of. "Most of the losses come from one group." "There is a lot of risk in that range." And one detail worth knowing: much sounds natural in a negative or a question, but strange in a positive sentence. Say "we do not have much data", and say "we have a lot of data" — not we have much data.

Then there are the figures themselves, and here English and Portuguese disagree twice. A decimal is a point, never a comma: 4.2 is "four point two", so a rate of four point two per cent, against a baseline of three point one, leaves a gap of one point one. And in American English a thousand takes no and: 14,200 is "fourteen thousand two hundred". Finally, three nouns that are plural in Portuguese and uncountable in English catch almost everybody: never informations, never datas, never advices.

1. Why is it "many customers" but "much data"?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Quantifiers and Figures

GRAMMAR

If you can put a number in front of it, use many. If you cannot, use much.

Stage 2: Put the Numbers Slide in Order

Order

This is how a good numbers slide is presented. Put it in order.

?"The default rate is four point two per cent this quarter."
?"The baseline was three point one, so the gap is one point one."
?"Roughly a third of the base is in the pilot, and the sample is big enough now."
?"The average limit is eight hundred, but hardly any customer sits there."
?"The range goes from three hundred to five thousand, and that is where the story is."
?"Most of the losses come from a small share of the customers."

Hans Rosling -- "The best stats you’ve ever seen" (TED)

Twenty minutes of a statistician reading figures out loud, which is exactly what you practised today. Percentages, averages, ranges and shares, one after another, in slow and very clear English — and he never says a number without saying what he is comparing it with.

Tip: turn the English subtitles on. Pause after each figure and repeat it out loud with point, not comma.
