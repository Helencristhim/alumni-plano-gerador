Chapter 4: The Code

One Past, Two Shapes

The fence that closes a sentence

Grammar Discovery

Both Are the Past. What Separates Them?

"He moved to Lisbon at sixteen and trained there for four years."

"He has never described those years as unhappy."

"He came home at nineteen, and he has not missed a deadline in three years."

"Have you ever given something up like that?" — "Yes, I did, in 2023."

Both colors are about the past. The red one puts a fence around it — an age, a year, a date — and the story closes. The green one leaves the fence out, because the count is still running.

JobFormExample
Finished, and the time is namedpast simple + a closed timeHe moved to Lisbon at sixteen.
Still counting, no closed timehave / has + past participleHe has never described those years as unhappy.
How long it has lasted, up to nowfor + a length · since + a starting pointHe has lived in Brazil since he was nineteen.
News, with the result still herejust · already · yetHe has already handed it in. / He has not decided yet.
A life experience, with no dateever · neverHave you ever trained twice a day?
The trapa named time kills the present perfectNot Last year I have traveled. Say Last year I traveled.
The other trapsince never takes the present simpleNot I live here since March. Say I have lived here since March.

In one line: name the time and the sentence closes. Leave the time out and the count is still running.

Listening 1 · A Voice Note

The Year I Stopped Being an Athlete

A former swimmer recording a voice note for a friend. Sound first — no text.

1. How long did she swim, and what did she achieve?
She swam for eleven years and made two national finals.
2. What actually happened on the Tuesday she describes?
She got to the pool, sat on the edge, and did not want to get in. That was the whole turning point.
3. What does she say she does not miss, and what does she say she does miss?
She does not miss racing as much as everybody warned her she would. What she misses is the intensity.
Listening 2 · A Podcast

The Eight Months Nobody Warns You About

A sports psychologist on what happens after an athlete stops. Sound first — no text.

1. Who does he say gets prepared, and for what is almost nobody prepared?
Everybody prepares an athlete for the decision, but almost nobody prepares them for what comes after it.
2. How does he describe the first weeks and months?
The first two weeks feel like a holiday, and the two months after that feel like freedom.
3. What is his advice, and how is it different from deciding what to do instead?
Before you stop, decide what the new demanding thing is going to be — not what you will do instead, but what will ask as much of you.
