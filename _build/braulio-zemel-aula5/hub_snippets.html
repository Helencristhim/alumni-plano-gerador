Traveling the World in English -- From Copenhagen to Tokyo

How to tell one travel scene so that a table leans in, instead of listing countries. Key expressions: a layover, jet lag, off the beaten track, a local, to get around, a landmark, packed, a rip-off, to wander, culture shock, to haggle, a stopover. Structure: inversion after negative adverbials -- never have I seen, not only did, only when.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

A layover -- a stop between two flights on the same journey
"We had a nine-hour layover in Dubai and we saw nothing of it."
Jet lag -- the tiredness you feel after crossing several time zones
"The jet lag from Asia takes me four days now. It used to take one."
Off the beaten track -- away from the places where all the tourists go
"The best meal of the trip was completely off the beaten track."
A local -- somebody who lives in the place you are visiting
"Ask a local, never the hotel. The hotel sends everybody to the same street."
To get around -- to travel from place to place inside a city
"In Copenhagen everybody gets around on a bicycle, including the ministers."
A landmark -- a famous building or place that people use to find their way
"I use one landmark and walk out from it in circles."
Packed -- completely full of people, with no space left
"The station at eight in the morning was packed, and nobody was in a hurry."
A rip-off -- something that costs far more than it is worth
"The restaurant next to the museum was a rip-off, and I knew it before I sat down."
To wander -- to walk with no fixed plan and no destination
"I wandered for three hours and found the part of the city I remember best."
Culture shock -- the confusion you feel when everything around you works differently
"The culture shock in Tokyo is supposed to be gentle and total at the same time."
To haggle -- to argue about a price until both sides agree
"I have never been able to haggle. My wife is extremely good at it."
A stopover -- a short stay in a city on the way to somewhere else
"We are thinking of a two-day stopover in Lisbon on the way back."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He has been to Asia, Europe and the United States, and this year to Denmark. What he has never done is tell any of it in English, because listing countries is not a story and he knows it.

In Copenhagen he noticed the bicycles first. Never have I seen a city where everybody gets around on two wheels, he says, including the ministers. He did what he always does in a new place: he chose one landmark, walked out from it in circles, and wandered for three hours with no plan at all. The restaurant next to the museum was a rip-off and he knew it before he sat down. The best meal of the week was completely off the beaten track, recommended by a local rather than by the hotel.

Japan is next, possibly next year, and his wife has been asking for ten years. The plan involves a two-day stopover in Lisbon on the way back, and a long layover he is not looking forward to. The jet lag from Asia takes him four days now; it used to take one.

A woman at breakfast who has been to Tokyo four times told him the thing nobody puts in the guides. Not only did the food surprise her, she said, but the silence did too: a packed station at eight in the morning and nobody talking. That, apparently, is the culture shock nobody warns you about. He cannot haggle and never could, but his wife is extremely good at it, so between them they will manage.

1. "Never have I seen a city where everybody gets around on two wheels." Why is the verb in this order?
A Because it is a question.
B Because a negative word at the front makes the sentence take question order.
C Because the sentence is in the past.
2. "Not only did the food surprise her, but the silence did too." Which half is inverted?
A Both halves.
B Only the first half, the one with not only.
C Only the second half, the one with but.
3. What is the difference between a layover and a stopover in this text?
A A stopover is a short stay in a city; a layover is a wait between flights.
B They mean exactly the same thing.
C A layover is longer than a stopover.
4. Why does the text say listing countries is not a story?
A Because he has not been to enough countries.
B Because what makes a table lean in is one scene with something surprising in it.
C Because nobody is interested in travel.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Inversion After a Negative

GRAMMAR

Move the negative or limiting word to the front of the sentence, and everything after it takes question order. It is a spotlight, and you use it once or twice, never three times.

FormWhat it saysExample
Never / Rarely / SeldomAuxiliary before subject, exactly as in a question."Never have I seen anything like it."
NOT "Never I have seen"
Not only... but alsoTwo surprises in one sentence. The first half inverts; the second half does not."Not only did the food surprise me, but the silence did too."
Only when / Only after / Not untilThe inversion lands on the MAIN clause, not on the only part."Only when I stopped planning did I start enjoying it."
Borrowing do / does / didIf there is no auxiliary in the sentence, English borrows one, and the main verb goes back to its plain form."I rarely travel alone" → "Rarely do I travel alone."
The trapDo not invert both halves, and do not keep the past tense on the main verb once did appears.NOT "did the food surprised"
NOT "Only when I stopped planning I started"

This structure is formal and emphatic at the same time. In a written article you will meet it constantly; at a dinner table it works because it is rare. Use it for the one detail you actually want people to remember.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a hotel breakfast abroad and the conversation is in English. Choose the best thing to say.

Somebody asks where you have just come from. The best answer is:
A "Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Germany and Portugal."
B "Copenhagen. Never have I seen a city where everybody gets around by bicycle."
C "I am traveling since two weeks."
You want to say that two things surprised you:
A "Not only the food surprised me, but also the silence."
B "Not only did the food surprise me, but the silence did too."
C "Not only did the food surprised me, but also the silence."
You want real advice, not the guidebook:
A "What are the main landmarks?"
B "Where would you go that is off the beaten track?"
C "Is it a nice city?"
You want to say that you only enjoyed the trip once you stopped organising it:
A "Only when I stopped planning I started enjoying it."
B "Only when I stopped planning did I start enjoying it."
C "Only when I did stop planning I was enjoying it."
The restaurant next to the museum charged you three times the normal price. You say:
A "It was a rip-off, and I knew it before I sat down."
B "It was very expensive, the price."
C "I would have preferred to have had the possibility of eating elsewhere."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is scoring it.

Record about ninety seconds about one trip. Do not give the itinerary. Give one scene: where you were, what you could see, what surprised you, and what you would do differently if you went again. Use one inversion, and only one. Finish with a question you would ask somebody who has been to the same place.
