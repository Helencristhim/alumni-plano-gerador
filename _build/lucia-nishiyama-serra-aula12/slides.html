Chapter 4: The Continuous Code

Have Been + -ing

for · since · and when to drop it

The Progress Map

One Form, Five Moves

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"We have been piloting the new review process since March."

"The team has been ramping up capacity for two quarters."

"Adoption has been climbing every week since the launch."

"I have been keeping up with the safety literature since I moved into this role."

What do all the highlighted groups have in common?

PartFormExample
The auxiliaryhave (I, you, we, they) · has (he, she, it)We have ... / Adoption has ...
been + -ingalways been, then the -ing verb — that part never changes... been piloting / been climbing
for + lengthhow long the activity has lasted... for two quarters / for three months
since + startthe point where it began: a date, a year, or a whole clause... since March / since I moved into this role
MeaningThe activity started in the past, it is still running now, and you are telling the listener how long. Use the simple form instead when you want the number: "We have piloted four teams."

Negative: has not been growing · Question: How long have you been running it?

Focus

The Clock or the Score

CONTINUOUS = the clock

The activity and how long it has lasted. It is still running.

"We have been piloting the process since March."

for · since · all year · lately

SIMPLE = the score

The result and the number. It is finished, or it is counted.

"We have piloted four teams so far."

four teams · three milestones · twice

In a status update you usually need both: the clock first, then the score.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I am working here for six years."
"I have been working here for six years."
"I have been knowing this team for twenty years."
"I have known this team for twenty years."
"We have been piloting the process since five months."
"We have been piloting the process for five months."

Duration always takes have been. State verbs take the simple form. For + a length of time, since + a starting point.

Where Does It Stand

M
Lucia, before the board sees the deck, give me the honest status of the review program.
L
We have been piloting it since March, so we are in month five, and adoption has been climbing every week since the launch.
M
How many teams are actually on it?
L
We have piloted four so far, and two of them are already live. That number is the score, not the story.
M
And the platform side? I keep hearing they are stretched.
L
They have been ramping up capacity for two quarters, but the workload has been growing faster than headcount all year.
M
Is anything stalled?
L
Nothing is stalled. We have been iterating on the intake design for three months, which is longer than I wanted, and I have been keeping up with the safety literature since I took the role, so the direction is still right.
M
Good. Give the board exactly that, in exactly that order.

Listening 2

The Correction She Makes Most Often

An interview coach names one habit. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. Why does she say "I am working here for six years" lands wrong?
Because to an American ear the present continuous points at right now or at next week, never at six years of history.
2. When does she say you should drop the continuous completely?
When the interviewer wants a number rather than a story -- a score takes the simple form.
3. Which verb does she use as her example of one that never takes the continuous?
"Know" -- I have known this team for twenty years, never "I have been knowing".
