Lesson 25 -- Pre-class

Cross-Border Transactions -- One Deal, Three Countries

Prepare a deal with a seller in New York, a buyer in Madrid and a target in Brazil, and compare the three countries. New vocabulary: cross-border, counterpart, local counsel, exchange rate, withholding tax, double taxation, antitrust clearance, condition precedent, apostille, prevailing language. Grammar: comparatives and superlatives -- slower than, more complicated than, not as fast as, the longest, the most complicated.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Cross-border -- involving companies or rules from two or more countries
"This is a cross-border deal, with three countries on one table."
Counterpart -- the person who does the same job as you on the other side of a deal
"My counterpart in New York is the seller's lawyer."
Local counsel -- a law firm you hire in another country because it knows the law there
"Our local counsel in Sao Paulo says it takes ninety days."
Exchange rate -- the value of one currency when you change it into another currency
"The exchange rate moved again, so the deal costs more in euros."
Withholding tax -- tax that the payer keeps back from a payment and sends to the government
"Brazil takes withholding tax on the payment to New York."
Double taxation -- paying tax twice on the same money, because two countries tax it
"A tax treaty exists to prevent double taxation."
Antitrust clearance -- official approval from the competition authority before a deal can close
"We need antitrust clearance from CADE before we close."
Condition precedent -- something that must happen before the deal can close
"The clearance is a condition precedent in the contract."
Apostille -- an official certificate that makes a public document valid in another country
"Every power of attorney needs an apostille before it travels."
Prevailing language -- the language that wins when two versions of a contract say different things
"English is the prevailing language of this contract."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The Campinas deal is a cross-border deal: the seller is a fund in New York, the buyer is the group in Madrid, and the target is in Brazil. Simone compares the three countries before the call. The Brazilian side is the most complicated. The group needs antitrust clearance from CADE, and CADE is slower than the authority in Spain, so the clearance is a condition precedent in the contract. The local counsel in Sao Paulo says it takes about ninety days, which makes it the longest step in the deal. The Spanish side is easier than the Brazilian side, but it is not as fast as Javier would like, because every power of attorney needs an apostille. Then there is the money. The price is in dollars, and the exchange rate is less stable than last year. The seller's lawyer in New York, Simone's counterpart, says the Brazilian withholding tax is higher than she expected, and nobody wants double taxation. Finally, the contract has two versions, and English will be the prevailing language, because one language is safer than two.

1. Why is the Brazilian side the most complicated?
A Because the price is in reais.
B Because the group needs antitrust clearance, and CADE is slower than the Spanish authority.
C Because nobody in Brazil speaks English.
2. Why is the Spanish side not as fast as Javier would like?
A Because every power of attorney needs an apostille.
B Because the Spanish authority takes ninety days.
C Because the contract is in Spanish.
3. Why will English be the prevailing language?
A Because it is cheaper than a translation.
B Because the fund is the buyer.
C Because one language is safer than two versions with the same weight.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

In a cross-border deal, everybody compares: this country is slower, that tax is higher, this step is the longest. Short words (slow, long, high, safe) take -er than for two things and the -est for the whole group. Long words (complicated, expensive, stable) take more / less ... than and the most. Never use both: "more slower" is wrong. And not as ... as is a softer way to say "less".

FormUseExample
short word + -er thancompare two things"CADE is slower than the Spanish authority."
more / less + long word + thancompare two things"Brazil is more complicated than Spain."
(not) as + adjective + asthe same, or not the same"Spain is not as fast as we would like."
the + short word + -estthe top of a group"The clearance is the longest step."
the most + long wordthe top of a group"Brazil is the most complicated side."

Key point: two irregular forms you use every week -- good, better, the best; bad, worse, the worst. Questions work the same way: "Which country is the slowest?" "Is Spain faster than Brazil?"

Stage 2: Put the Deal in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the steps of a cross-border deal in the correct order.

?You list every condition precedent and start the slowest one first, usually the antitrust clearance.
?You agree on the prevailing language and get an apostille on every document that has to travel.
?You map the deal: the countries, your counterpart on each side, and the law of the contract.
?You check the money: the exchange rate, the withholding tax, and the treaty against double taxation.
?You hire local counsel in each country, because the rules there are different from home.
