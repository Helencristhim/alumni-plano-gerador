Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

How Does She Compare? -- Comparatives in HR Models and Companies

Comparing companies, offers and people models in English: where you are better, the number that proves it, and where you are honestly worse. Key words: benefits, bonus, flexibility, hybrid, career path, workforce, perk, reputation, growth, competitor. Structures: short adjectives with -er, long adjectives with more and less, than, the irregulars better and worse, and as ... as.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the definition. These are the words of every job offer.

Benefits -- what a company gives you besides your salary
"Our benefits are better than the market average."
Bonus -- extra money you get for reaching a target
"Their bonus is bigger, but it is harder to reach."
Flexibility -- freedom to choose when and where you work
"We offer more flexibility than most retail companies."
Hybrid -- part of the week at the office, part at home
"Our model is hybrid: three days at the office."
Career path -- the steps you can take to grow inside a company
"Our career path is clearer than it was two years ago."
Workforce -- all the people who work in a company
"Sixty per cent of our workforce is in the stores."
Perk -- a small extra advantage of a job
"The gym is a perk, not a benefit."
Reputation -- what people say and think about a company
"Our reputation is stronger after the certification."
Growth -- getting bigger, or moving up in your career
"They offer faster growth, but longer hours."
Competitor -- another company that wants the same people or customers
"Our biggest competitor pays more, and loses more people."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Viviane compares her company with the competitor across the street every year. Their bonus is bigger than hers, and their average salary is higher than the sector average. On money, her company is worse. But her group is more flexible than the other three: two of them still ask for five days at the office, and her model is hybrid. Her career path is clearer than theirs, because they published it last year. And her store turnover is lower than the sector average — eleven points lower. Her salary is as competitive as the market, but not better. So when a candidate asks why she should choose them, Viviane says the true thing: "Our reputation is stronger than our salary."

1. Why does the text say "bigger" but "more flexible"?
A Because short adjectives take -er and long adjectives take more.
B Because "flexible" is an irregular adjective.
C Because the two sentences are in different tenses.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "Their bonus is more bigger than ours."
B "Their bonus is bigger than ours."
C "Their bonus is bigger that ours."
3. The text says her company is "worse" on money. Why not "more bad"?
A Because "bad" is irregular: bad becomes worse.
B Because "more bad" is too direct.
C Because "bad" has one syllable, so it takes -er: badder.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives -- Counting the Syllables

GRAMMAR

English asks one question before it compares: is the adjective short or long?

FormUseExample
short adjective + -erOne syllable, or two ending in -y (y becomes i).big → bigger · low → lower · easy → easier
more / less + long adjectiveTwo syllables or more. Never add -er to these.more flexible · less expensive
always with thanNever that, and never leave it out.Our turnover is lower than the market.
irregularThree adjectives ignore the rules.good → better · bad → worse · far → further
as ... asWhen the two things are equal.Our salary is as competitive as theirs.
the trapNever both forms in the same adjective.Never more bigger, never more better.
