Chapter 1: First Words

One Page, One Decision

This closes the first block. You have divided scope, set the flow, asked for people, compared solutions, reported progress, faced a client, explained a delay and survived a bad line. All of that ends in the same place: a recommendation somebody has to sign.

Think of a decision you had to recommend. What did you say when somebody asked how sure you were?

Today’s Goal

Three Missions

1

Ten words for the structure of a recommendation.

2

Read a decision memo the way a director reads one: for the assumptions.

3

Say how confident you are, precisely, without hiding and without overclaiming.

The target in one line: this will work is a promise you cannot keep; this is likely to work, provided the soil report confirms the bearing capacity is an engineering statement. The difference is one modal and one caveat, and it is the difference between being trusted twice and being trusted once.

One Question

How Sure Are You?

Two sentences, out loud, right now.

Take a technical opinion you hold. Say it as a certainty. Then say it again with the honest amount of doubt in it. Ask yourself which version you would rather have signed your name to, three years later.

Chapter 4: The Code

Will, May, Might, Is Likely To

Saying exactly how sure you are

Grammar Discovery

Order These From Most to Least Sure

"Option A will carry the future load." (a promise)

"Option A is likely to carry the future load." (probable)

"Option A may need a thicker slab." (possible)

"Option C is unlikely to meet the programme." (probably not)

Each blue expression sits at a different point on the same line, from a promise at one end to a near refusal at the other. Read the four out loud and ask, for each one: would I sign my name under this in three years?

Chapter 5: Real Speed

The Memo Itself, and Two Voices

One document, two recordings

Real Document

One Page, One Signature

DECISION MEMO
Industrial Block 2 · main frame · for decision by the project director
RecommendationOption A — reinforced concrete frame
RationaleFuture load capacity, first of three criteria
Criteria usedFuture capacity · programme · total installed cost
Ruled outOption C — on programme, not on cost
AssumptionBearing capacity above 200 kPa — not yet confirmed
ConstraintExisting foundation line cannot move
CaveatSlab may need to be thicker in the equipment area (+1 week)
Three lines carry all the doubt in this memo, and they are the three the reader looks for: the assumption that has not been proved, the constraint that could not be changed, and the caveat that limits the recommendation. Everything else is settled.
1. Which line could still be proved wrong by a document, and how would you say it?
The assumption: the recommendation holds provided that the soil report confirms a bearing capacity above 200 kPa.
2. What is the difference between the Assumption line and the Constraint line?
The assumption can be tested by the soil report; the constraint, the existing foundation line, cannot be changed at all.
3. Turn the Caveat line into a spoken sentence with the right modal.
One caveat: the slab may need to be thicker in the equipment area, which would add about a week.
Listening 1 · The Director

The Memo I Trust

A director explains how she reads a recommendation. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say she is really judging when she reads a memo?
Whether the person writing has shown her the edge of what they know.
2. Which memos worry her, and why?
The confident ones with no caveats, because nothing in a project is that certain.
3. What question does she ask in the meeting?
What would have to be true for this to be wrong.
Listening 2 · The Engineer Who Promised

The Word I Do Not Use Now

An engineer describes a recommendation he overstated eighteen months earlier. Sound first — no text.

1. What word did he use eighteen months ago that he avoids now?
Would, as a promise, instead of was likely to.
2. What did the twenty per cent turn out to be?
A ground condition in one corner of the site, which cost six weeks and a lot of money.
3. What does he answer when somebody asks whether he is being evasive?
That he is being exact, and that a promise only means something if he keeps it for promises.
