When Things Go Wrong -- Staying in the Conversation

What to say in the three seconds after you did not understand, while the room is waiting. Key words: to catch, to follow, to clarify, to rephrase, to misunderstand, to double-check, in other words, to interrupt. Structure: modal verbs could, should and might for repair and polite requests.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. None of these is about English. All of them are about staying in the room.

To catch -- to hear and understand a word at the moment it is said
"Sorry, I did not catch the last part of your question."
To follow -- to understand an idea while somebody is explaining it
"I am not following. Could you go back one step?"
To clarify -- to make an idea clearer, with different words
"Could you clarify what you mean by severe cases?"
To rephrase -- to say the same thing again, in different words
"Let me rephrase that. My question is about the control group."
To misunderstand -- to think somebody said one thing when they said another
"I think I misunderstood your question. May I try again?"
To double-check -- to confirm something you think you already understood
"Let me double-check: you mean the second group, not the first?"
In other words -- the phrase you use before you say it a simpler way
"In other words, you are asking whether it is age or severity."
To interrupt -- to speak while somebody else is still speaking
"Sorry to interrupt, but could you say that number again?"

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The question was long, fast and full of small clauses, and Dejair caught about half of it. He does not go silent, and he does not apologise for his English. He says: "Sorry, I did not catch the second part. Could you say it again, a little more slowly?" She repeats it in one short sentence. He checks the core: "In other words, you are asking about the control group." She says yes. Then he takes the blame off her before he answers: "I might have misunderstood earlier, so let me be clear." And only then does he answer. Four moves, about eight seconds, and the answer he finally gives is more precise than it would have been without them.
Why does he say "Could you say it again" and not "Can you say it again"?
A Because can is grammatically wrong here
B Because could is the safer choice in front of a room full of strangers
C Because the question was long
What does "I might have misunderstood" do in that moment?
A It takes the blame off the other person, so nobody is embarrassed
B It says his English is not good enough
C It ends the conversation politely

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Could, Should and Might

GRAMMAR

These are modals. After every one of them comes the plain verb, with no to and no -s. What changes is not the meaning of the request -- it is how careful it sounds.

direct, among colleaguescan + verbCan you repeat that?
careful, in front of the roomcould + verbCould you repeat that, please?
asking about the right momentshould + verbShould I ask now or wait until the end?
taking the blame yourselfmight have + past participleI might have misunderstood -- did you say the second group?
advice to somebody senioryou might want to + verbYou might want to check the follow-up in that paper.

No to after a modal: "Could you to repeat" is wrong. And the adverb is slowly, not slow: "speak more slowly".

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A "Repeat, please."
B "Could you speak a little more slowly, please?"
C "Sorry, my English is very bad."
You think she said the second group, but you are not sure. What do you say?
A "I might have misunderstood. Did you say the second group?"
B "I do not understand."
C "Could you to repeat that?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Find a recorded talk in English with a question and answer session at the end -- any congress on YouTube. Listen to one question from the audience. Then record yourself doing two things: the repair sentence you would use, and the core of that question in your own words, starting with 'in other words'. Do not apologise for your English at any point.

Six minutes on why the same request sounds different depending on the words around it. That is exactly the difference between can and could that you worked on today.

Tip: listen once with your eyes closed. Then listen again and write down every request you hear. Mark which ones use could or would, and which ones are direct. Notice who they are talking to in each case.

Conference Talk
"Don't insist on English!" -- Patricia Ryan, TED

An English teacher of forty years argues that good ideas get lost when people are judged by their English instead of by their thinking. This is the talk to watch on a day when your English feels like the problem.

Tip: watch it once, without subtitles, and see how much you follow. Then watch again with subtitles and count what you missed. That number is your listening measure -- write it down and repeat the test in four weeks.
