I See It Differently -- Disagreeing with Elegance

Disagreeing with a senior colleague, a client or a friend, and still being on the same side at the end of the call. Key words: to beg to differ, to take issue with, a fair point, to see where someone is coming from, to play devil's advocate, tentative, contentious, a gray area, common ground, to sit on the fence, to agree to disagree, point taken. Structure: hedging and softened disagreement.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To beg to differ -- a formal, polite way of saying that you disagree
"I beg to differ: the clause protects the seller, not the buyer."
To take issue with -- to disagree with one specific point and say so
"I take issue with only one sentence in the draft, but it matters."
A fair point -- an argument you accept as reasonable, even if you still disagree overall
"That is a fair point, and I had not thought about the tax side."
To see where someone is coming from -- to understand the reasons behind someone's opinion
"I see where you are coming from, but the client will not take that risk."
To play devil's advocate -- to argue the other side on purpose, to test an idea
"Let me play devil's advocate: what if the tribunal agrees with them?"
Tentative -- not yet certain; offered carefully and open to change
"This is only a tentative view until we see the documents."
Contentious -- likely to cause strong disagreement
"Fees are always the most contentious item on the agenda."
A gray area -- a situation where the rules are not clear
"Whether an email counts as formal notice is still a gray area."
Common ground -- the points that two sides already agree on
"Start with the common ground and leave the hard part for later."
To sit on the fence -- to avoid choosing a side in a discussion
"You cannot sit on the fence forever; the client needs an answer."
To agree to disagree -- to accept that neither side will change its opinion, and move on
"We agreed to disagree about the film and ordered dessert."
Point taken -- a short way of saying that you accept what someone has just said
"Point taken. I will make the second paragraph softer."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The video call with Megan, a partner in New York, starts badly. "Their offer is an insult," she says. "We reject it today." Rodrigo does not beg to differ straight away. First he finds the common ground: "I see where you're coming from. It is a low number."

Then he names the one point he takes issue with. "I'd say damages are more of a gray area. The tribunal might not accept our numbers, and I'm not entirely convinced the hearing is our best option." His words are soft; his point is not. It seems to Megan that he is sitting on the fence, so she plays devil's advocate: "What if they refuse a counteroffer?"

"That's a fair point," Rodrigo says. "Wouldn't it still be safer to try? Fees will be the contentious part, but we lose nothing by asking." Megan laughs. "We may have to agree to disagree on how polite to be. Point taken, though. Send me a draft." His view was tentative at nine o'clock. By ten, it is the plan.

1. "I'd say damages are more of a gray area." What does I'd say do here?
A It makes the opinion sound less absolute, but the point stays the same.
B It shows that he is talking about the future.
C It shows that he does not really know yet what he thinks about the damages figure.
2. Why does Rodrigo say "It is a low number" before he disagrees?
A He agrees with Megan completely.
B He accepts the common ground first, so that Megan keeps listening.
C He has changed his mind and now thinks they should accept the other side's offer today.
3. "Wouldn't it still be safer to try?" What is this negative question doing?
A It asks Megan for a piece of information about the hearing that he does not have himself.
B It complains about Megan's tone.
C It makes a suggestion that is polite and hard to refuse.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging and Softened Disagreement

GRAMMAR

Keep the point hard and the words soft. One hedge is polite; three is vague.

ToolFormExample
Distancing modalwould / might / could + verbI'd say it's too early.
Tentative verbseem / appear / tend toIt seems to me that we are missing a fact.
Softened negativenot + entirely / quite + positive wordI'm not entirely convinced.
Downtonera little / slightly / somewhatThe fee is slightly high.
Negative questionWouldn't it / Couldn't we... ?Couldn't we wait for the audit?
Acknowledge firsta fair point / I see where you are coming from + butThat's a fair point, but...
The limitone hedge per sentenceNot: Maybe perhaps it could possibly...

Never: I am not agree · In my opinion, I think... · With all due respect, you are wrong · Maybe perhaps we could possibly... · a softened sentence that no longer says what you think

Stage 2: Disagree in the Right Order

Order

Your co-counsel wants to reject the offer. Put what you say in the order a good negotiator would say it.

?"I'm not entirely convinced the hearing is our best option."
?"I see where you're coming from."
?"If they refuse, we go to the hearing and lose nothing."
?"But I'd say damages are more of a gray area."
?"Wouldn't it make more sense to send a counteroffer?"
?"It is a low number, I agree."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A client says your draft is too long. You agree only in part. You say:
A "That's a fair point. I'd say the first section could be shorter, but the warranties need to stay."
B "No, it is not too long."
C "In my opinion, I think that maybe it is perhaps a little bit long, but I am not really sure about it at all."
At dinner, a friend says that Rio has the best food in Brazil. You disagree, with a smile:
A "You are completely wrong about that, and anybody who has ever eaten in Salvador knows it."
B "I see where you're coming from, but I'd beg to differ. Have you been to Salvador?"
C "I am not agree with you."
After twenty minutes, neither you nor your co-counsel will change your view. You say:
A "You never listen to me."
B "Fine. Whatever you want."
C "I think we'll have to agree to disagree. Let's ask the client to decide."
You want to suggest waiting until the audit is finished. You say:
A "Couldn't we wait until the audit is finished?"
B "We wait for the audit."
C "Maybe perhaps we could possibly wait for the audit?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Think of a real disagreement you have at work right now -- with a client, a colleague or the other side. In one minute, acknowledge their position, name the one point you take issue with, and suggest an alternative with a negative question.
