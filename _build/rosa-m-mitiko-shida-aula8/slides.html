Chapter 3: The Code

Normal Then, Not Now

used to · would

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Contracts used to arrive on paper."

"A courier would bring them twice a week."

"I used to spend three days a week in front of clients."

"The fleet used to be half the size it is now."

Three of these could take either used to or would. One of them can only take used to. Which one, and what is different about it?

FormWhat it saysExample
used to + base verbIt was the normal state of things, and it is not any more. Works for both habits and states."Contracts used to arrive on paper."
"The fleet used to be smaller."
would + base verbA repeated action in the past. Warmer and more narrative than used to — this is the storytelling one."A courier would bring them twice a week."
"She would open the folder and read it out."
The one rule that mattersWould does not work for states. be, have, know, live, own, believe take only used to."I used to have thirty accounts."
NOT "I would have thirty accounts."
Negative and questionThe d disappears after did: did not use to, did you use to."I did not use to travel every week."
"Did you use to work in tourism?"
How to open a storyStart with used to to set the scene, then switch to would for the actions that repeated. That is how a native narrates twenty years."Contracts used to arrive on paper. A courier would bring them, and we would file them by hand."

One test decides it: is this an action that repeated, or a state that was true? If it is a state, only used to works.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I use to spend three days a week with clients."
"I used to spend three days a week with clients."
"Did you used to work in tourism?"
"Did you use to work in tourism?"
"The fleet would be half the size it is now."
"The fleet used to be half the size it is now."

The form is used to, with a d — except after did, where the d disappears: did you use to. And would only works for actions that repeated: a state like be, have, know or own takes used to and nothing else.

Thirty Years in One Office

R
That photograph on the wall behind you? That is this office in 2004. Those shelves used to be full of folders, one for every corporate client.
R
I have walked past it a hundred times and never asked. How did the contracts arrive, before the portal?
R
On paper. A courier would bring them twice a week, and we would file them by hand. If a client called about a fare, you walked to the shelf and read him the answer.
R
That sounds slow.
R
It was slow, and I am not nostalgic. Nobody wants the courier back. But everybody used to know where the paper trail was, and now half of it lives in a legacy system two countries still use.
R
So what do you actually miss? Not the paper, I imagine.
R
One habit. When the folder was on the shelf, you would open it and see the whole client. Twice a week, problem or no problem. That habit died out, and nothing replaced it.
R
I think I take that for granted. On the screen I only ever look at the invoice somebody is asking me about.
Comprehension

About Renato

1. What does the photograph on the wall show?
The office in 2004, with shelves full of folders, one for every corporate client.
2. How did contracts arrive before the portal?
On paper — a courier would bring them twice a week and they were filed by hand.
3. Is he nostalgic about the paper? How do you know?
No — he says it was slow and that nobody wants the courier back.
4. What exactly does he miss, then?
The habit of opening the folder twice a week and seeing the whole client, problem or no problem.
Listening

Why the System Looks Like That

The opening of a training session. Sound first — no text.

1. How did contracts arrive twenty years ago, and how often?
On paper — a courier would bring them to the office twice a week.
2. What did the account manager do when a client called about a fare?
She would open the client folder and read the answer from a printed page.
3. What is the one advantage of the old way that she wants back?
That everybody knew where the paper trail was, and nothing was lost in a system nobody could access.
Listening 2

It Was Never About Aircraft

A veteran on what really changed in twenty years. Sound first — no text.

1. What do people expect him to say has changed, and what does he say instead?
They expect technology. He says what changed is the amount of time.
2. How often did he use to see clients, and how often does he see them now?
Three days a week before, and twice a month now.
3. What does he say this business has always really been about?
Who picked up the phone at six in the evening.
