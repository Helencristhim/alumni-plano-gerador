Lesson 4 -- Reading the Clause

Grammar Video
"Need to, Must, Have to, Should — Obligations in English" — Jennifer Banks

Fifteen minutes on the difference in strength between must, have to, need to and should, in American English, with the situations each one belongs to. Connection to Lesson 4: the same choice you make in a clause, made out loud in ordinary speech.

Tip: after each pair of examples, say one sentence from a contract you are working on. If you cannot decide between two of them, that is exactly the clause worth taking to somebody else.

Podcast
"Why terms of service got so frustratingly awful" — Planet Money, NPR

How clicking "I agree" became a binding contract, and how contract law was quietly rewritten to allow it. Clear American English at conversational speed. Connection to Lesson 4: it is a whole episode about clauses nobody reads — the boilerplate of today's text.

Tip: listen once without the transcript, then write down three obligations and one permission you heard described. Then read the transcript and check which modal verb each one used.

Reading
"Using will, shall and must in commercial contracts" — Blake Morgan

A drafting note written for lawyers on which modal verb actually creates a binding obligation, and why shall is the most argued-about word in the profession. Connection to Lesson 4: this is the grammar of the lesson treated as a professional risk, not as a language point.

Tip: read it once, then open a contract of your own and find one shall you could replace with must without changing the obligation — and one where you could not. The second one is the interesting case.
