Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Reading the Clause — What a Contract Actually Says

A business feature on the four things every contract is built from and the order an experienced lawyer reads them in. Key expressions: a provision, to terminate, a breach, to indemnify, a waiver, liability, a remedy, to enforce, notice, a party, governing law, to assign, a cap, boilerplate. Structure: modals of obligation and permission — shall, must, may, shall not and is entitled to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen appear in the text you will read in class, and in almost every agreement you open at work.

A provision -- a single part of an agreement that states one rule
"Each provision was negotiated separately, and it shows."
To terminate -- to bring an agreement to an end before it runs out
"Either party may terminate the agreement on thirty days notice."
A breach -- a failure to do what the contract requires
"A late payment is a breach, even when nobody complains about it."
To indemnify -- to agree to cover a loss that falls on somebody else
"The supplier shall indemnify us against any third-party claim."
A waiver -- a decision not to enforce a right that you actually have
"Accepting one late delivery is not a waiver of the deadline."
Liability -- legal responsibility for damage, loss or cost
"Liability is capped at the value of the contract."
A remedy -- what the injured side is entitled to when something goes wrong
"The only remedy in that clause is a refund, which is almost nothing."
To enforce -- to make somebody comply with a rule or a right
"A clause that nobody can enforce is decoration."
Notice -- formal written communication that the contract requires
"Notice must be given in writing, and a message on the phone is not writing."
A party -- one of the companies or people bound by the agreement
"Neither party may walk away in the first year."
Governing law -- the law of the country or state that applies to the agreement
"The governing law is New York, and that was not an accident."
To assign -- to transfer a right or an obligation to somebody else
"You may not assign this contract to a competitor."
A cap -- an upper limit fixed in advance
"There is a cap on how much either side can lose."
Boilerplate -- standard clauses that appear in almost every contract
"Most of the boilerplate is harmless until the day it is not."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are four words in an English contract that decide what your company is committed to, and every one of them is short. Shall and must create an obligation: this has to happen. May creates a permission: this is allowed, and nobody is forcing it. Shall not is a prohibition. And is entitled to states a right that belongs to one party whether the other side is happy about it or not.

The dangerous one is may. In Portuguese, pode carries permission and possibility at the same time, so a clause that GRANTS something reads like a clause that REQUIRES it. Either party may terminate the agreement on thirty days notice means nobody is locked in. Translate that as an obligation and you have written a contract that forces both sides out of a relationship they wanted to keep. That mistake is usually found two years later, by which time it is somebody else's problem.

The form is simple and it is where the errors live. After shall, must and may the verb is bare: shall indemnify, never shall to indemnify. There is no third person -s: may not accept, never may not accepts. And the passive is everywhere, because the obligation matters more than the person: Notice must be given in writing, payment is to be made within thirty days.

One last habit worth building. When you read a provision, say out loud which of the three things it does — obliges, permits or prohibits. If you cannot say which, the clause is either badly drafted or you have not finished reading it. That single question is what separates reading a contract from looking at one, and it takes about four seconds.

1. According to the text, what does "may" create in a contract?
A An obligation on both parties.
B A permission — something that is allowed but not required.
C A prohibition.
2. Why is "may" described as the dangerous one for a Portuguese speaker?
A Because it is rare in contracts.
B Because "pode" carries permission and possibility at once, so a right reads like a duty.
C Because it cannot be used in the passive.
3. Which of these is correct after a modal verb?
A "The Supplier shall to indemnify the Buyer."
B "The Buyer may not accepts partial delivery."
C "Notice must be given in writing."
4. What habit does the text recommend when you read a provision?
A Reading the whole contract from the first line to the last.
B Saying out loud whether it obliges, permits or prohibits.
C Translating it into Portuguese first.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Obligation and Permission

GRAMMAR

Portuguese covers obligation, permission and possibility with a very small set of verbs, and English splits them into different words. Nothing here is difficult to understand — the whole difficulty is that choosing wrong changes the contract instead of sounding wrong.

FormWhat it doesExample
shall · mustObligation. Shall is the register of the contract itself; must is the same duty in ordinary English. Neither leaves any choice."The Supplier shall indemnify the Buyer."
"Notice must be given in writing."
mayPermission, never obligation. The commonest misreading in a translated contract: pode covers both, and English does not."Either party may terminate the agreement."
shall not · may notProhibition. May not withdraws a permission; shall not imposes a duty not to do it. In practice both close the door."The Supplier shall not assign this agreement."
is entitled to · is required toA right and a duty stated as facts about a party, not as verbs. This is how the same idea appears in the summary of a clause."The Buyer is entitled to a refund."
"The Seller is required to keep insurance."
is to · is not obliged toIs to is an instruction with a deadline attached. Is not obliged to removes a duty and leaves the permission standing — which is not the same as a prohibition."Payment is to be made within thirty days."
"The Buyer is not obliged to accept partial delivery."

One habit for this week: in every clause you read, underline the modal and write O, P or X beside it — obligation, permission, prohibition. Clauses where you cannot decide are exactly the clauses worth taking to somebody else.

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to the summary of how an experienced reader takes a contract apart, then put the steps in order.

?The agreement arrives and nobody has time to read a hundred pages.
?The termination clause is read first: how it ends, and what leaving costs.
?The liability cap and the indemnity are read next, because together they show the worst case.
?The governing law is checked, because it decides whose rules an argument happens under.
?Only then are the promises at the front of the contract worth reading.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are explaining a clause to a colleague. Choose the version that says what you mean.

You want to say that nobody is locked in and either side can walk away:
A "Either party must terminate the agreement on thirty days notice."
B "Either party may terminate the agreement on thirty days notice."
C "Either party is obliged to terminate on thirty days notice."
You want to state an obligation in contract register:
A "The Supplier shall to indemnify the Buyer."
B "The Supplier shall indemnify the Buyer."
C "The Supplier shall indemnifying the Buyer."
You want to say that a written message on the phone is not enough:
A "Notice must be give in writing."
B "Notice must to be given in writing."
C "Notice must be given in writing."
You want to say the other side has a right you cannot refuse:
A "The Buyer is entitled to a full refund."
B "The Buyer may be entitled for a full refund."
C "The Buyer shall entitled to a full refund."
A colleague asks where the real risk in the agreement is:
A "In the first three pages, where the promises are."
B "In the liability cap and the indemnity, which are two sentences at the back."
C "In the boilerplate, which nobody ever needs to read."
