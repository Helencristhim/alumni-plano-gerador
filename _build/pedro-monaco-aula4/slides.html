Chapter 3: The Code

Four Words That Change Everything

Shall, must, may, is entitled to

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The Supplier shall indemnify the Buyer against any third-party claim."

"Either party may terminate the agreement on thirty days notice."

"The Buyer is entitled to a full refund."

"The Supplier shall not assign this agreement without consent."

In two of these sentences somebody HAS TO do something. In one, somebody is ALLOWED to. In one, somebody HAS A RIGHT whether the other side likes it or not. Which is which?

FormWhat it doesExample
shall · mustObligation. Shall is the register of the contract itself; must is the same duty in ordinary English. Neither leaves any choice."The Supplier shall indemnify the Buyer."
"Notice must be given in writing."
mayPermission, never obligation. The commonest misreading in a translated contract: pode covers both, and English does not."Either party may terminate the agreement."
shall not · may notProhibition. May not withdraws a permission; shall not imposes a duty not to do it. In practice both close the door."The Supplier shall not assign this agreement."
is entitled to · is required toA right and a duty stated as facts about a party, not as verbs. This is how the same idea appears in the summary of a clause."The Buyer is entitled to a refund."
"The Seller is required to keep insurance."
is to · is not obliged toIs to is an instruction with a deadline attached. Is not obliged to removes a duty and leaves the permission standing — which is not the same as a prohibition."Payment is to be made within thirty days."
"The Buyer is not obliged to accept partial delivery."

The trap is may. In Portuguese pode carries permission and possibility at once, so a clause that grants a right reads like a clause that imposes a duty. Every misread contract you will ever see has one of these four words at the centre of it.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Either party must terminate the agreement on thirty days notice."
"Either party may terminate the agreement on thirty days notice."
"The Supplier shall to indemnify the Buyer."
"The Supplier shall indemnify the Buyer."
"The Buyer is entitled to receive a refund of the price paid, and may not accepts partial delivery."
"The Buyer is entitled to a refund of the price paid, and may not accept partial delivery."

The first is not a grammar mistake at all — it is a different contract. Must forces both sides to end the agreement; may lets them. The second is the modal with to, which never happens: after shall, must and may the verb is bare. The third is the third person -s surviving after a modal, which is the same rule seen from the other side.

Listening 1

The Shortest Paragraph

A contracts lawyer on the clause she reads first. Sound first — no text.

1. Which three clauses does she go to first, and in what order?
Termination, then the liability cap, then the governing law.
2. What does each of the three tell her?
Termination: how it ends and what leaving costs. The cap: the size of the worst possible day. Governing law: whose rules the argument happens under.
3. Why does she say the front of the contract can wait?
It is the part both sides are enthusiastic about, and enthusiasm is what changes least — reading it first burns your attention before the paragraph that matters.
Listening 2

One Word, Two Contracts

A translator on the mistake that gets found two years later. Sound first — no text.

1. What did the English version actually say?
That either party MAY terminate on ninety days notice — a permission.
2. What did the Portuguese version say instead, and when was it discovered?
That either party HAD TO terminate on ninety days notice. Nobody noticed for two years, because nobody wanted to leave.
3. What is his rule about shall, may and must?
Never translate them by feel: obligation, permission and prohibition are three different commitments, and if the drafter cannot say which one the clause does, it is not finished.
