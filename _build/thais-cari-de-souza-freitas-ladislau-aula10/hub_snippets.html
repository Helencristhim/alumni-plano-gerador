Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An overview -- a short description of the whole thing, before the detail
"Let me give you an overview first, and then the numbers."
A takeaway -- the one thing you want people to remember afterwards
"If you remember one thing, the takeaway is that the rate is one cohort."
To walk through -- to explain something step by step, in order
"Let me walk you through the six lines on this page."
A headline number -- the single most important figure, the one on the first slide
"The headline number is four point two, and everything else explains it."
Context -- the facts around something that help you understand it
"Without context, four point two is neither good nor bad."
To recap -- to say the main points again, briefly, at the end
"To recap: one driver, one decision, one thing I need from you."
An update -- new information about something people already know
"This is an update, not a new project, so I will keep it short."
Next steps -- what happens after this meeting, and who does it
"Next steps: I write the note, you talk to risk, we meet on Wednesday."
A blocker -- something that stops the work from moving at all
"There is one blocker, and it is a signature, not a number."
A summary -- a short version of a longer thing
"The summary is one page. The detail is behind it, if anybody asks."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nothing in this lesson is new grammar. What is new is that you choose, sentence by sentence, which of the nine structures to use — and that you hold the whole thing together with six small signals that carry no information at all.

The first signal opens: "Let me give you an overview first, and then the numbers." The second says how many parts there are: "There are three things here. The first one is the rate." The third takes somebody through a document step by step: "Let me walk you through the six lines." The fourth gives context before a figure means anything: "For context, the baseline was three point one."

The last two close it. "To recap: one driver, one decision, one thing I need from you." And then the ask: "What I need from you is one signature this week." Take these six out and every fact is still on the table, but the listener no longer knows what is coming, how long it will take, or when it has finished. They cost two seconds each, and they are the difference between five minutes that sound planned and five minutes that sound translated. One warning, and it matters more than the rest: if you are short of time, say the ask first. The detail is optional. The ask is not.

1. What do the six signals in this lesson have in common?
A They all contain a number.
B None of them carries information. They tell the listener what is coming and when it ends.
C They are all used at the end of a presentation.
2. You have two minutes instead of five. What does the text say to do?
A Say the ask first. The detail is optional and the ask is not.
B Speak faster and keep the same order.
C Cut the numbers and keep the story.
3. Which sentence gives context?
A "Let me walk you through the six lines."
B "For context, the baseline was three point one."
C "To recap: one driver, one decision."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Six Signals

GRAMMAR

Two seconds each, and they are what makes five minutes sound planned.

MomentSignalExample
OpenLet me give you an overview · There are three thingsLet me give you an overview first.
OrderThe first one · the second · and finallyThe first one is the rate.
Step by stepLet me walk you throughLet me walk you through the six lines.
ContextFor context · to put that in contextFor context, the baseline was three point one.
CloseTo recap · if you remember one thingTo recap: one driver, one decision, one ask.
AskWhat I need from you is · Next stepsWhat I need from you is one signature this week.
If time runs outthe ask comes first, not lastThe detail is optional. The ask is not.

Never: a five-minute update that ends without an ask · a figure with no comparison · reading the slide out loud instead of speaking in full sentences

Stage 2: Put the Five Minutes in Order

Order

This is a complete five-minute update. Put it in order.

?"Let me give you an overview first. There are three things, and I will finish with what I need."
?"The headline number is four point two per cent. For context, the baseline was three point one."
?"It moved because the April cohort reached its third payment this month."
?"We are tightening the rule again, and the trade-off is two quarters of growth."
?"To recap: the rate is one cohort, not the portfolio, and it is already handled."
?"What I need from you is one signature from risk this week. That is the only blocker."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A "So, the rate is four point two."

You give a figure. Which version is an analysis and not just a number?
A "The rate is four point two."
B "The rate is four point two. For context, the baseline was three point one."
C "The rate is a little high this quarter."
You have two minutes instead of five. What do you do?
A Speak faster and keep the same order.
B Say the ask first, then as much of the rest as fits.
C Ask to move the item to the next meeting.
Somebody interrupts you in the middle with a side question. You:
A Answer it fully and start again from the beginning.
B Answer in one sentence, then say "coming back to the numbers" and carry on.
C Say you will answer at the end and hope they forget.

Lesson 10 — My Work in Five Minutes

Talk
Melissa Marshall -- "Talk nerdy to me" (TED)

Four minutes, and the shortest talk on this list on purpose. It is about the exact problem of your five minutes: a specialist explaining technical work to a room that does not share the vocabulary. Her rule is the one behind this whole lesson — give the context before the number.

Tip: watch it twice. The second time, write down the structure she uses, not the content, and compare it with your own page.

Video
Nancy Duarte -- "The secret structure of great talks" (TED)

Eighteen minutes on the shape underneath every presentation that works: where you start, where the turn happens, and how it ends. She speaks slowly and repeats the structure three times, which makes it easy to follow and easy to copy.

Tip: turn the English subtitles on. Then take your one page and mark on it where her structure begins and ends.

Podcast
WorkLife with Adam Grant -- "Is it Safe to Speak Up at Work?"

Worth closing the block with. In lesson 5 you listened to it for the bad news said first; listen now for the whole shape of how the guests build an argument, and for the silences they leave between the parts. It is the same technique your last recording needs.

Tip: pick two minutes of the transcript, read them out loud at half speed, and leave a silence at every full stop.
