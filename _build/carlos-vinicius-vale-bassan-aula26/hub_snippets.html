Thinking on Your Feet -- Answering Without Preparation

At the leadership offsite, the questions come with no warning. Key words: impromptu, off the top of my head, buy time, ramble, gut feeling, ballpark figure, educated guess, on the fly. Structure: modals of deduction -- must, might, could, can't (+ have + past participle).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Impromptu -- done or said without preparation
"The CEO asked for an impromptu update on the integration."
Off the top of my head -- without checking or thinking for long
"Off the top of my head, it was about two hundred thousand."
Buy time -- to get a few more seconds or minutes before you answer
"Saying good question is a polite way to buy time."
Ramble -- to talk for too long without a clear point
"When I am nervous, I ramble, and my main point disappears."
Gut feeling -- a strong feeling that you cannot fully explain
"My gut feeling is that the next deal will be faster."
Ballpark figure -- a number that is approximately right, not exact
"I can give you a ballpark figure now and the exact number tomorrow."
Educated guess -- a guess based on some knowledge or experience
"I do not have the data, but I can make an educated guess."
On the fly -- quickly, while something is already happening
"We changed the agenda on the fly when the CFO arrived."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At the Northway offsite, the new CFO asks Carlos questions with no warning. He has no slides and no notes, so he says how sure he is with every answer.

When she asks why the Brightline team looks so happy, he says, “It must be the first payday. Every salary was correct.” When she asks about the next integration, he says, “It might take six months, but I need to check.” And when someone says the bridge had a thousand errors, he answers, “That can’t be right.” Later he thinks, “She must have read the post-mortem.”

Notice the pattern. Must = almost sure, yes. Might or could = maybe. Can’t = almost sure, no. After the modal, use the base form, or have + past participle for the past.

1. Why does Carlos say how sure he is in every answer?
A Because he has no slides and no notes, so he cannot check the facts.
B Because the CFO asked him to speak for a long time.
C Because he is sure about everything.
2. How sure is Carlos that the next integration will take six months?
A He is completely sure, because he has already checked the final plan.
B He is not sure: it might take six months.
C He is sure it will not take six months.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A “That mustn’t be right.”
B “That must to be right.”
C “That can’t be right.”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Deduction: Must, Might, Can't

GRAMMAR

When you do not have all the facts, use a modal to show how sure you are.

How sure?FormExample
Almost sure: yesmust + base formIt must be the old data.
Maybemight / could + base formIt might take six months.
Almost sure: nocan’t + base formThat can’t be right.
About the pastmust / might / can’t + have + past participleShe must have read the report.
No to after the modalmodal + base formIt must be (not must to be).

Never: It must to be · It mustn't be true (for a deduction) · She must read it yesterday · It might taking

Order

The CFO asks Carlos an unexpected question. Put his answer in a logical order.

?"I can confirm the numbers tomorrow."
?"For example, the payroll test should take one week, not three."
?"Off the top of my head, the next integration might take six months."
?"That's a good question. Let me think for a second."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Imagine your CEO stops you in the elevator and asks: “How is the M&A market going to change next year?” In one minute, with no notes, answer: buy time, say how sure you are with must, might or can’t, make one point with one reason, and stop.
