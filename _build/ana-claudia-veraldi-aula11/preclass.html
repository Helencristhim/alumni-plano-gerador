Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

Getting Used to the Quiet -- Normal, In Progress, Still Strange

Last week the habit was over. This week it is the opposite: the thing that is still strange, and the slow work of making it normal. Key words: to adjust to something, jarring, unsettling, to crave, deafening, bearable, to ease into something, culture shock, to be thrown by something, to grow on you, to find your feet, to take some getting used to. Structure: be used to and get used to, and the -ing that has to follow them.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To adjust to something -- to slowly change until a new situation feels normal
"It took me a whole year to adjust to the silence."
Jarring -- so different from what you expect that it gives you a shock
"After thirty years in the city, the first night here was jarring."
Unsettling -- making you uneasy for a reason you cannot quite name
"For the first year the silence at night was unsettling."
To crave -- to want something so much that the wanting feels physical
"For months I craved noise. I put the radio on for company."
Deafening -- so loud that nothing else can be heard, and, oddly, also used for silence
"The silence in that house is deafening. That is the English expression."
Bearable -- not pleasant, but possible to live with
"The first winter was hard. The second one was bearable."
To ease into something -- to start something slowly and gently instead of all at once
"She eased into the new schedule over three weeks."
Culture shock -- the confusion of being somewhere that everything works differently
"Nobody warns you that culture shock also happens on the way back."
To be thrown by something -- to be knocked off balance by something you did not expect
"I was completely thrown by how early everything closes here."
To grow on you -- something you disliked at first and slowly begin to like
"The quiet grew on me. It took about a year, and then it was mine."
To find your feet -- to become confident in a new situation after a hard start
"Give yourself six months to find your feet. Everybody needs them."
To take some getting used to -- to be hard at first, and to need time before it feels normal
"Four time zones in one working day takes some getting used to."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ana used to live in a city of twelve million people, twenty minutes from three supermarkets and a hospital. She does not live there any more, and the first night in the new house was jarring. Nothing was wrong. That was exactly the problem: for a whole year the silence was unsettling, and she used to put the radio on just to have a voice in the room.

Three years later the list has changed shape. She is used to driving twenty-five minutes for bread, and she is used to complete silence after eight. Neither one costs her anything now. The quiet grew on her: she goes back to the city twice a year and cannot sleep, which her brother finds very funny.

Some of it is still in motion. She is getting used to everything closing at six, and she is getting used to being the only person awake at five in the morning. And one line refuses to move at all: she still cannot get used to an empty street after dark, and she is no longer sure she ever will. Three years of culture shock in her own country, and every stage of it needs a different sentence.

1. "She is used to driving twenty-five minutes for bread." Why driving and not drive?
A Because the sentence is in the present, and the present always takes an -ing form.
B Because after be used to and get used to, the verb always takes -ing. There is no version with a bare verb.
C Because drive is an irregular verb and irregular verbs behave differently here.
2. What is the difference between "she is used to the silence" and "she is getting used to everything closing at six"?
A The first one is finished: it is normal to her now. The second is still in progress.
B The second one is simply a more polite way of saying the first.
C The first is about a thing and the second is about a place, and that is what changes the verb.
3. "She used to live in a city of twelve million people." What does this tell you?
A That living there feels normal to her now.
B That she is slowly becoming comfortable with living there.
C That it is over. There is no be and no get, so it is last week’s structure: a past habit that has stopped.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Same Two Words, Three Meanings

GRAMMAR

Look for a be or a get in front. That one detail decides everything that follows.

FormWhat it meansExample
used to + verbA habit that is over. Last lesson’s structure.I used to live in the city.
be used to + -ingIt is normal to me now. The adjusting is finished.I am used to driving everywhere.
be used to + nounThe same thing, with a thing instead of an action.I am used to the silence.
get used to + -ingThe process, not the result. It can still be happening.I am getting used to waking up early.
get moves freelyAny tense, because get is an ordinary verb.I got used to it · I will get used to it · I cannot get used to it.
The trapAfter be or get used to, never a bare verb.Not I am used to drive. Say driving.

Never: I am used to drive · I am getting used to wake up early · I use to live in the city · I am getting use to it.

Say it out loud too: in all three structures, used to is pronounced with a soft s, /YOOSS-tuh/, never /YOOZD too/.
