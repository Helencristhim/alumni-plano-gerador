Getting Used to the Quiet -- The First Year Somewhere New

The months nobody warns you about, and the three structures English uses to talk about them. Key words: to adjust, culture shock, overwhelming, eerie, homesick, to crave, solitude, to find your feet, a steep learning curve, unsettling, to ease into something, deafening. Structure: be used to and get used to for familiarity and adaptation.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To adjust -- to slowly change your habits so a new situation stops feeling wrong
"It took me a whole winter to adjust to the silence out here."
Culture shock -- the disorientation of arriving somewhere that works by different rules
"Moving three hours from the city gave me a small dose of culture shock."
Overwhelming -- so intense or so much at once that you cannot deal with it properly
"The first month was overwhelming, and I had expected it to be restful."
Eerie -- strange and slightly frightening in a way that is hard to explain
"The silence at three in the morning here is genuinely eerie."
Homesick -- sad because you are away from the place you think of as home
"I was never homesick for the traffic, only for two or three people."
To crave -- to want something so strongly that it is almost physical
"By week three I was craving noise, which shocked me completely."
Solitude -- being alone on purpose, and finding it good rather than lonely
"I moved here for the solitude and it still took months to enjoy it."
To find your feet -- to become confident in a new place after a period of not coping
"It was about six months before I really found my feet here."
A steep learning curve -- a period when you have to learn a great deal very fast
"Rural life is a steep learning curve if you arrive from a flat in the city."
Unsettling -- making you feel uneasy, without any obvious reason for it
"Having no neighbours within shouting distance was unsettling at first."
To ease into something -- to start doing something slowly and gently instead of all at once
"Nobody eases into a move. You arrive and the whole life starts on day one."
Deafening -- so loud that you cannot hear anything else — and, oddly, used about silence too
"People say the silence out here is deafening, and now I understand why."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ana used to live in a flat with traffic under the window, and for eleven years she never once thought about the noise. When she moved to the countryside she expected the silence to be restful. It was not. For the first three months it was eerie, and by the third week she was craving the sound of a road.

She was not used to the dark either, and she was certainly not used to driving forty minutes for a pharmacy. The whole first year was a steep learning curve: everything she knew about how a day works had been built for a city. It was overwhelming in a way she had not expected, because nobody warns you that the difficulty arrives in month three rather than week one.

It took her about a winter to get used to the quiet, and rather longer to get used to planning a whole week in advance. Now she is used to both. She goes back to the city twice a year, lasts about a day and a half, and comes home. She found her feet somewhere around month seven, and like everybody else she only noticed months later.

1. Why "used to live" in the first line, but "is used to" in the last paragraph?
A The first is a finished state in the past; the second is a state that is true now.
B The first is formal and the second is informal.
C There is no difference; both mean the same thing.
2. "She was not used to driving forty minutes." Why driving and not drive?
A Because the sentence is in the past.
B Because here to is a preposition, and a preposition is followed by -ing.
C Because drive is an irregular verb.
3. Why "it took her a winter to get used to the quiet" and not "to be used to the quiet"?
A Because get used to describes the process of becoming familiar, which is what took a winter.
B Because be used to cannot be used with periods of time.
C Because get is more polite than be in this context.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Used To, Be Used To and Get Used To

GRAMMAR

Three structures that look almost identical. One looks back; two look at now.

FormWhat it doesExample
used to + verbA habit or state that is finishedI used to live in the city.
be used to + -ing / nounAlready familiar with it, nowI am used to the silence.
get used to + -ing / nounThe process of becoming familiarI got used to driving everywhere.
why -ingHere to is a preposition, not an infinitiveused to driving, never to drive
negativeNormal negative, very often with yetI am not used to it yet.
any tensebe / get used to move freely in timeYou will get used to it.
the testIf accustomed to fits, use be / get used toI am accustomed to the quiet.

Never: I am used to drive · I am getting used to wake up early · I used to the silence now · it took me six months to used to it. Two of those forget that to is a preposition here, one drops the verb be, and one drops get and leaves the sentence without a verb.

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

Wes has just moved to Vermont and Ana is a year ahead of him. Put the exchange in a logical order.

?"Everyone told me the quiet would be the easy part. I was craving noise by the third week."
?"That happened to me too, and I felt ridiculous about it."
?"It is not ridiculous. Your ears spent thirty years filtering a city and suddenly there is nothing to filter."
?"The first winter here was genuinely unsettling. I am used to it now, but it took months."
?"How long, honestly? Because I am still not used to driving forty minutes for a pharmacy."
?"Give it a year. You will find your feet, and then you will get used to the silence without noticing."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

A colleague asks whether you have adapted to the countryside. You answer:
A "I am used to it now, although the first winter was hard."
B "I used to it now, although the first winter was hard."
C "I am used to adapt now, although the first winter was hard."
You want to say that becoming familiar with the long distances took six months. You say:
A "It took me six months to be used to drive everywhere."
B "It took me six months to get used to driving everywhere."
C "It took me six months to used to driving everywhere."
You want to say you have not yet adapted to planning a week ahead. The natural version is:
A "I am not used to plan a week ahead yet."
B "I do not use to plan a week ahead yet."
C "I am not used to planning a week ahead yet."
Somebody asks what your evenings were like in the city eleven years ago. You answer:
A "I used to sit in traffic until half past eight."
B "I was used to sit in traffic until half past eight."
C "I am used to sitting in traffic until half past eight."

Reflection
