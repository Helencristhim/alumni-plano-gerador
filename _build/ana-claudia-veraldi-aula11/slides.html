Chapter 3: The Code

Be Used To. Get Used To.

Three words that mean three different things

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I used to live in the city."

"I am used to living alone with the dogs."

"I am still getting used to driving at night."

"You will get used to the silence."

The blue one is about a life that ended. The green ones are about now. What is different about the verb that comes after to?

FormWhat it meansExample
used to + verbA past habit that has stopped. Last week's structure.I used to live in the city.
be used to + -ing / nounIt is already normal for you. The process is finished.I am used to living here.
get used to + -ing / nounThe process of becoming familiar. Still happening.I am getting used to the dark.
The trapAfter be/get used to the verb takes -ing, never the base form.I am used to drive → driving
Any tenseOnly be and get change. Used to never moves.I was not used to it. You will get used to it.
In one lineNo am, no -ing = the past. With am and -ing = now.

Two words carry the whole difference: am and -ing.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I am used to drive at night now."
"I am used to driving at night now."
"I used to live here, and I love it." (about today)
"I am used to living here, and I love it."
"You will get used with the silence."
"You will get used to the silence."

The second pair is the dangerous one: both sentences are correct English, but used to live means you moved away, and am used to living means you are still here.

Chapter 4: Somebody Else’s First Year

A Call with Wes

Five months in, and still counting the hours

Dialogue

You Will Get Used to It

W
Ana, I have been here five months and I am still not used to how quiet it gets after nine. It is pitch-black by seven and there is absolutely nothing.
A
That was the hardest part for me too. I am used to it now, but my first winter here was genuinely eerie.
W
Eerie is exactly the word. In Chicago there was always a siren, a train, somebody shouting. Here I can hear my own refrigerator.
A
You will get used to it, I promise. One day the silence stops being empty and starts being solitude. Nobody tells you which day.
W
I hope so. Right now I still crave noise, and I get homesick on Sundays, which honestly I did not expect at all.
A
Sunday is the worst day for that. Give it a year. This place grows on you, and by then you will not want to go back.
Comprehension

About Wes

1. How long has Wes been living there?
Five months, and he is still not used to the quiet.
2. What can he hear inside his house now?
His own refrigerator. In Chicago there was always a siren, a train or somebody shouting.
3. Which day of the week is hardest for him, and why?
Sunday. He gets homesick, and he says he did not expect that at all.
Listening

The First Six Months

What the hardest part really was. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What do people expect her to say was the hardest part, and what was it really?
They expect the distance, the roads or being far from a hospital. It was the silence.
2. How much did she sleep in the first month, and why?
About four hours a night. She was used to falling asleep with traffic outside the window.
3. How did she finally realize she had adjusted?
She went back to the city for a weekend and could not sleep there either. The city had become the loud one.
Listening 2

The Honest Advice

What he would tell somebody who is about to do it. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he say about the first month?
Do not expect to love it, because you will not. You are not used to the pace yet.
2. What does he call the trade-off?
Having to be far more self-sufficient. The store shuts at six, nobody delivers, and some weeks you make do with whatever is in the cupboard.
3. How long does he say you should give it, and what is his last warning?
Six months. It grows on you, but not on your schedule.
