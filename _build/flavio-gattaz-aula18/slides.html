Chapter 4: The Code

By, To, and Just Under

How English describes a number, and how it changes

Grammar Discovery

Where Did the Number Go?

"Revenue rose by 18 percent."

"Revenue rose to just under 485 million."

"There was an increase of 18 percent in revenue."

"The backlog is well over 900 million reais."

Compare the first two. Both are about revenue. Which one tells you how much it changed, and which one tells you where it arrived? In the third, what does of introduce, and what does in introduce? And in the last one, is 900 million exact?

ToolWhat it meansExample
rise / fall bythe size of the changeRevenue rose by 18 percent.
rise / fall to, from ... tothe new level, or the start and the endOverruns fell from 11 to 4 percent.
an increase / a fall ofthe size, as a nounan increase of 18 percent
an increase / a fall inthe thing that changedan increase in revenue
just under / nearly / almosta little less than the numberjust under 485 million
well over / just over / in the region ofmore than, or close to, the numberwell over 900 million; in the region of 15 percent
big numbersno -s after a number; millions of only without a number485 million reais — never 485 millions

In one line: by is the change, to is where you arrive; of is the size, in is the thing; and after a number, million never takes an -s.

Grammar Practice

Say the Number

Say it first, then click to compare

"Revenue: 410 million → 485 million." (rise from ... to) ___Revenue rose from 410 million to 485 million.
"Cost overruns: 11 percent → 4 percent. Say the size of the change." (fall by) ___Cost overruns fell by seven points, from 11 to 4 percent.
"Backlog: 912 million reais. Round it." (well over) ___The backlog is well over 900 million reais.
"Revenue: 484.6 million reais. Round it." (just under) ___Revenue was just under 485 million reais.
"Headcount: plus 12 percent. Use a noun." (an increase of ... in) ___There was an increase of 12 percent in headcount.

Real Document

The Form That Asks for Your Numbers

EXECUTIVE MBA APPLICATION — SECTION 3: YOUR ORGANIZATION
Example answers provided by the Admissions Office

Give approximate figures; round numbers are acceptable. For each figure, add one comparison that helps the committee understand its size.

3.1 Annual revenue: in the region of 300 million reais — about the size of the fifth-largest firm in our state

3.2 Employees: just over 600, including site staff

3.3 Change in the last three years: revenue rose by 40 percent; headcount rose to 610

3.4 One number you are proud of: cost overruns fell from 12 percent to under 5, against an industry average of around 15

Section 3 of 7 · figures may be given in the currency of your country
1. What does the form say about exact numbers?
Approximate figures are fine; round numbers are acceptable.
2. What does it ask the applicant to add to every figure?
One comparison that helps the committee understand how big the figure is.
3. In 3.3, what is the difference between “rose by 40 percent” and “rose to 610”?
“By” gives the size of the change; “to” gives the new level.
Listening 1 · An Interviewer

One Number, One Comparison

An admissions interviewer on how candidates use numbers. Sound first — no text.

1. What are the two ways candidates lose her with numbers?
Some give no numbers at all, only “a lot”. Others give so many numbers that she stops listening after the third one.
2. How does she want the headline figure said?
Rounded: “just under 485 million” or “almost half a billion”, never 484.6 million.
3. What should come after the headline figure, and what should the candidate do then?
One comparison, so she knows if the number is big or small. Then stop — if she wants the breakdown, she will ask.
Listening 2 · A Director

The Number Nobody Wanted to See

A construction director on presenting his worst number to a board. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the number, and how did the board react?
A cost overrun of twenty-two percent on a hospital project. The room went quiet.
2. Where did most of the overrun come from, and what was it without that?
Almost three quarters came from a ventilation change the client asked for after the pandemic. Without it, the overrun was around six percent.
3. What did the trend show, and what does he say really changed?
Overruns had peaked three years earlier and had been falling steadily. The number did not change — the comparison in the board’s heads did.
