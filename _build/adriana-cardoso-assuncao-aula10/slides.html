What the Document Never Taught You

Two Registers, One Meeting

Somebody who has learned English from documents arrives at an American meeting and finds that half of what is said is invisible in the grammar book. Nobody raises a topic: they bring it up. Nobody reviews the figures: they go over them. Nobody objects: they push back. And nobody revisits a decision: they circle back to it, usually on Thursday. These are not slang, and they are not lazy. They are the neutral register of the room, and the single-word version, used out loud, can make the speaker sound like a lawyer reading a statement.

The same person then writes the minutes of that meeting, and everything changes. What was brought up is recorded as raised. What was gone over is reviewed. What was pushed back on is objected to. The written record uses the single word, because a document is read by people who were not there and, sometimes, by people whose job is to interpret it. Neither register is more correct than the other. What is genuinely wrong is only one thing, and it is mechanical: with a separable phrasal verb, a pronoun goes in the middle. You bring it up. Never bring up it.

Chapter 4: The Code

Where the Pronoun Goes

Separable, inseparable, and the one thing that is never a matter of style

Grammar Discovery

Which Ones Can You Split?

I will bring the deadline up at the start.  →  I will bring it up at the start.
Let us go over the numbers.  →  Let us go over them.
We have to narrow the list down.  →  We have to narrow it down.
Nobody has signed off on the rollout.  →  Nobody has signed off on it.
Click to reveal the rule
Some phrasal verbs are separable: the object can go in the middle or at the end — bring the deadline up or bring up the deadline. But if the object is a pronoun (it, them, him), it must go in the middle: bring it up, narrow it down, point it out. Bring up it does not exist. Others are inseparable and never split, pronoun or not: go over them, push back on it, sign off on it, reach out to her — and notice that these ones carry a preposition with them. There is no way to tell by looking: you learn which is which with the verb, the way you learned the verb.

Closing the Meeting

M
We have four minutes. Anything else before we close?
A
One thing I want to bring up. Two site managers reached out to me this week about the March date.
M
And they are pushing back on it.
A
They are. And I want to point out that nobody has signed off on the global rollout, so we are arguing about a date that does not exist yet.
M
Fair. Can we narrow it down today?
A
Not in four minutes. We would have to go over the site calendars first, and I do not have them here.
M
Then let us circle back to it.
A
Thursday, and with the calendars in the room. If we circle back without a day on it, we will be having this conversation in June.

The Way You Speak, the Way You Write

Read the questions, then listen.

Bring up, go over, push back, circle back — because that is what the room says, and the formal word out loud can sound distant.
2. Which verbs go in the minutes or the policy?
The single words: raise, review, object, revisit — anything a lawyer might read takes those.
