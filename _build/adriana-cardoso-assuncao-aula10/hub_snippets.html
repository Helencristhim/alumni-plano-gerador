The Language of the Room

The phrasal verbs an American meeting runs on, and the single words the minutes use instead. Key words: to bring up, to go over, to point out, to push back, to sign off on, to circle back, to reach out, to narrow down. Structure: phrasal verbs, separable and inseparable.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each verb and read the example out loud. You will hear all eight in your first meeting in an American company.

To bring up -- to raise a topic in a meeting
"I want to bring up the March deadline before we go over the numbers."
To go over -- to review something in detail, step by step
"Let us go over the numbers before we decide anything."
To point out -- to draw attention to a fact people are ignoring
"I want to point out that nobody has signed off on this."
To push back -- to resist a decision or a demand
"If you want to push back on the date, push back today."
To sign off on -- to give the final approval to something
"Nobody has signed off on the global rollout yet."
To circle back -- to come back to a topic later
"We will circle back to this on Thursday, and that is the last time."
To reach out -- to make contact with somebody, usually first
"Two of you reached out to me about the March deadline."
To narrow down -- to reduce a long list of options to a short one
"If we cannot narrow it down to one date today, we lose the quarter."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

In the room, nobody raises a topic: they bring it up. Nobody reviews the figures: they go over them. Nobody objects: they push back on the date. And nobody revisits a decision: they circle back to it, usually on Thursday. Then the same person writes the minutes, and everything changes: what was brought up is recorded as raised, what was gone over is reviewed, what was pushed back on is objected to. Neither register is more correct than the other. Only one thing is genuinely wrong, and it is mechanical: with a separable phrasal verb, a pronoun goes in the middle. You bring it up, never bring up it. With an inseparable one, nothing moves: go over them, sign off on it, reach out to her.
Why is "bring up it" wrong?
A Because with a separable phrasal verb the pronoun must go in the middle
B Because bring up is too informal
C Because it is not a real object
Can you say "go the numbers over"?
A Yes — all phrasal verbs can be split
B No — go over is inseparable, so nothing goes in the middle
C Only in writing
According to the text, what decides whether you say bring up or raise?
A How formal the company is
B The channel: the spoken room takes one, the written record takes the other
C Nothing — they are interchangeable

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Phrasal Verbs

GRAMMAR

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle, and the two together mean something the verb alone does not. What you have to learn with each one is whether it separates.

separable, noun objecteither positionbring the deadline up = bring up the deadline
separable, pronounmiddle onlybring it up · narrow it down · point it out
inseparablenever splitgo over them · sign off on it · reach out to her
spoken roomphrasal verbbring up · go over · push back on · circle back to · sign off on
written recordsingle wordraise · review · object to · revisit · approve

Two things worth remembering: "bring up it" does not exist in any register, and the choice between bring up and raise is about the channel, not about how formal you want to sound. In the room, the single word can make you sound distant.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen, then record yourself. In a phrasal verb the stress falls on the particle: bring it UP, go OVer, push BACK. That is what makes it sound like the room and not like a textbook.

There is one thing I want to bring up before we close.
I want to point out that nobody has signed off on this yet.
I would push back on that date, and I will tell you why.
Let us circle back to it on Thursday, with the calendars in the room.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A "I will bring up it at the start."
B "I will bring it up at the start."
C "I will bring up at the start it."
You are writing the minutes of a meeting where two managers disagreed with the date. Which line?
A "Two managers pushed back on the date."
B "Two managers objected to the date."
C "Two managers pushed the date back."
Which sentence is grammatically impossible?
A "Let us go over them."
B "Let us go them over."
