Lesson 29 -- Pre-class

That Is a Fair Question -- Q and A Mastery

Field the difficult questions after a talk and say exactly how sure you are: hedging language ("It seems that", "tend to", "is likely to", "As far as I know"). New vocabulary: curveball, loaded question, deflect, concede, caveat, rule out, push back, stand by, get back to, grill.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Curveball -- an unexpected, difficult question or problem that you did not prepare for
"The CFO threw me a curveball about the exchange rate."
Loaded question -- a question built to push you toward one answer, or to trap you into accepting something
"Why did you lose control of the supplier is a loaded question."
Deflect -- to avoid answering a question directly by turning the attention to something else
"Do not deflect -- if you do not know, say so."
Concede -- to admit that a point is true, often when you would prefer not to
"I concede that the first delay was ours."
Caveat -- a warning or condition you add to a statement so that it is not misunderstood
"The number is right, with one caveat: it assumes the new route."
Rule out -- to say that something is not possible, or that you are not considering it
"We have not ruled out a second supplier."
Push back -- to disagree with a claim or resist a request, politely but firmly
"When he said the delay was our fault, I pushed back."
Stand by -- to say that you still believe something you said earlier is true
"I stand by the figure I gave you on Friday."
Get back to -- to reply to someone later, once you have checked the answer
"Let me check the contract and get back to you by Monday."
Grill -- to question someone intensely, with many difficult questions
"The partners grilled her for twenty minutes after the talk."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

On Monday night Maria Claudia prepares for the Houston call. She knows the partners will grill her about Pittsburgh, so she writes down the hardest questions first. For the October rate, her answer is short: she does not know yet, and she will get back to them by Thursday. For the loaded question -- "Why did you lose control of the supplier?" -- she will not accept the trap. She will push back: "I would not say we lost control. It seems that both delays had the same cause." As far as I know, the goods were ready on time, and she will stand by that. But she will also concede one point: carriers tend to change routes in the second half of the year, and a third delay is unlikely but possible. She cannot rule it out. So every number she gives will come with a caveat. She will not deflect, and if someone throws her a curveball, she will say so and promise a date.

1. Why does she say "It seems that both delays had the same cause"?
A Because she has no information at all.
B Because it is what the evidence shows, and she does not claim more than she knows.
C Because she wants to deflect the question.
2. What will she do with the loaded question?
A Answer it exactly as it was asked.
B Ignore it and change the subject.
C Push back on the part that is not true, then answer.
3. Why will every number come with a caveat?
A So that nobody reads a forecast as a promise.
B Because the numbers are wrong.
C Because Houston asked for it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging

GRAMMAR

In a Q and A, hedging lets you say exactly how sure you are: what you know, what you think is probable, and what usually happens. It is not a sign of weakness -- it is precision. After seem, appear, tend and likely comes to + the base verb; after it seems / it appears you can also use that + a full sentence.

How sureFormExample
it looks trueit seems / appears that + sentenceIt seems that both delays had one cause.
it looks true (past)subject + seems to have + participleThe goods seem to have been ready.
it usually happenstend(s) to + verbCarriers tend to change routes.
probablebe likely to + verbThe rate is likely to go up.
not probablebe unlikely to + verbA third delay is unlikely to happen.
possiblemay well / might + verbThere may well be another change.
the limit of what you knowas far as I know, I would sayAs far as I know, nothing is signed.

Key point: three traps. First, "is likely go" → "is likely to go". Second, "tend to changing" → "tend to change". Third, "as far I know" → "as far as I know". And one hedge per sentence is enough.

Stage 2: A Hard Question in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the steps of answering a hard question in the correct order.

?You say what you know for sure, and you hedge the part that is only probable.
?If there is a part you cannot answer, you say so and you commit to a date to get back to them.
?You listen to the whole question without interrupting, even if you already know where it is going.
?You add the caveat that the number or the forecast depends on.
?If the question is loaded, you take it apart and push back on the part that is not true.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best sentence for each situation.

A partner asks: "Why did you lose control of the supplier?" Which answer is best?
A "We lost control because the carrier changed the route."
B "I would not say we lost control. It seems that both delays came from one route change."
C "That is not important now. Let us talk about the budget."
You are asked for a number you do not have. Which answer is best?
A "I do not have that number yet. I will get back to you by Thursday."
B "It is probably around forty thousand, more or less, I think."
C "Next question, please."
You are asked whether June is guaranteed. Which answer is best?
A "Yes, definitely, one hundred percent."
B "June is likely go well."
C "June is likely, with one caveat: it depends on the carrier's new route."
