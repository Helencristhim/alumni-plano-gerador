Chapter 3 -- The Code

Exactly as Sure as You Are

it seems... tends to... is likely to... as far as I know

Discovery

Four Answers, One Question

A partner asked about the June delays. Take the highlighted words out -- what happens to each answer?

"It seems that both delays had the same cause."

"Carriers tend to change routes in the second half of the year."

"The rate is likely to go up again in October."

"As far as I know, nothing has been signed yet."

PartFormExample
it looks true, from what you seeit seems / appears + that + sentence"It seems that both delays had the same cause."
the same, with the subject firstsubject + seems / appears + to + verb"The goods seem to have been ready on time."
it usually happenssubject + tend(s) to + verb"Carriers tend to change routes."
it is probable (or not)be likely / unlikely + to + verb"The rate is likely to go up."
the limit of what you knowas far as I know · to some extent · I would say"As far as I know, nothing is signed."

Key idea: hedging is not weakness -- it is precision about how sure you are. In a Q and A you commit to what you know and hedge what you do not. Two traps: after tend, seem and likely comes to + base verb ("tend to changing" → "tend to change"), and one hedge per sentence is enough ("it might possibly perhaps be" sounds lost, not careful).

The Structure

Three Jobs in the Q and A

The same hedges, doing three different jobs.

1 · Answer without accepting the trap

"I would not say we lost control. It seems that both delays came from one route change."

You take the loaded question apart before you answer it.

2 · Separate what you know from what you think

"As far as I know, the goods were ready on time. The rate is likely to go up in October."

The room can see which sentence is a fact and which is a forecast.

3 · Promise only what you can keep

"December is likely, but it depends on one supplier."

A caveat today costs nothing. An apology in October costs the relationship.

Notice what is missing: "yes, definitely" and "no, never". Neither survives the next surprise.

The Wrong Question

Sound first -- no text on screen.

0:000:00
1. Which question does she say is actually easy?
The one you cannot answer: you say you do not know, and that you will get back to them by a date.
2. What is a loaded question, in her words?
A question with the answer built into it -- for example, why did you lose control of the budget?
3. What should you do before you answer a loaded question?
Take it apart: "I would not say we lost control; it seems that two costs arrived in the same month."
4. Why does she recommend an answer with a caveat?
Because it shows you have read the assumptions -- it does not make you look unsure.

The Hot Seat with Houston

T

"Thank you, Maria Claudia. Let me push back on one point. Two delays in two months -- is Pittsburgh still the right supplier for the joint venture?"

M

"That is a fair question. As far as I know, both delays had the same cause: the carrier changed the route. The goods seem to have been ready on time both times."

T

"Seem to have been? That sounds like you are not sure."

M

"I am sure about the plant; I have the dates. What I cannot rule out is that the carrier does it again. Routes tend to change in the second half of the year."

T

"So you are telling us there could be a third delay."

M

"I would say a third delay is unlikely, but it is possible, and I would rather give you that caveat now than an apology in October."

T

"Then why not move to a second supplier today?"

M

"I concede that we need a backup. Having said that, I stand by Pittsburgh for June. Let me price a second supplier and get back to you by the fifteenth."
