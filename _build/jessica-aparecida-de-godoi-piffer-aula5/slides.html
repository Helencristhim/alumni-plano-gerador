Chapter 3: The Past That Is Not About the Past

Real, and Not Real

The same word, and two completely different promises

Grammar Discovery

Which of These Will Never Happen?

"If I have time this weekend, I will finish the report."

"If I had more time, I would rewrite the whole process."

"If I were you, I would ask for one more week."

"If they move the deadline, we will be fine."

The blue ones could really happen this week. The red ones will not, and the verbs know it.

JobFormExample
it could really happenif + present, will + verbIf I have time, I will finish it.
it will not, or probably notif + past simple, would + verbIf I had time, I would rewrite it.
giving advicefixed: if I were youIf I were you, I would ask.
were, for every personI / he / she / it wereIf she were here, she would know.
softer than wouldmight, couldI might consider it. I could ask.
the past is not about timeit marks distance from realityIf I won tomorrow... (still the future)
the trapwould never goes on the if side. Not if I would have time. Say if I had time.

In one line: step the verb one tense back and the sentence stops being a plan and becomes an opinion.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Table After the Event

One conversation, two recordings, one set of cards

Dialogue

Dinner, and Nobody Mentions Work

G
There is a card on every table with a question on it. Ours says: if you had a year off, fully paid, what would you do?
J
Honestly? I would learn Spanish properly. I have been doing an hour a week for a year, and if I had a year I would not settle for an hour a week.
G
Not travelling? Everybody at this table said travelling.
J
A year of travelling is not for me. Three weeks, yes. A year, and by month two I would be having second thoughts about the whole thing.
G
That is fair. Mine is a bit of a long shot — I would try to write something. I have been saying that for eleven years.
J
Eleven years is a long time to say something. If I were you, I would start with an hour on Sunday and not wait for the year.
G
You sound like my wife. She has been trying to talk me out of waiting since about two thousand and eighteen.
J
Then she is right and I am only agreeing with her. What would you write about, if you started on Sunday?
Comprehension

About Greg

1. What would Greg do with a year off, and how long has he been saying it?
He would try to write something, and he has been saying that for eleven years.
2. How does he react when Jessica says a year of travelling is not for her?
He says "that is fair" and moves straight on to his own answer, without defending the table's opinion.
3. What does he say about his wife?
That she has been trying to talk him out of waiting since about two thousand and eighteen, and that Jessica sounds like her.
Listening 1 · An Event Producer

Why There Is a Card on Every Table

A producer on the question she puts in front of four hundred strangers. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say happens at a table with no card on it?
People talk about the sessions for four minutes and then look at their phones.
2. Why does she say the question has to be impossible?
Because a realistic question gets a realistic answer, and nobody remembers those. Nobody has a year off, so everybody has to invent, and inventing is where people say something true about themselves.
3. What does she say about the people who answer "travelling"?
That it is the safe answer, and that the interesting person at the table is usually the one who says something else.
Listening 2 · Science Radio

The Question People Answer Honestly

A short piece on why hypothetical questions get better answers than direct ones. Sound first — no text.

1. What two versions of the question did the researchers ask?
One group was asked whether they would take the job. The other was asked what they would do if a friend were offered it.
2. What was different about the answers?
The second group gave longer answers, named more disadvantages, and changed their minds far less often a month later.
3. What explanation does the piece give?
A hypothetical costs nothing to admit, so people stop defending a position they have not taken yet and just describe what they actually think.
Real Document

The Cards on Table Seven

CONVERSATION CARDS
table 7 · Jessica Piffer · seat 3
1. If you had a year off, fully paid, what would you do with it?
2. If you could live in any city for two years, where would it be, and what would you miss?
3. If you were starting your career again tomorrow, what would you do differently?
4. If you had to give up either reading or watching, which would you give up?
5. If somebody at this table asked you for advice tonight, what would it be?
Not one of these can actually happen, and that is deliberate. A question nobody can answer with a fact has to be answered with an opinion.
1. What do all five questions have in common in their grammar?
Every one of them is a second conditional: if plus a past form, and would in the other half.
2. According to the note, why can none of these questions really happen?
Because that is deliberate. A question nobody can answer with a fact has to be answered with an opinion.
3. Which card asks about something you would lose rather than gain?
Card 4, about giving up either reading or watching. Card 2 also asks what you would miss.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I step the verb back into the past when the situation is not real, and I never put would after if.
I can give advice with "if I were you" instead of telling somebody what to do.
I can soften an opinion with probably, might or honestly, so it does not sound harder than I mean it.
I can disagree at a dinner table and keep the conversation friendly.
I know the expressions: to be tempted to, a trade-off, to talk somebody out of something, in hindsight, to be torn, a long shot, to settle for, off the top of my head, to have second thoughts, it is not for me.
