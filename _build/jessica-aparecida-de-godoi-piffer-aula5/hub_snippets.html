If I Were You -- Saying What You Think Without Insisting On It

A dinner table, five impossible questions, and the form English uses for an opinion. Key expressions: to be tempted to, a trade-off, to talk somebody out of something, in hindsight, to be torn, a long shot, to settle for, off the top of my head, to have second thoughts, it is not for me. Structure: the second conditional.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To be tempted to -- to want to do something, even if you probably will not
"I was tempted to say no, and then I said yes anyway."
A trade-off -- something you accept losing in order to gain something else
"Working from home is a trade-off. You gain two hours and you lose the corridor."
To talk somebody out of something -- to persuade somebody not to do something
"My sister talked me out of it, and she was right."
In hindsight -- knowing now what you did not know at the time
"In hindsight, I would have asked one more question."
To be torn -- unable to choose between two things you both want
"I am torn. If I took the role, I would travel less, and I like travelling."
A long shot -- something with very little chance of succeeding
"It is a long shot, but if I asked, the worst answer is no."
To settle for -- to accept something less good than what you wanted
"I would not settle for that if I had another offer."
Off the top of my head -- without checking, from memory, so possibly not exact
"Off the top of my head, about forty. Let me confirm before you use it."
To have second thoughts -- to start doubting a decision you have already made
"I said yes on Monday and I was having second thoughts by Wednesday."
It is not for me -- a polite way to say you do not like something
"Everybody loves that series. It is not for me."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences that look almost the same. If I have time this weekend, I will finish the report. If I had more time, I would rewrite the whole process. The first one might really happen: it is a plan with a condition on it. The second one will not: it is an opinion wearing the clothes of a plan. Nothing in the words says which is which. The tense does.

The move is small and it does all the work. Step the verb after if one tense back — have becomes had, move becomes moved — and put would in the other half. The past there is not about the past. It marks distance from reality. That is why if I won the lottery tomorrow is in the past tense and about tomorrow at the same time, and why nobody finds that strange.

One phrase is worth learning as a single block: if I were you. English keeps were for every person here — if I were, if she were, if it were — and this is the standard way to give advice without giving an order. Alongside it sit the softeners: I would probably…, I might consider it, I could ask. In English an unsoftened opinion lands harder than the speaker usually intends, and that matters most when the other person is senior to you.

The trap for a Portuguese speaker is precise. Portuguese marks this situation with a past subjunctive of its own, and the English word that looks like its natural equivalent is would. So out comes if I would have more time. It is wrong in both conditionals for the same reason: would never appears on the if side of the sentence. Say if I had more time, and let would do its job in the other half.

1. What does the past tense mark in "If I had more time"?
A That the situation happened some time ago.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Second Conditional

GRAMMAR

Step the verb one tense back and the sentence stops being a plan.

JobFormExample
it could really happenif + present, will + verbIf I have time, I will finish it.
it will not, or probably notif + past simple, would + verbIf I had time, I would rewrite it.
giving advicefixed block: if I were youIf I were you, I would ask.
were, for every personI / he / she / it wereIf she were here, she would know.

the past is not about timeit marks distance from realityIf I won tomorrow... (still the future)
the trapwould never appears after if. Not if I would have time. Say if I had time.

Never: If I would have more time · If I was you, I will say no · If we will move to Lisbon, they would visit less · I would to consider it

Stage 2: Put the Advice in Order

Order

Somebody has asked you for advice. Put your answer in a logical order.

?"Can I ask you two things first, before I say anything?"
?"So you are torn between the money and the travelling."
?"If I were you, I would take it, and I would say that out loud on day one."
?"The trade-off would be the two evenings a week you are giving up."
?"In hindsight, that is the part I underestimated when I did it."
?"But it is your call, and I would not talk you out of the other one either."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A colleague asks what you would do in their position. You say:
A If I would be you, I will say no.
B If I were you, I would say no.
C If I am you, I say no.
Somebody asks for a figure and you do not have the file open. You say:
A Off the top of my head, about forty. Let me confirm before you use it.
B Forty. Definitely forty.
C I do not know anything about that.
Everybody at the table loves a series you dislike. The most natural reply is:
A That series is bad and you are all wrong.
B I have tried it twice, and honestly it is not for me.
C Yes, I love it too.
You want to describe an imaginary move to another country. You say:
A If we will move to Lisbon, my family would visit less.
B If we would move to Lisbon, my family visited less.
C If we moved to Lisbon, my family would visit a lot less.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Somebody at a dinner table asks you for advice tonight. Say what your advice would be, why you would give it, what the trade-off would be, and one thing you would do differently if you were starting again yourself. Not one word about tax.

Lesson 5 — If I Were You

Talk
Barry Schwartz — “The paradox of choice” (TED)

Twenty minutes on why more options make people less happy with what they choose — which is the mechanism behind every trade-off in this lesson. He argues by hypothetical: almost every example is a second conditional said out loud.

Tip: write down five of his if…would sentences exactly as he says them, then rewrite each about a choice of your own.

Podcast
Criminal — “The Mountain” (episode 365)

A story built entirely around a decision somebody could have taken and did not. The interviewer spends the whole episode asking versions of “what would you have done”, which is this lesson’s structure at native speed for thirty minutes.

Tip: stop at the halfway point and answer out loud, in three sentences, what you would have done at that moment. Then finish it.

Video
Brené Brown — “The power of vulnerability” (TED)

Twenty minutes from a researcher on behaviour, which is the reading you already do for pleasure. She is also one of the clearest speakers on TED for a non-native listener: long sentences, unhurried, and every argument built out of what she would and would not do.

Tip: she softens almost every claim. Count how many times she uses probably, I would say or maybe in the first ten minutes.
