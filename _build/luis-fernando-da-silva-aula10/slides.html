Today’s Goal

One Shape, Three Jobs

1. The Words

a division, a specialist, a representative, a sector, a point of contact, a vineyard, soil...

2. One Structure

the analyst who ... · the division which ... · the region where ...

3. Three Uses

a colleague, a company, and a valley where the nights are cold

Without this shape you are left with names and job titles, which carry no information. With it, you can tell somebody which one you mean, and why that is the one that matters.

One Question

Describe a Place You Actually Like

Pick a wine region, or a part of the country where your customers are. Describe it to somebody who has never been there, in four sentences.

Then do the same for one person you work with, in two sentences, without saying their job title.

Chapter 4: The Code

Who, Which, Where

One job, three shapes

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"She is the specialist who can approve this without going upstairs."

"That is the division which was created two years ago."

"The south is the region where most of our volume sits."

"It is a valley where the days are hot and the nights are cold."

Each blue word is attached to the noun just before it, and each one is doing the same job. Now try removing everything from the blue word onwards, and see what is left.

The noun isUseExample
a personwho (or that)the specialist who can approve this
a thingwhich (or that)the division which was created two years ago
a placewherethe region where most of our volume sits
Why it mattersthe clause says WHICH ONE you mean"the analyst" tells me nothing. "the analyst who handles the south" tells me everything.
When you can drop itonly when it is the object: the file (that) I sentthe file I sent yesterday · but never the analyst handles the south
Extra informationtwo commas, and never thatAna, who has been here nineteen years, will know.
The trapno extra pronoun after the clauseNot the region where the volume sits there. Say where the volume sits.

In one line: the clause is not decoration. It is the part that says which one, and it works the same for a person, a thing and a place.

Note: this is not the organisation chart and it never will be. The chart says who reports to whom. This sheet says who can answer a question in four minutes, which is a different piece of information and a far more useful one. It is kept by hand and it is out of date the day somebody leaves.
1. Turn the first two lines into full sentences, each with a relative clause.
For example: "Ana is the specialist who has been in the legal division for nineteen years and who can approve this without going upstairs." "Marco is our representative in the north, who knows every dealer personally."
2. According to the note, what is the difference between this sheet and the organisation chart?
The chart says who reports to whom. The sheet says who can answer a question in four minutes, which is a different and far more useful piece of information.
3. The last line is not about work at all. Why would somebody put it on a sheet like this?
Because knowing what makes a person talk for two hours is part of the same map. It is how you become somebody people want to talk to, rather than somebody who only appears when there is a problem.
Listening 1 · The Second Map

Two Hundred Names and None of Them Useful

Somebody who joined a group of this size and had to learn it the hard way. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say the organisation chart tells you, and why is that not what she needed?
It tells you who reports to whom, which is almost never the question you have. The question is who actually knows how something gets approved.
2. She gives two examples of people on her second map. Who are they?
A woman in the legal division who has been there nineteen years and can answer in four minutes what takes the official channel three weeks, and an analyst in the region where most of the volume sits, who knows every distributor personally.
3. What is her advice to anybody joining a company that size?
Stop reading the chart and ask one question in every conversation: who is the person to talk to about this. Ask it fifty times and you will have something the chart cannot give you.
Listening 2 · What Makes a Region

It Is Almost Never the Grape

Somebody who spends his life explaining wine regions. Sound first — no text.

1. What do people expect him to say makes a region, and what does he say instead?
They expect the grape. He says it is the place, and mostly the soil and what happens at night.
2. Why do hot days and cold nights matter, according to him?
The difference keeps the acidity in the fruit, and acidity is what makes a wine you can still drink with food twenty years later.
3. How were the famous names decided, and what makes a place interesting today?
Mostly in the nineteenth century, by whoever had a river to move barrels down. The interesting places today have the right soil, the right nights and no reputation yet, and are still run by families who cannot afford to make a wine that tastes like everybody else's.
