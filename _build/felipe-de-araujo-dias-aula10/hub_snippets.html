Getting What You Need Politely -- Requests and Permission

The words of asking and being asked: favor, heads-up, to chase, workaround, to sign off, reminder, to be swamped, bandwidth. Structure: can, could, may and would you mind, and what each one really costs. Mission: ask for an exception, and refuse one, without closing any doors.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Favor -- something helpful you do for somebody when you do not have to
"May I ask you for a favor before we start?"
Heads-up -- a warning given in advance so somebody has time to prepare
"Could you give my team a heads-up when your volumes change?"
To chase -- to contact somebody again and again because you are waiting for something
"I have been chasing that signature since Monday."
Workaround -- a temporary way around a problem, without fixing the cause
"We found a workaround, but the dock is still the problem."
To sign off -- to give official approval to something
"Could you sign off the amendment before Friday?"
Reminder -- a short message that helps somebody remember what they have to do
"This is a reminder, not a complaint."
To be swamped -- to have far too much work at the same time
"If I am swamped on Tuesday, I will sign on Monday evening instead."
Bandwidth -- the time and the energy a person or a team actually has available
"I will not have the bandwidth before Thursday."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Here is the whole email. May I ask you for a favor before we start? A quick heads-up: our board pack closes on Wednesday at noon. Could you sign off amendment 2 before Friday? Would you mind sending me only the two clauses that changed, so that nobody has to read forty pages? And if Friday is impossible, could you tell me when it is?

Four requests, four different doors. May I asks for permission and it is about me. Could you asks for an action and it works with anybody, anywhere. Would you mind is the most polite of all, and it takes the -ing form: would you mind sending, never to send. And after can, could and may the verb comes bare: could you sign, never could you to sign. English also has no I want that you.... The want disappears, and the question does the work.

1. Why is it Would you mind sending and not Would you mind to send?
A Because send is irregular.
B Because mind is always followed by the -ing form.
C Because the request is about the future.
2. What is the difference between May I and Could you in this email?
A May I is more formal, but they mean the same thing.
B May I asks for permission and is about the writer. Could you asks the other person to do something.
C May I is only used in writing.
3. Why does the last sentence exist at all?
A To make the email longer and therefore more polite.
B To leave the reader a way out: they can say later instead of no.
C To remind the reader of the deadline a second time.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Request and Permission

GRAMMAR

Four ways to ask for the same thing, and they are not interchangeable. The temperature changes, and so does what comes after the modal.

FormUse it forExample
Can you...?Fast and neutral, with people you work with every day.Can you send me the file?
Could you...?The safe default at work, with anybody, in any country.Could you sign it before Friday?
May I...?Permission, and it is about you, not about them. Formal.May I use your name in the email?
Would you mind + -ing?The most polite. Careful: yes means the person does mind, so it is a no.Would you mind signing it today?
No to after a modalcould you send, may I ask, can you copy. Never could you to send.
Saying no with a door openI am afraid I cannot this week · I would rather... · Could we do it on Monday instead?

The modal is half the job. The other half is the shape of the message: one sentence of warning, one request with the deadline inside it, and one way out. With those three, even a plain can sounds polite.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You need something from somebody who does not owe it to you. Choose the best thing to say.

You want a supplier to sign a document two days early. You say:
A I want that you sign it before Friday.
B Could you sign it off before Friday? Our board pack closes on Wednesday.
C You need to sign it before Friday.
You want permission to mention somebody name in an email. You say:
A Could you that I use your name?
B May I use your name in the email?
C Would you mind that I use your name?
You want to be as polite as possible about a signature today. You say:
A Would you mind to sign it today?
B Would you mind signing it today?
C Would you mind you sign it today?
Somebody asks you for something you cannot do this week. The best answer is:
A No, I am very busy, sorry, sorry.
B I am afraid I cannot this week. Could we do it on Monday instead?
C Maybe. I will see. It is difficult.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Think about one real thing you need from somebody this month, that they are not obliged to give you. Record about ninety seconds. Build the request out loud in three parts: one sentence of heads-up, one sentence with the request and the deadline inside it, and one sentence that leaves them a way out. Then imagine they say no, and answer that. Then turn it around: refuse a request somebody has made to you, give one reason and offer one alternative with a date. Use at least four words from this lesson. Do not stop to correct yourself.
