Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Convey -- to get an idea across so that the other person really understands it
"The memo was accurate, but it did not convey how urgent the deadline was."
Hedge -- to make a statement less certain on purpose, so that it cannot be used against you
"He hedged the answer because the ruling had not been granted yet."
Concede -- to admit that one part of the other side's argument is right
"I concede that the timing is aggressive, but the exposure is not zero."
Rebut -- to answer an argument by giving the reasons why it is wrong
"Let me rebut that with the treaty text before you decide."
Counterargument -- an argument raised against the one you are making
"That is a fair counterargument, and it deserves a real answer."
Tentative -- not final; still open to change if new information arrives
"My view on the ruling is tentative, because the authority has not answered."
Assertive -- clear and confident about your view, without attacking the other person
"Be assertive about the disclosure: it was filed, and it was complete."
Reframe -- to present the same fact from a different angle, so it is understood better
"Let me reframe it: this is a smaller change, not a full restructuring."
Overstate -- to describe something as bigger, safer or more certain than it really is
"I would not overstate it: the position is defensible, but nothing is beyond challenge."
Stand by an assessment -- to keep supporting what you concluded, even when somebody challenges it
"I stand by that assessment: the disclosure was complete."
Push back on -- to resist something and say why you do not accept it
"The client pushed back on our recommendation, and she was entitled to."
Walk someone through -- to explain something to somebody step by step, at their pace
"Let me walk you through the numbers once more."
On solid ground -- in a strong position that you can defend with evidence
"On the disclosure we are on solid ground."
Leave no room for doubt -- to say something so clearly that nobody can read it two ways
"On this point I want to leave no room for doubt."
Back up a claim -- to support a statement with evidence somebody else can check
"I can back up that claim with the treaty text."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Dana Whitfield has two opinions on her desk and they contradict each other. She opens the call without a greeting: "Your note says one thing and my auditors say the opposite." Leonardo does not shorten his answer. "Neither of us is wrong, and I would not overstate the difference. Let me walk you through where the two views separate." She wants a number, so he gives her one: fourteen million reais over two years, in the worst case. Then she attacks a word. Her note was told the risk was limited, and fourteen million does not sound limited to her. He concedes it. "Limited referred to the scope, not to the amount. The memo did not convey that, and it should have." When she reports what her auditors said -- that the whole structure would be challenged -- he does not agree and he does not fight. "They warned you that it might be. That is a fair counterargument, and I concede that the timing is aggressive. Let me rebut the rest: the ruling was requested in April, and nothing has been refused." She calls that hedging. He splits the answer in two, and this is the moment that changes the call. "On the disclosure I am on solid ground, and I will leave no room for doubt: it was filed, it was complete, and I stand by that assessment. On the ruling I am tentative, because the authority has not answered." She pushes back on the recommendation to restructure. He offers to back up the claim with the treaty text or to reframe it, and he ends where he started: what the firm recommends is a smaller change, not a full restructuring. She takes it to her board.

1. "The ruling was requested in April, and nothing has been refused." Why does Leonardo use the passive voice here?
A Because the passive voice is always more polite than the active.
B Because the action matters and the agent does not: nobody needs to be named or blamed.
C Because the passive voice is the only form allowed after a counterargument.
2. "They warned you that it might be" reports what the auditors said. Which reporting verb would have been weaker, and why?
A "Said" -- because it is neutral, and it hides the fact that they were flagging a risk.
B "Warned" -- because it is too emotional for a professional call.
C Neither -- the reporting verb never changes the meaning of the sentence.
3. "What the firm recommends is a smaller change." Why not "we recommend the client to make a smaller change"?
A Because "recommend" can never appear in a client call.
B Because a recommendation must always be written in the passive voice.
C Because "recommend" does not take a person plus an infinitive: it takes a gerund or a that-clause.
4. Why does splitting the answer into "on solid ground" and "tentative" work better than saying either one alone?
A Because it makes the answer shorter, and short answers sound confident.
B Because it shows exactly where the evidence is complete and where it is not, so neither part sounds like a guess.
C Because clients prefer advisers who never take a clear position on anything.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Register Control Under Pressure

GRAMMAR

This lesson adds no new structure. It puts three you already have -- reported speech, the gerund and infinitive patterns, and the passive voice -- into one sustained spoken turn, and adds the choice that sits on top of them: register. Formal advisory is what you write to a board. Semi-formal debrief is what you say to a colleague in a corridor. Both are correct English; only one is right for the room you are in.

What movesFormal advisorySemi-formal debrief
Modal verbswould recommend, would suggest, may, mightI would, I think, we should
The agenthidden: "the client was advised"named: "we told them"
Contractionsnone: "we have looked at this"natural: "we have looked at this and the risk is real"
Framing"It is our view that...""Honestly, I think..."
Length of the turnUnchanged. In both registers a good answer is four sentences: your view, the one thing that is genuinely uncertain, the point you concede, and what you would do.
Passive voiceAvailable in both. "The disclosure was filed", "the position can be defended", "before any filing is made". The formal register simply uses it more, because it keeps the agent out of the sentence.
Reported speechAvailable in both. "The client was advised that the ruling had not been granted" and "we told them the ruling was not in" carry the same backshift. The reporting verb still does the work: warned, confirmed, indicated, advised.
Gerund and infinitiveAvailable in both. recommend + gerund ("recommend avoiding"), suggest + gerund ("suggest revisiting"), agree + infinitive ("agreed to postpone"), and the gerund again after a preposition ("hold off on filing"). The pattern never relaxes because the register did.
The hedging scaleOne hedge protects you. Four hedges lose the room. "It may be" is precision. "I think maybe it could possibly be, perhaps" tells everyone there is no view behind it.
Concede, then rebutThe strongest answer under pressure gives ground first: "I concede the timing, but I have to push back on the exposure, and here is why." Conceding a small point is what buys you the big one.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each real conversation.

A client asks: "Can you guarantee that this structure holds up?" The best answer is:
A "Yes, absolutely. It will never be challenged."
B "I would not overstate it. I stand by the disclosure, and I am tentative on the ruling, because the authority has not answered."
C "I think maybe it could possibly be fine, perhaps."
The client's auditors disagree with your view. You want to answer without losing the room. You say:
A "They are wrong, and I do not want to discuss it."
B "If your auditors say so, then let us follow their view."
C "That is a fair counterargument, and I concede the timing. Let me rebut the rest with the treaty text."
You are writing to the client's board, not speaking to a colleague. Which sentence belongs in the letter?
A "We would recommend avoiding any structure that might be challenged by the tax authority."
B "Honestly, I would stay away from that one."
C "We recommend the client to avoid that structure."
A client interrupts you after your first sentence. What do you do?
A Stop there, and wait for the next question.
B Answer the interruption, then say "let me walk you through the rest" and finish the turn.
C Start the whole explanation again from the beginning.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Think of one meeting where somebody disagreed with you in English, without naming the client. Answer that person now, in ninety seconds and in at least four sentences: give your view, say the one thing that is genuinely uncertain, concede the point they were right about, and finish with what you would do. Do not overstate anything, and do not stop after the first sentence.
