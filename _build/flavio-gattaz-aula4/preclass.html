Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Why an MBA, and Why Now -- Reading the Program Page

Every program page says the same six things. The question underneath is the one the interview turns on. Key words: admissions, a cohort, a residency, tuition, a prerequisite, a deferral, faculty, to enroll, a capstone, to shortlist, to be on the fence, to make the case for something. Structure: purpose and result clauses (in order to, so that, so as to).

A residency -- a short period on campus inside a course that is otherwise online
"There are two residencies a year, one week each, and attendance is not optional."
Tuition -- the money a school charges for the teaching itself, before anything else
"Tuition does not include flights, and for a Brazilian candidate that is not a detail."
A prerequisite -- something you must already have before you are allowed to start
"Ten years of management experience is a prerequisite, not a preference."
A deferral -- permission to start the program a year later than you were admitted
"I asked for a deferral because two of our projects were finishing at the same time."
Faculty -- the teaching staff of a university, taken together as one body
"Half the faculty on this program still work in the industry they teach."
To enroll -- to sign up formally and become a student on a course
"Once you enroll, the deposit is not refundable."
A capstone -- the final project that pulls the whole program together at the end
"The capstone is a real problem from your own company, not a case study."
To shortlist -- to reduce a long list of candidates to the few who will be interviewed
"Nine hundred applied, ninety were shortlisted, and seventy enrolled."
To be on the fence -- to be undecided between two options, without leaning either way
"I was on the fence for two years. What moved me was seeing the ceiling."
To make the case for something -- to give the reasons that show why something should happen
"The essay is not a story about you. It makes the case for you."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A candidate writes the first line of the application: "I am applying in order to close a gap I can already see." That is a purpose, and a purpose in English takes a bare verb after to. He could have written to close, which is the neutral version, or so as to close, which is the formal one. What he could not write is for close, because for never introduces a verb of purpose — that is the single most common mistake a Portuguese speaker makes here, and it comes from the fact that one word in Portuguese does the job of two in English.

The second line changes shape, because the subject changes with it: "I chose an online format so that I could keep running the company." So that opens a full sentence, with its own subject and usually a modal — can, could, will, would. It is also what you use when the person who benefits is not the person who acts: I chose the evening group so that my uncle could stay on site. And for a negative purpose there is so as not to: I moved the trip so as not to miss the first residency.

Then there is the other half of the answer, which is not the reason but the consequence. "The residencies are one week each, so most participants keep working." That so has a comma in front of it and a full sentence after it, and it means the opposite of purpose: not why I did it, but what followed from it. The same job is done by which is why, pointing back at the whole sentence before: "The company grew faster than I did, which is why I am applying now." Admissions reads that line and knows what changed, which is exactly what the whole conversation is for.

1. Why is "for close a gap" wrong in English?
A Because "for" is too informal in an application.
B Because "for" never introduces a verb of purpose; that job belongs to "to".
C Because "close" is not a verb.
2. According to the text, when do you need "so that" instead of "to"?
A When a full sentence with its own subject follows, especially if the subject changes.
B When the sentence is in the past.
C When you are writing rather than speaking.
3. What is the difference between "so that" and ", so"?
A There is none; they are interchangeable.
B "So that" is spoken and ", so" is written.
C "So that" gives the purpose; ", so" gives the consequence that followed.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Purpose and Result

GRAMMAR

Two ways to answer why, and English gives them different shapes.

JobFormExample
Purpose, neutralto + base formI am applying to close a gap.
Purpose, formalin order to / so as to + base formI am applying in order to close a gap.
Purpose, subject changesso that + subject + can / could / will / wouldI chose it so that my uncle could stay on site.
Negative purposeso as not to / in order not toI moved the trip so as not to miss the residency.
Result, so + full sentenceThe weeks are short, so most people keep working.
Result, pointing back, which is why + full sentenceThe company grew faster, which is why I am applying.
Noun after forfor + noun is fine; for + verb is notI came for the network, but to learn finance.

Never: for improve my English · for that I can keep working · in order to keep running, so I chose it · the weeks are short, so that people keep working · I applied for close a gap.
