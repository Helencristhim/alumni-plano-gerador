The Target

Sixty Seconds, Three Moves

1. Your name, and the room hears it once.

Diagnostic

Who Do You Work For?

Somebody at the table asks: “And who do you work for?”

Answer in two sentences. Say what your clients sell, and say what you do for them.

Chapter 3: The Code

What I Do, and What I Do Not

One verb, three shapes

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I advise companies that buy and sell grain."

"We write the contracts, and we read the ones the other side sends."

"We do not go to a hearing every week."

"Do you work with contracts as well?"

Every red verb is exactly the same shape, in all four sentences. Now look at the blue words: they only turn up when the sentence says no, or asks a question.

JobFormExample
Something true every weekI / you / we + bare verbI advise grain traders.
Negativedo not + bare verbWe do not go to a hearing every week.
QuestionDo + subject + bare verbDo you work with contracts?
Question word in frontWhat / Who / How often + do + subject + bare verbWho do you work for?
Short answerYes, I do. / No, we do not."Do you read the contracts yourself?" — "Yes, I do."
How oftenevery week, twice a year, usually, neverI travel to Miami twice a year.
The trapno to be in front of the verbNot I am advise companies. Say I advise companies.

In one line: the verb never changes shape, and do carries the no and the question for it.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The First Session, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one letter

Dialogue

Ten Minutes Before the First Class

C
I do not think we have met. Claire Bennett, I teach on this programme. And you are?
E
Eduardo Alves. I am a lawyer, from Sao Paulo.
C
A lawyer covers a lot of ground. What do you do, exactly?
E
I advise companies that buy and sell grain. Corn and sugar, mostly, and a few clients who import diesel.
C
So you spend your week in court?
E
No, we do not go to a hearing every week. We write contracts and we read the ones the other side sends. I am a partner, so I answer for that work.
C
Good. Then you already read half of this reading list. Why this programme, and why now?
E
Because the contract is in English and the client is not. And you — what do you teach here?
Comprehension

About Claire

1. How does Claire open the conversation, and what does she say about the two of them?
She gives her name and says she teaches on the programme, and she says she does not think they have met.
2. Why is she not satisfied with the answer "I am a lawyer"?
She says a lawyer covers a lot of ground, so she asks what he does exactly.
3. What does she say about the reading list, and what does she want to know at the end?
She says he already reads half of it in his work, and she asks why he chose this programme and why now.
Listening 1 · The Welcome Session

How the First Week Works

A teacher opening the programme. Sound first — no text.

1. How often does the group meet, and on which days?
Twice a week, on Monday and on Thursday.
2. What three things does she say an introduction needs?
Your name, what you practise, and why you are here. She says that is enough.
3. What does she say the room expects, and what does it not expect?
It does not expect perfect English. It expects you to say what you think, and to ask when you do not follow.
Listening 2 · A Podcast

Do Not Translate Your Title

A coach on introducing yourself in a second language. Sound first — no text.

1. Which two sentences does he compare, and which one does he prefer?
He prefers "I advise companies that export grain" to "I am a specialist in agribusiness law", because the first one tells the work and the second one only gives a label.
2. What mistake does he say he hears every week?
The person who explains twenty years of career before anybody asks for it.
3. What is the one thing he says the room is listening for?
What the person actually does on a Tuesday morning.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Trade That Never Touches a Shop

A short business radio report on the grain trade. Sound first — no text.

1. Where are the three people in the chain the report describes, and what does each of them do?
A trader in Geneva buys the cargo, an insurer in London covers the ship, and a bank in New York pays for it.
2. What does the report say about the language of that chain?
Every step of it is written in English, and every step of it needs a lawyer.
3. Why does the report say Brazilian firms open a small office abroad?
Not to find new clients, but because the contract, the arbitration and the paperwork live in another language, and the client wants somebody who reads both.
