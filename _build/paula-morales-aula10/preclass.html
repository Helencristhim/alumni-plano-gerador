Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

What Do You Like? What Do You Want? -- Preferences, Desires and Goals

This is the lesson that closes the block, and it has one question only. Once a year Zurich sits down with each person for thirty minutes and asks: “What do you want?” — and most people answer “everything is fine, thank you”, go back to their desk and stay angry for a year. You will have three verbs so that you do not do that: like + verb with -ing (what you already do and enjoy), want to (what you want, straight) and would like to — which is only want in a tie. And here is the good news: you already learned this trick in lesson 9, when can got its could. It is the same move, with two other words. This Pre-class is a first contact: come to class with the three verbs in your ear, not on the tip of your tongue.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Six words — and all of them are about what you want, not about what you do. Listen to each one and repeat it out loud, alone. The four traps of the day are all the same one: the stress falls where you do not expect it. ka-RIR · ah-per-TOO-ni-ti · CHA-lenj · im-PRUUV. Tap the table on the strong syllable while you repeat — it works better than trying to remember.

Opportunity -- a good chance to do something
"This is a great opportunity for me." Say ah-per-TOO-ni-ti, with the stress on the third syllable. Put the stress at the end and the word becomes unrecognizable.
Career -- your working life, over many years
"I want to grow in my career." Careful: ka-RIR, stress on the second syllable — never "CA-ri-er". A career is not one job: it is all your jobs in a line, over twenty years.
Grow -- to become bigger or better
"I want to grow in this company." The sound is GROU — the W is not a separate sound, it only closes the O. A plant grows, a company grows, and so does a person.
Challenge -- something difficult that you want to do
"I like a challenge." This is trap number one: CHA-lenj, stress on the FIRST syllable, and the end is a soft J — never "cha-LEN-ge". Repeat it five times.
Goal -- what you want to reach
"My goal is to work with international clients." This one is free: it is the same word as a goal in football, and you already know how to say it. Only the meaning changes — here it is what you want to reach.
Improve -- to make something better
"I want to improve my English." Say im-PRUUV — stress on the second syllable, and the final E is silent.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions. The words in bold are the grammar of this lesson — look at what comes right after each like, each want and each would like.

Once a year, everyone at Zurich has thirty minutes with a director. One question only: “What do you want?” Paula opened the company intranet the night before and read what two people had answered.

O Ricardo, de relacionamento com corretores: "I like working with brokers. I like it more than anything else in this job. But my goal is bigger than my week. I want a challenge — I want to understand the numbers behind the policy, and I'd like to spend one month with the underwriting team."

A Sofia, gestora comercial: "For twelve years I did not answer this question. I said I was happy, and I went back to my desk. Then one year I said it out loud: I want your job. The director laughed, and then he wrote it down. Three years later I had it."

Paula closed the laptop and noticed two things. The first is grammar: after like the verb takes -ing (like working), and after want and would like comes to (want to understand, would like to spend). The second is not grammar: nobody there apologized for wanting something. And Ricardo likes his job and wants more — both things, at the same time, in the same sentence.

1. Why does it say "I like working" and not "I like to work" or "I like work"?
A Because Ricardo is working at this very moment.
B Because after like the verb takes -ing — that is how you talk about what you already do and enjoy.
C Because work is an irregular verb.
2. The text has "I want to understand" and "I'd like to spend". Why do both take to?
A Because want and would like are ordinary verbs, and an ordinary verb needs to in order to pull another verb along.
B Because both sentences talk about the future, and the future always takes to.
C Because Ricardo is being polite in both.
3. In lesson 9 you learned that after can the verb goes bare ("I can explain", never "I can to explain"). So why is it "I want to understand" now?
A Because the rule for can has changed.
B Because understand is a long word and long words ask for to.
C Because can is a helper — and a helper takes no to. Want is an ordinary verb, and an ordinary verb needs it.
4. Ricardo says he really likes his job and that he wants a bigger challenge. Is that a contradiction?
A Yes — someone who likes their job should not want anything else.
B No — both things are true at the same time, and they are in the same sentence on purpose.
C Yes — that is why he used but in the middle.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- like, want, would like: three verbs, two rules

GRAMMAR

In English the second verb changes its clothes depending on which verb comes before it. Three verbs today, and only two rules to remember.

VerboWhat comes afterInglêsWhen you use it
likeverbo + -ingI like working with brokers.Eu gosto de trabalhar com corretores.
wantto + verboI want to grow in this company.Eu quero crescer nesta empresa.
would like
(I'd like)		to + verboI'd like to learn underwriting.Eu gostaria de aprender underwriting.
Terceira pessoaleva -sSofia likes meeting clients. · She wants to grow.A Sofia gosta de conhecer clientes. Ela quer crescer.
Negativedon't / doesn'tI don't like working on weekends.The negative — the -ing stays.
Questiondo / would na frenteDo you like working here? · Would you like to join?The questions — and the same two rules hold.

The rule, in two lines: after like, the verb takes -ing. After want and would like, comes to. That is all. And notice what like does differently from the other two: it does not talk about the future, it talks about what you already do — and that is why its verb comes in the -ing form, the form of something happening.

Mistake number 1, and it is our fault: saying “I want grow”. In lesson 9 you learned that the verb goes bare after can (“I can explain”, never “I can to explain”) — and now the to is back. You are not being careless: you are being consistent. The reason is that can is a helper, not a real verb, and a helper takes no to. Want is an ordinary verb, like work and like — and an ordinary verb needs to to pull another verb along.

The test that proves it: can never takes an -s (“she can”, never “she cans”) because it is a helper. Want does (“she wants to grow”) because it is an ordinary verb, like works in lesson 4. Remember that, and both of today's rules fall into place on their own.

Want or would like? Both are correct. It is the same trick as can → could in lesson 9: would like is want in a tie. The practical rule is the same: colleague = want, director or client = would like. But listen carefully, because this is the point of the whole lesson: “I want to grow” is NOT rude in English. It is not arrogance, it is not bad manners — it is the answer to the question you were asked. At work, in English, that simply does not read as childish.

Why this matters: you have spent nine lessons learning to answer — your name, your routine, where things are, what you are able to do. All of them other people's questions. This is the first lesson where the answer is yours, and there is no wrong answer: nobody can correct you about what you want.

Stage 2: Two Minutes, in Order

Order

Build Paula's answer to the director in the order in which she says each thing — from what she already enjoys to the request at the end. Notice the path: it starts with the easy part (what you like), goes through what you want, and only then asks. This is exactly the two-minute answer you will give in class.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen to each sentence, then record your voice. Nobody is grading you — it is only you and your microphone. Two things to watch. First: I'd is I would squeezed together, and it comes out almost like a quick aid — if you listen and cannot find the "would", it is there, only hidden. Second: in the middle of a sentence, the to in want to almost disappears — an American says “I wanna grow”. You do not have to speak like that; you have to recognize it when you hear it.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record your answer. Speak for 90 seconds, with no script. Do not redo it more than twice. This is exactly the exercise that closes the block in class — and the homework audio afterwards.

Answer out loud the question the Zurich director asks once a year: “Why do you want to work here, and what are your goals?” Do it in this order, which is the easiest: first “I like...” (what you already do and enjoy), then “I want to...” (what you want), and at the end “I'd like to...” (what you are asking for). Use at least three of the new words. And here is the only rule of this recording: no sentence may end with a but about your English. No "I want to grow, but my English is not good". Not today. Notice that nobody can correct you here: there is no wrong answer to what you want — and this is the first time in ten lessons that this is true.
