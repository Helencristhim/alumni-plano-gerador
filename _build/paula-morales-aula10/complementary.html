Lesson 10 -- What Do You Like? What Do You Want? Preferences, Desires and Goals

Film
The Pursuit of Happyness · 2006 · HBO Max — included in the subscription

This film is here because of one scene, and it lasts three minutes. Chris Gardner arrives at the most important interview of his life in the wrong clothes — covered in paint, no dress shirt, late, and everybody in the room notices. It is exactly the nightmare you have before speaking English: I am going to walk in there and they will see that I am not good enough. And then he opens his mouth and says what he wants. He does not apologize for the clothes, he does not start with “sorry, I know that…”. He answers the question. And he gets the job — not in spite of the clothes, but because he was the only one who answered. The distance between being ready and being chosen is not the clothes, and it was never your English: it is saying what you want in the clothes you have.

Tip: watch it with English subtitles — the goal is not to understand everything (there is a lot of stockbroking vocabulary you do not need right now). Do ONE thing only: when you get to the interview scene, hit pause and look at how he answers the question — straight, with no but about himself. If you only want one piece: the interview scene is the one that counts, and it is about 40 minutes in. Then answer the same question out loud, in English, however it comes out.

Podcast
Espresso English — #157 “Common error: I LIKE vs. I'D LIKE” (with Shayna, American teacher, ~5 min, free)

This is the shortest episode of the week and it is today's lesson in five minutes. Shayna takes exactly the two sentences you are going to mix up — “I like…” and “I'd like…” — and shows that the silent little apostrophe in the middle changes EVERYTHING: “I like coffee” is what you always enjoy, in life; “I'd like a coffee” is what you want right now. One letter of difference, two meanings that look nothing alike. It is the most common mistake in the world at this point, she hears it every day, and you will hear the difference before the episode ends.

Tip: listen twice — it is five minutes, it fits in the coffee queue. The second time, do the task that counts: stop the audio and say one sentence of your own with each. “I like working with brokers” (what I always enjoy) and “I'd like to grow” (what I want, now). If both come out without you thinking, you arrive in class with half of it done. Open and free, no sign-up: the player is on the page itself.

YouTube
Use WOULD LIKE for Polite Questions or Requests in English — A1 · lingoni ENGLISH (~5 min, free)

Look at the end of the title: A1. This video was made for a beginner level — it is not a hard video you will struggle to follow, it is one your size, in American English, with a teacher who speaks slowly on purpose. In five minutes she settles would like from the side our lesson will not have time to cover: the question. “Do you want some tea?” and “Would you like some tea?” want the same thing — and they are two different social situations. You will hear “Would you like…” in every meeting, every hotel and every plane of your life; and answering is always easier when you recognize the question.

Tip: it is five minutes — watch it to the end, in one go. Do this: every time she says “would like”, repeat it out loud along with her, even if you trip over it. The goal is not to understand (you already do), it is to get your mouth to make the I'd movement — the only physically hard part of tomorrow's lesson. Open channel, no sign-up.
