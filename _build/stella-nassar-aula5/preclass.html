Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read it in context. Half of these belong to the debate about heritage; the other half belong to anyone who wants to argue carefully.

Restitution -- the return of something that was lost or taken to its rightful owner
"Calls for restitution have grown louder in recent decades."
Repatriation -- the return of objects or people to their country of origin
"The debate over repatriation is rarely only legal."
Provenance -- the documented history of who has owned an object and how it moved
"Its provenance has been questioned for two centuries."
Patrimony -- the cultural heritage that a nation or community inherits from the past
"For many Greeks, the sculptures are national patrimony."
Stewardship -- the responsible care of something that belongs to others or to the public
"The museum presents its role as one of stewardship."
Custodian -- a person or institution responsible for protecting something valuable
"A museum may describe itself as a custodian rather than an owner."
Deaccession -- to officially remove an object from a museum collection
"The law makes it difficult to deaccession objects."
Contested -- disputed; the subject of serious disagreement
"The legality of the removal remains contested."
Irrevocable -- impossible to change or undo
"Some fear that a single return would set an irrevocable precedent."
Ostensibly -- apparently or officially, although perhaps not in reality
"The document ostensibly granted permission."
Purportedly -- according to what is claimed, without proof
"The original permit, purportedly issued in 1801, has never been found."
Arguably -- used to say that a statement can reasonably be supported, though not proven
"It is arguably the most famous restitution case in the world."
Concede -- to admit that something is true, often reluctantly, before continuing an argument
"Even critics concede that the sculptures were well preserved in London."
Legitimacy -- the quality of being fair, legal or acceptable
"The legitimacy of the acquisition is at the heart of the dispute."
Entrenched -- firmly established and difficult to change
"Both positions have become deeply entrenched."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Students who are new to academic argument often assume that confidence means certainty. The strongest essay, they believe, is the one that never admits doubt. Experienced readers tend to react in exactly the opposite way. A writer who presents every claim as beyond question quickly loses their trust, because very few questions worth arguing about are that simple.

This is where hedging becomes useful. A hedge adjusts the strength of a claim to match the strength of the evidence. The evidence suggests that opinion is changing is more defensible than opinion is changing, and arguably the most famous case admits, without apology, that a reasonable reader might name another. Some hedges also signal distance from a claim that belongs to someone else. A permit that was ostensibly granted, or purportedly issued, tells the reader that the writer has seen the claim but has not seen the proof.

Concession is the other half of the move. Instead of ignoring the strongest objection, the writer states it fairly and then answers it. While the museum has cared for the sculptures, their removal remains contested. Granted, the sculptures survived in London; yet survival alone does not establish ownership. Although the legal question may never be settled, the fact remains that law and legitimacy are not the same thing. In each case the concession earns the writer the right to disagree.

Both techniques can be abused. An essay in which every sentence is hedged has no position at all, and a concession that is never answered simply hands the argument to the other side. The skill lies in deciding which claims are genuinely uncertain and which ones the writer is prepared to defend without qualification.

Admissions interviewers listen for this skill even when they do not name it. A student who can say that a question is more complicated than it first appears, and then explain exactly why, sounds less like a debater and more like a scholar.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging and Concession

GRAMMAR

A hedge matches the strength of a claim to the strength of the evidence. A concession admits the strongest opposing point before answering it. Together they make an argument trustworthy.

FormWhat it doesExample
arguably / ostensibly / purportedlyadjust certainty, or distance the writer from a claimIt is arguably the most famous case.
The evidence suggests that / It has been argued thatattribute a claim to evidence or to othersThe evidence suggests that opinion is changing.
While / Although X, Yconcede in the subordinate clause, argue in the main clauseWhile the museum cared for them, their removal is contested.
Granted, X; yet Y / To be sure, X. However, Ygrant a point explicitly, then answer itGranted, they survived; yet survival is not ownership.
It does not necessarily follow thatblock an exaggerated conclusion politelyIt does not necessarily follow that museums will be emptied.
The C2 traphedging every claim, or conceding without answering. "Although X, but Y" is also a classic error: use although or but, never both.

Before you hedge, ask: is this claim genuinely uncertain? If you can defend it, state it plainly.
