Chapter 4: Verbs Into Nouns

Where Did the Verbs Go?

The grammar of every institutional text you read

Grammar Discovery

Where Did the Verb Go?

"The work interrogates the organization of time."

"It foregrounds the absence of the patient."

"Through the documentation of the hours, the waiting becomes visible."

"The accumulation of small objects took three years."

Every red word is a verb or an adjective turned into a noun. Find the verb inside it, then ask who is doing it.

RuleFormExample
Verb into noun-tion, -ment, -ance, -al, -ureorganize → organization, engage → engagement, refuse → refusal
Adjective into noun-ity, -ness, -encefragile → fragility, absent → absence, empty → emptiness
The patternthe + NOUN + of + whatthey document the hours → the documentation of the hours
What it gives youa whole process in one word, so you can say something about itThe documentation took three years.
What it coststhe person doing it disappearsthe documentation of the hours — by whom?
The balanceone abstract noun per sentence, and the artist as a subject somewhereI record how long each patient waits.

In one line: turn a verb into a noun to talk about a process, and turn it back when the reader needs to know who did it.

Listening 1 · The Juror

Thirty Seconds, Four Hundred Files

Somebody who reads statements for an open call, on what he looks for first. Sound first — no text.

1. How many statements does he read, and what does he look for first?
About four hundred for one open call. He looks for a sentence in which the artist does something: I record, I collect, I interview.
2. What happens if he cannot say what the artist physically does by the end of the first paragraph?
He moves on. He says he is not proud of it, but that is the reality of a jury with four hundred files.
3. Why does he say words like interrogate and foreground have stopped working, and which statements stay with him?
They have been used so often that they have stopped carrying anything. The plainest ones stay with him, written by people who know the theory and decided not to show off with it.
Listening 2 · The Editor

Find the Nouns That Hide Verbs

Somebody who edits statements with artists, on how she does it. Sound first — no text.

1. Is the statement she receives usually too simple or too abstract, and what is the first thing she does?
Too abstract. The first thing is to find the nouns that are hiding verbs, like documentation, representation and investigation.
2. What plain question does she ask about each of those nouns, and where does the answer go?
Who does it, and to what? The answer goes into the new version as a sentence with the artist as the subject.
3. What is her final test, and how many rounds does it usually take?
The artist reads it aloud to somebody outside the art world. If that person can repeat what is at stake in one sentence, it is finished. It usually takes three rounds.
