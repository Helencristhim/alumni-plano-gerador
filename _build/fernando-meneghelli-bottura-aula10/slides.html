Chapter 3: The Code

Asking a Stranger Something Direct

The same question, in a form that opens the door instead of closing it

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"What did the previous CFO find hardest?" → "Could I ask what the previous CFO found hardest?"

"Which figure does the board look at first?" → "I would be interested to know which figure the board looks at first."

"Is the shared service center funded?" → "May I ask whether the shared service center is funded?"

"What has to be true in a year?" → "I am curious what would have to be true, twelve months from now."

Both versions are correct. What changes in the word order after the opening phrase?

OpeningWhat followsExample
Could I ask...Statement word order — no auxiliary, no inversionCould I ask what the previous CFO found hardest?
I would be interested to know...The same — and it becomes a statement, not a questionI would be interested to know which figure the board looks at first.
May I ask whether / if...For yes-no questions, use whether or ifMay I ask whether the centre is funded?
The rule in one lineAfter the opening, the second half stops behaving like a question: the board looks, never does the board look. In Portuguese the order stays the same, which is exactly why this one survives.
Do not overdo itOne or two indirect questions sound senior. Five in a row sound uncertain. Mix them with direct questions once the room is warm.
Why it matters todayIn the last five minutes you will ask a stranger something quite direct: what the previous person found hardest, what the board actually worries about. The indirect form is what makes a hard question land as interest instead of as an interrogation.

The final question mark stays only if the sentence began as a question. I would be interested to know... ends with a full stop.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Could I ask what did the previous CFO find hardest?"
"Could I ask what the previous CFO found hardest?"
"I would be interested to know which figure does the board look at first."
"I would be interested to know which figure the board looks at first."
"May I ask is the shared service center funded?"
"May I ask whether the shared service center is funded?"

After the opening phrase, the rest of the sentence behaves like a statement. And a yes-no question needs whether or if.

The Move

Three Questions, Every Time

1. The number

"I would be interested to know which figure this board looks at first."

2. The last person

"Could I ask what the previous CFO found hardest here?"

3. The year ahead

"What would have to be true, twelve months from now, for you to say this worked?"

Nobody prepares for the third one. Watching a board try to answer it in front of you is the most useful five minutes of the whole process.

Comprehension

About the Process

1. How many candidates are left, and what does Claire tell him about one of them?
Three, and one of them is internal — which she would rather he knew before the room.
2. Who will be on the panel, and what should he expect?
The hiring manager, the Group CFO and HR. Two competency questions and something close to a case study on working capital.
3. What does she say the previous CFO never managed to do?
Get the Brazilian numbers to arrive in the same week as the European ones — in two years.
Listening

Eight Minutes After You Leave

What the panel actually says. Sound first — no text.

1. How long does the panel have, and what does the debrief actually consist of?
About eight minutes before the next candidate. Somebody says one sentence, and that sentence usually decides it.
2. What happens if you ask nothing, or only about the process?
You have given them no sentence to say about you.
3. Why does she advise against asking about the notice period or the package early?
Not because wanting to know is wrong, but because it will be the only thing anybody remembers.
Listening 2

The Three He Always Asks

A CFO on the last five minutes. Sound first — no text.

1. What is his first question, and what does the answer reveal?
Which figure the board looks at first when it opens the pack — because that tells him what they actually worry about, and it is never the public number.
2. Why does he say it does not matter whether they answer the second question honestly?
Because the pause before they answer tells him most of it.
3. What is the third question, and why does he value it so highly?
What would have to be true in twelve months for them to say the appointment had worked — because nobody prepares for it, and watching a board try to answer it is the most useful five minutes of the process.

That he interrupts, and that it is not a signal of anything — so an interruption should not be read as a bad sign.

Simulation

The Full Interview

Scenario: Second round for the CFO role. Group CFO, twenty-five minutes, entirely in English. Two competency questions, one case on working capital, interruptions without warning, and the last five minutes are yours.

No keywords, no notes on screen, no help. If you go blank, run the protocol out loud. If you miss the end of a question, ask for it again. Finish with your three questions.

Debrief

Four Numbers, No Adjectives

Blanks

How many, and how long each one lasted in seconds

Paraphrases

How many times you said the question back before answering

Headlines

How many answers opened with the conclusion

Questions

How many you asked at the end, and how many were about their problem

These four numbers are the measurement of the first half of the programme. Nothing here is a judgement about whether you are good enough for the role.
