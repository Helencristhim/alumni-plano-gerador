Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words a headhunter uses on the phone. You will hear them far more often than you say them, so the target here is recognition at speed.

A shortlist -- the final small group of candidates still being considered
"You are on the shortlist, which at this stage means three people."
A headhunter -- the person paid to find you, not to hire you
"The headhunter works for the client, and remembering that changes what you say."
A hiring manager -- the person the role reports to, and who decides
"HR runs the process. The hiring manager makes the decision."
A panel interview -- an interview with several people at the same time
"A panel interview is three conversations happening in one room."
A competency question -- a question that asks for one specific past example
"Tell me about a time when. That opening means it is a competency question."
A case study -- a business problem you are asked to solve live
"In a case study the reasoning out loud matters more than the answer."
References -- the people who will be asked about you afterwards
"References are taken after they have decided, not before."
A notice period -- the time you owe your current employer before leaving
"Ask about the notice period at the offer stage, never in a first meeting."
A second round -- the stage where the questions get specific to the company
"The first round is about you. The second round is about them."
To be in the running -- to still be a live candidate in a process
"You are still in the running, which is not the same as being the favorite."
To progress -- to move to the next stage of a process
"They would like to progress you to a panel with the Group CFO."
A debrief -- the eight minutes the panel spends talking about you afterwards
"Somebody says one sentence in the debrief, and that sentence usually decides it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every interview ends the same way: so, what questions do you have for us. It is the only part of the conversation the candidate controls completely, and it is the part that produces the one sentence the panel repeats in the debrief afterwards.

I ask three, always. I would be interested to know which figure this board looks at first when it opens the pack, because that tells me what they actually worry about. Could I ask what the previous CFO found hardest here — and it does not matter whether the answer is honest, because the pause before it tells me most of what I need. And then the third: I am curious what would have to be true, twelve months from now, for them to say the appointment had worked.

Notice what happens after the opening phrase. The second half stops behaving like a question: which figure the board looks at, never does the board look. For a yes-no question you need whether: May I ask whether the shared service centre is already funded. And there is one thing I never ask in a first meeting, which is the notice period or the package — not because wanting to know is wrong, but because it becomes the only thing anybody remembers.

1. Why is it "which figure the board looks at first" and not "which figure does the board look at first"?
A Because after the opening phrase the second half behaves like a statement, with no auxiliary and no inversion.
B Because "look" is a regular verb and does not take "does".
C Because the sentence is about a habit rather than a single event.
2. What do you need in an indirect yes-no question?
A Nothing extra — you keep the original word order.
B "Whether" or "if", followed by statement word order.
C A question tag at the end.
3. Why does he say the honesty of the second answer does not matter?
A Because the pause before the answer tells him most of what he needs.
B Because he can verify it later with the headhunter.
C Because the question is only there to fill the time.
4. What is the reason given for not asking about the package in a first meeting?
A Because it is inappropriate to want to know.
B Because it becomes the only thing anybody remembers about you.
C Because headhunters are not allowed to discuss it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Asking a Stranger Something Direct

GRAMMAR

The indirect form is what makes a hard question land as interest rather than as an interrogation. The rule is one line long, and Portuguese does not have it — which is why it survives.

OpeningWhat followsExample
Could I ask...Statement word order — no auxiliary, no inversionCould I ask what the previous CFO found hardest?
I would be interested to know...The same, and the sentence becomes a statementI would be interested to know which figure the board looks at first.
May I ask whether / if...For yes-no questionsMay I ask whether the centre is funded?
Could you tell me...The same rule, in a softer registerCould you tell me what the notice period is?
The rule in one lineAfter the opening, the rest behaves like a statement: the board looks, never does the board look. Portuguese keeps the same order in both, which is exactly why this mistake survives at advanced level.
PunctuationKeep the question mark only if the sentence began as a question. Could I ask what...? takes one. I would be interested to know what... ends with a full stop.
Do not overdo itOne or two indirect questions sound senior. Five in a row sound uncertain. Mix them with direct questions once the room is warm.

This is the form you will use for the three closing questions — the part of the interview you control completely.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The last five minutes are yours. Choose the version that gives the panel a sentence to repeat about you.

"What questions do you have for us?" The strongest first question is:
A "I would be interested to know which figure this board looks at first when it opens the pack."
B "Could you tell me what the next steps in the process are?"
C "No, I think you have covered everything, thank you."
Which sentence is correct?
A "Could I ask what did the previous CFO find hardest?"
B "Could I ask what the previous CFO found hardest?"
C "Could I ask what found the previous CFO hardest?"
You want to ask a yes-no question indirectly. You say:
A "May I ask is the centre already funded?"
B "May I ask whether the centre is already funded?"
C "May I ask that the centre is already funded?"
In a first meeting, the question to avoid is:
A Anything about the package or the notice period.
B Anything about what the previous person found difficult.
C Anything that the panel might not be able to answer.
The headhunter tells you three candidates are in the running, one internal. That means:
A You have not been eliminated, and one competitor already knows the company.
B The internal candidate has already been chosen.
C References have already been taken on all three.
