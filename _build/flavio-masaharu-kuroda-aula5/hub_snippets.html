Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

Ask Before You Pitch -- The Discovery Call

Everybody arrives at the second call with slides. The ones who win arrive with six questions and no slides at all. Key words: a pain point, a workflow, a bottleneck, a legacy system, manual, a workaround, to map, downtime, a headcount, to escalate. Structure: subject and object questions.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A pain point -- the part of a process that costs the most time or money
"Their pain point is not the software. It is the three days nobody notices anything."
A workflow -- the fixed order of steps a team follows to get something done
"Their workflow has eleven steps, and four of them are somebody copying a file."
A bottleneck -- the one step that slows down everything after it
"The bottleneck is one person, and she is on holiday every August."
A legacy system -- old software a company still runs because replacing it is hard
"The legacy system works. That is exactly why nobody has replaced it."
Manual -- done by a person by hand, not by the system
"Two of the eleven steps are manual, and both of them are at the end."
A workaround -- a temporary way around a problem that nobody has fixed
"It is a workaround, and it has been the same workaround for six years."
To map -- to write down every step of a process so that everybody can see it
"Before we talk about tools, can we map what happens today?"
Downtime -- the time when a system is not working
"Four hours of downtime in a month is nothing. Four hours at month-end is everything."
A headcount -- the number of people working on something
"They are not asking to cut the headcount. They are asking to stop hiring."
To escalate -- to pass a problem to somebody more senior
"Nobody escalates it, because escalating it means admitting it has been there for years."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The second call is where deals are lost, and they are lost the same way every time: somebody opens a deck. The moment the deck is open, the customer stops describing their own problem and starts reacting to your product, and the original description never comes back.

A discovery call is six questions and a lot of silence. Who handles the invoices today? What happens when a supplier changes the format? How long does the closing take? Notice that the first two have no do and the third one does. It is not style. In the first two, the question word itself is the one doing the action, so the verb simply takes the -s and no auxiliary appears. In the third, the closing does the taking, so the auxiliary comes back.

What you are listening for is a number and a workaround. A number gives the problem a size: nine days instead of four. A workaround gives it a history — if somebody fixes it by hand every month, the pain point has been there long enough to become a habit, and habits have owners. Ask what is manual, ask where the bottleneck is, ask how much downtime there is at month-end, and ask what the headcount on that step is.

The last question is the one people leave out. Who inside the company asks about that number? If nobody does, nobody will escalate it, and a problem nobody escalates is a problem nobody funds. Before you talk about any tool, map what happens today. Then, and only then, show the part of your product that matches the page you have just filled in.

1. "Who handles the invoices today?" Why is there no do?
A Because the question is about the present, and the present never takes do.
B Because who is the one doing the handling, so the verb takes -s and no auxiliary appears.
C Because questions with who never take an auxiliary in any situation.
2. "How long does the closing take?" Why does this one need does?
A Because the closing is the one doing the taking, and how long is not the subject.
B Because the question is longer than three words.
C Because take is an irregular verb.
3. Why does the text say a workaround gives the problem a history?
A Because workarounds are always written down somewhere.
B Because if somebody fixes it by hand every month, the problem has lasted long enough to become a habit.
C Because a workaround proves the legacy system is broken.

GRAMMAR

One question sorts them all: who is doing the action?

Stage 2: Put the Discovery Call in Order

Order

Six discovery questions, out of order. Put them in the order that gets you the most.

?"Who handles that today?"
?"What happens when the process fails?"
?"And what do you do when that happens?"
?"How long does the closing take today?"
?"Who asks you about that number?"
?"And what do those nine days cost you, apart from the time?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to know which person deals with the invoices. You ask:
A "Who does handle the invoices today?"
B "Who handles the invoices today?"
C "Who is handle the invoices today?"
You want to know the duration of the month-end closing. You ask:
A "How long take the closing?"
B "How long the closing takes?"
C "How long does the closing take?"
Three questions in, the customer says: just show me what you do. The best move is:
A Open the deck and start the demo.
B "Two more questions, so that I show you the right part and not all of it."
C "Sorry, I always ask a lot of questions."
They have described the problem. You want the sentence that gives it a size. You ask:
A "How long does it take today, and how long should it take?"
B "So you need our platform, right?"
C "That sounds very bad. I understand you completely."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Take a real company you would like to sell to. In one minute, ask the six discovery questions out loud, in the order you would ask them: who does it today, where the bottleneck is, what the workaround is, what the number is, who inside the company feels it, and what it costs. Do not say one word about your product.
