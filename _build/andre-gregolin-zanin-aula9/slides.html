Chapter 3: One Comma, Two Meanings

One Comma, Two Meanings

The half you could delete, and the half you could not

Grammar Discovery

Which Half Could You Delete?

"The teacher who taught me English moved away when I was sixteen."

"Franca, which is famous for basketball, is four hours from here."

"The exam I am preparing for is taken by eighty thousand people."

"My old coach, whose son plays professionally now, still messages me."

Try deleting the coloured part of each sentence. The green ones survive: what is left still means the same thing, only with less detail. The red ones break: without that part you do not know which teacher or which exam. Now look at the punctuation and you will see English marking the difference for you.

JobFormExample
Essential — it says which onewho · that · which, no commasThe teacher who taught me English moved away.
Extra — you could delete it, which · , who + commasFranca, which is famous for basketball, is four hours away.
Never that in the extra kind, which — never , thatNot Franca, that is famous…. Say Franca, which is famous…
People, things, placeswho · which · whereThe city where I grew up has one cinema.
Possessionwhose, for people and thingsMy old coach, whose son plays professionally, still messages me.
You can drop the pronounonly when it is the object of the clauseThe exam [that] I am preparing for is very competitive.
The trapnever repeat the object at the endNot the exam that I am preparing for it.

In one line: no commas means it tells you which one. Commas mean you could delete it and still know what you were talking about.

Grammar Practice

Comma or No Comma?

Say it first, then click to check

“The teacher ___who taught me English moved away.” (essential — which teacher?)
“Franca, ___which is famous for basketball, is four hours away.” (extra — there is only one Franca)
“The city ___where I grew up has one cinema.” (a place, essential)
“My old coach, ___whose son plays professionally now, still messages me.” (possession, extra)
“The exam ___I am preparing for is very competitive.” (the pronoun is dropped — four words)
“He trained twice a day, ___which is to say he had no afternoons.” (restating the whole idea)
Chapter 4: Real Speed

Being Asked to Be Specific

One conversation, three recordings, one comma that changed a rule

Dialogue

Which One, Exactly?

H
So what kind of work do you actually want to do? And do not say helping people, everybody says helping people.
A
Fine. Something where the decision happens fast. To be more specific, where you find out within an hour whether you were right.
H
Okay, that is much better. Can you elaborate? Because that describes about forty jobs.
A
Then let me narrow it down. Work where the person in front of you is the whole problem, and there is no report to write about it afterwards.
H
Now that is a real answer. Where does that come from? Somebody you know?
A
A coach I had in Franca, whose whole method was deciding in five seconds and living with it. He was wrong a lot, and it did not seem to cost him anything.
H
And that is the part you actually admired, isn’t it. Not the deciding. The living with it.
A
Yes. Which is to say I am not sure any of this is about work at all. What about you — what did you say when people asked you that at eighteen?
Comprehension

About Hannah

1. What does she say before he even answers, and why?
She tells him not to say helping people, because everybody says helping people. She rules out the vague answer in advance.
2. What does she say about his second answer?
That it is much better, but that it still describes about forty jobs, so she asks him to elaborate.
3. What does she notice at the end that he had not said directly?
That the part he admired was not the deciding, but the living with it.

Listening 2 · A Podcast

The Widest True Thing You Can Say

An editor on why vague answers are usually honest ones. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say a vague sentence actually is?
The widest true thing a person can say, because widening is what you do when you are not sure the narrow version is true.
2. What is the useful move, instead of accusing somebody of being vague?
To offer them a narrower sentence and let them correct it.
3. What does he say people were actually missing?
Not the precise answer, which they had the whole time, but permission to be wrong in public.
Listening 3 · World Service

The Comma That Changed a Rule

A short radio piece on a punctuation mark worth millions. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the case about, and what was the whole argument about?
Overtime pay for delivery drivers, and the entire argument was about one missing comma.
2. What did each side say the phrase meant?
The company said it meant two separate activities, packing and distribution. The drivers said it meant one: packing, for the purpose of shipment or distribution.
3. What does the piece say the case proves?
That punctuation is not decoration — it is where the meaning lives.
Real Document

Two Readings of the Same Line

CLUB REGULATIONS · ARTICLE 7
eligibility · the line that was rewritten in 2019
Original wordingPlayers who have trained with the senior squad are not eligible
Reading AOnly those particular players are excluded
Reading BAll players are excluded, and by the way they have all trained
Which reading is correctA — because there is no comma
Wording after 2019Players, who have trained with the senior squad, are not eligible — withdrawn
Reason for withdrawalThe commas changed who was excluded
One line, two commas, and a completely different rule. Without commas the clause tells you WHICH players. With commas it says something extra about ALL of them. Nothing else in the sentence changed.
1. What does the original wording mean, and why?
That only the players who have trained with the senior squad are excluded, because there is no comma, so the clause says which players.
2. What did the 2019 version accidentally say?
That all players are excluded, and that all of them happen to have trained with the senior squad.
3. According to the note at the bottom, what was the only thing that changed?
The commas. Nothing else in the sentence changed.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I use who for people and which for things, and I never use which for a person.
I know that a comma means the information is extra, and no comma means it says which one.
I never write a comma before that. After a comma it is always which.
I never repeat the object at the end: the exam I am preparing for, not for it.
I know the words: precision, to elaborate, to specify, to clarify, to distinguish, relevant, essential, which is to say, to narrow it down, to be more specific.
