Connecting Ideas Like a Native -- The Comma That Changes the Meaning

Two facts in one sentence instead of two, and a comma that decides which half you could delete. Key words: precision, to elaborate, to specify, to clarify, to distinguish, relevant, essential, which is to say, to narrow it down, to be more specific. Structure: defining and non-defining relative clauses.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Precision -- saying exactly the thing you mean, and not a slightly wider thing
"Precision is not about long words. It is about leaving out what you did not mean."
To elaborate -- to say more about something you have already said, when asked
"Could you elaborate on that? I am not sure which part you mean."
To specify -- to name the exact one, out of several possible ones
"You said a teacher. Can you specify which teacher?"
To clarify -- to make something clearer that somebody has misunderstood
"Let me clarify: I did not stop playing. I stopped competing."
To distinguish -- to see and show the difference between two things that look alike
"English distinguishes the two with a comma, and Portuguese does not."
Relevant -- connected to the point being discussed, right now
"It is true, but it is not relevant to the question you asked."
Essential -- so necessary that removing it changes what the sentence means
"If you can delete it and nothing changes, it was not essential."
Which is to say -- introduces a restatement of what you just said, in clearer words
"He trained twice a day, which is to say he had no afternoons."
To narrow it down -- to reduce a wide group to a small one, by adding conditions
"Eleven thousand results. Let us narrow it down."
To be more specific -- announces that a sharper version of the same statement is coming
"I want to work with people. To be more specific, with people in a hurry."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A relative clause is a way of putting two facts into one sentence without starting again. The teacher taught me English. She moved away. becomes The teacher who taught me English moved away. The pronoun depends on what you are talking about: who for people, which for things, where for places, whose for possession. In Portuguese que covers almost all of them, which is why the teacher which taught me is such a common sentence and such an obvious one to a native ear.

Now the part that carries the meaning. Compare two sentences that differ only in punctuation. The teacher who taught me English moved away tells you which teacher, out of several. Franca, which is famous for basketball, is four hours away does something different: there is only one Franca, so the middle is extra. The test is simple and it always works. Delete the clause. If what is left still means the same thing, the clause was extra and it takes commas. If what is left leaves you asking which one?, it was essential and it takes none. And one rule with no exceptions: after a comma, always which, never that.

Two more things and they are both about leaving something out. First, when the relative pronoun is the object of its clause, English simply drops it: The exam I am preparing for is complete and natural. Second, and this is the error that survives longest in Brazilian English: once the pronoun has done its job, do not say the noun again at the end. Not the exam that I am preparing for it. The that already is the exam. Finally, one construction worth stealing: which can refer back to a whole idea, not just a noun. He trained twice a day, which is to say he had no afternoons.

1. What is the test for deciding whether a clause takes commas?
A Whether the sentence is long or short.
B Delete it: if what is left still means the same, it was extra and takes commas.
C Whether it comes at the beginning or the end.
2. Which rule does the text say has no exceptions?
A After a comma, always which, never that.
B Always use who for people and never that.
C Never drop the relative pronoun.
3. Why is “the exam that I am preparing for it” wrong?
A Because for cannot end a clause in English.
B Because that is already the exam, so saying it again repeats the object.
C Because the clause needs a comma.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Defining and Non-defining Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

No commas means it tells you which one. Commas mean you could delete it and still know what you meant.

JobFormExample
Essential — it says which onewho · that · which, no commasThe teacher who taught me English moved away.
Extra — you could delete it, which · , who + commasFranca, which is famous for basketball, is four hours away.
Never that in the extra kind, which — never , thatNot Franca, that is famous…. Say Franca, which is famous…
People, things, placeswho · which · whereThe city where I grew up has one cinema.
Possessionwhose, for people and thingsMy old coach, whose son plays professionally, still messages me.
You can drop the pronounonly when it is the object of the clauseThe exam [that] I am preparing for is very competitive.
The trapnever repeat the object at the endNot the exam that I am preparing for it.

Never: the teacher which taught me · Franca, that is famous… · the exam that I am preparing for it · the person who his father coached

Order

Somebody asks you to describe where you are from and who mattered. Put it in a logical order.

?"He decided everything in five seconds, which is to say he was wrong quite often."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to say which teacher, out of four. You say:
A The teacher, who taught me English, moved away.
B The teacher who taught me English moved away.
C The teacher which taught me English moved away.
There is only one Franca, and you want to add a fact about it. You say:
A Franca that is famous for basketball is four hours away.
B Franca, that is famous for basketball, is four hours away.
C Franca, which is famous for basketball, is four hours away.
You want to talk about the exam you are studying for. You say:
A The exam that I am preparing for it is very competitive.
B The exam I am preparing for is very competitive.
C The exam which I am preparing for it is very competitive.
Somebody has given you a vague answer. The strongest response is:
A I do not understand you.
B Could you elaborate on that? I am not sure which part you mean.
C Please repeat, more specific.

Lesson 9 — Connecting Ideas Like a Native

Series
The Crown — Season 1, Episode 1

Written English spoken out loud: long, precise, heavily subordinated sentences delivered slowly enough to follow. Almost every line carries two facts welded together, which is exactly the shape you practised today, and it is the register you will need for anything academic.

Tip: watch fifteen minutes with English subtitles and write down three sentences with a relative clause. Then say each one as two short sentences and hear what is lost.

Talk
Robert Waldinger — “What makes a good life? Lessons from the longest study on happiness” (TED)

Twelve minutes on eighty years of following the same people, and on which of them ended up well. It answers the question from today listening — what kind of day do you want to have for forty years — with data instead of opinion.

Tip: watch it once for the content. The second time, write down four sentences where he uses who, which or whose to add a fact without starting a new sentence.

Podcast
British Council — “How to use relative clauses”

A short, spoken lesson on the two kinds and the comma between them, with examples at natural speed. Free, and it covers the dropped pronoun that most courses skip.

Tip: after each example, say the same idea as two separate sentences and then weld it back. Doing it in both directions is what makes it automatic.
