Lesson 22 — Pre-class

Exam Week Survival

About ten minutes. Eight study words — cram, flashcards, highlighter, mind map, deadline, brain break, study buddy, all-nighter — and how to give advice, warn a friend and tell a rule from a choice: should, ought to, had better, mustn't, don't have to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud.

Cram -- to study a lot in a very short time, just before a test
"You'd better not cram everything the night before."
Flashcards -- small cards with a question on one side and the answer on the other
"I made forty flashcards for the science test."
Highlighter -- a bright pen you use to mark important words
"If the whole page is yellow, the highlighter isn't helping."
Mind map -- a drawing with one big idea in the middle and other ideas around it
"You should try a mind map for history."
Deadline -- the last day or time to finish something
"The deadline for the project is Friday."
Brain break -- a short rest from studying
"Take a brain break every forty minutes."
Study buddy -- a friend you study with
"My study buddy quizzes me on the bus."
All-nighter -- a night when you stay awake to study and don't sleep
"I pulled an all-nighter, and I couldn't remember a thing."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read Sophia's message to a friend in Rio. The words in bold give advice, a warning, a rule or a choice.

Exam week, again! — My first test is on Monday, so this weekend I should stay home and study. My mom says I ought to start with math, because it's the hardest.

Ruby and I are study buddies now. Her advice: "You'd better not pull an all-nighter, or you'll fall asleep in the test!" And our teacher's rule: you mustn't bring your phone into the classroom.

The good news? The history project is optional, so I don't have to do it. I'm going to do it anyway. I love history!

"You mustn't bring your phone into the classroom." What is it?
A A rule.
B A choice: she can bring it if she wants, but it's better to leave it at home.
C A good idea.
"I don't have to do the history project." What does it mean?
A The teacher says she can't do it, because she is already doing too many projects.
B It isn't necessary. She can choose.
C She has to do it.
Which sentence is correct?
A You'd better to sleep early tonight, because the test is very long.
B You ought start with math.
C You'd better not pull an all-nighter.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Advice, warning, rule or choice?

  • should / ought to + verb = a good idea: You ought to start a week before.
  • had better (not) + verb = a strong warning, now: You'd better not cram, or you'll forget everything.
  • mustn't + verb = it is against the rules: You mustn't check your phone during the test.
  • don't have to + verb = it is not necessary, you choose: You don't have to highlight every line.

Watch out

  • had better is about now, not the past, and it has no to: you'd better go. But ought always takes to: you ought to go.

Remember: your life up to now is still have: Have you ever failed a test? No, I haven't.
