Chapter 4: The Code

Shall on Paper, Has To in the Room

One rule, two registers

Grammar Discovery

The Page and the Voice

Page: "The Concessionaire shall maintain the port." → Voice: "The operator has to maintain the port."

Page: "provided that the Grantor approves" → Voice: "as long as the state approves"

Page: "in the event of a breach" → Voice: "if there is a breach"

Page: "notwithstanding clause 9" → Voice: "even if clause 9 says something different"

The rule does not change. What changes? Look at the blue words, and at who the subject is: the Concessionaire, or the operator?

Chapter 5: Real Speed

The Clause on the Screen

A document, a moderator and a former regulator

Real Document

Clause 14, on the Screen

PORT CONCESSION AGREEMENT — CLAUSE 14
Maintenance, breach and termination · example prepared for the panel
14.1The Concessionaire shall maintain the port infrastructure in good working order for the full term of the Agreement.
14.2In the event of a breach of clause 14.1, the Grantor may, subject to a prior notice of ninety days, apply a penalty or terminate the Agreement.
14.3Notwithstanding clause 14.2, the Grantor shall not terminate the Agreement during a declared state of emergency.
14.4The Grantor may waive the penalty, provided that the Concessionaire remedies the breach within the notice period.
Grantor = the state. Concessionaire = the private operator of the port.
1. What does the state have to do before it applies a penalty or ends the contract?
It has to give the operator ninety days of notice.
2. When can the state NOT end the contract, even after a breach?
During a declared state of emergency.
3. How can the operator avoid the penalty?
By correcting the problem within the ninety days. Then the state can waive the penalty.
Listening 1 · The Moderator

Sixty Seconds per Clause

The forum moderator explains the format of the panel. Sound first — no text.

1. What will each panelist see on the screen, and how long will they have?
One real clause from a contract or a regulation, and sixty seconds to explain it.
2. What does she ask the panelists not to do?
Not to read the clause aloud. The audience can read it.
3. Who is the audience, according to her?
Mostly investors and engineers, not lawyers.
Listening 2 · The Former Regulator

The Plain Language Test

A former regulator on explaining rules to the public. Sound first — no text.

1. What test did his agency use before publishing a rule?
They asked one person outside the agency to explain the rule back to them in one minute.
2. What three questions does he say every explanation must answer?
Who has to do what, by when, and what happens if they do not.
3. Why does he say plain language is not the same as simple law?
Because the rule stays exactly as precise. Only the words change.

Chapter 7: Wrap-Up

From the Page to the Room

Survival Card

Five Phrases for Explaining a Clause

In plain English, the operator has to keep the port in good condition.
The state can end the contract only if there is a breach.
The penalty can be waived, as long as the problem is fixed in time.
Even if there is a breach, the contract cannot end during an emergency.
So, in short: who has to do what, by when, and what happens if they do not.
Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I say has to, cannot and can where the document says shall, shall not and may.
I use as long as, if, only if and even if for provided that, in the event of, subject to and notwithstanding.
I use the present after provided that and as long as, never will.
I explain a clause in sixty seconds: who, what, when, and what happens if not.
I know the words: a provision, to stipulate, binding, to waive, a breach, jurisdiction, in force, a remedy, to terminate, a party.
