Showing the Client

You described this one exactly: the client is in the room, you show how the project looks so far, you explain what was decided and why, and then you have to find out what they actually think.

Chapter 1: First Words

Do You Like It? Is Not a Question

Last week the report told a director where the project stands. This week the audience is different: the client sees the project for the first time in three months, and what you need from the room is not approval, it is information.

Think of the last time you showed a client work in progress. What did you ask them, and what did they actually tell you?

Today’s Goal

Three Missions

1

Ten words for a client review of work in progress.

2

Ask a question that is easy for a client to answer honestly.

3

Take a concern, repeat it back correctly, and propose a compromise.

The target in one line: do you like it? gets you a polite yes and no information; I was wondering whether the loading bay works for your operation gets you the sentence that changes the drawing. Softer is not weaker. Softer is more precise.

One Question

Which Question Gets a Real Answer?

Two versions of the same question, out loud, right now.

Take something you need a client to tell you. Ask it directly. Then ask it again, starting with Could you tell me or I was wondering whether. Notice which one a busy client would actually answer in detail.

Chapter 3: The Code

Could You Tell Me, I Was Wondering

The question inside the question

Grammar Discovery

Two Ways to Ask the Same Thing

"Could you tell me how many pallets you move in a day?"

"I was wondering whether the loading bay works for your operation."

"Would you mind if we came back to the corridor width?"

"Do you have any sense of when operations could review this?"

Each sentence has a real question inside it, and an opening in red in front. Look closely at the word order inside the question: is it the order of a question, or the order of a statement?

JobFormExample
The openingCould you tell me... / I was wondering... / Do you know...Could you tell me how many pallets you move?
Word order insidesubject before verb, like a statementNot how many pallets do you move. Say how many pallets you move.
No auxiliary do / does / didit disappears insideNot where did the truck enter. Say where the truck entered.
Yes or no question insidewhether (or if)I was wondering whether the bay works for you.
Softening a proposalwould rather / might / it may be worthIt might be worth looking at the corridor again.
The trapthe question mark follows the OPENING, not the insideCould you tell me where it is? — but I was wondering where it is.

In one line: an opening in front, statement order inside, no do / does / did, and whether for a yes-or-no.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Review, at Real Speed

One walkthrough, three recordings, one feedback sheet

Dialogue

The Walkthrough and the Real Answer

C
Before I start, could you tell me how much time we have? I can do the full walkthrough or just the ground floor.
R
We have about forty minutes. The ground floor is what my operations people care about.
C
Then let me walk you through the ground floor layout first, and I will leave the renderings for the end.
R
That works. I will say now that my main concern is the distance from the loading bay to the storage area.
C
Thank you for saying it early. I was wondering whether the current distance works for your operation, or whether it costs you time on every trip.
R
It costs us time. About ninety seconds each way, and we do that forty times a shift.
C
That is a real number, and it changes the priority. We can address it by moving the door six metres, but the compromise is a narrower corridor on the north side.
R
I would take that. Would you mind sending the two options to my operations manager before you draw anything?
Comprehension

About Rebecca

1. How much time does Rebecca have, and which part matters to her?
About forty minutes, and the ground floor, because that is what her operations people care about.
2. What number does Rebecca give about the loading bay?
About ninety seconds each way, forty times a shift.
3. What does Rebecca ask for at the end?
That the two options are sent to her operations manager before anything is drawn.
Listening 1 · The Client

Why I Said Yes and Meant Nothing

A client explains why she stopped giving useful feedback in design reviews. Sound first — no text.

1. What question does she say she cannot answer usefully?
Do you like it, because she is not a designer and liking is not her job.
2. What happened in the review where she said the layout was fine?
A door that was fine on the drawing turned out to be in the wrong place for the forklifts.
3. What kind of question does she say gets a real answer from her?
A question about her operation, such as how many times a shift her team makes a particular trip.
Listening 2 · The Design Manager

Say the Bad Part Yourself

A design manager on how to present work in progress without losing the room. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say you should do with the weakest part of the design?
Show it yourself, early, before the client finds it.
2. Why does he say people hide the weak part?
Because they hope the client will not notice, and the client always notices.
3. What does he say a concern is, in his experience?
Usually a requirement nobody wrote down, so it is information rather than an attack.
Listening 3 · The Operations Lead

The Difference Between a Preference and a Requirement

An operations lead explains why that distinction saves projects. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say most feedback lists fail to separate?
Preferences from requirements.
2. What example does she give of a requirement?
The turning circle of the forklift, which is fixed and cannot be negotiated.
3. What does she recommend asking at the end of a review?
Which of these would stop you working, and which would only annoy you.
Real Document

The Sheet That Sorts the Comments

CLIENT REVIEW — FEEDBACK SHEET
Industrial Block 2 · ground floor layout · review of May 9
Comment 1Loading bay to storage: 90 s each way, 40 trips a shift
Type 1Requirement — operational cost, measured
Comment 2Offices would be preferred on the south side
Type 2Preference — no operational impact
Proposed responseMove the bay door 6 m; corridor narrows on the north side
Open questionWould operations accept the narrower corridor?
Next stepTwo options to the operations manager before anything is drawn
Every comment on this sheet is labelled either requirement or preference, and the open question is written as a question somebody can answer without defending themselves. That is the whole difference between a feedback sheet and a list of complaints.
1. Which comment is a requirement, and what makes it one?
Comment 1, because it has a measured operational cost: ninety seconds each way, forty times a shift.
2. Turn the Open question line into a softened question you would say out loud.
I was wondering whether operations would accept a narrower corridor on the north side.
3. Why does the next step happen before anything is drawn?
Because drawing first turns a proposal into a defence, and the operations manager has not seen the options yet.
