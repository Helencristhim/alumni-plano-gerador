Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

Showing the Client -- Presenting Progress and Collecting Feedback

The client is in the room and you need information, not approval. Key words: a walkthrough, a rendering, a layout, to walk someone through, a concern, to address, a preference, to accommodate, a compromise, buy-in. Structure: indirect questions and softening language.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A walkthrough -- a guided tour through a design or a process, step by step
"Let me give you a short walkthrough of the ground floor."
A rendering -- a realistic image made from the model, to show how something will look
"The rendering shows the east face with metal cladding."
A layout -- the arrangement of spaces and equipment on a floor
"The layout puts the control room next to the process area."
To walk someone through -- to explain something to somebody step by step
"I will walk you through the changes since February."
A concern -- a worry that somebody wants taken seriously
"My main concern is the distance from the loading bay."
To address -- to deal with a concern or a problem directly
"We can address that by moving the door six metres."
A preference -- what somebody would rather have, when both options work
"That is a preference, not a requirement, so we have room."
To accommodate -- to change something so that it fits what somebody needs
"We can accommodate the extra pallet space on this side."
A compromise -- a solution where each side gives up part of what it wanted
"The compromise is a narrower corridor and a wider bay."
Buy-in -- real agreement and support from the people affected
"Without buy-in from operations, the layout will change later."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The client arrives with forty minutes. Cassio opens with a question, not with a slide: “Could you tell me how much time we have?” Then he offers a short walkthrough of the ground floor layout and leaves the renderings for the end.

Rebecca has a concern: the distance from the loading bay. Instead of defending the drawing, Cassio asks: “I was wondering whether the current distance works for your operation.” She answers with a number: ninety seconds each way, forty times a shift.

That number turns a preference into a requirement. They address it by moving the door six metres, and the compromise is a narrower corridor. Before anything is drawn, the two options go to operations, because without buy-in from the people who use the building, the layout changes again later.

1. Why does Cassio ask “I was wondering whether the current distance works for your operation”?
A To avoid talking about the drawing.
B Because an indirect question about her operation is one the client can answer with real information.
C Because he is not sure the drawing is finished.
2. What turns the loading bay comment into a requirement?
A The fact that the client raised it in the meeting.
B The measured operational cost: ninety seconds each way, forty times a shift.
C The fact that the corridor is narrow.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A “Could you tell me how many pallets do you move?”
B “Could you tell me how many pallets you move?”
C “Could you tell me how many pallets move you?”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions and Softening

GRAMMAR

An opening in front, statement order inside, and no do, does or did. Softer is not weaker: it is the version people answer honestly.

JobFormExample
The openingCould you tell me... / I was wondering... / Do you know...Could you tell me how many pallets you move?
Word order insidesubject before verb, like a statementNot how many pallets do you move. Say how many pallets you move.
No auxiliary do / does / didit disappears insideNot where did the truck enter. Say where the truck entered.
Yes or no question insidewhether (or if)I was wondering whether the bay works for you.
Softening a proposalwould rather / might / it may be worthIt might be worth looking at the corridor again.
The trapthe question mark follows the OPENING, not the insideCould you tell me where it is? — but I was wondering where it is.

Never: Could you tell me how many pallets do you move · I was wondering where did they enter · Do you know when can they review it · I was wondering that the bay works

Stage 2: Put the Client Review in Order

Order

You are opening a review with a client who has forty minutes. Put it in a logical order.

?"Before I start, could you tell me how much time we have?"
?"Then let me walk you through the ground floor first."
?"I should say that the east corner is the part we are least happy with."
?"I was wondering whether the loading bay distance works for your operation."
?"We can address that, but the compromise is a narrower corridor on the north side."
?"Which of these would stop you working, and which would only annoy you?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to know how many pallets the client moves daily. You say:
A "How many pallets do you move?"
B "Could you tell me how many pallets you move in a day?"
C "Could you tell me how many pallets do you move?"
You want to ask a yes-or-no question politely. You say:
A "I was wondering that the bay works for you."
B "I was wondering whether the bay works for you."
C "I was wondering does the bay work for you."
You want to return to a topic without pressing. You say:
A "Would you mind if we came back to the corridor width?"
B "Would you mind if we come back the corridor width?"
C "Would you mind that we come back to the corridor?"
You want to sort the feedback list at the end of a review. You say:
A "Do you like all of these?"
B "Which of these would stop you working, and which would only annoy you?"
C "Are all of these important?"
