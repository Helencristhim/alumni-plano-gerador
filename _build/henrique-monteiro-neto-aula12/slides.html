Chapter 3: The Code

The Tense of Knowing the End

Only a narrator who knows how it finished can use it

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"He was to become the most quoted economist of his generation."

"She would go on to found three institutions, none of which survived her."

"He was about to abandon it altogether when the letter arrived."

"Neither of them was ever to see the result."

Every one of these is set in the past and points forwards. Who is the only kind of person who can speak like this?

FormWhat it doesExample
was / were to + infinitiveA future seen from a point in the past, told by somebody who knows it happened. It carries a sense of destiny, and it is the signature form of biography."He was to become the most quoted economist of his generation."
would + base form
would go on to		A later development, narrated from earlier. Would go on to is the standard formula for what somebody did AFTER the moment being described."She would go on to found three institutions."
was / were about toOn the edge of happening. Almost always followed by an interruption — when something else arrived."He was about to abandon it when the letter came."
was never to
was not to + infinitive		The negative destiny, and the saddest form in English. It says the thing never happened, and that the person could not know that."Neither of them was ever to see the result."
was / were going toAn intention held in the past — and it usually implies the intention was NOT carried out. This is the contrast worth hearing."He was going to publish it in 1974." (and did not)

These forms are only available to a narrator who already knows the ending, which is why they belong to biography, to history and to the retelling of your own life. The moment you use one, you have told your listener that the story has been decided — and you have quietly told them what you think it added up to.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"He was to became the most quoted economist of his generation."
"He was to become the most quoted economist of his generation."
"She would went on to found three institutions."
"She would go on to found three institutions."
"He was about to abandoning it when the letter arrived."
"He was about to abandon it when the letter arrived."

One rule covers all three: after was to, would and about to comes the base form of the verb, never a past and never an -ing. The time is already carried by the first part of the structure, and adding it twice is the commonest slip in the whole family.

The Biographer and the Missing Decade

M
The hardest part of a biography is not the research. It is deciding, on page one, what the life was for — and then trying not to let that decision write the rest of the book.
H
But you already know how it ends. Every sentence you write is written by somebody who knows.
M
Which is why the language gives itself away. The moment I write she was to spend eleven years in Bologna, I have told the reader that those years mattered, before I have shown them anything.
H
And the decade she was overshadowed — the one she never mentioned. How do you write ten years of silence?
M
Badly, in the first draft. I had her waiting for the letter from Bologna, which made the decade a prologue. It was not a prologue to her. It was ten years.
H
That is the honest problem with the form. Nothing in a life is a prologue while you are living it.
M
No. And the paper she wrote in a fortnight, the one that was to be vindicated thirty years later — she filed it under distractions. I have the folder.
H
Which is either very consoling or extremely alarming, and I have not decided which.
Comprehension

About Marta

1. What does Marta say is the hardest part of writing a biography?
Deciding on page one what the life was for, and then not letting that decision write the rest.
2. How does she say the language gives the writer away?
Writing "she was to spend eleven years there" tells the reader those years mattered before showing anything.
3. What was wrong with her first draft of the missing decade?
She had the subject waiting for the letter, which turned ten real years into a prologue.
4. What did the subject herself do with the paper that was later vindicated?
She filed it under distractions.
Listening 1

The First Sentence

An obituary writer on his craft. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say the first sentence has to do?
Decide what the life was about, in a single line, before anybody has read the rest.
2. Which obituaries does he say are the best to write?
Those of people nobody has heard of.
3. What does he say he never writes?
That somebody lost a battle with an illness.
Listening 2

Against the Tidy Life

A historian objecting to biography itself. Sound first — no text.

1. What is her objection to biography?
That it imposes a shape on a life which nobody had while living it.
2. What grammatical form does she single out?
"Was to" — because it announces a destiny the person could not have known.
3. What does she concede at the end?
That she reads biographies constantly and enjoys them.
