Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. All twelve work at a dinner table and in a consulting room. These twelve stay here in your material.

Chronic -- lasting for years rather than for days
"It is chronic rather than serious, which is a distinction nobody makes until they need it."
Prevention -- acting early so that the problem never arrives
"Everything interesting in medicine now is prevention, and almost none of it is dramatic."
Diagnosis -- the doctor naming what is actually happening
"The diagnosis took four months and about eleven minutes to explain."
Immunity -- the body's ability to resist an illness
"Immunity drops a little every decade, which is why the same cold lasts longer now."
Cognitive -- having to do with memory, attention and reasoning
"Cognitive decline is not a single thing, and most of it is not inevitable."
Sedentary -- involving sitting still for most of the day
"Thirty years of a sedentary job leave marks that a good weekend does not undo."
Well-being -- the state of being healthy and reasonably content
"Well-being is the word doctors use when they mean more than blood pressure."
Routine -- a fixed set of things done in the same order
"The routine matters more than the intensity, and nobody wants to hear that."
To deteriorate -- to get slowly and steadily worse
"My hearing has deteriorated in exactly the way my father said it would."
Inflammation -- swelling and heat where the body is fighting something
"Half of what people call aging is inflammation with a longer name."
Longevity -- how long a life lasts, as a subject of study
"Longevity is a fashionable word for a very old question."
Screening -- a routine test done to catch something early
"The screening is twenty minutes once a year and I still have to be reminded."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two answers to the question how are you, and most people only own one of them. The first is fine, thank you, which is true, polite and finished. The second is the whole file: fourteen minutes of dates, tablets and a scan from 2019, at a table where four people were about to talk about something else.

The middle setting has a shape, and it takes about eight seconds. One fact, one measure of what it costs you, and then a question that hands the conversation back. It is chronic rather than serious, so I am managing it rather than fixing it. It takes a toll on me, although less than it did last year. What about you, are you still running?

In a consulting room, in a country where you do not speak the language, the rule reverses completely. There you should be boring. Give the date it started. Say what has deteriorated and what has not. Say what you stopped taking and in which month. Say whether the last screening found anything. And never round a number downwards to make it sound better, which is exactly what most people do when they are nervous.

The grammar underneath all of this is one small trap. I stopped smoking means you are not a smoker. I stopped to smoke means you interrupted your walk to have a cigarette. I remember reading it is a memory. Remember to read it is an instruction. The -ing form points backwards at something real; the to form points forwards at something intended. Behind you, or ahead of you.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Behind You or Ahead of You

GRAMMAR

Four common verbs change meaning completely depending on whether an -ing form or a to form follows them. The test is always the same: is the thing behind you, or ahead of you?

FormWhat it doesExample
stop + verb-ing / stop + to + verbEnded the activity for good, versus interrupted something else in order to do this."I stopped smoking."
"I stopped to smoke."
remember + verb-ing / remember + to + verbA memory of something done, versus an obligation still ahead of you."I remember booking it."
"Remember to book it."
try + verb-ing / try + to + verbAn experiment you can abandon, versus an attempt that may simply fail."Try walking before breakfast."
"I tried to walk and could not."
go on + verb-ing / go on + to + verbContinued the same thing, versus moved on to the next thing."He went on talking."
"He went on to talk about prevention."
The testThe -ing form points backwards at something real. The to form points forwards at something intended.Behind you = -ing
Ahead of you = to

A practical note. Only these four verbs really matter for this, and of the four, stop is the one that changes a sentence into its own opposite. If you learn one pair properly, learn that one: stopped smoking and stopped to smoke are two different lives.
