Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

If This Goes Through

Risk said out loud: what happens if they sign, and what would happen if the clause came out. Key words: to terminate, to indemnify, liability, arbitration, penalty clause, good faith, deadlock, to walk away. Structure: first and second conditionals -- the if that will, and the if that would.

Stage 1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Four words from the clause, four from the room where it is negotiated. Listen to each one and read it in context.

to terminate -- to end a contract early, using a right the contract gives you
"A breach of the delivery clause gives us the right to terminate."
to indemnify -- to promise to pay for a loss that the other side suffers
"We indemnify the buyer only for losses caused by our own delay."
liability -- the legal responsibility to pay when something goes wrong
"Our liability is capped at the value of the shipment."
arbitration -- a private way of deciding a dispute, outside the courts
"Arbitration is faster and private, but it costs more at the start."
penalty clause -- the part of the contract that says how much a late or failed delivery costs
"Their penalty clause is two per cent a week, and that is too high for us."
good faith -- the honest intention to keep the agreement, even where it says nothing
"We negotiate in good faith, and we expect the same from the other side."
deadlock -- the point where neither side will move and the talk stops
"We reached a deadlock on the cap and parked it until the morning."
to walk away -- to leave a negotiation without a deal
"If that is the final position, we will have to walk away."

Stage 3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

Most of a contract negotiation is not about the law. It is about consequences, said out loud, by somebody who can see them earlier than everybody else in the room. And in English, consequences have two shapes.

The first shape is for what is on the table. "If they sign on Friday, the first shipment will leave in three weeks." Nothing imaginary here: signing is likely, and the sentence is a plan. The verb after if stays in the present, and the will waits for the other half. That is the whole form, and the one mistake worth remembering is putting will in both halves.

The second shape is for what you are imagining in order to show what is wrong with it. "If we capped our liability at one shipment, a serious quality problem would cost us five times what the buyer could claim." Nobody is capping anything. The past form does not point to yesterday: it marks the sentence as hypothetical, and the would carries the consequence.

This second shape is worth more than its grammar. It is how a lawyer says no without closing the door. You are not refusing the person in front of you; you are showing the room what happens if the idea goes through. "If I gave you London and the cap on the same day, I would have nothing left to take to my director" is a refusal, and it still sounds like cooperation.

The same shape opens doors. At a deadlock, "if we parked this clause and came back to it tomorrow, we would both think more clearly" is a proposal that nobody has to lose in order to accept. And when the talk really is over, the first shape comes back, plain and calm: "if that is your final position, we will have to walk away."

Stage 4: Grammar Tip -- first and second conditionals

GRAMMAR

Two sentences start with the same word and do opposite jobs. The question is not about the verb: it is about whether the thing is on the table or in your imagination.

FormUseExample
First conditional
if + present, will + verb		real and likely: it is on the table todayIf they sign on Friday, we will start production.
Second conditional
if + past, would + verb		hypothetical: you imagine it to show what is wrong with itIf we capped the liability, we would lose money.
Never will after ifsay "if they sign", never "if they will sign". The will belongs to the other half of the sentence.
were, not wasin the formal second conditional, be becomes were for everybody: "if our liability were unlimited". "If I were you" is the same rule.
Either half can come first"We will start production if they sign on Friday." When the if comes second, the comma disappears.
What it is forthe first conditional offers. The second conditional refuses and proposes without pressing. Both keep the deal alive.

Quick test before you speak: is this on the table, or am I imagining it in order to show a consequence?

Quiz

Real situations from your week. Only one option is both correct and natural.

A If they will sign on Friday, the shipment will leave in three weeks.
B If they sign on Friday, the shipment will leave in three weeks.
C If they signed on Friday, the shipment will leave in three weeks.
D If they sign on Friday, the shipment would leave in three weeks.
2. You want to refuse a liability cap without closing the door. What do you say?
A No. That cap is impossible for us and we will not discuss it.
B I am afraid that is the one I cannot give you. If we capped it there, a quality problem would cost us far more than you could claim.
C If we would cap it there, we would have a big problem.

3. Which sentence describes arbitration correctly?
A It is the clause that says how much a late delivery costs.
B It is a private way of deciding a dispute, outside the courts.
C It is the promise to pay for a loss the other side suffers.
D It is the point where neither side will move.
4. The talk has reached a deadlock and everybody is tired. Which sentence proposes without pressing?
A We will stop here and you will send us a new proposal tomorrow.
B If we will park this clause, we will think more clearly tomorrow.
C If we parked this clause and came back to it tomorrow, we would both think more clearly.
