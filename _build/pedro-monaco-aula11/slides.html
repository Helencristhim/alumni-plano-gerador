Chapter 3: The Code

The Shape of the Second Verb

Drafting, to draft, for drafting — and who decides

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The role involves drafting contracts in English every week."

"I decided to take ownership of the templates that nobody wanted."

"They expected me to grow into the job within a year."

"I am responsible for reviewing every supplier contract we sign."

Every one of these sentences has two verbs in a row. The second one changes shape each time. What decides it — and is it ever your choice?

PatternWhen it happensExample
verb + -ingAfter involve, enjoy, avoid, consider, keep, risk, suggest, recommend, mind, deny. The second verb behaves like a noun: it is the thing itself."The role involves drafting contracts."
verb + to + infinitiveAfter decide, manage, agree, offer, refuse, hope, plan, learn, tend, fail, expect, seem. The second verb is the direction you are heading in, not the thing itself."I decided to take ownership of the templates."
verb + object + to + infinitiveAfter expect, want, ask, allow, encourage, advise, tell, the person comes first and the to comes after them — never a that clause."They expected me to grow into the job."
never "they expected that I grow"
preposition + -ingAnything after for, in, of, on, about, before, after takes -ing. And in look forward to the to is a PREPOSITION, so it takes -ing too."responsible for reviewing", "interested in moving", "I look forward to hearing from you."
the pairs that change meaningremember / stop / try / regret / go on take both, and the meaning moves. -ing looks BACK at what happened; to looks FORWARD at the purpose."I remember reading it" (the memory)
"I remembered to read it" (the task)

Portuguese joins two verbs with an infinitive almost every time (decidi assumir, a vaga envolve redigir), so there is nothing in your first language telling you that English sometimes wants -ing. This is memory, not logic: the verb in front carries the pattern with it.

Chapter 4: The Room

Forty Minutes with the Hiring Manager

Rebecca has your CV open and one question you cannot prepare for

Dialogue

Walk Me Through It

R
Let us start where everybody starts. Walk me through what you do now, and tell me why you are drawn to this role.
P
I draft and review commercial contracts in-house, and last year I took ownership of our contract templates. I am drawn to this role because you are opening the office my clients keep asking me about.
R
The job involves negotiating in English most weeks. Everybody tells me they enjoy that. Tell me about a time it went badly.
P
A supplier agreed to a cap and then asked to remove it the night before signature. I remember reading the new draft at eleven and deciding to stop the whole thing.
R
So you stopped a signature to protect one clause. Did anybody thank you that week?
P
Nobody. Three months later the same supplier missed a deadline, and my boss remembered to mention it in front of the board.
R
Good answer. Here is the one nobody prepares for. What is your blind spot — what would your team say about you that you would not say about yourself?
P
I would rather answer that honestly than well. I tend to assume that people read what I send them. I have learned to say the important thing twice: once in writing, once out loud.
Comprehension

About Rebecca

1. How does Rebecca ask him to begin?
She asks him to walk her through what he does now, and to say why he is drawn to the role.
2. What does she say everybody tells her about negotiating in English?
That they enjoy it — and then she asks for a time when it went badly.
3. What does she ask after he explains that he stopped the signature?
Whether anybody thanked him for it that week.
4. Which question does she say nobody prepares for?
What his blind spot is: what his team would say about him that he would not say about himself.
Listening 1

The Question Nobody Prepares For

A hiring manager on what the unexpected question is actually testing. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say the unexpected question is really for?
Not to catch anybody out — to see how the person thinks when the prepared answer has run out.
2. What does he say about candidates who have rehearsed everything?
They sound excellent for twenty minutes and then the first real question arrives and there is nobody there.
3. What does he advise a candidate to do in the five seconds after a hard question?
Say out loud that it is a good question and take the time — nobody has ever lost a job for thinking for five seconds.
Listening 2

Adjectives Are Free, Stories Are Not

A legal recruiter on the answer that separates two identical CVs. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say happens to most answers about strengths and weaknesses?
They come out as adjectives, and adjectives are free — anybody can claim them.
2. What does she ask for instead?
A story with a date, a decision and a consequence: what happened, what the person did, and what came of it.
3. What does she say about candidates working in a second language?
They lose points for hiding behind short answers, not for their grammar — a long, imperfect story beats a short, perfect one.
