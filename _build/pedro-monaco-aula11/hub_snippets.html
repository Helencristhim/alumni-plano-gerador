Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to the day of an interview, not to the CV that got you there.

A shortlist -- the small group of candidates a company decides to interview
"The shortlist was four people and three of them were already in-house."
A hiring manager -- the person who will be your boss and who makes the final decision
"The hiring manager had read the contract I wrote before we spoke."
To land a role -- to succeed in being offered the job
"She landed the role after three rounds and one very bad Tuesday."
To be a good fit -- to suit the team and the way it works
"On paper I was a good fit, and the interview was about proving it."
A steep learning curve -- a situation where you have to learn a great deal very fast
"The first year in-house was a steep learning curve and nobody pretended otherwise."
To take ownership of -- to accept full responsibility for something until it is finished
"I took ownership of the contract templates that nobody wanted."
To thrive on -- to do your best work in a particular kind of pressure
"I thrive on deadlines that other people find uncomfortable."
A stepping stone -- a job you take because of where it leads next
"The role was a stepping stone and I said so in the interview."
To be drawn to -- to be attracted to something for a particular reason
"I was drawn to the company because it is opening an office where my clients are."
To come across as -- to give a particular impression to other people
"I did not want to come across as somebody who only reads contracts."
A blind spot -- a weakness you cannot see in yourself
"My blind spot was assuming that everybody read the email I sent."
To grow into -- to become able to do a role that is bigger than you are now
"They hired somebody they expected to grow into the job."
A curveball -- a question you did not see coming
"The last question was a curveball and I had five seconds to find an answer."
To walk somebody through -- to explain something step by step, from start to finish
"Let me walk you through what I actually did, rather than what the CV says."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Almost every useful sentence in a job interview has two verbs stuck together, and English decides the shape of the second one for you. After some verbs it becomes -ing: the role involves drafting contracts, I enjoy negotiating, I avoid promising what I cannot deliver. After others it takes to: I decided to take ownership of the templates, I managed to close it that week, I learned to say no earlier.

There is no rule you can work out from meaning, which is the uncomfortable part. The verb in front carries the pattern with it, the way a noun carries its gender in Portuguese. You learn involve together with -ing, and decide together with to, in the same way you learned the expression itself.

Two patterns cause most of the damage. The first is the person in the middle: they expected me to grow into the job, never they expected that I grow. The second is any preposition: after for, in, of, about, before and after, the verb is always -ing. I am responsible for reviewing every supplier contract. And in look forward to that little word is a preposition too, which is why the correct sentence is I look forward to hearing from you.

Finally, a small group takes both and changes meaning: remember, stop, try, regret, go on. The -ing form looks BACK at something that happened; the to form looks FORWARD at a purpose. I remember reading the draft is a memory. I remembered to read the draft is a task you did not forget.

1. Why is it "the role involves drafting" and not "involves to draft"?
A Because drafting sounds more formal in an interview.
B Because the verb in front, involve, carries the -ing pattern with it.
C Because the sentence is in the present.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A They expected that I grow into the job.
B They expected me to grow into the job.
C They expected me growing into the job.
3. Why is "I look forward to hearing from you" correct?
A Because look forward to is always followed by a noun.
B Because the to in look forward to is a preposition, and prepositions take -ing.
C Because hearing is in the past.
4. You want to say that the memory is still with you. Which one?
A I remembered to read the new draft at eleven.
B I remember reading the new draft at eleven.
C I remember to read the new draft at eleven.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Gerunds and Infinitives

GRAMMAR

Portuguese joins two verbs with an infinitive nearly every time, so nothing in your first language warns you that English sometimes wants -ing. Treat the pattern as part of the word: learn involve and -ing together, the way you learned the expression itself.

PatternWhen it happensExample
verb + -ingAfter involve, enjoy, avoid, consider, keep, risk, suggest, recommend, mind, deny. The second verb behaves like a noun: it is the thing itself."The role involves drafting contracts in English."
verb + to + infinitiveAfter decide, manage, agree, offer, refuse, hope, plan, learn, tend, fail, expect, seem. The second verb is where you are heading, not the thing itself."I decided to take ownership of the templates."
verb + object + to + infinitiveAfter expect, want, ask, allow, encourage, advise, tell, the person comes first and to comes after them. A that clause here is the commonest Portuguese transfer of all."They expected me to grow into the job."
never "they expected that I grow"
preposition + -ingAfter for, in, of, on, about, before, after, the verb is always -ing. And the to in look forward to is a preposition, not an infinitive."responsible for reviewing"
"I look forward to hearing from you."
the pairs that change meaningremember, stop, try, regret, go on take both. The -ing form looks BACK at what happened; the to form looks FORWARD at the purpose."I remember signing it" (the memory)
"I remembered to sign it" (the task)

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to how a first interview runs from the shortlist to the last question, then put the steps in order.

?You are put on a shortlist, and somebody decides you are worth an hour of their day.
?The hiring manager asks you to walk her through what you do now, and why you are drawn to the role.
?You answer with examples rather than adjectives, and she stops you to ask when it happened.
?A curveball arrives, and you take five seconds before you say anything at all.
?You ask your own questions, which is where they find out whether you would be a good fit.

Reflection

Answer the first question of any interview: tell me about yourself, and why you are drawn to this kind of work. Speak for about ninety seconds. Give one story with a date, a decision and a consequence -- not a list of adjectives -- and use at least four pairs of verbs joined together.
