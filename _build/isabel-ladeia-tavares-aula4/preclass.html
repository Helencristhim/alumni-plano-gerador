Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Read the Protocol, Do Not Guess -- The Recipe on the Bench

A protocol has no opinions and almost no subjects, and one word buried in the middle of a step can cost you an afternoon. Key words: a protocol, a reagent, to dilute, to incubate, a batch, to discard, a hazard, to label, to rinse, to weigh out, to make up a solution, thoroughly. Structure: the sentence that starts with a verb, and the small words that decide the order.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. You already do all twelve of these things every week.

A protocol -- the written set of steps that tells you exactly how to run an experiment
"Do not run the protocol the way it is printed. Step four changed last month."
A reagent -- a substance you add in order to make a reaction happen or to test for something
"This reagent must not be handled outside the fume hood."
To dilute -- to make a liquid weaker by adding water or another liquid to it
"Dilute the sample one to ten before you measure anything."
To incubate -- to keep something at a fixed temperature for a fixed time, so a process can happen
"Once the mixture is clear, incubate the tubes for twenty minutes."
A batch -- a whole group of samples prepared and processed together, at the same time
"The batch came out cloudy every single time."
To discard -- to throw something away, deliberately and in the right place
"Waste must be discarded in the marked container by the door."
A hazard -- something with the potential to cause harm, before anything has gone wrong
"The warning tells you where the hazard is, and it is written as must not."
To label -- to write on something what it is, so that the next person is not guessing
"Label every tube before you fill it, not after."
To rinse -- to wash something with clean water in order to remove what is left on it
"Do not rinse the glassware until the whole batch is finished."
To weigh out -- to measure out the exact quantity of a solid that a step asks for
"First, weigh out the powder. Then make up the solution."
To make up a solution -- to prepare a solution by mixing the substance with liquid to a set volume
"Make up the solution with the buffer from the fridge, not the one on the shelf."
Thoroughly -- completely and carefully, leaving nothing behind. The opposite of quickly
"Rinse thoroughly, except after the final incubation."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Here is protocol 7B, and every line of it is doing a different job. Before you start, label every tube. Weigh out two and a half grams of the powder. Make up the solution with the buffer from the fridge, not the one on the shelf. Notice what is missing from all three: there is no I, no you and no we. The sentence opens on the verb, because the protocol is not describing what somebody did. It is telling the next person what to do.

Allow the tubes to stand for five minutes before adding the reagent. Dilute each sample one to ten. Once the mixture is clear, incubate at thirty-seven degrees for twenty minutes. Those small words carry the whole experiment. Once and after mean that something has to have happened already; before and until mark where a step stops. Do not rinse the glassware until the batch is complete.

Then there are the two lines that are not steps at all. This reagent must not be handled outside the fume hood, and waste must be discarded in the marked container. Must is not should: it marks the hazard, and it is in the document because somebody was hurt. Finally, the line that catches everybody: rinse thoroughly, except after the final incubation. One word, in the middle of a step, and if you read at speed that is the word you miss.

1. "Label every tube." Who is being told to do it, and where is that word?
A The author of the protocol, and the word is missing by mistake.
B Whoever is reading it. The word is not there on purpose, because instructions have no subject.
C Nobody. The sentence is a description of what usually happens.
2. "Do not rinse the glassware until the batch is complete." When do you rinse?
A While the batch is running, whenever there is time.
B After the batch is finished. Until marks where the prohibition stops.
C Never, in this protocol.
3. Why does the text say that must is not the same as should?
A Because must marks a hazard and is a rule, not advice. It is there because somebody was hurt.
B Because must is more formal, and the meaning is the same.
C Because should is used in speech and must in writing.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Sentence That Starts With a Verb

GRAMMAR

The verb comes first, the small word decides the order, and must is never a suggestion.

UseFormExample
An instructionbase verb, no subjectDilute the sample.
A prohibitionDo not + base verbDo not rinse the glassware yet.
OrderFirst, then, after that, finallyFirst weigh out the powder. Then make up the solution.
Something must already be trueonce / afterOnce the mixture is clear, incubate.
Where a step stopsbefore / untilDo not rinse until the batch is complete.
The rule you cannot breakmust / must not + be + third formWaste must be discarded in the marked container.

Never: You must to weigh out the powder · Not rinse the glassware yet · Until the mixture is clear, incubate · This reagent should not be handled outside the fume hood · Waste must discarded in the container.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real protocol would use.

You are writing step one of your own protocol. You write:
A "You must to label every tube."
B "Before you start, label every tube."
C "Every tube is labelled by you first."
The mixture has to be clear before the tubes go into the incubator. You write:
A "Until the mixture is clear, incubate the tubes."
B "Once the mixture is clear, incubate the tubes."
C "Before the mixture is clear, incubate the tubes."
A reagent is dangerous outside the fume hood. The protocol says:
A "This reagent must not be handled outside the fume hood."
B "This reagent should not be handled outside the fume hood."
C "Try not to handle this reagent outside the fume hood."
You are unsure whether a step applies to your samples. The right move is:
A Run it anyway and write a note about it afterwards.
B Stop and ask. It costs two minutes to ask and an afternoon to guess wrong.
C Skip the step, because an unclear step is usually optional.
