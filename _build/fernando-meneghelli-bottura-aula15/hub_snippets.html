The last rehearsal: the first four seconds, the shape of an answer, the takeaway line and the closing statement, plus what you do in the four hours after the room. Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These twelve are for disagreeing with somebody more senior than you, in a language that is not yours — firmly, and never defensively.

A dress rehearsal -- the last full practice, done exactly as the real thing
“Today is the dress rehearsal: no stopping, no notes, no second attempts.”
A dry run -- a practice with no audience and no consequences
“I do one dry run the night before, out loud, in the room I will be sitting in.”
To sign off -- to approve something formally, so it can go ahead
“The board signed off the plan in November, and that is the sentence I use.”
A closing statement -- the last thing you say, prepared in advance
“My closing statement is three sentences, and it has not changed in two weeks.”
A takeaway line -- the one sentence you want them to repeat about you
“The takeaway line is the cash cycle: sixty days into thirty, without losing a client.”
To follow up -- to send what you promised, quickly, after the meeting
“I follow up the same afternoon, with the number I said I would send.”
An open loop -- something you promised and have not closed
“An open loop after an interview is worse than a weak answer inside it.”
To rehearse -- to practise something out loud before it matters
“Rehearsing is not memorising: it is choosing the order once, and keeping it.”
Muscle memory -- what comes out without thinking, because you trained it
“The protocol is muscle memory now, and that is the point of fifteen lessons.”
A cold start -- beginning with no warm-up, the way real interviews begin
“Every interview is a cold start, and the first sentence is the one that costs.”
To debrief -- to go through what happened, with numbers, straight after
“We debrief with four numbers, and never with adjectives.”
To land the role -- to be given the job at the end of the process
“Landing the role is not the target today. Being the same person twice is.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

This is the dress rehearsal. Nothing new goes in today, and everything that is already there gets said out loud once more: the first four seconds, the shape of an answer, the takeaway line, and the closing statement that I decided two weeks ago and have not changed since.

What I learned in fifteen lessons is that an interview is not a test of English. It is a cold start with no warm-up, and the first sentence is the one that costs. So I have one line for the moment nothing comes, one shape for every answer, and one sentence I want them to repeat about me when I am not in the room.

And it does not end when I leave. I follow up the same afternoon with whatever I promised, because an open loop after an interview is worse than a weak answer inside it. Then I write down every question in their words, choose one thing to change, and stop. That last part is the one I used to get wrong.

1. What is the purpose of a dress rehearsal?
A To learn something new before the real thing.
B To say out loud, once more, what is already prepared.
C To test whether the English is good enough.
2. Why does the first sentence matter so much?
A Because the interview is a cold start with no warm-up.
B Because interviewers only listen to the beginning.
C Because it is the only part you can prepare.
3. What is an open loop?
A A question you answered badly.
B Something you promised and did not send.
C A question the panel forgot to ask.
4. What does the text say to do after the interview?
A Replay the worst thirty seconds until you understand it.
B Send what you promised, write the questions down, choose one thing, and stop.
C Wait for feedback before writing anything.

Why it matters in an interview"I used to find that difficult, and I am used to it now" answers half the hostile questions in any process. It shows change without claiming to have been perfect — which is precisely what a panel is listening for.

The three together make a complete arc in one breath: what you used to do, what you are used to now, and how long it took to get used to it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The question has an accusation inside it. Choose the answer that is not defensive.

The interview starts with no greeting and a two-minute question. You:
A Ask for a moment to get organised.
B Open with a headline, give two reasons, and stop.
C Start with the year you left university.
How many times do you say your takeaway line?
A Once, slowly, where it belongs.
B Three times, so nobody misses it.
C In the first minute, to set the tone.
The interviewer asks if there is anything they should have asked. You:
A Say no, to keep it short.
B Ask what would make them say no to you.
C Repeat your strongest answer.
You promised to send a figure. You send it:
A The same afternoon.
B When they contact you again.
C Only if you get to the next round.
After a difficult interview, the useful thing to do is:
A Replay the worst part until you understand it.
B Write the questions down, choose one thing to change, and stop.
C Ask a friend whether it sounded bad.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is correcting this one, and there is no score.

Record your closing statement: three sentences, thirty seconds, no question asked. What you do, the proof, and what you would do in their company. One take, no notes. Then record the two-minute career answer as a cold start, with no warm-up. Listen to both once and check two things only: did the first sentence carry the headline, and did you stop after two reasons?
