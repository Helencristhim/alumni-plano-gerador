Chapter 4: Real Speed

Three Acronyms in One Sentence

One fast call, two recordings, one cheat sheet

Dialogue

Three Letters with Ingrid

I
Before the pilot, our DPO wants the DPA signed, and the SLA has to cover the close.
F
Understood on the SLA. What does DPO stand for, in your company?
I
Data protection officer. She signs off anything that touches customer data.
F
Thank you. And the DPA is the data processing agreement? I want to double-check, because we use a different name in Brazil.
I
Exactly. And the close is four days a month, so the uptime there is ninety-nine point nine, or it is no use to us.
F
Sorry, did you say ninety-nine point nine or ninety-nine point five?
I
Point nine during the close. The rest of the month, point five is fine.
F
Let me paraphrase, so there is no misunderstanding: the DPA is signed before the pilot, and the SLA guarantees point nine during the four days of the close. Are you saying the rest of the month can be the standard SLA?
Comprehension

About Ingrid

1. What does Ingrid want before the pilot starts?
The DPA signed, because her data protection officer has to approve anything that touches customer data.
2. Why does the close matter so much to Ingrid?
Because the close is four days a month when finance cannot stop, so the system has to be available at ninety-nine point nine during those days.
3. What uptime does Ingrid accept for the rest of the month?
Ninety-nine point five. Only the four days of the close need ninety-nine point nine.
Listening 1 · The Third Option

Nod, Apologize, or Ask

A negotiation consultant on the second after you miss something. Sound first — no text.

1. What are the two things most people do when they do not understand?
They nod and hope it becomes clear later, or they apologize for their English and ask for everything again.
2. Why does he say both of those are expensive?
Nodding creates a misunderstanding that appears weeks later in the contract; asking for everything sends the call back to the beginning and makes you look like the problem.
3. What is the third option, and what do people hear when you use it?
Repeat the part you caught and ask only for the missing piece. People hear somebody who is paying attention, not somebody with a problem.
Listening 2 · From the Other Side

Who She Trusts Afterwards

A partner manager on the people who ask questions. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say about the people who ask the most questions?
That they are usually the people she trusts the most afterwards.
2. What two things does a paraphrase tell her?
That the person was listening, and that if they understood it wrong, she can correct it now in ten seconds instead of later with lawyers.
3. Which people worry her, and why?
The ones who never ask anything, because the proposal later shows they understood the gist but not the details.

Four acronyms and one question that covers every other one. Notice that none of the questions on the right is about the letters themselves: once you know what an acronym stands for, the useful question is what it means for this deal. End of day is the best example. It sounds like a time, and between São Paulo and Utrecht it is two different times.
1. Why is "end of day your time or mine?" the best example on the card?
Because end of day sounds like one time, but between São Paulo and Utrecht it is two different times, so a deadline can be missed without anybody noticing.
2. The note says the letters are not the useful question. What is?
What the acronym means for this specific deal: which SLA, who signs, whether the PO is ready or only requested.
3. What is the last line of the card for?
Every acronym that is not on the card. One question covers all of them: what does that stand for?
