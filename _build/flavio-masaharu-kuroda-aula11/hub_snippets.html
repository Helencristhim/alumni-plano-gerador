Say That Again -- When You Do Not Understand on the Call

You understood ninety per cent of the sentence, and the missing ten per cent was the number or the acronym. Key words: to clarify, to paraphrase, to catch, an acronym, jargon, a misunderstanding, to double-check, to spell out, to lose track, the gist. Structure: checking questions and paraphrase.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To clarify -- to make something easier to understand by explaining it again
"Could you clarify one thing for me before we go on?"
To paraphrase -- to say what somebody said, using your own words
"Let me paraphrase, to be sure I have it right."
To catch -- to hear and understand a word somebody says
"Sorry, I did not catch the last number."
An acronym -- a word made from the first letters of other words, like ERP
"Every company has its own acronyms, and nobody explains them."
Jargon -- words that only people inside one industry or company understand
"Half of that sentence was internal jargon, and she did not notice."
A misunderstanding -- a situation where two people understand the same thing differently
"A misunderstanding on the call becomes a problem in the contract."
To double-check -- to check something a second time, to be sure
"Can I double-check the date? I have the fifteenth."
To spell out -- to explain something clearly and in detail, with nothing left implied
"Could you spell out what the pilot includes?"
To lose track -- to stop following what is being said or what is happening
"I lost track after the second acronym."
The gist -- the main idea of something, without the details
"I got the gist, but I missed the numbers."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

On a fast call you rarely lose the whole sentence. You lose one piece: the number, the date, or three letters that everybody else seems to know. What you do in the next second decides how the call continues. If you nod, you create a misunderstanding that appears three weeks later, in the contract. If you apologize and ask for everything again, the call goes back to the beginning.

The better move is a checking question: repeat the part you did catch, and ask only for the missing piece. "Did you say the fifteenth or the fifth?" gives the other person two options, so the answer takes one word. "What does DPO stand for?" asks about the letters of an acronym; "What does onboarding mean for you?" asks about a word. In both, the auxiliary is does, and the verb after it has no -s.

Sometimes you know every word and still do not know the message. Then you check with a statement and let your voice go up: "So you mean the pilot is paid?" Or you check a conclusion with a full sentence: "Are you saying the budget is already approved?" Do not drop the verb: the budget approved is not a sentence.

Finally, paraphrase to close the point: "In other words, the DPA comes before the pilot." Two things to avoid. What means DPO? is a translation, not English. And Can you repeat again? says the same thing twice, because repeat already means again. A good checking question does not sound like a problem. It sounds like somebody who is paying attention and wants the gist and the details.

1. According to the text, why is nodding when you do not understand expensive?

2. Why does "Did you say the fifteenth or the fifth?" work so well?

B It asks the other person to repeat the whole sentence more slowly.

3. What is wrong with "Can you repeat again?"
A The auxiliary should be does, not can.
B Nothing. It is the most polite way to ask.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Checking Question

GRAMMAR

Repeat the part you caught. Ask only for the part you missed.

JobFormExample
Two options, pick oneDid you say + X + or + Y?Did you say fifteen or fifty?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The partner says "the SLA and the DPA before the pilot", and you do not know what DPA means. You say:
A "Sorry for my English, can you repeat everything?"
B "Understood on the SLA. What does DPA stand for?"
C "What means DPA?"
You heard a date, but it could be the fifteenth or the fifth. You say:
A "Sorry, did you say the fifteenth or the fifth?"
B "Can you repeat again the date?"
C Nothing. You write the fifteenth and check it later by email.
The partner says "the board likes it, but the timing is complicated". You want to check what that means. You say:
A "Are you saying the project delayed?"
B "Great, thank you."
C "Are you saying the project is moving to next year?"
At the end of a complicated point, you want to be sure you both understood the same thing. You say:
A "OK, OK, perfect."
B "Let me paraphrase, so there is no misunderstanding: the DPA comes first, then the pilot."
C "What does it stands for?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Think of a real call where you did not understand something: an acronym, a number, or a vague answer. Record one minute replaying that moment in English. Ask for the missing piece with one checking question, check one number with two options, and close with a paraphrase that starts "In other words".
