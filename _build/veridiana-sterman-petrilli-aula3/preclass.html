Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The belt at baggage claim stops, everybody else walks away with a suitcase, and yours is not there. The first sentence you say at the desk decides how the next hour goes, and English has one tense for exactly this moment: a problem that started before now and is still open. "My bag has not arrived." No day, no hour, no story. Just the fact, and the fact is not finished.

Four small words live inside this tense. Just means a minute ago, and it sits between have and the verb: "They have just told me it is in Panama." Already means sooner than the other person expects, and it sits in the same place: "I have already filed a claim." Yet means not up to now, and it goes at the end, in a negative or in a question: "It has not arrived yet." "Have you filed a claim yet?"

The fourth is still, and it carries the impatience the other three do not: "It is nine in the evening and I still have not received it." Notice where it goes — in front of have not, not after it. Say that sentence at a desk and the agent hears how long you have been waiting without you having to complain.

One trap, and it is the Brazilian one. The moment you say when, English changes tense. "I filed the claim yesterday, at the airport" — simple past, because yesterday is finished. "I have filed a claim" — present perfect, because the case is open. Same claim, two tenses, and the difference is not the claim: it is whether you name the time. And when the reference number is in your hand and the bag is delayed, not missing, there is one more sentence worth saying: "What am I entitled to tonight?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect with Just, Already, Yet and Still

GRAMMAR

No when, no simple past. If the problem is still open, English says have plus the participle.

JobFormExample
A problem that is still openhave / has + past participleMy bag has not arrived.
A minute agohave + just + participleThey have just told me.
Sooner than expectedhave + already + participleI have already filed a claim.
Not up to nowyet, at the end, in a negative or a questionIt has not arrived yet.
Not up to now, and I am tired of waitingstill, before have notI still have not received it.
QuestionHave / Has + subject + participleHave you filed a claim yet?
In speechhave not becomes haven’t · has not becomes hasn’tIt hasn’t arrived yet.

Never: my bag did not arrive yet · I have filed the claim yesterday · have you already the reference number · I have still not received it
