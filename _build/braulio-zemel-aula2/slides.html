Chapter 3: The Code

The Story You Almost Did Not Tell

If I had known · I would have said

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If I had known they were Americans, I would have said something."

"If she had not introduced us, we would never have met."

"If I had stayed at the company, I would not have built the house in Bahia."

"I would have gone to Denmark with her earlier if I had had the time."

All four sentences are about things that did not happen. Which half of each sentence is the condition, and which is the result?

FormWhat it saysExample
If + past perfect
If I had known		The condition that was never true. It is finished, and it cannot change."If I had known they were Americans..."
would have + past participle
would have said		The result that never happened, because the condition never happened."...I would have said something."
Negative in either halfPut not where you need it. Either half can be negative, or both."If she had not introduced us, we would never have met."
Result firstYou can start with the result. No comma when the if comes second."I would have gone earlier if I had had the time."
How it really soundsIn speech, would have becomes would’ve and had almost disappears. Train the sound, not the spelling."If I’d known, I would’ve said something."

This is the structure that makes people lean in at a table. A fact is I retired this year. A story is if I had retired two years earlier, I would have seen Japan already.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If I would have known, I would have said something."
"If I had known, I would have said something."
"If she had not introduced us, we would never met."
"If she had not introduced us, we would never have met."
"If I had had more time, I will have gone with her."
"If I had had more time, I would have gone with her."

The if half never takes would — it takes the past perfect. The result half always keeps have before the participle, even with never in the middle. And a finished, impossible past never takes will. Say the correct versions aloud twice, fast, until would’ve comes out as one sound.

The Chair Next to Elena

E
You are Marina's husband, is that right? I am Elena. I have been sitting here rehearsing something to say to you for two courses.
B
Then we are in the same situation. I am Braulio. What were you rehearsing?
E
Something about the weather, which is why I gave up. Where are you from originally?
B
Sao Paulo. Although half of me is in Bahia now — I built a house there and I go every month.
E
Every month. If I had known that was possible, I would have done it years ago. I am from Milan, and I have been here eleven years.
B
Eleven years. Do you keep up with what is happening in Italy?
E
Obsessively. My husband says I read more Italian news than Italians do.
B
Then we have found some common ground, because I do the same with three newspapers every morning. Ask me anything about Brazilian politics.
Comprehension

About Elena

1. Where is Elena from, and how long has she been in Sao Paulo?
She is from Milan, and she has been here for eleven years.
2. What had she been doing for two courses before she spoke?
Rehearsing something to say to him.
3. Why did she give up on her first idea?
Because it was about the weather.
4. What does her husband say about her news habit?
That she reads more Italian news than Italians do.

Listening 2

Why the Weather Is Not the Point

Ninety seconds from a radio programme. Sound first — no text.

1. According to the programme, what is the weather question really for?
It is a test signal that says the speaker is open and friendly.
2. What did researchers find about the topic of a first conversation?
That the topic almost never matters.
3. What is the one thing the programme says to take away?
Do not worry about your opening line -- worry about your second question.
