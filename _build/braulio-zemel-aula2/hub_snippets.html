The Art of Small Talk -- The Table You Never Sat At

The second minute of a conversation, and the structure that turns a fact into a story. Key expressions: to break the ice, to strike up, small talk, awkward, to bring up, common ground, to hit it off, to pick up on, to keep up with, tongue-tied, spontaneous, to get on with. Structure: the third conditional -- if I had known, I would have said something.

Vocabulary

To break the ice -- to say or do something that makes a first conversation easier
"Somebody has to break the ice, and it is usually not me."
To strike up -- to start a conversation with somebody you do not know
"He struck up a conversation with me before the first course arrived."
Small talk -- light conversation about nothing important, used to open a door
"Small talk is not empty. It is the door to the real conversation."
Awkward -- uncomfortable and slightly embarrassing
"There was an awkward silence, and I let it grow."
To bring up -- to introduce a subject into a conversation
"She brought up the election, and suddenly I had something to say."
Common ground -- an interest or an opinion that two people share
"We found common ground in about forty seconds: both of us read too much news."
To hit it off -- to like each other immediately
"We hit it off, and my wife could not believe it."
To pick up on -- to notice something small that other people miss
"He picked up on my accent and asked where in Brazil I was from."
To keep up with -- to follow something that keeps moving or changing
"I keep up with American politics better than some Americans do."
Tongue-tied -- unable to speak because you are nervous
"I was not tongue-tied in Portuguese. I had plenty to say."
Spontaneous -- happening naturally, without any planning
"The best part of the evening was completely spontaneous."
To get on with -- to have a good, easy relationship with somebody
"I get on with her husband far better than I expected to."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

For about fifteen years he was the quiet one at dinner. His wife's English was better than his had ever been, so at every table abroad she talked and he ate. People thought he was serious. He was not serious. He was tongue-tied.

What changed was not a course. It was one evening in Lisbon, when a man across the table picked up on something he had said about the price of housing and asked him a direct question. He answered in five words. The man asked another. Twenty minutes later they were still going. If that man had not asked the second question, he would have gone home the same man he arrived as.

Since then he has watched how it works, and it is less impressive than people think. Somebody breaks the ice with something small and slightly boring. Somebody else brings up a subject with a little weight in it. Two people find common ground and hit it off, and the rest of the table relaxes. Small talk is not the opposite of real conversation. It is the corridor you walk down to reach it.

The part he got wrong for fifteen years was thinking he had to be fluent to join in. He did not. He had to be curious out loud. If he had understood that at forty, he would have had fifteen more years of dinners instead of fifteen years of nodding politely at his plate.

1. Why is "if that man had not asked the second question" in the past perfect and not the past simple?
A Because it is a condition that never happened -- the third conditional needs the past perfect.
B Because it happened a very long time ago.
C Because the sentence is a question.
2. "He would have gone home the same man he arrived as." Did he go home the same man?
A Yes, nothing changed that evening.
B No -- the result never happened, because the man did ask the second question.
C The text does not say.
3. What does the text say small talk is?
A A waste of everybody's time.
B The corridor you walk down to reach the real conversation.
C Something only fluent speakers can do.
4. What does tongue-tied mean here?
A Unable to get the words out because of nerves.
B Uninterested in the conversation.
C Speaking too much and too fast.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Third Conditional

GRAMMAR

One structure, two halves, and neither of them happened. This is how a fact becomes a story people lean in for.

FormWhat it saysExample
If + past perfect
If I had known		The condition that was never true. It is finished and it cannot change now."If I had known they were Americans..."
would have + past participle
would have said		The result that never happened, because the condition never happened."...I would have said something."
Negative in either halfPut not where the meaning needs it. Either half can be negative, or both."If she had not introduced us, we would never have met."
Result first, no commaYou can begin with the result. When the if comes second, drop the comma."I would have gone earlier if I had had the time."
The trapThe if half never takes would, and the result half never drops have.NOT "If I would have known..."
NOT "we would never met"

In speech nobody says the full forms. If I had becomes if I’d, and would have becomes would’ve, which sounds almost like would of. Train the sound, because that is what you will hear at the table.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a dinner in Sao Paulo and most of the table is speaking English. Choose the best thing to say.

The woman next to you has just introduced herself. The best opening is:
A "Nice to meet you." (and you look at your plate)
B "I am Braulio. How do you know Marina?"
C "I am not speaking very well the English, sorry."
She mentions she is from Milan. You want to keep it going:
A "Do you keep up with what is happening in Italy?"
B "Italy is a very beautiful country." (and you stop)
C "I am never going in Italy."
You want to say that you regret not travelling with your wife last year:
A "If I would have had more time, I would have gone with her."
B "If I had had more time, I would have gone with her."
C "If I have more time, I will have gone with her."
There is an awkward silence at your end of the table. The most useful thing you can do is:
A Wait for somebody else to speak.
B Ask the person next to you a second question about something they already said.
C Apologise for your English.
You want to tell a story about the house in Bahia that makes people lean in:
A "I have a house in Bahia."
B "If I had not retired this year, I would never have finished the house in Bahia."
C "It would have been interesting if I would be able to have finished that house before."
