Lesson 27 -- Pre-class

Precision in Real Time -- Repairing a Mistake, Live

A live podcast interview where numbers and ideas go wrong, and how to fix them in two seconds. Key words: to rephrase, to misspeak, a slip of the tongue, to backtrack, to qualify, off the top of my head, a ballpark figure, to double-check, precise, verbatim. Structure: sorry, I mean; or rather; let me rephrase that; what I meant was.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To rephrase -- to say something again with different words to make it clearer
"Let me rephrase that: the rule applies only to new contracts."
To misspeak -- to say something wrong by accident
"Sorry, I misspoke. The deadline is in March, not May."
A slip of the tongue -- a small mistake you make when you are speaking
"Saying Chile instead of Peru was just a slip of the tongue."
To backtrack -- to go back to an earlier point, or to change what you said
"The minister had to backtrack on his promise."
To qualify -- to add a limit to a statement so that it is more exact
"Let me qualify that: the rule applies only to new concessions."
Off the top of my head -- without checking, from memory, so maybe not exact
"Off the top of my head, there were about thirty bids."
A ballpark figure -- a number that is close to the real one, but not exact
"Can you give me a ballpark figure for the investment?"
To double-check -- to check something again to be completely sure
"Let me double-check the date and send it to you."
Precise -- exact and correct in every detail
"In a contract, every number must be precise."
Verbatim -- using exactly the same words that were said or written
"The court asked for a verbatim transcript of the hearing."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Aguinaldo is live on an international podcast. The host asks when the first port concession was signed. He says: "In 2019, sorry, I mean 2016." One second, and he moves on.

Then she asks for a ballpark figure. "Off the top of my head, thirty bids, or rather, twenty-eight. I can double-check after the show."

Later he says something too strong: "The rule is bad." He repairs the whole idea: "Let me rephrase that: the rule has one weak point. What I meant was the transition period." Notice: small fix, sorry, I mean; big fix, let me rephrase that; then he continues.

1. How long does his first repair take?
A About one second.
B About two minutes, because he apologizes many times to the host and to the listeners.
C He does not repair it, because nobody on a podcast notices a wrong year.
2. Why does he use "let me rephrase that" and not "sorry, I mean" for "the rule is bad"?
A Because "sorry, I mean" is only for numbers, and "bad" is an adjective, not a number.
B Because he wants to fix a whole idea, not just one word.
C Because "let me rephrase that" is more polite with women, and the host is a woman.
3. What does he offer to do after the show?
A To double-check.
B To write a book.
C To call the agency.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Sorry, I Mean / Or Rather / Let Me Rephrase That

GRAMMAR

Self-repair is part of professional speaking. Small fixes go after sorry, I mean or or rather; big fixes start with let me rephrase that or what I meant was.

JobFormExample
Fix one word or number..., sorry, I mean + correct word2019, sorry, I mean 2016.
Make a number more exact..., or rather, + exact wordthirty, or rather, twenty-eight.
Say a whole idea betterLet me rephrase that: + new sentenceLet me rephrase that: the rule has a weak point.
Explain your intentionWhat I meant was + correct ideaWhat I meant was new concessions.
Add a limitLet me qualify that: + limitLet me qualify that: only in the south.
Ask someone to repairDid you mean...? · Sorry, was that 2016 or 2019?Did you mean new concessions?

Never: What I wanted to say it is · or better instead of or rather · Let me to rephrase · sorry, sorry, my English...
