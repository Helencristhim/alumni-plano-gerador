Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

It Was the Contract That Changed Everything -- Choosing What the Room Looks At

Three hours, six issues, and only one of them is the real one. Key words: the crux, a sticking point, to reframe, a trade-off, an assumption, to narrow down, a bargaining chip, leeway, to sidestep, to press somebody on, tangible, to bring somebody round. Structure: cleft sentences.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

The crux -- the single point on which everything else turns, however small it looks
"The crux is not the fee. It is one line about future editions."
A sticking point -- the one issue that keeps stopping the agreement from being signed
"We agreed five things in twenty minutes. The sticking point took the rest of the afternoon."
To reframe -- to describe the same facts in a different way, so that they lead somewhere else
"She reframed it: not a release, a renegotiation. Nothing changed except what it could be called."
A trade-off -- accepting less of one thing in order to get more of another
"The trade-off is simple: a smaller figure in exchange for the credit on every edition."
An assumption -- something everybody has taken as true without anybody actually checking it
"The whole morning ran on an assumption: that she wanted more money."
To narrow down -- to reduce a large set of possibilities until only a few useful ones are left
"We narrowed six issues down to one before anybody said a number."
A bargaining chip -- something you hold only because it is worth something to the other side
"The back payments were never the point. They were a bargaining chip and everybody knew it."
Leeway -- the freedom to act or decide, but only inside limits somebody else set
"I have leeway on the figure and none at all on the credit."
To sidestep -- to avoid a question without appearing to refuse it
"He sidestepped it twice, politely, and the third time I asked it he answered."
To press somebody on -- to keep asking somebody about one point until you actually get an answer
"Press him on the date. He has sidestepped it in every meeting since March."
Tangible -- real enough to be counted, measured or pointed at, rather than promised
"Give her something tangible. Goodwill in a paragraph is not tangible."
To bring somebody round -- to change somebody's mind slowly, by talking rather than by pressure
"Nobody brought him round with an argument. It was one question that did it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Six issues are on the table and three hours have gone. Five of them were agreed before eleven o'clock; the sixth has not been discussed at all. It is not the fee that is holding this up. The crux is one line about future editions, and the whole morning ran on an assumption that nobody checked: that what the client wanted was more money.

Look at how that second sentence is built. The ordinary version — the fee is not holding this up — says the same thing and chooses nothing. The long version spends four extra words to put one item under a light and push the rest into the dark. English has several of these. It was the contract that changed everything puts a thing in the spotlight. What she wants is a credit announces that the important part is coming and buys you the half second a room needs to stop talking. All she is asking for is one line narrows it to a single item. And the reason we are here is that nobody read clause seven gives the cause, with that and never with because.

This is not decoration either. At a table with six issues, whoever says the only thing that matters here is this one decides what the next hour is about. That is a sentence pattern, not a talent. One warning: after what, the verb agrees with the clause and not with what follows it — what she wants is two things, never what she wants are. And do not lose the that: not it was the contract changed everything.

1. "It is not the fee that is holding this up." What does the long version do that the short version does not?
A It makes the sentence more polite.
B It chooses one item, puts it under a light and pushes everything else into the dark.
C It changes the meaning of the verb.
2. "What she wants is two things." Why "is" and not "are"?
A Because the verb agrees with the "what" clause, not with what comes after it.
B Because "things" is uncountable in this sentence.
C Because the sentence is about the future.
3. According to the text, why does this structure matter at a negotiating table?
A Because longer sentences sound more professional.
B Because whoever names the one issue that matters decides what the next hour is about.
C Because it hides the weakest part of your case.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences

GRAMMAR

You are spending extra words to choose what the room looks at. That is the whole point.

JobFormExample
Put one thing under the lightIt is / It was + the thing + thatIt was the contract that changed everything.
Rule one thing outIt is not + the thing + thatIt is not the fee that is holding this up.
Announce that the point is comingWhat + clause + is / wasWhat she wants is an acknowledgement.
Narrow it to one thing onlyAll + clause + is / wasAll she is asking for is a credit.
Give the causethe reason / the thing / the problem + is thatThe reason we are here is that nobody read it.
People and placesit was + person + who · it was + place + whereIt was the designer who saw it first.
The two trapsthe verb agrees with what; never drop the thatWhat she wants is two things. Not it was the contract changed everything.

Never: what she wants are two things · it was the contract changed everything · what took me three hours working out which one · the reason we are here is because · all she is asking are two things.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The other side is certain the money is the problem. You want to rule it out. You say:
A "The fee is not the problem, I think, maybe."
B "It is not the fee that is holding this up."
C "It is not the fee is holding this up."
You want the room to stop and hear the one thing your client needs. You say:
A "What she wants is a credit that survives every future edition."
B "What she wants are a credit that survives every future edition."
C "The thing what she wants is a credit."
You want to explain why the session exists at all. You say:
A "The reason we are here is because nobody read clause seven."
B "The reason we are here is that nobody read clause seven."
C "The reason of we are here is nobody read clause seven."
You want to make the request sound as small as it really is. You say:
A "She only wants a small thing, one line."
B "All she is asking for is one line on the copyright page."
C "All that she is asking are one line on the copyright page."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Open the last hour of a session, in two minutes. Say what has already been agreed, rule out the issues that are not the problem, name the crux, say what your client will trade for it, and finish with what you need from the other side before six o'clock. Use at least three different cleft patterns. If a sentence comes out wrong, finish it anyway.
