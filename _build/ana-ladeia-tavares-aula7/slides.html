One Question

Which One Is the Real One?

Six issues are on the table: the fee, the credit, the timing, the wording of the announcement, who pays the mediator, and one line about future editions. Five of them are noise.

Say it out loud, in one sentence: which is the real one, and say it in a way that makes the room stop.

Chapter 3: The Code

Longer on Purpose

The sentence that puts one thing under a light

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"It was the contract that changed everything."

"It is not the fee that is holding this up."

"What she wants is an acknowledgement."

"The reason we are here is that nobody read clause seven."

Every one of these could be said in half the words. Rewrite the first one as a short ordinary sentence in your head, and then say what the long version does that the short one cannot.

JobFormExample
Put one thing under the lightIt is / It was + the thing + thatIt was the contract that changed everything.
Rule one thing outIt is not + the thing + thatIt is not the fee that is holding this up.
Announce that the point is comingWhat + clause + is / wasWhat she wants is an acknowledgement.
Narrow it to one thing onlyAll + clause + is / wasAll she is asking for is a credit.
Give the causeThe reason / the thing / the problem + is thatThe reason we are here is that nobody read it.
People and placesit was + person + who · it was + place + whereIt was the designer who saw it first.
The trapthe verb agrees with what, not with the thing after itWhat she wants is two things. Not what she wants are.

In one line: you are spending extra words to choose what the room looks at. That is the whole point of the structure.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Table, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one term sheet

Dialogue

Six Issues, One That Matters

D
Three hours and we have moved on nothing. My client thinks the number is the problem, and honestly, so did I this morning.
A
It is not the fee that is holding this up. The crux is one line about future editions, and we have spent three hours nowhere near it.
D
Say that again more slowly, because if that is right then the whole morning ran on an assumption.
A
It did. What my client wants is a credit that survives every future edition. All she is asking for is that one thing, and everything else is a bargaining chip.
D
Then I have leeway. It is the money I was told to protect, not the copyright page. Give me a form of words and I will take it in.
A
Here is the trade-off. She drops fifteen per cent of the figure, and the credit appears on every edition from the next printing. That is tangible for both sides in one sentence.
D
You could have said that at ten o'clock. What stopped you, out of interest?
A
Nobody asked. And I will be honest with you, Daniel: what took me three hours was working out which of the six it actually was.
Comprehension

About Daniel

1. What did Daniel believe the problem was when the day started, and what does he say about his own client?
He thought the number was the problem, and he says his client thinks so too. He admits he believed it himself that morning.
2. What does Daniel say he was told to protect, and what does that give him?
The money, not the copyright page. That gives him leeway on the credit.
3. What does Daniel ask at the end, and what does the question reveal about the cost of the morning?
He asks what stopped her saying it at ten o'clock. It reveals that three hours were spent because nobody named the real issue, not because the sides disagreed about it.
Listening 1 · The Trainer

Finding the One That Matters

A negotiation trainer on how to find the real issue in a room full of issues. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say happens to a negotiation with six issues on the table?
Five of them are noise, and the room spends its time on whichever one was mentioned first rather than on the one that decides anything.
2. What is the question he tells his students to ask, and why does he say it works?
If everything else went your way, would you sign today? He says it works because it makes the other side name the crux themselves, and nobody feels they have given anything away by answering it.
3. What does he say about people who are good at this?
That they are not quicker or cleverer. They simply say the sentence out loud, and most people never do, because naming the real issue feels like showing your hand.
Listening 2 · The Mediator

Same Facts, Different Sentence

A mediator on reframing, and on why it is not a trick. Sound first — no text.

1. What example of reframing does she give, and what changed?
Calling something a renegotiation instead of a release. Not one fact changed; what changed was what the other side could agree to without setting a precedent.
2. Why does she say reframing is not dishonest?
Because every description is already a choice, and the first description in the room was a choice too. She says pretending otherwise is the only dishonest option.
3. What does she say the sentence pattern gives a young lawyer?
Time. She says the long form announces that something important is coming, and the half second it buys is enough for a room to stop talking and listen.
Listening 3 · Radio Report

Where Deals Actually Die

A radio report on why agreements collapse in the last five per cent. Sound first — no text.

1. What figure does the report give about where negotiations fail, and what does it say people expect instead?
That most collapse in the last five per cent of the discussion. People expect deals to fail at the start, over the big difference, and they almost never do.
2. What explanation does the report give for that pattern?
That the issue nobody named at the beginning is still there at the end, and by then both sides have spent so much on everything else that they have nothing left to trade for it.
3. What does the researcher at the end recommend, and what does she say gets in the way?
Naming the hardest issue in the first twenty minutes. What gets in the way is that it feels like weakness, when it is the opposite: it is the only move that gives you the whole session to solve it.

The One Page Everybody Is Looking At

Five of the six were agreed before eleven o'clock. Item six has not been discussed at all, and it is the only one our client has ever mentioned without being asked. Do not trade it. Everything above it is available.
1. Which item is the crux, and how does the note tell you without using the word?
Item six. The note says it is the only one the client has ever mentioned without being asked, and the only one still open.
2. Say item one and item six back as one sentence that puts the second under the light.
It is not the fee that is holding this up; what she wants is a credit that survives every future edition.
3. The note says "do not trade it". Why would a term sheet need that written down?
Because five agreed items build momentum, and in the last stretch it is easy to give away the one thing that mattered in exchange for finishing.
