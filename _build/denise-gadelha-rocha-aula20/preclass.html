Lesson 20 -- Pre-class

Owning the Room in Any Language -- Cross-Cultural Executive Communication

The same position said at three levels, and why the choice decides the result. Key words: high-context, low-context, save face, deference, directness, bluntness, soften, norms, calibrate, mirror, register, cultural shorthand. Structure: register shift -- the markers that raise and lower directness, and what each one signals about hierarchy.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These name something you already do in Portuguese without thinking about it.

High-context -- a culture where most of the meaning sits in what is implied, not in the words
"Milan is high-context: the objection was in the pause, not in the sentence."
Low-context -- a culture where the words carry the message and nothing is left to be inferred
"In a low-context room, if it was not said, it was not meant."
To save face -- to protect someone's standing in front of other people
"I put it in the note as a group issue so that he could save face."
Deference -- visible respect for rank, shown in how you phrase something rather than in what you concede
"Deference in Milan is a form of politeness. It is not a form of agreement."
Directness -- saying the point itself, with nothing placed in front of it
"Directness reads as confidence in Amsterdam and as pressure in Bogota."
Bluntness -- directness with the cushioning removed, which reads as disregard rather than clarity
"The line between directness and bluntness is drawn by the listener, never by you."
To soften -- to make a statement land more gently without changing what it says
"I softened the sentence and I kept the position."
Norms -- the unwritten rules a group follows about how things are said
"Every region here has its own norms, and none of them is written down anywhere."
To calibrate -- to adjust something precisely so that it fits the person in front of you
"You do not translate the message, you calibrate it."
To mirror -- to match the register the other person is using, on purpose
"He was being careful, so I mirrored him and asked instead of stating."
Register -- the level of formality and directness you choose for a particular listener
"Same content, three registers, three completely different results."
Cultural shorthand -- a phrase a group understands instantly and outsiders read at face value
"That would be difficult is cultural shorthand, and what it means is no."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Watch what happens to the same position in four different hands.

The proposal went out to four regions on the same morning, in the same language, and came back in four different registers. Amsterdam replied in two lines: the date does not work, and here is why. Milan replied with a question — would it be possible to look at the timing again? Bogota agreed warmly and raised nothing at all. Tokyo said that January would be a little difficult.

Read as English, three of those four are hesitation. Read as register, only one of them is. Amsterdam is not being rude; the point simply comes first there. Milan is not being evasive; the question is how a senior person is contradicted without anyone paying for it. And the sentence from Tokyo is not a reservation at all: that would be difficult is cultural shorthand, and what it means is no.

What Denise did next is the whole lesson. She did not adopt anybody's style and she did not translate her own. She asked the second question — when you say difficult, are you telling me it does not work? — and then she moved the objection into the note as a group issue, so that nobody had to be the person who blocked January. The date moved to February. Nothing that mattered was conceded, and four regions stayed in the room.

1. "Would it be possible to look at the timing again?" Why a question and not a statement?
A Because a question lets a senior person be contradicted without anyone losing standing: the content is a disagreement, the form is deference.
B Because the writer was not sure of the facts and wanted to check them.
C Because Italian has no direct way of expressing disagreement.
2. What does "that would be difficult" mean in this text?
A A reservation that can still be negotiated.
B A refusal, phrased so that nobody has to say the word no.
C A request for more information before deciding.
3. Why did she move the objection into the note as a group issue?
A So that nobody had to be the person who blocked the date — it protects the other side's standing and changes nothing about the outcome.
B Because she was not certain which region had objected.
C Because issues raised by the group are decided faster than individual ones.
4. What is the point of the second question?
A It tests whether the other person is telling you the truth.
B It shows the other person that their first answer was not clear enough.
C It turns an indirect signal into information you can plan on, and it gives the other person a way to say it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Register Shift

GRAMMAR

The position never moves. These are the switches that move everything around it — and each one tells the listener something about hierarchy.

MarkerThe same point, movedWhat the listener hears
Modal: can → could → mightIt might be worth revisiting the date.You may decline, and it will cost you nothing.
Hedge: I wonder whetherI wonder whether we might look at that again.This is open. Nobody is being overruled.
Question, not statementShould we look at the timing again?The decision is shared, and rank is left intact.
Passive voiceThe timing was raised by two of the regions.Nobody is contradicted, because nobody is named.
NominalisationThere is some concern about the timing.A position exists with no author — for writing that will be forwarded.
Bare assertionI disagree, and here is why.Confidence in Amsterdam, pressure in Bogota. The same eight words.
Two traps of formAfter a modal the verb is bare: it might be, never it might to be. And inside an indirect question the order goes back to normal: I wonder whether we should, never I wonder whether should we.
The one that costs nothingConcede with and, never with but: That is fair, and the timing is still what worries me. With but, everything you said before it is deleted.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

Your head has already signalled that he prefers January. He asks for your view, in front of six people. You disagree.
A "I wonder whether we might look at the timing again before we fix it."
B "I disagree. January will not work."
C "I agree, and we can always review it later."
A colleague in another region says January "would be a little difficult". You:
A Record it as agreement with a small reservation.
B Ask the second question: when you say difficult, are you telling me it does not work?
C Move on, since he did not actually refuse.
In the weekly regional call everyone agrees, and afterwards nothing moves. The most useful next step is:
A Say the level of commitment out loud and ask for it back: is this a decision or an intention?
B Escalate it to the group and let Milan decide.
C Send the same proposal again, in more detail.
Which sentence keeps the concession alive?
A "That is a fair point, but the timing is what worries me."
B "That is a fair point, and the timing is still what worries me."
C "I take your point, but that is not really the issue here."
You have to record an objection that came from a peer, in a note that will be forwarded. You write:
A "The timing was raised across the regions, and the date is moving to February."
B "Kenji objected to January, so the date is moving to February."
C "One region was unable to commit to January, so the date is at risk."
There is no native speaker of English anywhere in your working week. The right target is:
A An American accent and more idiomatic phrasing.
B The Milan style, since your head is Italian.
C A register the four cultures in the room can act on, with the level of commitment made explicit.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Take one thing you actually have to say in the next two weeks — a date that does not work, a number you will not commit to, a decision you disagree with. Say it three times: once to a Dutch colleague who wants the point first, once to your head in Milan in front of other people, and once in a written note that will be forwarded to people you have never met.
