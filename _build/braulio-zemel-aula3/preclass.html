Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Talking About the World -- The Room Where They Argue

The language of an opinion about the news: not the facts, but the machinery around them. Key expressions: coverage, bias, an outlet, to weigh in, to play devil's advocate, turnout, sanctions, to escalate, a ceasefire, polarized, to take it with a grain of salt, a backlash. Structure: modal perfects -- should have acted, must have known, might have been, could have been avoided.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

Coverage -- the way the news reports a story, and how much of it there is
"The coverage was enormous for two days and then it disappeared."
Bias -- an unfair preference that shows in the way a story is told
"Every outlet has a bias. The useful question is which one."
An outlet -- a newspaper, a channel or a site that publishes the news
"I read three outlets every morning, on purpose."
To weigh in -- to give your opinion on something people are already discussing
"Everybody had spoken, so I weighed in at the end."
To play devil’s advocate -- to argue a position you do not hold, in order to test it
"Let me play devil’s advocate for one minute."
Turnout -- the number of people who actually go and vote
"The turnout was the real story, and almost nobody reported it."
Sanctions -- official measures used by one country to punish another
"Sanctions rarely work as fast as the people who announce them expect."
To escalate -- to become more serious, more intense or more dangerous
"It escalated in a week, and by then nobody could stop it."
A ceasefire -- an agreement to stop fighting, usually a temporary one
"The ceasefire held for eleven days."
Polarized -- split into two groups that no longer agree about anything
"The country is polarized, and the dinner table is where you feel it."
To take it with a grain of salt -- to not believe it completely, and to wait for more
"When a story breaks I take it with a grain of salt for the first day."
A backlash -- a strong, angry reaction against a decision or an idea
"The backlash was bigger than the decision that caused it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He has read three newspapers every morning for thirty years, and he can tell you within a paragraph what each one wants you to feel. Every outlet has a bias, he says, and the useful question is never whether it has one. It is which one, and where it shows.

Last month the story was the vote. The coverage was enormous for two days and then it disappeared, and almost nobody reported the number that mattered: the turnout was the lowest in twenty years. He thinks they should have waited for the second report. Somebody in that room must have known what the numbers were, because the numbers were not a secret.

Abroad, the same week, a ceasefire held for eleven days and then began to escalate again, and a new round of sanctions was announced on a Friday afternoon. Whoever chose that hour must have wanted a quiet weekend. The whole thing, he says, could have been avoided, and the backlash that followed was bigger than the decision that caused it.

He is careful about one thing. He takes the first twenty-four hours of any story with a grain of salt, because the country is polarized and the first version is always the loudest one. When everybody at the table has spoken, he weighs in at the end. And once or twice an evening, just to see what happens, he plays devil’s advocate.

1. "They should have waited for the second report." What does this add to the fact?
A Criticism -- the right thing was not done, and it is too late now.
B Certainty that they did wait.
C A prediction about the next vote.
2. "Somebody in that room must have known." How sure is the writer?
A Not sure at all -- it is only a possibility.
B Almost certain, because the evidence leaves no other reading.
C Certain that nobody knew.
3. "The whole thing could have been avoided." Was it avoided?
A Yes, it was avoided in the end.
B No -- it was possible, and it did not happen.
C The text does not say.
4. Why does he take the first twenty-four hours of a story with a grain of salt?
A Because the country is polarized and the first version is the loudest.
B Because he does not read the news in the morning.
C Because outlets never publish anything true.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Perfects

GRAMMAR

The fact goes in the past simple. The opinion about the fact goes in a modal perfect. Four of them cover almost everything you will want to say.

FormWhat it saysExample
should have + participleCriticism or regret: the right thing was not done, and the moment has passed."They should have waited."
"I should not have stayed quiet."
must have + participleNear certainty, built on evidence rather than proof."Somebody must have known."
might / may / could have + participleA possibility, claimed carefully and no further."It might have been a mistake."
could have + participleSomething that was possible and did not happen: an open door nobody used."It could have been avoided."
The trapAfter a modal the next word is always have, never had. And without have the sentence is about NOW, not about the past.NOT "should had acted"
"It must be a mistake" = now
"It must have been a mistake" = then

In speech these collapse: should’ve, must’ve, might’ve, could’ve. They sound almost like should of. Train the sound, because that is what you will hear in a bulletin and at a table.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at the club's news group and the conversation is in English. Choose the best thing to say.

Diane asks what you made of the vote. The best answer is:
A "It was on Thursday." (and you stop)
B "I think they should have waited for the second report."
C "They should had waited the second report."
You are almost certain the officials knew about the numbers. You say:
A "Somebody must have known about the numbers."
B "Somebody must know about the numbers at that time."
C "Somebody had to knowing about the numbers."
Diane takes the opposite position and argues it well. You disagree, politely:
A "No, you are wrong about that."
B "That is a fair point, but I still think it was avoidable."
C "Maybe. I do not know." (and you stop)
You want to say that the escalation was not inevitable:
A "The escalation could be avoided."
B "The escalation could have been avoided."
C "The escalation would be able to avoid."
Somebody quotes a story that broke this morning. The most useful thing you can add is:
A "I read it too."
B "I take the first twenty-four hours with a grain of salt. What did the other outlets say?"
C "It would have been preferable if we would have had more information available."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is scoring it.

Choose one story you have followed this month. Record about ninety seconds: what happened, in three short sentences; what you think should have been done differently; what somebody in the room must have known; and one thing about the coverage that annoyed you. End by asking a question you would genuinely like somebody to answer.
