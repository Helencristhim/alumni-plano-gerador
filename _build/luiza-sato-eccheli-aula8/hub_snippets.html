To waive -- to officially give up a right or a claim
"By accepting, users waive the right to a class action."
Consent -- permission for something to happen, given freely
"The app cannot share your location without your consent."
To opt out -- to choose not to take part in something
"You can opt out of marketing emails in the settings."
To terminate -- to officially end an agreement
"The company may terminate your account if you break the rules."
A dispute -- a serious disagreement, especially one that goes to a court or an arbitrator
"Any dispute will be decided under Brazilian law."
Arbitration -- a way to settle a dispute outside court, with a private decision maker
"The contract says every dispute goes to arbitration."
A third party -- a person or a company that is not one of the two sides of an agreement
"Your data may be shared with a third party for payment."
Void -- describes a clause or a contract that has no legal effect
"A clause that removes all liability can be void under consumer law."
To be entitled to -- to have the right to have or to do something
"You are entitled to a refund if the order never arrives."
A refund -- money that is paid back to you, for example when a product or service fails
"I asked for a refund, and it arrived in five days."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Legal English uses a very small set of words to create duties, options and prohibitions, and terms of service use all of them. Once you recognize them, a long clause in the fine print becomes a simple question: who has to do what?

Duties are marked with shall in formal legal texts and with must or is required to in both legal and everyday English: “The user shall keep the password confidential.” Options and permissions are marked with may: “You may opt out of marketing emails at any time.”

Prohibitions use may not, shall not or is prohibited from + -ing: “Users may not share their account.” A right is often written with is entitled to: “Customers are entitled to a refund if the order never arrives.”

The most dangerous pair for Brazilian speakers is must not and do not have to. “You must not share it” means it is forbidden. “You do not have to give your phone number” means it is optional. In a contract, confusing the two is not a small mistake: it changes a liability, a right, and sometimes the whole dispute.

1. According to the text, which words mark a duty?
A may and can
B shall, must and is required to
C may not and is entitled to
2. What does “You do not have to give your phone number” mean?
A It is optional.
B It is forbidden.
C It is required by law.
3. Why does the text call must not and do not have to a dangerous pair?
A Because they sound informal.
B Because they are only used in British English.
C Because confusing them changes a duty into an option, or the reverse.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Obligation and Permission in Legal English

GRAMMAR

Shall and must for duties, may for options, may not for prohibitions, entitled to for rights.

JobFormExample
A formal dutyshall + verbThe user shall keep it confidential.
A dutymust / is required to + verbThe company must protect data.
An optionmay + verbYou may cancel at any time.
A prohibitionmay not / is prohibited from + -ingUsers may not share their account.
A rightis entitled toCustomers are entitled to a refund.
No dutyis not required to / does not have toYou do not have to give your birthday.

Never: you must not give your number (if it is optional) · users may to cancel · entitled of a refund · prohibited to resell
