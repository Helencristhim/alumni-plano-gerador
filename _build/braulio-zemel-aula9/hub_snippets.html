Listening Like a Native -- The News You Already Follow, in English

How to follow political analysis by ear when you already know the story, and how to say what happens next. Key expressions: a faction, mediation, humanitarian, an insurgency, a treaty, electoral, an incumbent, a truce, a stalemate, to broker, a proxy war, a flashpoint. Structure: future perfect and future continuous -- will have happened, will be happening.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. You have read every one of these. The exercise is saying them. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

A faction -- a smaller group inside a larger political body, with its own agenda
"The vote failed because one faction of his own party walked out."
Mediation -- a process where a neutral third party helps two sides talk
"Three weeks of mediation, and the only result so far is a truce."
Humanitarian -- concerned with reducing human suffering, not with winning
"If the trucks are still waiting on Wednesday, the humanitarian side collapses first."
An insurgency -- an armed uprising against the government of a country
"What the government calls an insurgency, the region calls a civil war."
A treaty -- a formal written agreement between countries
"The treaty was signed in March and nobody has honored it since."
Electoral -- relating to elections and to how votes are counted
"The electoral rules changed twice before anybody voted."
An incumbent -- the person who currently holds the office and is running again
"The incumbent is defending a record that half his own party has abandoned."
A truce -- a temporary stop to the fighting, agreed by both sides
"The truce has held for six days, and by the weekend we will know about the seventh."
A stalemate -- a position where neither side can move and neither will give way
"It is a stalemate, and both governments prefer it to the alternative."
To broker -- to arrange an agreement between two sides that will not talk directly
"Three countries tried to broker it. The one nobody expected succeeded."
A proxy war -- a conflict where two big powers fight through somebody else's army
"Nobody calls it a proxy war on the record, and everybody knows what it is."
A flashpoint -- the place or the moment where a conflict is most likely to catch fire
"Watch the northern crossing on Tuesday. That is the flashpoint."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He reads three newspapers before nine in the morning, so the content is never the problem. He knows which faction walked out of the vote, which treaty nobody has honored, and why the mediation has produced a truce and nothing else. Then he opens the same story in English, and forty seconds in, it is gone.

Nothing is wrong with his ear. He is simply doing two jobs at once, decoding the language and the content in the same second, which is the hardest possible version of the exercise. Interpreters do not work that way and neither should he. Two paragraphs in Portuguese first, and most of the difficulty disappears before the audio starts.

Then there is the skeleton. In a report, the proper nouns are stressed and slow: names of people, names of places, days of the week. Catch those and the connections come free, because in a news story the connections are obvious once you know who and where. The incumbent is defending a record. The electoral season has not started. The flashpoint is a crossing in the north, and the humanitarian convoy is waiting at it.

And the analysts themselves live in two tenses. By Thursday the negotiators will have been in the same building for eleven days. This time next year we will still be arguing about the same border. By the time the electoral season starts, the record will have been rewritten twice. Those two forms carry every prediction on the radio, and once you hear them you cannot stop hearing them.

1. "By Thursday the negotiators will have been in the same building for eleven days." What does this tell us?
A That they arrive on Thursday.
B That when Thursday arrives, eleven days will already have passed.
C That they were there for eleven days last week.
2. "This time next year we will still be arguing." Why the continuous form?
A Because the arguing is in progress at that moment, not finished by it.
B Because the arguing will be over by then.
C Because it is happening right now.
3. According to the text, why does the story disappear forty seconds in?
A Because his hearing is failing.
B Because he is decoding the language and the content at the same time.
C Because he does not know enough vocabulary.
4. What is the skeleton of a news report, in this text?
A The proper nouns: names of people, names of places, days of the week.
B The connecting words between the sentences.
C The opinion of the analyst at the end.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Two Tenses of a Prediction

GRAMMAR

Every prediction you hear on the radio picks one of two shapes: finished before a moment, or still going on at that moment. Both exist in Portuguese, so nothing here is foreign. What is new is how often English puts them at the front of the sentence.

FormWhat it saysExample
will have + past participleFinished BEFORE a moment in the future. The moment is always named."By Thursday they will have signed it."
will be + verb-ingIn progress AT that moment. Not finished then, and not starting then."At this hour on Tuesday the results will be coming in."
will have been + forHow long something will have lasted by that moment. The analyst's favorite shape."By December they will have been at war for three years."
The trigger wordsby, by the time, this time next year, at this hour. When one of them arrives, one of the two forms is coming."By the time the season starts..."
The trapsA plain will after by the time puts the event AT the moment instead of before it. A duration always needs will have been. And the present continuous cannot carry a future moment.NOT "By Thursday they will sign it."
NOT "By December they will be at war for three years."
NOT "This time next year we are still talking."

A practical note for a dinner table. A prediction with a date in it sounds like analysis. The same sentence without a date sounds like a mood. They will sign something is a feeling; by March they will have signed something is a position you can be judged on, and that is what makes people lean in.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a dinner where the conversation has turned to the news. Choose the best thing to say.

You think the coalition falls apart before the vote. You would say:
A "By the time the vote comes, the coalition will collapse."
B "By the time the vote comes, the coalition will have collapsed."
C "By the time the vote comes, the coalition is collapsing."
You want to say the war will have lasted three years by December:
A "By December they will have been at war for three years."
B "By December they will be at war for three years."
C "By December they are at war for three years."
Two big powers are fighting through somebody else's army. That is:
A an insurgency
B a proxy war
C mediation
Neither side can move and neither will give way. The word is:
A a stalemate
B a truce
C a treaty
Somebody asks how you follow all of this in English. The most useful answer is:
A "I do not. It is too fast for me."
B "I read it in Portuguese first, and then I listen for the names and the days."
C "I listen ten times until I understand everything."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is scoring it.

Record about ninety seconds. You are the analyst closing the broadcast. Take one story you have been following this month. Say where it stands now, name one flashpoint and the day to watch, and finish with two predictions -- each one with a date, one using will have and one using will be.
