Chapter 3: The Code

Four Forms, One Breath

Not four rules again. The order in which they arrive.

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

One executive answer, twenty seconds long. Four different structures, in the order the meeting needs them.

We should have flagged the capacity gap in April. I wish we had sequenced it the other way round. The regional heads told me they had already stopped quoting for two weeks. So next quarter is likely to land between eleven and thirteen, and I would rather firm that up after the dry run.

Four colours, four grammars, and not one of them is new to you. What decides the order they arrive in?

The moment in the roomWhat you reach forWhy that one and not another
The quarter is closed and you have to account for itmodal perfect — should have flagged, could have absorbedit judges a finished action without putting a person in the sentence. Past simple accuses; the modal perfect assesses
You need the room to know you would do it differentlywish + past perfect — I wish we had sequenced itit names the alternative reality once and closes it. Say it twice and the session turns into the post-mortem you were leaving behind
Somebody who is not in the room already said something that mattersreported speech — they told me they had stopped, he asked when it would be signedit carries the decision in without re-opening it. Backshift, and no inversion in the reported question
You are asked for a number you cannot yet stand behindhedging — is likely to, I would rather, appears toit states the confidence, not the doubt. Always with a driver and a date attached, or it reads as evasion
The order itselfPast judged → regret named once → other voices carried in → forward number hedged. Move the hedge to the front and the room stops listening before you reach the facts; move the regret to the end and you close on an apology.
Trap 1 — hedging what is settledThe past does not take a hedge. It seems that we should possibly have flagged it tells the room the post-mortem is still open. Say it flat: we should have flagged it.
Trap 2 — reporting with today's certaintyHe said the handover is signed makes it your claim, now. He said the handover had been signed keeps it his, then. In a cross-functional room that difference decides who is answerable.

The Executive Point

Same Four Forms, Two Different Rooms

The order that closes

"We should have flagged it in April. I wish we had sequenced it the other way round. The regions told me they had stopped quoting. Next quarter is likely to land between eleven and thirteen, and I will firm that up on the twenty-second."

Facts first, regret once, other voices carried in, and the only uncertainty at the end — where it belongs, with a date on it.

The order that loses the room

"It is probably somewhere around eleven to thirteen, more or less. I really wish we had done this differently, I keep thinking about it. Somebody said the regions stopped quoting. Anyway, we should have flagged it in April."

Not one grammatical error in it. The hedge arrives before any fact, the regret has no end, the report has no author, and the session closes on the failure.

Same speaker, same four structures, same information. Only the sequence changed, and with it who is trusted with the next quarter.

Chapter 4: The Live Session

Matteo, Group Operations

Dialogue

Ninety Minutes, Four Decisions

M
Denise, I have ninety minutes and two things to get out of them: the quarter closed, and a number for the next one. I would rather not spend the first hour on what went wrong.
D
Then let me close it in three sentences. We should have flagged the capacity gap in April. We could have absorbed it if the handover to your team had been written down. It was not, and that part is mine.
M
That is the shortest post-mortem I have had this month. Is that the whole of it, or is there something underneath?
D
There is one thing underneath, and I would like to bottom it out today. I wish we had sequenced it the other way round: pricing was agreed before capacity was confirmed, so every renewal after that was a negotiation with ourselves.
M
The regional heads told me the same thing last week. Bogota said they had stopped quoting for two weeks, and Mexico asked me when the handover would be signed. I could not tell them.
D
Then let me close that loop today. The handover is signed on the fifteenth, my name is on it, and the sequence goes in writing, so that nobody has to ask you again.
M
Good. Now the number, and we have forty minutes for it. Before you hedge it, tell me how much of the hedge is real.
D
It is likely to land between eleven and thirteen. Eleven I will stand behind today. Thirteen depends on two renewals that we have not pressure-tested yet, so I would rather firm it up after the dry run on the twenty-second than give you a figure that moves in front of the group.
M
The twenty-second, then. Bring me one number and what would have to be true for it to be wrong. If it moves after that, it moves with a reason attached, not an apology.

Listening

A Session, Not an Update

A group chief of staff on the hour that actually decides something. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he call an hour in which four people only tell each other what they already know?
Not a session — a newsletter that costs four salaries.
2. How does he build the agenda, and what does he ask after each line?
Backwards, starting from the decisions, one line each — then who has to be in the room for that decision to be legal. Usually fewer people than he thought.
3. Why does he hold the clock even when the conversation is good?
Because a good conversation is the most expensive way to avoid a decision.
4. What does he do in the last minutes, and why does he do it out loud?
He reads back what was closed, with a name and a date on each line. If nobody objects, it happened; if somebody objects, better then than three weeks later.
Listening 2

Two Functions, One Number

A group CFO on how the gap gets named, and what an open loop costs. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she say the two functions actually disagree about?
Not the number — which question the number answers. Then they argue for an hour as if it were arithmetic.
2. What is the first thing she does, in front of everybody?
She reconciles the two versions: their figure, hers, the gap, and exactly what the gap is made of.
3. What is the gap usually made of, and how long does the number take after that?
One assumption, and never the one anybody expected. Once it has a name, landing on a single number takes about four minutes.
4. What does she say happens to a loop that nobody closes?
It does not stay open — it closes by itself, in the worst way, and you hear about it from somebody else.
Chapter 5: Practice

The Sentences You Will Actually Say

Spot the Error

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "We should have to close that loop in March."
"We should have closed that loop in March." (should have + past participle — should have to is a crossing of should with have to, and it says something else entirely)
2. "He asked me when would the handover be signed."
"He asked me when the handover would be signed." (a reported question keeps statement order — the inversion belongs to the original question only)
3. "We need to take a decision on the number today."
"We need to make a decision on the number today." (the Portuguese and Spanish verb here translates as take, but in American English a decision is something you make)
4. "Can you send me the reconciled figures until Thursday?"
"Can you send me the reconciled figures by Thursday?" (Portuguese and Spanish use one preposition for both ideas; in English until means continuously up to that day, and by means at the latest on that day)

Five items, ninety minutes, and one of them will not fit

Write which one you would cut, and the sentence you would use to cut it before the session starts.

1. Why does every line say what closed means, instead of naming the topic and leaving it there?
A topic can be discussed for ever and still be there next month. A decision either exists at the end of the item or it does not, and writing it in advance is what makes the line actionable rather than a conversation with a title.
2. One item on this agenda cannot really be closed inside the session. Which one, and when do you say so?
Landing on the Q4 number: two renewals have not been pressure-tested, so the figure gets firmed up after the dry run. You say that when you send the agenda, not at minute eighty.
