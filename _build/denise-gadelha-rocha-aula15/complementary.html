Lesson 15 -- Block Capstone: From Post-Mortem to Projection

Series
Suits -- any episode with a partners' meeting

Pick one scene in which two people from different sides of the firm have to leave a room with one decision. Ignore the plot and watch the sequence: who states the finished facts first, who names a regret and how quickly they drop it, who brings in what somebody outside the room said, and who is the last to put a number or a commitment on the table. It is the same four moves you ran today, at twice the speed and with worse manners.

Tip: pause the moment somebody hedges. Ask yourself whether a fact came before it. If it did not, notice how fast the other person takes over the room.

Podcast
Think Fast, Talk Smart (Stanford GSB) -- episodes on running the room

This block was never about four separate grammars; it was about holding a sequence while somebody interrupts you. Choose an episode on structure under pressure or on speaking in meetings, and listen for how the guests move between what happened, what they would change and what they will commit to -- and how little time they spend on the middle one.

Tip: write down one sentence a guest uses to move the conversation forward without closing the topic rudely. You will need exactly that sentence when you hold your own timebox.

YouTube
Celeste Headlee -- 10 Ways to Have a Better Conversation (TED)

Half of a working session is not talking; it is deciding what not to say again. Headlee is ruthless about the habits that make a conversation longer without making it better -- repeating yourself, equating your experience with the other person's, and filling silence. Watch it with your own last cross-functional meeting in mind and count how many of the ninety minutes were spent on the second sentence of something you had already said.

Tip: her rule about not repeating yourself is the same rule as saying the wish sentence only once. Test it in your next session and see how much of the clock comes back.
