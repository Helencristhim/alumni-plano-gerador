Lesson 15 -- Pre-class

Block Capstone -- From Post-Mortem to Projection in a Live Executive Scenario

Ninety minutes with another function in the room: close the quarter, then commit to the next one. Key words: close the loop, reconcile, sequence, handover, dry run, firm up, working session, land on a number, pressure-test, timebox, actionable, bottom out. Structure: nothing new -- modal perfects, wish, reported speech and hedging, in the order a real session needs them.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words of the hour in which something is actually decided -- not reported.

To close the loop -- to come back to an open item and confirm it is finished, so that nobody is still waiting on it
"Let me close that loop today, with a name and a date on it."
To reconcile -- to bring two sets of figures into agreement, or to explain exactly what the difference is made of
"Before we talk about next quarter, let us reconcile the two sets of figures."
To sequence -- to decide what has to happen first so that the rest can happen at all
"Let us sequence this properly: capacity confirmed, then pricing agreed, then the announcement."
Handover -- the point at which work passes to somebody else, with everything they need in order to carry it
"The handover to operations happens on the fifteenth, and nothing goes with it that is still open."
Dry run -- a full rehearsal of something, done before the occasion that counts
"I would rather firm it up after the dry run on the twenty-second."
To firm up -- to turn something provisional into something you will stand behind
"Eleven I will stand behind today. Thirteen I will firm up after the renewals."
Working session -- a meeting in which the work is done in the room, rather than reported to it
"This is a working session, not a review. Nobody presents anything."
To land on (a number) -- to arrive at one figure that both sides accept, and stop moving it
"Once the gap has a name, we land on a number in about four minutes."
To pressure-test -- to challenge an assumption out loud, in the room, to see whether it holds when somebody pushes
"Thirteen depends on two renewals that we have not pressure-tested yet."
To timebox -- to give an item a fixed amount of time, and to stop when the time is gone
"I want to timebox this item at fifteen minutes and move on."
Actionable -- specific enough that somebody can do something with it tomorrow morning
"None of that is actionable until somebody's name is next to it."
To bottom out (an issue) -- to keep going into a problem until you reach the real cause and there is nothing left underneath
"There is one thing underneath, and I would like to bottom it out today."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account of the session and answer the questions. Four different structures go past, and the order they arrive in is the point.

Operations gave Denise ninety minutes and asked for two things: the quarter closed, and a number for the next one. He said he would rather not spend the first hour on what had gone wrong, so she closed it in three sentences. They should have flagged the capacity gap in April. They could have absorbed it if the handover had been written down. It had not been, and she said that part was hers.

Then, once and only once: I wish we had sequenced it the other way round. Pricing had been agreed before capacity was confirmed, so every renewal after that was a negotiation with themselves. She did not say it twice, and she did not apologise for it. There was one thing underneath, and she asked for twenty minutes to bottom it out.

He had heard the same story elsewhere. The regional heads had told him that Bogota had stopped quoting for two weeks, and Mexico had asked him when the handover would be signed. He could not answer them. She closed that loop in the room: the fifteenth, her name on it, the sequence in writing.

Only then the number. It is likely to land between eleven and thirteen. Eleven she will stand behind today; thirteen depends on two renewals nobody has pressure-tested yet, so she would rather firm it up after the dry run on the twenty-second than give him a figure that moves in front of the group. He agreed, and asked her to bring one more thing with the number: what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

1. "They should have flagged the capacity gap in April." Why this form, and not "they did not flag the capacity gap in April"?
A The modal perfect passes judgement on a finished action without putting a person in the sentence. The past simple states the fact and, in a cross-functional room, sounds like an accusation.
B They mean exactly the same thing; the modal perfect is simply more formal.
C Because the action might still happen, and the past simple would rule that out.
2. The account says she used the wish sentence "once and only once". Why does that matter in a ninety-minute session?
A Because "wish" is informal and should be limited in an executive meeting.
B The wish names the alternative reality and closes it. Repeated, it turns the session back into the post-mortem the other side asked not to spend the hour on.
C Because a second wish sentence would need a different tense and is easy to get wrong.
3. "Mexico had asked him when the handover would be signed." What has happened to the original question here?
A It has lost the inversion and moved one step back in time: reported questions take statement order, so "when would it be signed" becomes "when it would be signed".
B Nothing structural; only the pronoun has changed.
C It has become an indirect order, which is why "would" appears.
4. Why does the hedged number come last, and not at the start of her answer?
A Because the number was not ready when the session started.
B Because hedging is always more polite at the end of a conversation.
C A hedge that arrives before any fact reads as evasion. Placed after the facts, the regret and the reported voices, the same words read as calibration -- and it carries a date and a driver with it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Which Form, and When

GRAMMAR

Nothing new here. You already own all four forms. This table is about the moment each one belongs to, and the order they arrive in.

The moment in the roomWhat you reach forWhy that one
1. The quarter is closed and you have to account for itmodal perfect
should have flagged · could have absorbed · must have started		it judges a finished action without putting a person in the sentence. Past simple states; the modal perfect assesses
2. The room needs to know you would do it differentlywish + past perfect
I wish we had sequenced it · if only we had confirmed it first		it names the alternative reality and closes it. Once per session. Twice, and you are back in the post-mortem
3. Somebody who is not in the room already said something that mattersreported speech
they told me they had stopped · he asked when it would be signed		it carries the decision in without re-opening it. Backshift one step, and no inversion in a reported question
4. You are asked for a number you cannot yet stand behindhedging
is likely to · I would rather · it appears that		it states how much confidence sits behind the figure. Always with a driver and a date, or it reads as evasion
The order itselfPast judged → regret named once → other voices carried in → forward number hedged. Move the hedge to the front and the room stops listening before you reach the facts. Move the regret to the end and you close the session on an apology.
Trap 1 -- hedging what is already settledThe past does not take a hedge. It seems that we should possibly have flagged it tells the room the post-mortem is still open. Say it flat: we should have flagged it.
Trap 2 -- reporting with today's certaintyHe said the handover is signed makes it your claim, now. He said the handover had been signed keeps it his, then. Across two functions, that difference decides who is answerable.
Trap 3 -- the double hedgeOne hedge per statement. It is probably likely to be around eleven or so is four hedges and no number. It is likely to land between eleven and thirteen is one hedge and a range.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

Operations opens with: "I would rather not spend the first hour on what went wrong." The strongest reply is:
A "Then let me close it in three sentences." -- and then three sentences, no more.
B "Neither would I, but there is a lot of background you need first."
C "Fine, let us go straight to next quarter." -- and skip the quarter entirely.
You want to say the gap should have been caught in April, in a room with the team that missed it. You say:
A "Your team did not flag the capacity gap in April."
B "We should have flagged the capacity gap in April."
C "It seems that the capacity gap should possibly have been flagged in April."
A regional head asked you last week when the handover would be signed. Reporting that to operations today, you say:
A "Mexico asked me when would the handover be signed."
B "Mexico asked me when the handover would be signed."
C "Mexico asked me when is the handover signed."
He wants one figure today; two renewals are still untested. The answer that keeps your credibility is:
A "Eleven I will stand behind today. Thirteen depends on two renewals we have not pressure-tested, and I will firm it up on the twenty-second."
B "Probably around thirteen, more or less, but do not hold me to it."
C "Thirteen." -- give him what he wants and correct it later if the renewals fall through.
An agenda line reads "Discuss the capacity gap." What is missing before it can close in the session?
A Nothing -- the topic is clear enough for everyone in the room.
B What closed means, an owner and a timebox. Without them it is a conversation with a title, not an actionable item.
C A longer slot, so that the discussion is not cut short.
You need the reconciled figures on Thursday at the latest. You write:
A "Can you send me the reconciled figures until Thursday?"
B "Can you send me the reconciled figures by Thursday?"
C "Can you send me the reconciled figures on Thursday at the maximum?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer -- bring the recording to class.

Take the last quarter you actually had to account for, in front of another function. In ninety seconds, run the four moves in order: judge what should have happened, say once what you wish had been sequenced differently, carry in what your regions or your counterpart told you, and end on a number with the confidence that really sits behind it and a date to firm it up. Do not start with the number.

Lesson 15 — Block Capstone -- From Post-Mortem to Projection in a Live Scenario

Series
Suits -- any episode with a partners' meeting

Pick one scene in which two people from different sides of the firm have to leave a room with one decision. Ignore the plot and watch the sequence: who states the finished facts first, who names a regret and how quickly they drop it, who brings in what somebody outside the room said, and who is the last to put a number or a commitment on the table. It is the same four moves you ran today, at twice the speed and with worse manners.

Tip: pause the moment somebody hedges. Ask yourself whether a fact came before it. If it did not, notice how fast the other person takes over the room.
