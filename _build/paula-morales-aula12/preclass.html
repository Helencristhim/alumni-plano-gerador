Lesson 12 -- Pre-class

Can You Say That Again? -- Clarification and Survival Strategies

This is the lesson that solves the worst moment of speaking English: the other person talks fast, one word flies past, and you freeze. Here is the secret that changes everything: native speakers miss things half the time too — and they simply ask. “Sorry, could you repeat that?” is not a beginner's sentence; it is the sentence the Miami director uses most. There is no new grammar today: you take the could from lesson 9 and the questions with does from lesson 8 and build a survival kit — six words and five sentences that keep any conversation alive. This Pre-class is a first contact: come to class with the five sentences in your ear, not on the tip of your tongue.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Six words — and all of them are tools for when you do NOT understand. Listen to each one and repeat it out loud. The four sound traps of the day: ri-PEET · un-der-STAND · uh-GHEN · MOH-ment. Tap the table on the strong syllable while you repeat.

Repeat -- to say the same thing again
"Sorry, could you repeat that?" Say ri-PEET, stress on the second syllable, with a dry T at the end — never "RE-pit". This is the most used sentence of the day.
Understand -- to know what the words mean
"I don't understand." Say un-der-STAND — the stress falls at the END, on the third syllable. This is the emergency button: when no other sentence comes out, this one saves you.
Mean -- to have a certain meaning
"What does that word mean?" Say MIIN, with a long E — never "men". Use it when the problem is ONE word, and not the whole sentence.
Catch -- to hear and get what someone said
"Sorry, I didn't catch that." Say KATCH, with an open A. It is not catching a ball — it is catching what was said. The most natural English sentence for "I missed that".
Again -- one more time
"Could you say that again?" Say uh-GHEN — stress on the second syllable, and the end rhymes with "ten", not with "rain".
Moment -- a very short time
"Just a moment, let me check." Say MOH-ment — stress on the FIRST syllable. Use it to buy three seconds without going silent.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions. The sentences in bold are today's kit — notice that all of them are made of things you have already learned: the could from lesson 9 and the questions with does from lesson 8.

On Thursday Greg, the Miami director, called Paula about a report. He was in a hurry and spoke fast. The first sentence flew past. Paula breathed and said: "Sorry, could you speak more slowly?" — and Greg slowed down.

Then he used a word she did not know: deductible. She did not pretend to know it. She asked: "What does that word mean?" Greg explained — it is the part the client pays before the insurance starts. She needed three seconds to find the number in the file, and instead of going silent she said: "Just a moment, let me check."

At the end, the line went bad and she missed the last part. She did not guess. She said: "Sorry, I didn't catch that." Greg repeated it, and the call ended well. She did not understand every word — and she did not need to. She used the kit, and the conversation lasted to the end.

1. The sentence "Could you speak more slowly?" starts with Could you. Where does that structure come from?
A It is a new rule, only from this lesson.
B From lesson 9 — could is the polite request, and the verb comes bare after it.
C From lesson 4 — the third person singular.
2. Why is the question "What does that word mean?" and not "What means that word?"
A Because a present-tense question needs does (lesson 8), and the verb mean goes back to its bare form.
B Because mean is an irregular verb.
C Because Paula was being polite.
3. When Paula did not understand the word deductible, what did she do?
A She pretended to understand and carried on.
B She switched back to her own language.
C She asked what the word meant — and Greg explained.
4. What does the text say made the call a success?
A Paula understood every word Greg said.
B Paula did not understand everything — and did not need to: she used the kit and the conversation lasted to the end.

The three mistakes that feel natural to make, and the fix for each one:

1) "Repeat, please!" — the bare order. It is not ungrammatical, but it sounds like an order to a waiter, not a request to a director. The fix is the Could you from lesson 9: "Could you repeat that, please?"

2) "What means this?" — the word-for-word translation. In English a present-tense question needs does (lesson 8): "What does this mean?"

3) "I no understand." — the negative without don't. Same as lesson 3: "I don't understand."

Why this matters: you have spent eleven lessons learning to speak when you understand. This is the lesson about what to do when you do NOT understand — and that is the skill that separates the people who freeze from the people who carry on. Asking someone to repeat is not weakness: in insurance, whoever guesses a number loses the account; whoever asks does not. The Miami director himself asks people to repeat all day long.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record your answer. Speak for 60 to 90 seconds, with no script. Do not redo it more than twice. This is exactly the homework audio — start building it here.

Imagine you are in a video meeting in English and the other person speaks fast, uses a word you do not know, and the line goes bad. Act the scene out loud and use at least three sentences from the Survival Kit naturally: ask them to repeat, ask what the word means, ask them to slow down, or buy time with "Just a moment". The only rule of this recording is: you may not freeze and you may not switch language. If you do not understand, ask — and carry on. Nobody is measuring whether you understood everything; the only thing that counts is the conversation continuing.
