Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to every word and read the example. These are the words you need to say who your people are: your husband, your son, your brother, your friends. Think of one real person for each word before you move on.

Outgoing -- friendly, and happy to meet new people
"I'm the outgoing one in the family, so I do the talking."
Shy -- quiet and nervous with people you do not know
"My husband is shy in English, but not in Portuguese."
Patient -- able to wait calmly, without getting angry
"He's very patient: three hours in the airport, and he just reads."
Easygoing -- relaxed; small problems do not worry this person
"She's so easygoing: the wrong room, and she just laughed."
Generous -- happy to give things and to share
"My brother is the most generous person at any table."
Stubborn -- never changes their mind, even when they are wrong
"He's a little stubborn: the map is wrong, never him."
Talkative -- a person who talks a lot, all the time
"I'm the talkative one, and he's the quiet one."
Hardworking -- a person who works a lot and does not stop early
"My son is hardworking: he studies and works at the same time."
To get along with -- to have a good, friendly relationship with someone
"We get along very well: forty years, and we still travel together."
To take after -- to look like or act like an older person in your family
"My son takes after me: he talks to everybody."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions below.

It is eight in the morning in Sorrento, and Rosângela is alone at a breakfast table. A man from Chicago asks if the seat is free, sits down, and asks the question every traveler asks: "Are you here alone?" The old answer was four words: "No, with my husband." And then silence. This time she keeps going. "He's on the balcony. He doesn't speak English, so I do the talking for both of us. He's quieter than me — much quieter — but he's more easygoing than anybody I know."

Frank laughs, because his wife is the same. "Susan isn't as patient as I am with new languages," he says. "She just points at the menu." So Rosângela tells him about her son, who is twenty-four and hardworking, and who speaks better English than she does. And about her brother, who is more outgoing than her husband and gets along with everybody in five minutes. "My son takes after me," she says. "He never stops talking either."

Twenty minutes later, they are still at the table. Nothing in that conversation needed perfect grammar — it needed the words for people, and one small word to put two of them side by side: than. Short adjectives take -er (quieter, younger, happier). Long adjectives take more (more patient, more generous). Good becomes better. And when two people are the same, or almost, you use as ... as.

"He's quieter than me." Why not "more quiet than me"?
A Because "quiet" cannot be used in a comparison.
B Because "quiet" is a short adjective, so it takes "-er" — and an adjective never takes "-er" and "more" together.
C Because "more" is only for people you do not know.
"He's more easygoing than anybody I know." Why "more" here?
A Because "easygoing" is a long adjective, and long adjectives take "more ... than".
B Because the sentence is about a husband.
C Because "easygoing" is a negative word.
"Susan isn't as patient as I am." What does this sentence mean?
A Susan and Frank are exactly the same.
B Susan has less patience than Frank — it is a gentle way to say he is more patient.
C Susan is more patient than Frank.
Her son "speaks better English than she does". Why not "more good English"?
A Because "good" is never used about languages.
B Because "more good" is only for questions.
C Because "good" is irregular: its comparative is "better", never "gooder" and never "more good".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparing Two People

GRAMMAR

To introduce a person, you almost always compare them with somebody else. English has two frames for that: than (one has more) and as ... as (the two are the same). Here is the whole map.

AdjectiveComparativeExample
short (quiet, calm, young, old)+ -er thanHe is quieter than me. · My brother is younger than me.
ending in -y (easy, funny, happy)y → -ier thanShe is happier than her sister. · It is easier than last year.
long (patient, outgoing, generous, easygoing)more + adjective + thanHe is more patient than me. · She is more generous than anybody.
good / bad (irregular)better than / worse thanMy son speaks better English than I do.
the same amount(not) as + adjective + asHe is as old as my brother. · He is not as brave as me.

The three rules to remember: (1) -er OR more, never both — "quieter than", never "more quieter than". (2) The comparison word is than, never "that". (3) With as ... as the word "as" appears twice, on both sides of the adjective — "not as patient as", never "not as patient than".

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen to the model, then record yourself. Take your time: listen twice before you speak. Two things to watch: in "quieter than me", the "-er than" runs together and stays quick and soft; and in "as patient as", both little words are short — the strong word is always the adjective.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Pick the best answer for each moment at the table.

A guest asks: "Are you traveling alone?" Your husband is upstairs and speaks no English. You say:
A "No." (and you say nothing else)
B "No, I'm here with my husband. He doesn't speak English, so I do the talking for both of us."
C "Sorry, my English is bad."
You want to say your husband talks less than you do. You say:
A "He's more quieter than me."
B "He's quieter than me."
C "He's quieter that me."
Your brother meets new people very easily. You say:
A "He's outgoinger than my husband."
B "He's more outgoing than my husband, and he gets along with everybody."
C "He's more outgoing that my husband."
Your son's English is excellent, and yours is still growing. You say:
A "My son speaks better English than I do."
B "My son speaks more good English than me."
C "My son speaks gooder English than me."
You want to say, kindly, that your husband has less courage than you with English. You say:
A "He isn't as brave than me with English."
B "He isn't as brave as me with English."
C "He is as brave than me with English."
The guest finishes telling you about his brother. To keep the conversation alive, you say:
A "Okay." (and you look at your coffee)
B "And you? Do you travel with your family?"
C "My English is not good, sorry."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt. There is no right or wrong version. Speak for 60 seconds, with no script.

Imagine you are at a breakfast table in Italy and somebody asks about your family. Introduce three real people: your husband, your son, and one more person you love. For each one, say what kind of person they are (outgoing, shy, patient, easygoing, generous, stubborn, talkative, hardworking) and give ONE comparison: "He's quieter than me", "She's more generous than anybody", "He isn't as patient as I am". Use "as ... as" at least once, and finish by asking the other person about their family. For years, somebody else introduced your family in English. This time, do it yourself, out loud, with no script.
