Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

A yardstick -- the standard you are measuring against, chosen before you measure
"Change the yardstick and the ranking changes with it."
Like for like -- comparing things that are genuinely equivalent
"It is not a like-for-like comparison and both sides know that."
To lag behind -- to be persistently below a comparable group
"They lag behind on that measure and lead on two others."
To outperform -- to do better than somebody or something comparable
"The smaller economies outperform the region as a whole, consistently."
Per capita -- divided by the number of people, so that size does not distort it
"Per capita it looks entirely different, which is why the total is quoted."
A gulf -- a very large difference that does not look like closing
"There is still a gulf between the two capitals, let alone the two countries."
On a par with -- at the same level as something else
"On that indicator they are on a par with countries three times richer."
To close the gap -- to reduce the distance to whoever is ahead
"They closed the gap in a decade and then stopped."
To punch above one's weight -- to achieve more than the size or resources would predict
"The country punches above its weight in that field and nowhere else."
In relative terms -- compared with something else rather than in absolute numbers
"In relative terms the improvement is enormous, and in absolute terms it is small."
A benchmark -- a reference point everybody agrees to measure against
"The benchmark was set in 1998 and nobody has revisited it since."
To rank -- to occupy a position in an ordered list
"It ranks eleventh on one index and forty-second on another."
Head and shoulders above -- clearly and obviously better than the rest
"On road safety they are head and shoulders above their neighbours."
To be no match for -- to be clearly weaker in a direct comparison
"The old system was no match for the one that replaced it."
To level off -- to stop rising or falling and stay roughly steady
"The curve levels off after 2016 and nobody agrees why."
Disparity -- a difference that is large and considered unfair
"The regional disparity inside the country is wider than the gap between countries."
To even out -- to become more equal over time
"Give it two decades and the figures may even out."
Broadly comparable -- similar enough for a comparison to mean something
"The two systems are broadly comparable, which is as strong as I would put it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The more you look at an international ranking, the less it tells you about the countries in it. That is not cynicism; it is what a ranking is. Somebody chose a yardstick, decided how heavily each indicator would count, and turned a set of value judgements into arithmetic. Change the weighting and a country that ranks eleventh appears forty-second, without a single figure underneath it having moved.

The clearest example is a country whose measured economy roughly doubled overnight, because the base year was finally updated after decades. Nothing changed. It did not outperform anybody; it became better measured. Until that morning, every index containing it had been comparing a modern measurement with a very old one, which is the opposite of like for like. A ranking compares measurements, and measurements are not countries.

The traps are ordinary and constant. Totals flatter large countries and per capita flatters small ones, so whichever suits the argument is the one that gets quoted. A country may lag behind on one indicator, be on a par with far richer neighbours on a second, and punch above its weight on a third. In relative terms a gap can close dramatically while, in absolute terms, the gulf remains. Both sentences are true and they leave opposite impressions.

None of which means comparison is impossible — refusing to compare is not neutrality, it simply leaves the field to people with fewer scruples and larger platforms. Two questions do most of the work. What is the benchmark, and is the comparison broadly comparable in the first place? And one more thing worth knowing about your own country: the internal disparity is frequently wider than the gap between nations, which is a more useful number and almost never the one on the front page.

1. "The more you look at a ranking, the less it tells you." What does this structure express?
A Two quantities moving together, one as the consequence of the other.
B A condition and its result.
C A comparison between two countries.
2. Why did the country's measured economy double?
A Because it grew very fast that year.

3. What does the text say about totals and per capita figures?

B Each flatters a different kind of country, so the convenient one gets quoted.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparative and Proportional Structures

GRAMMAR

A comparative on its own says only the direction. These structures say how much, and how one quantity moves with another — which is where careful speakers become careless, because the Portuguese equivalents are built differently.

FormWhat it doesExample
the + comparative,
the + comparative		Two quantities moving together. BOTH halves need the. The comparative can be an adjective, an adverb or more/less + noun."The more you look, the less obvious it becomes."
"The sooner we decide, the better."
twice / three times / half
as + adjective + as		Multiples take as … as. Two times more likely is a direct translation and is not idiomatic; twice as likely as is."Twice as likely as the national average."
"Half as expensive as it was."
nowhere near as
not nearly as
every bit as		Emphatic comparison. Nowhere near as good means MUCH worse, not almost as good — and that misreading changes the meaning completely."Nowhere near as simple as that."
"Every bit as good as claimed."
by a factor of
by X per cent
from X to Y		The precise vocabulary of change. By a factor of three = multiplied by three. Note also: prices rise by two points, to a level, from a level."Rose by a factor of three."
"Fell by two points, to nine per cent."
degree adverbs:
marginally · slightly
considerably · substantially
· vastly		These attach the SIZE to the comparative. Without one of them you have told your listener only the direction — which is the most respectable way there is to mislead."Marginally higher." vs "Vastly higher."

One habit worth building, and it costs nothing: never let a comparative leave your mouth without a size attached. Higher is almost information. Marginally higher and substantially higher are information, and the difference between them is frequently the whole argument.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are discussing an international comparison with somebody who has just quoted a ranking. Choose the precise version.

You want two quantities to move together:
A "The more you look, more obvious it becomes."
B "The more you look, the more obvious it becomes."
C "More you look, more obvious it becomes."
You want to express a multiple:
A "They are two times more likely than the average."
B "They are twice as likely as the average."
C "They are the double likely of the average."
You want to say something is FAR less simple than claimed:
A "It is not near as simple as that."
B "It is nowhere near as simple as that."
C "It is nothing near simple as that."
Spending was multiplied by three:
A "Spending rose by three per cent."
B "Spending rose by a factor of three."
C "Spending rose in three times."
Somebody says the gap has closed. You want to give both true pictures:
A "In relative terms it has closed considerably; in absolute terms it is still a gulf."
B "The gap is nowhere near as closed than before."
C "It is on a par of the other countries now."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Choose one comparison between Brazil and another country that you hear repeated and do not entirely believe. In about ninety seconds: say what is probably being measured, identify where the comparison stops being like for like, present the same data once so that it flatters Brazil and once so that it does not — without changing a single fact — and finish by naming the one figure that would settle it for you.
