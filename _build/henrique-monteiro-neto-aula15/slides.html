Chapter 3: The Code

When One Thing Moves With Another

The structures that hold two quantities together

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The more you look at the figures, the less obvious the ranking becomes."

"They are twice as likely to be counted as unemployed."

"It is nowhere near as simple as the headline suggests."

"Spending has risen by a factor of three since 2010."

Four ways of putting two quantities in one sentence. Which of them says how MUCH, and which only says which is bigger?

FormWhat it doesExample
the + comparative,
the + comparative		Two quantities moving together. Both halves need the, and the second half is the consequence — Portuguese drops the second the and English cannot."The more you look, the less obvious it becomes."
"The sooner, the better."
twice / half / three times
as + adjective + as		Multiples take as … as, never more … than. Two times more likely is what a Portuguese speaker produces and it is not idiomatic."They are twice as likely as the national average."
nowhere near as
not nearly as
every bit as		Emphatic comparison. Nowhere near as good means MUCH worse — the negative surprises people who read it as almost as."It is nowhere near as simple as that."
by a factor of
by X per cent
from X to Y		The precise language of change. By a factor of three means multiplied by three, and it is not the same as three per cent or three times more."Spending rose by a factor of three."
"Unemployment fell by two points."
marginally · substantially
considerably · slightly
+ comparative		How much bigger. Without one of these, a comparative says only the direction — and direction without size is the commonest way to mislead honestly."Marginally higher." / "Substantially higher."

A comparative on its own tells your listener the direction and nothing else. Adding the size — twice, marginally, by a factor of three — is what separates a claim that can be checked from one that only sounds like information.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"The more you look at the figures, more obvious the problem becomes."
"The more you look at the figures, the more obvious the problem becomes."
"They are two times more likely to be counted as unemployed."
"They are twice as likely to be counted as unemployed."
"It is not near as simple as the headline suggests."
"It is nowhere near as simple as the headline suggests."

The first is the classic: the structure needs the in BOTH halves, and Portuguese offers only one. The second is a multiple, which English builds with twice as … as, not with more than. The third simply needs the fixed phrase: nowhere near as, or not nearly as — never not near as.

Chapter 4: Two Countries, One Ranking

Two Countries, One Ranking

A statistician who is paid to compare, and refuses to say which is better

Dialogue

Which Country Is Better?

P
Journalists always ask me the same question, and it is the one question I will not answer. Which country is better. There is no such measurement and there never was.
H
Although you do produce a ranking. Eleventh on one index, forty-second on another — somebody has to explain that.
P
And I will, gladly. The two indices measure different things, they weight them differently, and one of them is per capita and the other is not. That is the whole mystery.
H
So the more you look at the two lists, the less either of them means?
P
The more you look, the more you learn — about the people who built the index. Every ranking is an argument about what matters, expressed as arithmetic.
H
That is a sentence I would put on a wall. What would you tell somebody who just wants an answer?
P
Ask what the yardstick is, and ask whether it is like for like. Two questions, and about half the comparisons in the newspaper do not survive them.
H
And the other half? Because I would not want to end up refusing to compare anything at all.
Comprehension

About Petra

1. What question does Petra refuse to answer?
Which country is better — because there is no such measurement.
2. How does she explain the two very different rankings?
The indices measure different things, weight them differently, and one is per capita and the other is not.
3. What does she say every ranking is?
An argument about what matters, expressed as arithmetic.
4. What two questions does she recommend?
What the yardstick is, and whether the comparison is like for like.
Listening 1

The Country That Doubled Overnight

A statistician on a revision that changed everything. Sound first — no text.

1. What happened to the country's reported economy?
It roughly doubled overnight, when the base year was updated.
2. Had anything actually changed in the economy?
No — only the measurement, which had been using a base year from decades earlier.
3. What does she say the episode shows about international rankings?
That they compare measurements, not countries, and that the two are not the same thing.
Listening 2

In Defence of the Ranking

An economist answering back. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he concede about rankings?
That the weighting is a value judgement and is rarely explained.
2. What is his objection to the critics?
That refusing to compare at all is not neutrality — it just leaves the field to worse comparisons.
3. What does he propose instead?
Publishing the ranking with the weighting attached, so the argument is about the weighting.
