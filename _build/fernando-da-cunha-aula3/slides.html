Lesson 3 · The Call You Would Take Back

The Decision That Cost Money

Everybody who runs something has one. The hard part is not having it — it is being able to talk about it, in English, without sounding either defensive or careless.

Chapter 1: First Words

The One You Would Take Back

Last time you gave a buyer the output, the capacity and the downtime. Every one of those numbers came out of a decision somebody made, and at least one of those decisions was wrong.

Think of one business decision you would take back. Do not tell the story yet — just say what it cost, with a number.

Today’s Goal

One Story, Three Moves

1. The Words

a trade-off, a margin, to write off, a quote, upfront, a contingency, hindsight...

2. One Structure

the past that did not happen, and the judgement about it

3. Your Answer

the call, the cost, and what you do differently now

This is the structure behind every serious business conversation abroad: the review after a failure, the renegotiation, the question an interviewer asks when they want to know how you think. What would have happened and what you should have done are two different sentences.

One Question

What Would Have Happened If You Had Known?

Answer it out loud, right now, in one sentence.

Then the harder one: was the decision wrong, or was it a reasonable decision that ended badly? English has different words for those two things, and mixing them up is how people either blame themselves too much or learn nothing.

Chapter 3: The Code

The Past That Did Not Happen

One structure for the alternative, one for the judgement

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"If we had visited their plant first, we would have seen it in twenty minutes."

"We should have asked for a reference before the first order."

"If the tariff had not changed, the contract would still have worked."

"It could have been worse. We might have lost the client."

Every red part is have plus a past participle. Two of the sentences also carry a blue half, and those two are doing something the other two are not.

JobFormExample
A whole different pastIf + had + participle, would have + participleIf we had visited the plant, we would have seen it.
Criticism of the real pastshould have + participleWe should have asked for a reference.
A possibility that did not happencould have / might have + participleWe might have lost the client.
NegativeIf + had not + participle · would not have + participleIf the tariff had not changed, it would have worked.
Formal, without ifHad + subject + participle, ...Had we known about the tariff, we would have priced it in.
Softening the criticismIn hindsight / with what we knew at the timeIn hindsight it was a judgement call, and it was wrong.
The trapwould NEVER goes in the if halfNot if I would have known. Say if I had known.

In one line: the conditional builds another past; should have and could have judge the one you actually lived.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Review, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one form

Dialogue

The Review, Six Weeks Later

E
So. Walk me through it. Not who decided — what you knew on the day you decided.
F
We had a quote eleven per cent below our usual supplier, on the same specification. On that volume it was real money, so we moved half the order.
E
Had you been to their plant?
F
No. If we had visited, we would have found out in twenty minutes what took us four months. They were cutting corners — the work was going somewhere else with their label on it.
E
And what did that cost, in the end?
F
We wrote off most of a shipment, and we paid the old supplier a premium to take the volume back at short notice. The margin on that quarter went with it.
E
Here is the question nobody likes. Was the decision wrong, or was it a reasonable decision that ended badly?
F
In hindsight, both. It was a judgement call and the eleven per cent was never eleven per cent — it was eleven minus a risk nobody had priced. But we also had no contingency, and that part was not a judgement call. That part we should have had.
Comprehension

About Erin

1. How does Erin open the review, and what does she explicitly refuse to ask about?
She asks him to walk her through it, and she refuses to ask who decided. She wants to know what he knew on the day he decided.
2. What is the one short question Erin asks about the supplier, and why is it the key one?
Whether he had been to their plant. It is the piece of information that existed and was never collected, which is what the whole failure turns on.
3. What distinction does Erin ask him to make, and how does she introduce it?
Whether the decision was wrong or whether it was a reasonable decision that ended badly. She introduces it as "the question nobody likes".
Listening 1 · The Owner

Eleven Per Cent That Was Never Eleven Per Cent

A manufacturer telling the sourcing decision that still bothers him. Sound first — no text.

1. Why did he move half the order to the new supplier?
The new supplier came in about eleven per cent cheaper on the same specification, and on that volume it was a lot of money.
2. What would visiting the plant have shown him, and how long would it have taken?
Twenty minutes, against the four months it actually took: that the supplier was sending the work to somebody else and putting their own label on it.
3. What does he say the eleven per cent really was, and whose job was it to see that?
Eleven per cent minus a risk that nobody had put a number on, and he says he was the person who should have put a number on it.
Listening 2 · The Consultant

Why Most Reviews Are a Wasted Afternoon

A consultant on what a post-mortem should actually ask. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does she say most reviews after a failure are wasted?
Because people arrive having already decided that the answer is a person, and once you have said somebody made a bad call the meeting is over.
2. What question does she say actually works, and what does it usually find?
At what point could somebody have seen this coming, and what would they have needed to see it. Nine times out of ten a piece of information existed weeks earlier and nobody had asked for it.
3. What do the best owners she works with also review, and why?
The good outcomes as well, because a decision can be wrong and still work out, and reviewing only the failures never finds those.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

When the Tariff Changes Mid-Contract

A business radio report on what buyers and suppliers do next. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does the reporter say "who pays" is not the interesting question?
Because in most cases the contract already says who pays. The interesting question is what the two sides do next.
2. What happens to buyers who simply enforce the clause, and to those who split the difference?
Those who enforce it get one more delivery and then a supplier quietly looking elsewhere. Those who split the difference, even badly, keep the relationship and usually get it back within a year.
3. What did the manufacturers say they could not absorb?
Not the cost, which they could have absorbed. Finding out about it from a customs broker rather than from the person they had dealt with for six years.

ClassificationJudgement call — process gap
A plant visit was available and was not requested. Had one been carried out before the first order, the sub-contracting arrangement would have been visible on the day. The price advantage was real but was never adjusted for a risk nobody had priced. Action: no single-source switch above 25% of volume without a site visit and a named fallback.
1. The form separates "judgement call" from "process gap". Which part of this failure is which?
Trusting the eleven per cent was the judgement call. Having no contingency and no plant visit was the process gap, and only the second one can be fixed with a rule.
2. One sentence in the paragraph builds a past that did not happen. Which is it, and how do you know?
"Had one been carried out before the first order, the sub-contracting arrangement would have been visible on the day." It uses Had plus a participle instead of if, and would have in the second half.
3. What is the action at the end, and why is it written as a rule rather than as a lesson?
No single-source switch above twenty-five per cent of volume without a site visit and a named fallback. A rule survives the next decision; a lesson only survives the meeting.

I can separate a judgement call from a process gap, and say which one I am describing.
