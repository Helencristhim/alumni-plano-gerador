Lesson 15 -- Pre-class

The Conversation You Start

Starting a conversation at a global event and asking for the floor in a call, before you feel ready. Key words: speak up, chip in, jump in, volunteer, put yourself forward, small talk, icebreaker, mingle, common ground, build on. Structure: question tags (It's a big event, isn't it?).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To speak up -- to say what you think clearly, especially in a group where people are quiet
"When the director asked for ideas, I decided to speak up."
To chip in -- to add a short comment or idea to a conversation that other people are having
"Can I chip in here? We had the same problem in Brazil."
To jump in -- to start speaking in a conversation that has already started
"Sorry to jump in, but I have the number for that."
To volunteer -- to offer to do something without anybody asking you to do it
"I volunteered to present our reconciliation process."
To put yourself forward -- to offer yourself for a role, a task or a presentation
"My manager told me to put myself forward for the global meeting."
Small talk -- light, friendly conversation about simple topics, like travel or the weather
"Five minutes of small talk before the session makes the whole day easier."
An icebreaker -- a simple question or comment that helps two strangers start talking
"How was your flight? is the easiest icebreaker at any conference."
To mingle -- to move around at an event and talk to different people
"During the coffee break, I try to mingle with people from other entities."
Common ground -- something that two people share, like an experience, a problem or an interest
"We found common ground quickly: both teams close on day five."
To build on -- to add a new idea to what another person has just said
"I would like to build on what Diego said about the BPO."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Most people at an international event wait for somebody else to start. But the first line does not have to be clever. Listen to this one: You're from the Mexico team, aren't you? It is a statement and a question at the same time. The other person only has to say yes, I am, and the conversation has started.

That small question at the end is a question tag. When the sentence is positive, the tag is negative: It's a big event, isn't it? When the sentence is negative, the tag is positive: You haven't presented here before, have you? The tag repeats the auxiliary of the sentence, like is, have or can. If there is no auxiliary, you use do, does or did: Your team closes on day five, doesn't it?

In small talk, your voice goes down at the end of the tag, because you expect the other person to agree. It is not a real doubt; it is an invitation to keep talking. And the same idea works in a call: after the director asks for comments, one short sentence is enough to jump in.

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

You start a conversation with Diego at the coffee break. Put your lines in order.

?"Hi, you're Diego from the Mexico team, aren't you? I'm Keitma, from São Paulo."
?"Nice to meet you, Diego. It's a big event, isn't it?"
?"So your team closes on day five too, doesn't it? We have some common ground there."
?"By the way, I volunteered to present our reconciliation process tomorrow at eleven."
?"If you want, you can jump in after me and build on it with the Mexico numbers."
?"Great. I'll send you my slides tonight, so you can see them before the session."
