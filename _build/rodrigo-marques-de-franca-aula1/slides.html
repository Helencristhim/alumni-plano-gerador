Chapter 3: The Code

What Is Over, and What Is Not

Two tenses, and the time word decides which one

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I have worked in corporate law for twenty years."

"I studied English at a small school in Bahia in the nineties."

"I have not taken on a new arbitration this year."

"I moved to São Paulo in 2005 and the work changed."

Look at the blue words. Two of them name a time that is closed. Two of them name a period that is still open. The red verb follows.

JobFormExample
A period still openhave / has + past participleI have worked here for twenty years.
A time that is closedpast simpleI studied there in the nineties.
Life experience, no datehave / has + participleI have never argued a case in English.
The date arrivesswitch to past simpleI argued one in 2019.
Not yet, up to nowhave / has not + participleI have not signed it yet.
Time words that decidefor, since, ever, never, yet, this yearall of them take have
The trapnever the simple presentNot I work here since 2005.

In one line: if the period is still running, use have. If the clock stopped, use the past.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Coffee Break, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one firm profile

Dialogue

Coffee Break, Second Morning

S
I have been trying to work out who everybody is. You were in the arbitration session yesterday, were you not? What do you do?
R
I am a corporate lawyer. I mostly review pleadings and read contracts, and some of that ends up in arbitration. I am based in São Paulo.
S
How long have you been doing that?
R
I have worked in corporate law for twenty years. I started in Salvador, where the work did not need English at all, and I moved south in 2005.
S
That is a long track record. Was there a moment when it changed?
R
The move was the turning point. After a short stint in litigation I went back to the corporate side, and I have not left it since.
S
And is it as demanding as everyone says?
R
Some weeks I am juggling four matters that all became urgent on the same afternoon. And you? Have you always been on the disputes side?
Comprehension

About Sarah

1. What had Sarah been trying to do before she started the conversation, and where had she seen him?
She had been trying to work out who everybody was, and she had seen him in the arbitration session the day before.
2. Sarah asks two questions about time. What are they, and what is she trying to find out?
She asks how long he has been doing that, and whether there was a moment when it changed. She is looking for the shape of the career, not the job title.
3. Her last question is have you always been on the disputes side. Why not were you always on the disputes side?
Because his career is still running. A closed question about a finished period would need the past simple, and she is asking about a period that includes now.
Listening 1 · A Lawyer

How I Ended Up in Disputes

A lawyer on a panel, answering the question she is asked most. Sound first — no text.

1. What did she study first, and how did she describe those two early years?
She studied tax law and hated it. She then spent two years doing corporate deals in a firm where she was the most junior person in the room.
2. How did she actually get into arbitration?
A partner asked her to sit in on an arbitration because somebody was ill, and she has not really left that room since.
3. Which cases does she say she remembers, and why?
Not the ones they won. The two or three where she understood halfway through that she had been arguing the wrong point for months.
Listening 2 · A Partner

What the Job Actually Looks Like

A corporate partner answering a question from outside the profession. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say most of the job is, and what does he write afterwards?
Most of it is reading: contracts other people wrote, and the two paragraphs that will decide everything. Then he writes a note explaining in ordinary language what the client has just agreed to.
2. What do people picture when they think of this job, and what is the real number he gives?
They picture a courtroom. He says he has been in a courtroom maybe eight times in twenty years.
3. What did he give up, and what did he protect instead?
He gave up trying to protect his evenings. He protected the first hour of the morning, and that is what kept him in the profession.

Admitted 2001 becomes a past simple, because the year is closed. With the firm since 2011 becomes a present perfect, because the period is still open.

That they are written in language clients can actually use.
3. The paragraph says he is hands-on. What is the evidence it gives?
That he reads the documents himself, rather than only supervising other people who read them.
