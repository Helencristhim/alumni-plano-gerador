The Room Where They Decide If You Fly -- Conditions With a Deadline

Six hundred decisions before lunch, and every one of them starts with the same word. Key words: a backup plan, to go ahead, a knock-on effect, to call something off, a window, to reroute, worst case, to buy time, to be on standby, tight. Structure: the first conditional, and what English never allows after if.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A backup plan -- a second plan you use if the first one fails
"If the file does not open, we have a backup plan."
To go ahead -- to happen as planned, or to start doing something
"If the weather holds, the flight will go ahead."
A knock-on effect -- a problem that causes another problem somewhere else
"One late aircraft in Chicago has a knock-on effect in four cities."
To call something off -- to cancel something that was planned
"If the crew times out, they will call the flight off."
A window -- a short period of time in which something is possible
"There is a forty-minute window, and after that nothing leaves."
To reroute -- to send something or somebody along a different path
"If the storm moves east, they will reroute everything over Denver."
Worst case -- the least good result that could realistically happen
"Worst case, everybody sleeps in Atlanta and flies out at six."
To buy time -- to delay a decision so that you have longer to decide
"Announcing a one-hour delay does not fix anything. It buys time."
To be on standby -- ready to act immediately if you are needed
"Two crews are on standby until the last aircraft is on the ground."
Tight -- with almost no extra time or space
"The connection is tight, but it works if nothing goes wrong."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Here is a sentence about tomorrow: If the storm moves east, we will reroute everything. Both halves are about the future, and only one of them says so. The half with will in it is the result. The half after if is in the present simple, even though nothing about it has happened yet. That is the whole of the first conditional, and it is one of the few rules in English with no exceptions worth learning.

For a Portuguese speaker this is not a matter of memory. Portuguese has a tense for exactly this job — se chover, se a auditoria encontrar — and English simply does not. So the brain reaches for the nearest future marker it owns, and out comes if it will rain. It is the fastest signal of direct translation there is, and it disappears the moment you accept that English fills the gap with the present.

Three details finish the picture. The result does not have to be a promise: we might need a backup plan and I can take the call are both first conditionals, and both sound less absolute than will. The order of the halves is free, and the comma only appears when if comes first. And unless already contains the negative — it means if not — so a second negative after it breaks the sentence: unless we decide today, never unless we do not decide today.

The same present-tense rule runs after when, as soon as, until and before. I will send the summary as soon as the meeting finishes. And there is one habit worth stealing from people who do this professionally: attach a time to every condition. A duty controller writes decide by 06:45 next to each line, because a condition with no deadline never goes ahead and never gets called off. It is not a decision. It is a worry.

1. Why does the text say Portuguese speakers produce "if it will rain"?
A Because Portuguese has a tense for exactly that job and English does not, so the brain reaches for the nearest future marker.
B Because they have not memorised the list of irregular verbs.
C Because "rain" can be both a noun and a verb.
2. According to the text, when does the comma appear?
A Always, between the two halves.
B Only when the if half comes first.
C Only when the result uses might instead of will.
3. What does the text say turns a condition into a decision?
A Writing it down in a formal document.
B Getting somebody senior to approve it.
C Attaching a time to it. Without a deadline it is a worry.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The First Conditional

GRAMMAR

The future lives in only one half of the sentence, and never the half with if.

HalfFormExample
the conditionif + present simpleIf the storm moves east...
the resultwill + bare verb...we will reroute everything.
softer resultmight, may, can, should...we might need a backup plan.
order and commacomma only when if comes firstWe will reroute if the storm moves east.
unless= if not, so never negative after itUnless we decide now, we will lose it.
same rule afterwhen, as soon as, until, beforeAs soon as it lands, I will call you.
the trapPortuguese has a future subjunctive. English does not, and fills the gap with the present simple.

Never: If the audit will find something · as soon as the meeting will finish · unless we do not decide today · We will reroute everything, if the storm moves east

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the sentence a careful speaker would use.

You are describing a risk for next quarter. You say:
A If the review will find a problem, we will escalate it.
B If the review finds a problem, we will escalate it.
C If the review found a problem, we will escalate it.
You want to promise a summary right after a call ends. You say:
A I will send it as soon as the call will end.
B I will send it as soon as the call ends.
C I will send it as soon as the call is ending.
There is one condition and no time attached to it. Your colleague says "we might have a problem". The most useful reply is:
A Let me know if anything changes.
B What happens if it goes wrong, and when do we have to decide?
C I hope it will not be a problem.
You want to say the deadline is the last possible moment. You say:
A Unless we do not decide today, we will lose the window.
B Unless we decide today, we will lose the window.
C Unless we will decide today, we lose the window.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Recommend a decision on a real risk you are facing. Cover what you recommend, what happens if it works, what happens if it does not, what your backup plan is, and by when the decision has to be made. Attach a deadline to every line.

Tip: he builds several if-then sentences out loud. Write down four of them, then rewrite each one about a decision of your own.
