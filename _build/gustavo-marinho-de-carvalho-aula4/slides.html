Grammar Discovery

Which Ones Are Imagined?

"If you send it today, I will have it ready by Friday."

"If we moved to a four-day week, we would need far fewer meetings."

"If it were my decision, I would start with the buses."

"It would make sense to try it in one district first."

The red one might really happen this week — that is the shape you learned last lesson. The blue ones are imagined, and English marks that by putting the condition in the PAST even though it is about now. The orange one has no if at all, and it is the most useful sentence on this slide.

JobFormExample
Real and likely (last lesson)if + present simple, willIf you send it today, I will have it ready.
Imagined, unreal or unlikelyif + past simple, would + base verbIf we moved the office, we would lose half the team.
Putting yourself in their placeif I were you, I would...If I were you, I would try one district first.
Softening an opinion, with no ifit would + verb / I would sayIt would make sense to start smaller.
The past here is not the pastit marks distance, not timeIf I had more time, I would read it properly. (I do not.)
The trapnever would in the if halfNot If I would have time. Say If I had time.
The second trapwere for every person, in careful EnglishNot If I was you. Say If I were you.

In one line: the past tense here does not move the sentence back in time. It moves it one step away from reality.

Listening 1 · Business Radio

One Step Away from the Person

A short radio piece on the tense that keeps a disagreement open. Sound first — no text.

1. What two sentences does she compare, and what does she say they have in common?
“That policy is wrong” and “if it were my decision, I would start somewhere else”. They say the same thing.
2. Why is the second one easier to answer?
Because it puts the idea one step away from the person saying it, so the other side can argue with the idea without arguing with the person.
3. She says it is not weakness. What does she say it is for?
Keeping a conversation open long enough for somebody to change their mind — including you.
Listening 2 · A Voice Note

Six Words That Change the Argument

A voice note about the most useful question in a professional disagreement. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the question, and what two questions does he say it is better than?
“What would change your mind here.” Better than “why do you think that”, which sounds like an attack, and “are you sure”, which sounds like doubt about them.
2. What two things happen when you ask it?
They slow down, because they have to think instead of repeat. And sooner or later they ask you the same question back.
3. What does he say you have if nothing would change your mind?
Not an opinion but a position, and he says those are much less interesting to talk about.
Real Document

Two Letters, Same Newspaper

Letters to the Editor
Two readers on the same proposal · Thursday edition

M. Reid writes: This scheme is a disaster and everybody knows it. It punishes ordinary people and it will be reversed inside two years. Whoever signed it should not be making decisions about this city.

J. Okafor writes: There is a real trade-off here and I would rather we said so. If the money went into buses first and the charge started a year later, most of the objections would disappear. As it stands, the downside falls on the people with the fewest alternatives, and in the long run that is what will decide whether it survives.

Sample letters written for this lesson. The writers and the newspaper are invented.
1. Which of the two names a downside of the position they are defending?
J. Okafor. The second letter defends the idea and still says the downside falls on the people with the fewest alternatives.
2. Find the second conditional in the second letter. What is it doing there?
“If the money went into buses first and the charge started a year later, most of the objections would disappear.” It offers a change without demanding it, so it invites an answer instead of a fight.
3. Which letter is easier to answer, and why is that a question about grammar?
The second. The first states a verdict, so the only replies are agreement or a fight. The second puts its idea in the hypothetical, so there is something specific to accept, change or refuse.
