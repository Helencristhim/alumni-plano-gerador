Chapter 3: The Code

If I Had, I Would Be

The conditional that keeps one foot in the past and one in this week

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If I had retired five years earlier, I would be much further along with the grandchildren."

"If I had not worked those hours, I would have a different relationship with my son today."

"If I were the kind of man who says these things, I would have said it at his wedding."

"If she were not so much like her mother, she would never have forgiven me."

In every one of these four, the two halves of the sentence are in different times. Which half is in the past, and what does the other half describe?

FormWhat it doesExample
If + past perfect, would + bare verbA condition in the past, with a result you are living with right now."If I had retired earlier, I would be closer to them now."
If + past simple or were, would have + past participleA permanent condition about who you are, with a result back in the past."If I were that kind of man, I would have said it then."
Which half carries which timeThe if half carries the time of the CONDITION. The other half carries the time of the RESULT. They do not have to match."If I had not worked those hours (then), I would have a different relationship (now)."
Were, never wasIn this conditional English uses were for every person, including I and she."If I were you..."
"If she were less like her mother..."
Why it matters hereRegret about family is almost always mixed: something that happened decades ago and a consequence you are living with this week."If I had said it then, we would not be here now."

One form carrying both times is the natural shape of family regret, and most languages let the listener work out which half is which. English makes you choose it in the verb, and the choice is what tells the other person which half you are still carrying.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If I would have retired earlier, I would be closer to them now."
"If I had retired earlier, I would be closer to them now."
"If I was you, I would have said something at the time."
"If I were you, I would have said something at the time."
"If I had not worked those hours, I would had a different relationship today."
"If I had not worked those hours, I would have a different relationship today."

Three traps, one each. Would never goes inside the if half — it lives on the other side of the comma, always. In this conditional the verb to be is were for every person, which is the one place where English stops agreeing with itself. And would is a modal, so what follows it is bare: would have, never would had. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Chapter 4: Four Minutes on One Person

Somebody Else's Family

A friend whose children live on three continents

Dialogue

Nobody Fell Out. That Is the Problem.

N
Three children, three continents, and I speak to one of them properly. I have stopped pretending that is an accident.
B
Was there something? A row, a moment, anything you could point at?
N
Nothing. We grew apart, which is a much harder thing to explain than a fight. There is nobody to forgive.
B
If I had retired five years earlier, I would be much further along with my own. That is the sentence I keep saying to myself.
N
And would you? Or would you have found something else to be busy with? I ask myself that one too.
B
Probably the second. If I were a different sort of man, I would have said something at his wedding.
N
You still can. The thing nobody tells you is that they are waiting, and they are not going to start it.
B
That is worse than anything else you could have said, and I suspect that is why you said it.
Comprehension

About Nora

1. What does she say about her three children?
That they are on three continents and she speaks properly to only one of them -- and she has stopped pretending that is an accident.
2. What does she say is harder to explain than a fight?
Growing apart -- because there is nobody to forgive.
3. How does she answer Braulio's regret about retiring earlier?
She asks whether he really would have, or whether he would have found something else to be busy with -- and says she asks herself the same question.
4. What is the thing she says nobody tells you?
That they are waiting, and that they are not going to start the conversation themselves.
Listening

The Generation That Provided

A man of seventy on the radio. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does he say his generation was taught, and what was it never taught?
Taught to provide -- and never once taught to explain, or to say anything out loud.
2. What does he say about the word distance, and how long did it take?
That there was never a fight, only distance, and that it took about six years to arrive.
3. What does he say he would do differently, and what does he immediately admit?
He says he would have said things at the time -- and immediately admits he might well have found something else to be busy with.
Listening 2

Say the Specific Thing

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does she say goes wrong when people describe the people they love?
They use the largest possible words -- he is a good man, she is wonderful -- which say nothing and cannot be answered.
2. What is the rule she gives instead?
One specific habit, one specific moment, and what it tells you about the person -- exactly the way you would describe a character in a film.
3. What does she say about the tenses in this kind of talk?
That family talk almost always mixes times, and that English asks you to choose which half is in the past and which half is now.
