Chapter 3: The Code

How Sure Are You?

One small structure that measures your certainty about the past

Grammar Discovery

Same Friday. How Sure Are We?

Someone must have used his login.

He can't have approved it himself: he was on a plane.

The account might have been set up just for this.

The bank could have made a mistake with the dates.

Which one is almost certain? Which one is almost impossible? What do the red words have in common?

How sureFormExample
Almost certain: yesmust have + past participleSomeone must have used his login.
Almost certain: nocan't have / couldn't have + participleHe can't have approved it.
Possiblemight / may / could have + participleThe bank could have made a mistake.
Possible: nomight not / may not have + participleHe might not have read it.
Passivemodal + have been + participleThe account might have been set up for this.
In progress at that momentmodal + have been + -ingHe must have been flying over the Atlantic.
QuestionCould / Might + subject + have + participle?Could he have shared his password?
The trapsno to, no had · never can have or mustn't have for a deductionNot must to have, not must had, not he can have forgotten.

In one line: the facts are in the participle; how sure you are is in the modal.

Chapter 4: Real Voices

The Case of the Friday Payment

One call, two recordings, one log

Dialogue

Who Approved the Payment?

D
Rodrigo, thanks for calling back. On Friday, a payment of a hundred and eighty thousand dollars went out to a supplier nobody here has ever heard of.
R
Before we jump to conclusions, let's piece together what we know. Who approved it?
D
The system says Marcos Prado, our finance manager. But I have a hunch he had nothing to do with it.
R
What makes you say that?
D
He was on a flight to Lisbon. The approval came in at two thirty-two, and his plane took off at one.
R
Then he can't have approved it himself. Someone must have used his login. Have we ruled out the IT team?
D
Not yet. And there's another discrepancy: the supplier was registered only three days before the payment.
R
So the account might have been set up just for this. That's plausible. Is there a paper trail? A contract, a purchase order?
D
Nothing. Could Marcos have shared his password with someone? It sounds far-fetched to me.
R
It does, but let's not give anyone the benefit of the doubt yet. I'll ask the bank to corroborate the times, and we'll tie up the loose ends before we talk to Marcos.
Comprehension

About Dana

1. How much money did Dana's company pay, and to whom?
A hundred and eighty thousand dollars, to a supplier nobody at the company had ever heard of.
2. Why does Dana think Marcos had nothing to do with it?
He was on a flight to Lisbon: his plane took off at one, and the approval came in at two thirty-two.
3. What second discrepancy does Dana mention?
The supplier was registered only three days before the payment.
Listening 1 · The Novelist

The Clues Come Last

A crime novelist on how she builds a mystery. Sound first — no text.

1. Where does she start when she plans a mystery?
At the end: she decides what really happened, then works backwards and leaves small clues for the reader.
2. Why do readers love the moment of the deduction, according to her?
Because it is exactly how we think in real life: we see a few facts and fill in the gaps.
3. When does her detective rule out an explanation?
Only when a fact makes it impossible. Until then, she writes down every explanation, even the far-fetched ones.
Listening 2 · The Forensic Accountant

Follow the Paper Trail

A forensic accountant on how he investigates missing money. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he do in his first week on a case?
He does not interview anyone. He follows the paper trail: invoices, bank records, emails and the time of each approval.
2. Why does he trust documents more than people?
Because people's memories are not reliable, but documents are.
3. What has he learned in twenty years?
The person who looks guilty on day one is usually not the one who did it.
Real Document

The Log Does Not Lie

System Access Report · Kestrel Foods do Brasil
Prepared for Rodrigo Marques de França · confidential

Tue 08:47 · Supplier registered: Orvane Trading Ltda · by user tsilva

Fri 13:02 · Flight TP 088, São Paulo to Lisbon, departs · passenger: M. Prado

Fri 14:21 · Login: user mprado · computer FIN-06, São Paulo office, 6th floor

Fri 14:32 · Payment approved: Orvane Trading Ltda · 180,000 dollars · user mprado

Fri 14:35 · Logout: user mprado · computer FIN-06

No contract or purchase order found for Orvane Trading Ltda · badge records for the 6th floor requested
1. Where was the login made from, and what does that tell you?
From computer FIN-06, in the São Paulo office. Marcos was on a plane, so somebody in the office must have used his login.
2. Who registered the supplier, and when?
User tsilva, on Tuesday morning, three days before the payment.
3. What can you say with must have, and what only with might have?
Somebody on the 6th floor must have used Marcos's login. The person behind tsilva might have been involved, but registering a supplier does not prove it: the badge records could corroborate it.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I use must have + participle when I am almost sure something happened.
I use can't have when I am almost sure it did not happen, and never mustn't have.
I use might have, may have or could have for a possibility, and never can have.
I lead a fact-finding conversation without jumping to conclusions.
I know the words: a hunch, to rule out, a discrepancy, a paper trail, to corroborate.
