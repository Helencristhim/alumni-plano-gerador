Lesson 17 -- Pre-class

What Might Have Happened -- Deduction and Hypothesis

How to work out what happened from the evidence, and say exactly how sure you are. Key words: a hunch, to jump to conclusions, to piece together, to rule out, plausible, far-fetched, a discrepancy, to account for, to corroborate, a paper trail, a loose end, to give somebody the benefit of the doubt. Structure: past modals of deduction -- must have / might have / can't have + past participle.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A hunch -- a feeling that something is true, before you have any proof
"I have a hunch the problem started in the warehouse."
To jump to conclusions -- to decide that something is true too quickly, before you know all the facts
"Let's not jump to conclusions: we have only seen one email."
To piece together -- to understand a situation by putting separate facts together
"We are still piecing together what happened on Friday."
To rule out -- to decide that something is not possible, or is not the explanation
"The bank has ruled out a system error."
Plausible -- likely to be true; easy to believe
"His explanation is plausible, but we still need to check it."
Far-fetched -- very unlikely to be true; hard to believe
"The idea that the CEO sent it himself sounds far-fetched."
A discrepancy -- a difference between two things that should be the same
"There is a discrepancy between the invoice and the bank records."
To account for -- to explain why something happened, or where something went
"He could not account for the missing two hours."
To corroborate -- to confirm a story or a fact with new evidence
"The flight records corroborate her version of events."
A paper trail -- documents that show what somebody did and when
"Every payment should leave a paper trail."
A loose end -- a detail that is not explained or finished yet
"There are still a few loose ends to tie up before the meeting."
To give somebody the benefit of the doubt -- to believe somebody, even though you are not completely sure they are right
"I'll give him the benefit of the doubt: he has never lied to us before."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

On Monday morning, Dana Whitfield called Rodrigo with a problem. On Friday, a payment of 180,000 dollars had gone to a supplier that nobody at her company knew. The system said that Marcos, the finance manager, had approved it. Dana had a hunch that he was innocent, and Rodrigo did not want to jump to conclusions either.

Together, they pieced together the facts. Marcos was on a plane to Lisbon at the time, so he can't have approved the payment himself. Somebody must have used his login. The supplier was registered three days earlier, which was another discrepancy: the account might have been set up just for this payment. That theory was plausible, but there was no paper trail to prove it.

Could Marcos have shared his password? Dana thought it was far-fetched, but Rodrigo did not want to rule anything out. Before talking to anyone, he asked the bank to corroborate the times, so that there would be no loose ends.

1. "He can't have approved the payment himself." What does Rodrigo mean?
A He is almost sure Marcos did not approve it.
B Marcos did not have permission in the system to approve a payment of that size on his own.
C Marcos approved it, but by mistake.
2. "The account might have been set up just for this payment." How sure is Rodrigo?
A Completely sure, because the bank has already confirmed every detail of the account.
B He thinks it is possible, but he has no proof yet.
C He thinks it is impossible.
3. Why did Rodrigo ask the bank to corroborate the times?
A To cancel the payment.
B Because Dana asked him to accuse Marcos and he needed the bank to sign a formal complaint first.
C To confirm the facts before talking to anyone.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Modals of Deduction

GRAMMAR

The modal says how sure you are. The form after it is always the same: have + past participle.

How sureFormExample
Almost certain: yesmust have + past participleSomeone must have used his login.
Almost certain: nocan't have / couldn't have + participleHe can't have approved it.
Possiblemight / may / could have + participleThe bank could have made a mistake.
Possible: nomight not / may not have + participleHe might not have read it.
Passivemodal + have been + participleThe account might have been set up for this.
QuestionCould / Might + subject + have + participle?Could he have shared his password?
In speechhave is weak: must've, might've, can't haveShe must've left early.

Never: He must to have used it · They must had sent it · She can have forgotten · He mustn't have read it (for a deduction)

Stage 2: Run the Fact-Finding Call in Order

Order

Facts first, then the impossible, then the likely, then the proof. Put the call in that order.

?"Before we jump to conclusions, let's piece together what we know."
?"Who approved the payment, and at what time?"
?"He was on a plane, so he can't have approved it himself."
?"Someone in the office must have used his login."
?"The supplier might have been set up just for this payment."
?"I'll ask the bank to corroborate the times before we talk to anyone."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Your colleague did not come to the 9 a.m. meeting. He is never late, and his phone is off. You say:
A "He must to have had a problem on the way, so let's start without him and update him later."
B "Something must have happened. He is never late."
C "He can have forgotten."
A client says she signed a document on 10 March. Her passport shows she was in Japan from 1 to 20 March. You say:
A "She can't have signed it in São Paulo on that date."
B "She mustn't have signed it in São Paulo on that date, because the passport shows she was abroad all month."
C "She must have signed it."
An email from the other side never arrived. You have no information yet. You say:
A "They are lying."
B "It might have gone to spam. Let's not jump to conclusions."
C "It must had gone to spam, because that is what happens with almost every email from that firm."
A friend tells you a story that is hard to believe, but he has always been honest with you. You say:
A "It sounds far-fetched, but I'll give you the benefit of the doubt."
B "It sounds plausible."
C "I'll rule you out."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Think of a case, at work or in your life, where at first nobody knew what had happened. In about sixty seconds, give the evidence, then three theories: one with must have, one with can't have and one with might have. Say which one turned out to be true.
