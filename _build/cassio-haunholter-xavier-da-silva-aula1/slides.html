The First Ninety Seconds

Chapter 1: First Words

Twelve Engineers, One Sentence

Every two or three weeks a new call opens with people from Sweden, Finland or Houston, and the first thing that happens is a round of introductions. In Portuguese that round costs you nothing. Today we build the English version.

In one sentence: what does your department do, and what is on your desk this week?

Three Missions

Ten words for a role, a team and a project portfolio.

2

The target of the day in one line: I manage the projects department is who you are; I am working with the Houston office on a joint plant is what you are in the middle of. English marks the difference with the verb, and the room hears it.

One Question

Always True, or Only This Month?

Two sentences, out loud, right now.

One about your job that was true last year and will be true next year. One about something that started recently and will be finished soon. Do not think about the grammar yet: just say them.

Chapter 3: The Code

Who You Are, or What You Are Doing?

Two present tenses for one professional life

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Show?

"I report to the regional director every week."

"This month I am working with the Houston office on a joint plant."

"We usually design industrial buildings for clients in Europe."

"At the moment we are mobilizing a structural team for the new plant."

Look at the time words in red. Some of them mean always, in general: every week, usually. Others mean right now, not forever: this month, at the moment. The verb in blue changes with them.

JobFormExample
Your role, always truepresent simple: I / we + verbI manage the projects department.
A routine, how oftenpresent simple + every week / usually / alwaysI report to the regional director every week.
Something temporary, happening nowam / is / are + verb-ingAt the moment I am working with the Houston office.
A project in progress, not finishedam / is / are + verb-ing + this month / right nowWe are mobilizing a structural team this month.
The two questionsWhat do you do? / What are you working on?What do you do here? — What are you working on at the moment?
The trapverbs of state stay simpleNot I am knowing that client. Say I know that client well.

In one line: the simple form says who you are; the -ing form says what you are in the middle of.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Kick-off, at Real Speed

One call, three recordings, one directory sheet

Dialogue

The Round of Introductions

M
Good morning, everyone. I am Marika, and I am coordinating this project from Helsinki. Let us do a quick round. Cassio, would you like to start?
C
Sure. Good morning. I am Cassio, and I oversee the projects department at the Brazilian office.
M
Thank you. And how big is the team there?
C
Twelve engineers report to me, across four disciplines: structures, architecture, piping and electrical.
M
Good. And what are you working on for us at the moment?
C
At the moment we are preparing the layout for the second industrial block. My portfolio is mostly industrial buildings and process plants.
M
Perfect. I am your main contact here, and my counterpart in Houston is Brian. He joins every second call.
C
Understood. And the workload on our side is heavy this quarter, so I will send you a capacity note this week.
Comprehension

About Marika

1. Where is Marika calling from, and what is her role on the project?
She is in Helsinki, and she is coordinating the project.
2. Who is Marika’s counterpart in Houston, and how often does he join?
Brian, and he joins every second call.
3. What does Marika ask Cassio, after the introduction?
How big the team is, and what he is working on at the moment.
Listening 1 · The Coordinator

How I Open a Kick-off Call

A project coordinator in Helsinki explains what she wants from the round of introductions. Sound first — no text.

1. How long does she give each person in the round?
About one minute, or ninety seconds.
2. What is the mistake she hears most often?
People give only their job title, and nobody understands what they actually do.
3. What three things does she ask each person to say?
Their role, the size of their team, and what they are working on right now.
Listening 2 · The Houston Engineer

What I Do, and What I Am Doing Now

A process engineer in Houston describes his role and his current project. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he do in general?
He designs process plants for the food and chemical industries.
2. What is he working on at the moment?
A joint project with the Brazilian office, on the second industrial block.
3. Why does he say the two tenses matter on a project call?
Because his general job and his current task are different things, and people on the call need to know which one he is talking about.
Listening 3 · The Onboarding Recording

How the Offices Fit Together

An internal recording explains the structure of a global engineering group. Sound first — no text.

1. Where are the headquarters, and what do they decide?
In Stockholm, and they decide the standards, the tools and the big commercial questions.
2. What does the recording say each subsidiary keeps?
Its own client base, its own disciplines and its own way of running a project.
3. What does the recording say is the most common source of confusion?
Job titles, because the same title means a different level in each country.

Notice how the sheet separates the line that never changes from the line that changes every month: Role is who you are, Current task is what you are in the middle of. That is exactly the difference between the two tenses of this lesson.
1. Which line stays the same all year, and which line changes every month?
Role stays the same all year (present simple: I head the projects department). Current task changes (present continuous: I am working on the layout of block 2).
2. How much time do Brazil and Helsinki share in a working day?
One hour, from thirteen hundred to fourteen hundred.
3. Turn the Team line into a full sentence.
Twelve engineers report to me, across four disciplines: structures, architecture, piping and electrical.
