If It Rains, We Move -- Plans, Promises and Backup Plans

Making plans that might change, promising things with a condition and warning people in advance. Key words: a heads-up, to sort out, to be up for, otherwise, to make it, a backup plan, to approve, a budget. Structure: the first conditional (if plus the present simple, will plus the base verb), with might, can, should and unless.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. Do not translate — the example sentence is the meaning.

A heads-up -- a warning you give before something happens
"Give me a heads-up if you're going to be late."
To sort out -- to solve a problem or organize something
"Leo says he will sort out the music."
To be up for -- to want to do something that is being suggested
"I'm up for dinner on Saturday if you are."
Otherwise -- if not, or if that does not happen
"Text me, otherwise I'll assume you're not coming."
To make it -- to manage to go somewhere or arrive on time
"If I finish early, I'll make it to the class."
A backup plan -- a second plan for if the first one fails
"If it rains, we have a backup plan."
To approve -- to say officially that something can happen
"If the client approves the concept, we start on Monday."
A budget -- the money available for a project
"If they cut the budget, we'll change the venue."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Saturday is happening, and it has two possible versions. If the weather is good, we will meet at the park at eleven. If it rains, we will move the whole thing to Leo's place, so nobody has to cancel anything. That is the backup plan. If everyone brings one thing, we will not need to buy anything, and if you are not up for cooking, bread or ice is perfectly fine. Give Leo a heads-up by Friday night, otherwise he will assume you are not coming.

Work is a different story this week. The client has cut the budget, so we need a decision today. If we keep the same venue, we will have to drop the second stage. If we move to the smaller space, we will keep everything else and nobody will notice the difference. If I approve the smaller venue this morning, Daniel will have the new plan ready by six. And if we wait until tomorrow, we lose the date.

1. Why does the text say "If the weather is good" and not "If the weather will be good"?
A Because the if half always uses the present simple, even when the sentence is about the future.
B Because the weather is happening right now.
C Because good weather is certain on Saturday.
2. What does "Give Leo a heads-up by Friday night, otherwise he will assume you are not coming" mean?
A That Leo will call everybody on Friday night to confirm.
B That if you do not warn him in time, he will think you are not going.
C That everybody is invited, with or without an answer.
3. In the second paragraph, what is the consequence of keeping the same venue?
A Nobody will notice the difference.
B They will have to drop the second stage.
C Daniel will have the new plan ready by six.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Condition and the Consequence

GRAMMAR

One shape, two halves. That is the whole lesson.

HalfFormExample
The conditionif + present simple. Never will, even though the sentence is about the future.If it rains, ...
If the client approves, ...
The consequencewill + base verb. In speech, almost always the contraction: I'll, we'll, they'll...., we'll move to my place.
..., we'll start on Monday.
Either order"If you send the brief, I'll review it today." = "I'll review the brief today if you send it." With if first, use a comma. With if second, no comma.
NegativeEither half can be negative: "If you don't answer, I won't book the table."
Softer than willmight = maybe, can = it is possible, should = advice. "If they change the brief, we can adjust the plan."
unless and otherwiseUnless means "if not": "Unless it rains, we meet at the park." Otherwise starts the consequence of not doing it: "Text me, otherwise I'll book for six."

There is only one thing to remember here: will never lives in the if half. Everything else in this structure works the way you already expect.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are making plans and negotiating. Choose the best thing to say.

A friend invites you to dinner on Saturday. You want to go, but you have work to finish. You say:
A "I'm up for it, if I finish the presentation on Friday."
B "I am up for it, if I will finish the presentation on Friday."
C "I will be up for it if I finished the presentation."
The client might approve the concept today. You want to promise a start date. You say:
A "If the client will approve it today, we start Monday."
B "If the client approves it today, we'll start on Monday."
C "If the client approve it today, we will started on Monday."
You are not sure about the result, and you do not want to promise anything. You say:
A "If we launch in March, we will definitely reach a younger audience."
B "If we launch in March, we might reach a younger audience."
C "If we will launch in March, we reach a younger audience."
You need an answer by tonight and you want to be clear about the consequence. You say:
A "Give me a heads-up by ten, otherwise I'll book for six."
B "Give me a heads-up by ten, otherwise I book for six yesterday."
C "Give me a heads-up by ten, or else I would have booked for six."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is correcting this one.

The client has just cut the budget by twenty per cent and asks: "So what happens now?" Speak for about ninety seconds. Present two options as conditions, say what you will lose in each one, recommend the one you prefer and explain why, then finish with what you need from them and by when. Use if at least four times. If a sentence breaks in the middle, say "let me start that again" out loud and keep going.
