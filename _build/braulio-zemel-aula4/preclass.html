Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

The Listening Gap -- Why Americans Sound So Fast

What actually happens to English at speed, and the four sentences that get the information back. Key expressions: to mumble, to trail off, to catch, the gist, to zone out, a filler word, to slur, background noise, to rephrase, to follow, linking, to lose the thread. Structure: wish + past simple for now, wish + past perfect for a past you cannot go back to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

To mumble -- to speak quietly and unclearly, without opening your mouth much
"He mumbles, and everybody at the table pretends they understood."
To trail off -- to stop speaking gradually, before the sentence is finished
"She trailed off, and I never found out how it ended."
To catch -- to hear and understand something that was said quickly
"I caught the name and the day, and nothing in between."
The gist -- the general meaning of something, without the detail
"I got the gist. I did not get the numbers."
To zone out -- to stop paying attention without deciding to
"After twenty minutes at native speed I zone out, and I know it is happening."
A filler word -- a sound or a word used to fill the space while you think
"Half of what sounds fast is filler words with nothing inside them."
To slur -- to run words together so that they lose their edges
"Americans slur, and what should be three words arrives as one."
Background noise -- the other sound in the room, competing with the voice
"In a restaurant the background noise costs me forty per cent."
To rephrase -- to say the same thing again in different words
"Could you rephrase that? is more useful than could you repeat that?"
To follow -- to understand something while it is being said
"I can follow one person easily and three people not at all."
Linking -- the way one word runs into the next in fast speech
"Linking is why did you eat arrives as one long word."
To lose the thread -- to stop being able to follow where the conversation is going
"I lost the thread somewhere in the second story and never got it back."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He has said the same thing for fifty years: Americans speak too fast. It is a reasonable description and it is the wrong diagnosis. Fast English is not the same sentence played faster. It is a different sentence, with material taken out of it.

Three things happen at speed. Words run into each other — linking — so that did you eat arrives as one block. Speakers slur, and the edges of the words go soft. And a good deal of what is left is filler words carrying no information at all. Add background noise in a restaurant and the signal is thinner than anybody admits.

So he does what most people do. He gets the gist and misses the numbers. He manages to catch a name and a day and nothing in between. Somebody mumbles, somebody else trails off in the middle of a sentence, and twenty minutes in he zones out and loses the thread entirely. He can follow one person comfortably and three people not at all.

I wish I understood films without subtitles, he says, and I wish I had studied the sounds when I was twenty-four. Both sentences are true and only one of them is useful. The useful one is shorter and it is not about the past at all: Sorry, could you rephrase that?

1. "I wish I understood films without subtitles." What does this tell us?
A That he understands them now.
B That he does not understand them now, and would like to.
C That he understood them in the past.
2. Why is "I wish I had studied the sounds" in the past perfect?
A Because the wish is about a past that is finished and cannot change.
B Because the studying is happening now.
C Because wish is always followed by the past perfect.
3. According to the text, what is the wrong diagnosis?
A That English has too many words.
B That Americans simply speak too fast.
C That background noise makes no difference.
4. What does linking mean here?
A Words running into each other in fast speech.
B Speaking quietly and unclearly.
C Stopping in the middle of a sentence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Wish, and the Step Backwards

GRAMMAR

After wish, every verb steps one tense backwards. That step is how English marks something as not real.

FormWhat it saysExample
wish + past simpleAbout NOW: it is not true today, and you would like it to be."I wish I understood the films."
wish + past perfectAbout the PAST: it did not happen, and the moment is closed."I wish I had studied the sounds earlier."
wish + couldAbout an ability you do not have now. Can steps back to could."I wish I could follow three people at once."
wish + wouldAbout somebody else's behaviour, with a small complaint inside it."I wish they would speak more slowly."
The trapNothing after wish stays in the present, and nothing goes into the future.NOT "I wish I can"
NOT "I wish they will"
"I wish I studied" = a habit, not a regret

If only works exactly the same way and carries more feeling: if only I had started ten years ago. Useful at a dinner table, where feeling is half of what you are communicating.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a talk and afterwards at dinner, and everything is in English. Choose the best thing to say.

You missed the last twenty seconds. The most useful thing to say is:
A "Sorry, my English is very bad."
B "Sorry, could you rephrase that?"
C "Yes, yes." (and you nod)
You want to check that you understood the new day of the meeting:
A "Just so I have it right, you are saying Thursday?"
B "I am not understanding nothing."
C "Repeat, please, everything again."
You want to say that you cannot follow three people at once, and you would like to:
A "I wish I can follow three people at once."
B "I wish I could follow three people at once."
C "I wish I will follow three people at once."
You regret not working on pronunciation at twenty-four:
A "I wish I studied the sounds at twenty-four."
B "I wish I had studied the sounds at twenty-four."
C "I wished I would study the sounds at twenty-four."
Three people are talking over each other and you lost one name. The best move is:
A Wait until the end and hope somebody says it again.
B "I am following you, but I lost the name. Who moved to Lisbon?"
C "Could you all start from the beginning, please?"
