Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to every word and read the example. These are the words of a journey you make on your own: the airport, the shuttle, the street at night, the door of the hotel. Picture the moment you would need each one before you move on.

Directions -- the words that tell you how to get to a place
"I asked a woman at the station for directions, and she walked me to the corner."
Straight ahead -- in front of you, with no turn
"Go straight ahead for two blocks, and you will see the hotel."
Around the corner -- very close, at the end of this street
"The shuttle stop is around the corner, right behind that building."
A shuttle -- a small bus that carries people between two places
"There is a free shuttle from the airport to the center every twenty minutes."
On foot -- walking, not by car, bus or train
"It is only ten minutes on foot, and the street is flat."
To get lost -- to stop knowing where you are
"I got lost near the station, so I asked a woman in a shop."
A backup plan -- a second plan, for when the first one does not work
"The last shuttle leaves at midnight, so my backup plan is a taxi."
Just in case -- because something may happen, even if it probably will not
"I take a picture of the address just in case my phone stops working."
To head to -- to start going toward a place
"After the shuttle, I am heading to the hotel on Garden Street."
Confident -- sure that you can do something, and calm about it
"After four trips, I feel confident at an information desk."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions below.

It is ten at night and Rosângela is standing in an arrivals hall with a suitcase and no telephone signal. Four years ago this was the moment she called her son. Tonight she walks to the information desk, waits for the man to look up, and says: "Excuse me, sorry to bother you. Could you tell me where the shuttle to the center stops?" Not "where is the shuttle" — the short version, the one that gets a five-second answer and a man already looking at the next person in the line.

Tomas tells her the stop is outside door four: straight ahead to the end of the hall, and then around the corner, on the left. She asks a second question, and this one has no question word, so it needs a small extra word: "Do you know if it still runs at this time?" Until midnight, he says, every forty minutes after nine. She asks a third: "Could you tell me how long it takes to the center?" About twenty-five minutes. Then he asks where she is heading to, and when she says the name of the hotel, he tells her something she never asked for: get off at the second stop, four minutes on foot, and take a picture of the address just in case.

That extra information is not luck. It is what happens when a question arrives with a door open in front of it. And the shape of the door is always the same: after "could you tell me" or "do you know", the sentence goes back to normal — subject first, verb second — the word "does" disappears, and a yes-or-no question takes if. Twenty minutes later she is at the front desk of the Garden Hotel, confident, with nobody on the phone.

"Could you tell me where the shuttle stops?" Why not "where does the shuttle stop"?
A Because "shuttle" cannot be used with "does".
B Because after an opening like "could you tell me", the question goes back to normal order and the word "does" disappears.
C Because the question is about the future.
"Do you know if it still runs at this time?" Why is "if" there?
A Because the question has no question word and the answer is yes or no, so "if" takes that place.
B Because the sentence is about the night.
C Because "if" is always used after "do you know".
What is the difference between "Where is the shuttle?" and "Could you tell me where the shuttle stops?"
A The first one is wrong English.
B They are exactly the same, only longer.
C Both are correct, but the long one gives the other person a second, and it usually gets a fuller answer.
Tomas tells her to take a picture of the address just in case. What does that mean?
A That her phone is definitely going to stop working.
B That it probably will not be necessary, but it costs nothing to be ready if something goes wrong.
C That she has to show the picture at the hotel.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions

GRAMMAR

An indirect question is a normal question with an opening in front of it. The opening is polite, and it changes what comes after it. Here is the whole map.

Short questionIndirect questionWhat changed
Where is the gate?Could you tell me where the gate is?The verb moved after the subject.
What time does it leave?Could you tell me what time it leaves?Does disappeared, and the verb took the -s.
How do I get there?Could you tell me how I get there?Do disappeared; subject before verb.
Is the hotel far?Do you know if the hotel is far?No question word, so if takes that place.
Does this bus go downtown?Do you know if this bus goes downtown?If again, and does disappeared.
Can you help me?I was wondering if you could help me.The softest frame, for a favor.

The three rules to remember: (1) After the opening, the sentence goes back to subject + verb — "where the gate is", never "where is the gate". (2) Do and does disappear, and the main verb keeps its ending: "what time it leaves". (3) A yes-or-no question needs if — "do you know if the hotel is far", never "do you know the hotel is far". And the openings that buy you the answer: Excuse me, Sorry to bother you, Could you tell me, Do you know, I was wondering if you could.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt. There is no right or wrong version. Speak for 60 seconds, with no script.

Do the rehearsal out loud, alone, the way Teresa did it in her kitchen. Four stops, one question at each one, and every question with an opening. Stop one, the gate: you are not sure it is the right one. Stop two, arrivals: you need the shuttle to the center. Stop three, the street: you want to know if you can walk to the hotel. Stop four, the front desk: you want to know what time breakfast starts. Use at least four of today's words, and finish with the sentence that saves any evening: "Sorry to bother you, I think I am lost." Nobody is going to ask these questions for you this time.
