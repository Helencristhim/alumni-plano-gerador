From Answering to Asking

Tonight You Ask

A trip never goes exactly the way the paper says. The gate changes. The shuttle stops somewhere else. The street is closed. None of that is a language problem — it is an asking problem. Tonight you rehearse the whole journey, and you learn the four openings that make a stranger stop, look up, and tell you more than you asked for.

Chapter 3: Could You Tell Me

The Question That Gets a Real Answer

Same information. Same stranger. Two very different answers.

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Where is the gate?" → "Could you tell me where the gate is?"

"What time does it leave?" → "Could you tell me what time it leaves?"

"Does this bus go downtown?" → "Do you know if this bus goes downtown?"

"Can you help me?" → "I was wondering if you could help me."

On the left, the short question. On the right, the same question with an opening in front of it. Look at the green words: where did the little verb go in the long version? And what appeared in the third one, where there is no question word?

Short questionOpeningWhat changes
Where is the gate?Could you tell me......where the gate is. The verb moves after the subject.
What time does it leave?Could you tell me......what time it leaves. The word does disappears.
Does this bus go downtown?Do you know......if this bus goes downtown. No question word, so if takes its place.
Is the hotel far?Do you know......if the hotel is far. Again if, and the verb stays after the subject.
Can you help me?I was wondering......if you could help me. The softest one, for a favor.

One rule, two halves: after the opening, the sentence goes back to subject + verb, and a yes-or-no question needs if.

The Anatomy

Four Cards, One Question

The Toolkit

Short Question, Long Question

The short one is not wrong. It is just faster than the person can be kind. The long one gives them a second, and they give you the whole answer.

With a question word

"Could you tell me where the shuttle stops?"
"Could you tell me what time the last one leaves?"

Yes or no: use "if"

"Do you know if the hotel is far?"
"Do you know if the shuttle runs on Sunday?"

And when you need a favor and not information, the softest frame of all: "I was wondering if you could write it down for me." Long on purpose — and almost nobody says no to it.

The Opening

Four Words Before the Question

You never start with the question. You start with the four words that make the other person stop and look up.

Excuse me, could you tell me where the shuttle stops?

opening + question, in that order, always

"Sorry to bother you, do you know if this is the right stop?"
"I was wondering if you could write the address down for me."

Notice what is not there: no apology for your English. You are not asking to be forgiven. You are asking for the shuttle.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Could you tell me where is the gate?"
"Could you tell me where the gate is?" — after the opening, the subject comes back before the verb.
"Do you know what time does the shuttle leave?"
"Do you know what time the shuttle leaves?" — the word "does" is gone, and the verb takes the -s.
"Do you know the hotel is far?"
"Do you know if the hotel is far?" — a yes-or-no question always needs "if".

Three little checks: did the subject come back before the verb? Did does disappear? And is there an if, when the answer is yes or no?

Expression of the Day

I Think I Am Lost

Sorry to bother you — I think I am lost. Could you tell me where I am on this map?

= I need help, I am calm about it, and I am telling you exactly what to do

This sentence does three jobs at once. It opens the door, it says what is wrong, and it gives the other person something easy to do — point at a map.
Getting lost is not a failure of English. It happens to everybody, in every language. What decides the evening is whether you ask in the first two minutes or in the first two hours.

Your turn, out loud: say it once quickly, and once slowly, the way you would say it at ten at night.

Chapter 4: The Rehearsal

The Information Desk

Where the whole evening is decided in four words

Listening

Eleven Hours Behind a Desk

Martin answers questions all day, and he knows in four words what kind of answer he is going to give. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Martin says something decides the answer he gives, and it is not your grammar. What is it?
The first four words of your question. A flat "where is the shuttle" gets five seconds and then he looks at the next person; "excuse me, could you tell me where the shuttle stops" makes him stop and look up.
2. What extra information does he give the traveler who opens the question politely?
Not only the stop, but also the time, the fact that the second stop is closer to their hotel, and that on Sunday it only runs every forty minutes.
3. What does Martin tell you to do the night before your trip, instead of studying verbs?
Learn four openings by heart — "excuse me, could you tell me", "do you know if", "sorry to bother you", "I was wondering if you could help me" — and say them in the mirror until they come out without thinking.
Listening 2

The Woman Who Rehearsed Her Kitchen

Teresa is sixty-nine, and two weeks before her first trip alone she did the entire journey out loud, to nobody. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What exactly did Teresa do in her kitchen, two weeks before her trip?
She did the whole journey out loud, in English, to nobody: the gate, then the arrivals hall, then the street, then the front desk. It took forty minutes, and she repeated it night after night until the sentences came out by themselves.
2. Three weeks later, what went wrong at the real airport, and what did she do?
At ten at night the shuttle was not where the paper said it was. She did not freeze: the sentence came out already made — "excuse me, could you tell me where the shuttle stops now" — a woman pointed around the corner, and she was at her hotel in twenty minutes.
3. According to Teresa, what is a rehearsal really for?
Not for learning the words. For putting them somewhere you can reach them with your hands shaking — the sentences have to be ready before you are scared.
Dialogue

Ten at Night, Arrivals Hall

R
Excuse me, sorry to bother you. Could you tell me where the shuttle to the center stops?
T
Of course. It stops outside door four. Go straight ahead to the end of this hall, and then it is around the corner, on your left.
R
Thank you. Do you know if it still runs at this time? It is almost ten.
T
It runs until midnight, but after nine it only comes every forty minutes. The last one tonight leaves at eleven fifty.
R
Good, I have time then. Could you tell me how long it takes to the center?
T
About twenty-five minutes at this hour. Where are you heading to?
R
The Garden Hotel, on Garden Street. Do you know if it is far from the stop? I was wondering if I could go on foot.
T
Get off at the second stop and it is four minutes on foot, all flat. Take a picture of the address just in case. And if you get lost, everybody in that square knows the hotel.
Comprehension

About Tomas

1. Where does Tomas say the shuttle stops, and how do you get there?
Outside door four: straight ahead to the end of the hall, then around the corner, on the left.
2. What does Tomas say about the times tonight?
It runs until midnight, but after nine it only comes every forty minutes, and the last one leaves at eleven fifty.
3. How long does Tomas say the ride to the center takes, and what does he ask after that?
About twenty-five minutes at that hour. Then he asks where she is heading to.
4. Tomas gives two pieces of advice that nobody asked him for. What are they?
To take a picture of the address just in case, and that if she gets lost, everybody in that square knows the hotel. He also tells her which stop to get off at, and that it is four minutes on foot, all flat.

The journey is not long. It is four questions long.
gate"Excuse me, could you tell me if this is the gate for the flight to Lisbon?"
arrivals"Could you tell me where the shuttle to the center stops?"
street"Do you know if the hotel is far? Can I go on foot?"
front desk"Good evening, I have a reservation. Could you tell me what time breakfast starts?"
People who have to translate for youNobody
1. Look at the gate line and the arrivals line. One uses "if" and the other uses "where". Why?
The gate question has no question word and the answer is yes or no, so it needs "if". The arrivals question already has "where", so "where" does that job and no "if" is needed.
2. Read the front desk line out loud. What would be wrong with "what time does breakfast start"?
Nothing, as a short question on its own. But after "could you tell me", the "does" has to go: "could you tell me what time breakfast starts".
3. Add a fifth stop to the card — a taxi, a pharmacy, a supermarket. Say your question out loud, with an opening.
For example: "Sorry to bother you, could you tell me where the nearest pharmacy is?" Or: "Excuse me, do you know if there is a supermarket around the corner?"
