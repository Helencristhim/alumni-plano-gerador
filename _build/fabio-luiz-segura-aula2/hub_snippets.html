Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Warm Engine

Day one, nine in the morning: you understand half. Day two, three in the afternoon: you understand almost everything. This lesson plans those two hours instead of losing them. Key words: opening line, conversation starter, registration desk, badge, lanyard, networking floor, session schedule, to run into somebody, to warm up, jet lag. Structure: present perfect with for and since.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An opening line -- the first sentence you use to start talking to a stranger
"My opening line is always the same: is this your first time here?"
A conversation starter -- a question or a remark that makes the other person want to answer
"What have you seen so far that was worth it? That one is a real conversation starter."
The registration desk -- the counter where you collect your name card when you arrive
"I queue at the registration desk on purpose, because that is where I warm up."
A badge -- the card with your name and your company on it that you wear
"Everybody reads your badge before they decide what to ask you."
A lanyard -- the strap around your neck that holds your name card
"I read the lanyard while I shake hands, so I never ask for the company twice."
The networking floor -- the open area where people meet between the talks
"Half of my meetings started on the networking floor, not in a room."
A session schedule -- the timetable that says which talk is where, and when
"I keep the session schedule open on my phone all day."
To run into somebody -- to meet somebody by chance, without planning it
"I ran into a counterpart from Frankfurt in the coffee queue."
To warm up -- to get ready gradually, until you are working at full speed
"My ear takes two hours to warm up, so I plan those two hours."
Jet lag -- the tired feeling after flying across several time zones
"Day one is always jet lag plus cold English. Day two is neither."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two people meet in the queue at the registration desk. One says: I attended this conference last year. The other says: I have attended this conference for eleven years. Both sentences are about the past, both are true, and they do different work. The first one closes: it names a year, and the year is over. The second one is still running — no finished time is mentioned, so next autumn it becomes twelve.

That is the rule. Use the past simple when the sentence names a finished time: last year, in 2023, on Tuesday, three weeks ago. Last year I attended the same conference. In 2023 we sent two people and booked nothing. Use the present perfect — has or have plus the past participle — when there is no finished time and the situation is still true today. And two small words do most of the work here. For answers how long: I have worked in trade finance for nine years. Since answers from when: I have led this team since 2019. A length of time takes for. A starting point takes since.

There are two traps, and a Portuguese speaker meets both. The first: naming a finished time makes the present perfect impossible, so it is never Last year I have attended. The second is more fossilised, because Portuguese uses a present tense in this exact sentence: since never goes with the present simple. Not I am in this role since 2019, but I have been in this role since 2019. Finally, remember the small words that pull the present perfect with them: just, already, yet, ever, never. I have just come from the panel. We have not launched there yet. Have you been to this conference before?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect with For and Since

GRAMMAR

For is how long. Since is from when. A named year closes the sentence.

JobFormExample
How LONG something has lastedhas / have + past participle + for + a length of timeI have worked in trade finance for nine years.
WHEN something startedhas / have + past participle + since + a starting pointI have led this team since March.
The difference in one linefor = how long · since = from whenfor six years · since two thousand and nineteen
News, with the result still herejust · already · yetI have just come from the panel. We have not launched there yet.
Experience, with no dateever · never · a number of timesHave you been here before? I have been three times.

Stage 2: Put the First Two Hours in Order

Order

This is how somebody who knows her ear needs warming up spends the first two hours. Put the steps in a logical order.

?"Arrive early, before the registration desk gets busy."
?"Join the queue instead of the fast lane, and talk to whoever is next to you."
?"Use the same opening line you always use, so you do not have to invent one."
?"Collect the badge and the lanyard, and read the next person's company from it."
?"Take a coffee on the networking floor and have two more conversations that cost nothing."
?"Only then walk into the first meeting that actually matters."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds telling a stranger who you are, using time. How long you have worked in this field, how long you have led this team, what your bank has launched recently, what you have not done yet, and what you have just come from. Finish with a question back.

Talk
Georgie Nightingall — “Talking to strangers: how to have a meaningful conversation” (TEDx)

Thirteen minutes on the exact move this lesson asked you to practise: turning a queue, a coffee line or a corridor into a real conversation with somebody you have never met. She is specific about which questions open people and which ones close them.

Tip: pick one of her questions and add it to the two opening lines you already have. Use it this week, on somebody in your own building, before you use it abroad.
