Lesson 1 · Who You Are

Who You Are, and What You Actually Do

A shareholder lands once or twice a year, walks in with a coffee, and asks what you do. You get about two minutes, and there is no second take.

Chapter 1: First Words

So, What Do You Do?

Four words, and everybody asks them. The person asking has never heard of your company, has no idea what a Brazilian farm buys in October, and is holding a coffee.

Answer it now, out loud, the way you would answer it at the coffee table. Where did you run out of words?

Today’s Goal

Two Minutes, Three Moves

1. The Words

crop protection, a grower, a dealer, to be responsible for, to deal with, a wide range of...

2. One Choice

what is true every week, and what happened once and finished

3. Your Answer

the job, the customers, how you got there — two minutes

You already understand far more than you produce. What is missing is not new grammar: it is a short set of words for your own work, and one clean decision between what you do and what you did.

One Question

And How Did You End Up There?

Nobody decides at eighteen that they want to work in credit. Tell the story in three sentences.

Then the second question, which is the one that keeps the conversation alive: what is the hardest part of the job?

Chapter 3: The Code

What You Do, and What You Did

Two jobs, two forms, one decision

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I work in credit and collections."

"My colleague handles the north region."

"I started in customer service in 2011."

"I moved into credit two years later."

Two of the red verbs describe a normal week. Two describe something that happened once and is over. Look at the blue part: it appears on one side only, and that is the clue.

JobFormExample
True every weekthe base verbI work in credit. We make crop protection products.
True every week, he / she / itbase verb + -sMy colleague handles the north. A grower pays after the harvest.
Happened once, finishedthe past form (-ed, or irregular)I started in 2011. I moved into credit. I went to Canada.
Negative, every weekdo not / does not + base verbI do not deal with exports. She does not handle the south.
Negative, finisheddid not + base verbI did not study English at university.
QuestionDo / Does ... ? · Did ... ?Do you handle the whole region? · How did you end up in credit?
The traponly ONE part of the verb carries the pastNot Where did you studied? Say Where did you study?

In one line: the base form says what is true now, the past form closes the door on something finished.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Visit, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one visit card

Dialogue

The Coffee Table, Ten Minutes Before the Tour

C
Claire Bennett. I sit on the board, and this is my first time at this site. And you are?
L
Luiz. I work in credit and collections. I am responsible for the farms and the dealers in this region.
C
Credit. So you are the one who decides whether a farm gets the product before it can pay for it.
L
That is more or less it. I deal with about two hundred customers, and they are a wide range of people. One is a family farm. The next one is a group with an office in the capital.
C
And how did you end up in credit? Nobody plans that at eighteen.
L
Nobody does. I studied business, I started in customer service, and I moved into credit in my second year here. I am currently the only analyst for this region.
C
That is a lot of ground for one person. What is the hardest part?
L
Timing. A grower pays after the harvest, so I am not looking at this month. I am looking at the whole season. My background is in numbers, but the job is mostly about people.
C
I ran a distribution business for eleven years before I joined this board, so I know that season very well. We lost a whole year once because we read one customer wrong.
L
Then you know exactly why I sleep badly in February. How long are you here for?
Comprehension

About Claire

1. Who is Claire, and what is new for her today?
She sits on the board of the company, and it is her first time at this site.
2. Claire does not repeat his job title. How does she describe his job in her own words?
She says he is the one who decides whether a farm gets the product before it can pay for it.
3. What did Claire do before she joined the board, and what does she say went wrong once?
She ran a distribution business for eleven years, and they lost a whole year once because they read one customer wrong.
Listening 1 · A Board Member

Ten Minutes Before He Walks In

A voice note he records for his own team on the way to the site. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say he does not want on this visit, and what does he want instead?
He does not want the tour, because he has already seen the warehouse. He wants ten minutes with the people who actually talk to the customers.
2. What does he say about his own Portuguese?
That it is about four words long, and that he knows exactly how unfair that is.
3. What does he say he listens to when somebody stops in the middle of a sentence?
Not the English. He listens to what the person knows. He says the people he remembers are never the ones with perfect grammar.
Listening 2 · The Same Job, in English

People Hear Credit and Picture a Bank

A credit analyst at an American crop protection company, on a company podcast. Sound first — no text.

1. Who are her customers, and why does a farm not pay every month?
Farms and the local dealers who sell to farms. A farm pays after the harvest, so she says she is lending a whole season and not thirty days.
2. She gives two very different examples of customers. What are they, and what does she say about them?
Growers who have bought from the company for thirty years, and a dealer who opened last spring. She says those two people need completely different conversations.
3. How did she get into credit, and how does she describe the job now?
She started as an assistant in customer service in 2011 and moved into credit two years later, mostly because nobody else wanted the job. She calls it the most interesting desk in the building.

Note for visitors: the customer base runs from single family farms to groups with an office in the capital. Payment follows the harvest, not the calendar month, which is the single hardest thing to explain to somebody from outside agriculture.
1. Two lines on this card talk about time in opposite ways. Which are they, and what is the difference?
"Portfolio today: about 200 customers" is true right now. "Joined the company: in customer service, moved into credit two years later" is finished and closed.
2. The card says the session lasts twenty minutes and has no slides. Why would a board visitor prefer that?
Because they can read numbers on their own. What they cannot read anywhere is what the person who talks to the customers knows, and that only comes out in conversation.
3. According to the note, what is the hardest thing to explain to somebody from outside agriculture?
That payment follows the harvest and not the calendar month.
