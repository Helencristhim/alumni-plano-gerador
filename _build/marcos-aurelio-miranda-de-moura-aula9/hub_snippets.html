Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Hold On, I Have a Question -- The Four Seconds Before You Answer

Silence feels four times longer to the person speaking than to the room. The answer is not to think faster: it is to buy the time out loud. Key words: to interrupt, to confirm, to rephrase, to hesitate, a pause, to buy time, on the spot, to lose the thread, to take a step back, to come back to somebody. Structure: past continuous for background, with past simple for the interruption.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To interrupt -- to start talking while somebody else is still speaking
"Somebody interrupted me in the middle of the second point."
To confirm -- to say clearly that something is true, so nobody has to guess
"Do not answer first. Confirm the question first."
To rephrase -- to say the same thing again, with different words
"Let me rephrase that, because I do not think I said it well."
To hesitate -- to stop for a moment because you are not sure what to say
"I hesitated for four seconds, and in my head it was an hour."
A pause -- the short silence you leave on purpose, before the important sentence
"The people who sound most senior pause before the important sentence."
To buy time -- to give yourself a few seconds before you answer, by talking
"Four seconds of talking bought me the answer."
On the spot -- with no warning and no time to prepare
"She asked me on the spot, in front of eleven people."
To lose the thread -- to forget where you were in what you were saying
"I lost the thread after the second question and never got back to it."
To take a step back -- to go back to the bigger picture before answering the detail
"Let me take a step back before I answer that, because the number alone is misleading."
To come back to somebody -- to answer later, on purpose, with a date attached
"I do not know. I will come back to you on Thursday with the number."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Here is what happened, and the way English tells it. "I was explaining the coverage number when somebody interrupted me." Two things, two different lengths. The explaining had already started and was still going on; the interruption took a second. English marks that with two different forms: was or were plus -ing for the thing that was going on, and the past simple for the thing that cut into it.

The joining words follow the same logic. When goes in front of the short one: I was presenting when she asked. While goes in front of the long one: while I was repeating the question, I remembered the answer. And if both things were going on at the same time, both take the -ing form: I was talking and they were looking at their phones. If one simply followed the other, both stay in the past simple: I hesitated and then I answered. Never say I presented when she asked — that makes it sound as if you started presenting after the question.

Now the reason this matters more than grammar usually does. When somebody asks you something on the spot, the worst thing you can do is hesitate in silence, because silence feels four times longer to you than to the room. So buy time out loud instead. Confirm the question. Rephrase it in your own words. Say that you want to take a step back. Each of those takes three or four seconds, which is usually all you needed. If the number is genuinely not in your head, say so and come back to somebody with a date. And when the question is finished, say the sentence that stops you losing the thread: coming back to where I was.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

Was plus -ing is what was already going on. The past simple is what cut into it.

JobFormExample
The background, going onwas / were + verb-ingI was explaining the number.
The thing that cut itthe past simpleSomebody interrupted me.
Joining the twowhen + short · while + longI was presenting when she asked. While I was presenting, she asked.
Two things at onceboth in was / were + -ingI was talking and they were looking at their phones.
One after the otherboth in the past simpleI hesitated and then I answered.
Negative and questionwas not / were not + -ing · Was / Were + subject + -ingI was not looking at my notes. Were you presenting?
State verbs stay simpleknow, remember, understandI remembered the answer. Not I was remembering.

Never: I presented when she interrupted me · I explaining the coverage number · when I was repeating the question (use while) · she was asking and then I was answering.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Somebody interrupts with a question you need a second to think about. You say:
A Nothing. You think in silence until you have the answer.
B "Let me make sure I have that right. You are asking whether the number includes the two new states."
C "Sorry, my English is not very good. Can you repeat?"
You want to describe what was happening when the question came. You say:
A "I was presenting the numbers when she interrupted me."
B "I presented the numbers when she interrupted me."
C "I presenting the numbers when she was interrupting me."
You genuinely do not have the figure. You say:
A "I think it is around thirty per cent, more or less."
B "I do not have it in my head and I am not going to guess. I will come back to you on Thursday."
C "Somebody on my team probably knows that."
The question is answered and you want the room back. You say:
A "Ok, so, where was I?"
B "Coming back to where I was: the second point is the discount, and it is short."
C "Any other questions before I continue?"
