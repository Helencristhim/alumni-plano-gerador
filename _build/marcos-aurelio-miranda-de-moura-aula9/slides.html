Chapter 3: The Code

I Was Explaining, and Then She Asked

The long thing, and the thing that cut it

Grammar Discovery

Which One Was the Background?

"I was explaining the coverage number when somebody asked about the two new states."

"The room was following me until that moment."

"While I was repeating the question, I remembered the answer."

"I hesitated for four seconds, and then I answered."

The red verbs describe something that was going on for a while. The blue ones happened in a second. Look at the last sentence: it has no red at all, and that changes what it means.

JobFormExample
The background, going onwas / were + verb-ingI was explaining the number.
The thing that cut itthe past simpleSomebody asked about the two states.
Joining the twowhen before the short one · while before the long oneI was presenting when she asked. While I was presenting, she asked.
Two things at onceboth in was / were + -ingI was talking and they were looking at their phones.
One after the otherboth in the past simpleI hesitated and then I answered.
Negative and questionwas not / were not + -ing · Was / Were + subject + -ingI was not looking at my notes. Were you presenting?
The trapthe background is never the past simpleNot I presented when she asked. Say I was presenting when she asked.

In one line: was plus -ing is what was already going on, and the past simple is what cut into it.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Three Questions You Did Not Expect

One conversation, three recordings, one card

Dialogue

Three Interruptions, Three Ways Back

M
Coverage is at nineteen per cent, and the second point is what we decided about the...
D
Sorry, does the nineteen include the two states we added in June?
M
Let me confirm the question. You are asking whether the nineteen per cent already includes the two new states. It does not. With them it is twenty-one.
D
And what is the cost per workshop this year against last year?
M
Let me take a step back before I answer that, because the number on its own is misleading. The cost went up, and it went up because the first visit to a new workshop is the expensive one.
D
Fine. Then give me the exact figure for the south.
M
I do not have it in my head and I am not going to guess. I will come back to you on Thursday with the exact figure.
M
Coming back to where I was: the second point is what we decided about the discount, and it is short.
Comprehension

About Diane

1. At what moment does she interrupt for the first time, and what does she want to know?
In the middle of his sentence about the second point. She wants to know whether the nineteen per cent includes the two states added in June.
2. What does she ask second, and what does she ask third?
Second, the cost per workshop this year against last year. Third, the exact figure for the south.
3. Does she ever say the answers are not good enough? What does that tell you?
No. She accepts a corrected number, a step back and a promise for Thursday. It tells you the room is testing whether he can be interrupted and still be found, not whether he knows everything.
Listening 1 · A Director

The Four Seconds That Felt Like an Hour

A director remembering a question she could not answer. Sound first — no text.

1. What was she doing when the question came, and who asked it?
She was explaining the coverage number, and somebody she did not know asked whether it included the two states they had just added.
2. How long did she hesitate, and how long did it feel?
About four seconds. In her head those four seconds were an hour.
3. What advice from a colleague saved her, and what happened while she was following it?
Do not answer, confirm the question first. While she was repeating the question, she remembered that the number did not include the states.
Listening 2 · A Podcast

Silence Is Longer for You

A business podcast on answering hard questions in front of senior people. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say about how long silence feels?
Four times longer to the person speaking than to the person listening. To the room it is a normal pause, and most of them were looking at their phones.
2. What does he say the fix is, and what is it not?
It is not to think faster. It is to buy the time out loud: repeat the question, check you understood it, or say you want to take a step back.
3. What does he say to do when you genuinely do not know, and what does he contrast it with?
Say so and give a date. He says nobody has ever been fired for saying they will come back on Thursday, and that people have been fired for inventing a number.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

What a Senior Room Actually Notices

Twenty senior executives on presenting in a second language. Sound first — no text.

1. How many of them mentioned grammar, and what did the two who mentioned accent say?
Almost none mentioned grammar. Two mentioned accent, and both said it stopped mattering after a minute.
2. What did they notice instead, and how did one of them put it?
Whether the person could be interrupted and still find their way back. One said she did not need the person to be fluent, she needed them to be findable.
3. What did several of them say about pace?
That the people who sound most senior are not the fastest. They pause before the important sentence, and the room leans in.

Every sentence on this card takes between three and five seconds to say. That is not a coincidence. It is the whole design: they are not answers, they are time, and the time is what you were missing.
1. Two lines on this card are marked in the accent colour. What do they have in common?
Both are about not answering now: one postpones the answer to a date, and the other brings the room back to where the speaker was.
2. According to the note, why does every sentence take three to five seconds?
Because they are not answers. They are time, and the time is what was missing.
3. Which line would you use for a question about a number you do not have, and why not one of the others?
The fourth one, coming back on Thursday. The others buy seconds, but if the number is genuinely not in your head, seconds do not help and a date does.

Chapter 6: Your Turn

Interrupted, Three Times

Role-Play 1 of 3 — Guided

Three Interruptions, Three Different Sentences

Scenario: Present the coverage number. You will be interrupted three times: once to check a figure, once for a comparison, and once for a number you do not have. Use a different time-buying sentence each time, and come back to where you were at the end.

Keyword chips:

Let me make sure I have that right Let me take a step back I will come back to you on Thursday Coming back to where I was
Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Now You Interrupt

Scenario: Somebody else is presenting a coverage number and you do not believe it. Interrupt twice, politely, to check what the number includes and how it compares with last year. Then let them finish.

Keywords:

Sorry, does that include...? Can I stop you there? to confirm against last year
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Three Minutes, Four Interruptions

Scenario: Present anything real from your own quarter for three minutes. You will be interrupted four times, without warning, and one of the questions will have no possible answer. Buy the time out loud every time, and find your way back to where you were.

No keywords, no notes, three minutes. One of the four questions is unanswerable on purpose.

What I Can Do Now

When a question comes on the spot, I buy time out loud instead of going silent.
I say I was presenting when she asked, not I presented when she asked.
When I do not know, I say so and I give a date. I never invent a number.
After an interruption, I say the sentence that takes the room back to where I was.
