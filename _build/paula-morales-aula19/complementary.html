Comparative & Superlative Adjectives in English: Complete Guide (engVid, with Rebecca, free)

A whole class about today's grammar, in another voice and with other examples. Rebecca writes on the board and goes straight to what slips most: when it is -er, when it is more, and why “more cheaper” does not exist. She also shows the spelling of the -er (the big → bigger, the busy → busier) slowly, with the word on screen.

Tip: watch it with English subtitles (the video's own CC). Do one thing only: every time she writes an example, pause and say ONE sentence of your own in the same mould, about the quotes on your desk (“This quote is cheaper”, “That coverage is more complete”). By the end of the video you should have said about five. Open channel, no sign-up.

Short video / audio
Comparatives and superlatives — 6 Minute Grammar (BBC Learning English, free)

Six minutes, two British presenters talking: the same rule, now through the ear. This is where you find out something the table does not tell you — in real speech the than almost disappears (it becomes a weak “than”, glued to the word before it), and that is why you do not hear the whole sentence on the Miami calls.

Tip: listen twice. The first time, just listen. The second time, repeat EVERY comparative sentence they say, copying the rhythm and keeping the than very weak. Then take the five sentences from your Survival Card and say them all with the same rhythm. Open and free, no sign-up.

Video story
Let's Learn English — Level 1, Lesson 31 (VOA Learning English, free)

It is not a grammar class: it is a story acted out, with subtitles on screen and American English spoken slowly on purpose. Anna is trying to get to a baseball game and spends the whole lesson comparing routes — which one is faster, which one is the fastest of all. The comparatives (faster) and superlatives (the fastest) turn up dozens of times in a real conversation, and not in a table. The page has the video, the transcript and exercises — all open.

Tip: watch it once only, without stopping, and do one thing: write down every word that ends in -er or -est. Then close the video and answer out loud, about your own week: which of the two quotes on your desk is cheaper, and which one is better. You do not have to understand every word; the goal is to get your mouth producing -er and than without freezing. VOA is an American public service, free and with no sign-up.
