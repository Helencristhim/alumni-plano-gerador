Lesson 19 -- Pre-class

Comparing Options -- Two Quotes, One Recommendation

In lesson 18 you told what was happening when warehouse 4 caught fire. Today the Santos client wants to renew, and there are two quotes on your desk: one is cheaper, the other is safer. Today's grammar is three letters and one word: a short adjective takes -er (cheaper, safer, bigger) and the thing compared comes after than; a long adjective takes more in front (more expensive, more complete) — and never both together. To point at one among all, the superlative comes in: the cheapest, the most complete. And only two irregulars: good → better → the best and bad → worse → the worst. You take six new words: cheap, safe, option, quote, coverage, discount. This Pre-class is a first contact — come to class with the six in your ear.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Six words that fit on the same desk: the two Santos renewal quotes. Listen to each one and repeat it out loud. The sound traps of the day: CHEEP (the I is long, otherwise it becomes chip) · SEIF (one syllable) · OP-shan (the -tion sounds like “shan”) · KA-ve-ridj (three syllables, stress on the first).

Cheap -- with a low price
"The second quote is cheap, but the coverage is small." Say CHEEP, with a long I — if it comes out short it becomes chip.
Safe -- with no danger
"Option A is safe: the plant has an alarm and two exits." Say SEIF, one syllable — and careful: the insurance you sell is insurance, another word.
Option -- one of the things you can choose
"We have two options for the renewal." Say OP-shan — the -tion sounds like “shan”, never “ti-on”.
Quote -- a price the insurer gives for a risk
"I sent the client two quotes on Monday." Say KWOUT, one syllable — the qu sounds like “kw”.
Coverage -- what the policy pays for
"Option A has complete coverage: fire, flood and transport." Say KA-ve-ridj — it is the noun of the verb cover, from lesson 4.
Discount -- money taken off the price
"The new alarm system gives the client a twelve percent discount." Say DIS-kaunt, stress on the first syllable.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the story and answer the questions. The words in bold are today's forms — notice two things: what the adjective gained (-er at the end or more in front) and the than that comes after it.

The Santos renewal, told by Paula. “After the fire in warehouse 4, the client asked for two quotes. Both arrived on Monday. Option B is cheaper than Option A — fifty thousand reais cheaper.”

“But I opened them side by side. The coverage in Option A is more complete: fire, flood and transport. Option B covers fire only. And the client moves goods by truck every day. So: Option A is safer for this operation.”

“There is more. The discount is bigger in Option A: twelve per cent for the new alarm. And the claim response time: twenty-four hours against five days. On Thursday the client will ask me for the cheaper one. And I will tell the truth: the cheapest option is not always the best one.”

1. Why is it “cheaper than” and not “more cheap than”?
A Because the sentence is about money.
B Because cheap is a short word (one syllable) — a short word keeps the comparison at the end, with -er.
C Because the comparison is between three things.
2. And why is it “more complete” and not “completer”?
A Because complete is a long word — a long word takes more in front.
B Because the word ends in e.
C Because the sentence is in the past.
3. Option B is cheaper. Why does Paula not recommend Option B?
A Because the client does not like the other insurer.
B Because Option A has more complete coverage, a bigger discount and a faster claim response — and the client moves goods every day.
C Because Option B is more expensive.
4. In “the cheapest option”, why is it not “cheaper”?
A Because cheaper only exists in questions.
B Because here she points at one among all the options, and does not compare only two — and that asks for the + -est.
C Because the word has two syllables.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- comparing two, pointing at one

GRAMMAR

Two questions settle every case: (1) am I comparing two things or pointing at one among all? (2) is the adjective short or long? Count the syllables: one syllable (cheap, safe, big) is short; three or more (ex-pen-sive) is long.

FormIn EnglishWhen you use it
Short — two thingsOption B is cheaper than Option AOption B is cheaper than Option A
Long — two thingsthis quote is more expensive than that onethis quote is more expensive than that one
Short — one among allit is the cheapest of the threeit is the cheapest of the three
Long — one among allit has the most complete coverageit has the most complete coverage
Irregular 1good → better than → the bestgood → better than → the best
Irregular 2bad → worse than → the worstruim → pior que → o pior

The spelling of -er changes a little depending on how the word ends: cheap → cheaper (just add it) · safe → safer (it already ends in e, so only the -r) · big → bigger (one vowel in the middle, double the consonant) · busy → busier (ends in -y, swap it for -ier).

The two mistakes that feel natural to make, and the fix for each one:

1) “Option B is more cheap.” — this mistake comes from putting the “more” in front, as your first language always does. In English a short word keeps the comparison at the end: “Option B is cheaper.” And never both together — more cheaper does not exist.

2) “This quote is more good.” — good is irregular: it takes neither -er nor more, it becomes another word. The right form is “This quote is better.” Same with bad → worse.

The detail that settles the sentence: the superlative always takes the in front — the cheapest, the safest, the most complete, the best. Without the the, the sentence sounds unfinished to whoever is listening.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record your answer. Speak for 60 to 90 seconds, with no script. Do not redo it more than twice. This is exactly the homework audio — start building it here.

The Santos client asks you one question: “Which option do you recommend?” Answer out loud, comparing the two quotes: price, coverage, discount and claim response time. Use at least five comparatives (cheaper, safer, bigger, more complete, better) and at least one superlative (the cheapest, the best). Close with the recommendation in one single sentence. It does not have to be perfect — it has to be yours.
