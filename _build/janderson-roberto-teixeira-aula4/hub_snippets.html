Bigger, Cheaper, Better -- Comparing Two Options

How to compare two options and defend the one you chose: better than what, for whom, and at what cost. Key expressions: a deal, value for money, convenient, reliable, overpriced, to compare, to make up your mind, a downside, roughly, to go for. Structure: comparatives and superlatives.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These ten stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

A deal -- an arrangement where you get a good price
"The second hotel was a much better deal than the first one."
Value for money -- good quality for the price you pay
"The second one is better value for money, even with the longer journey."
Convenient -- easy to reach and easy to use
"The taxi is the most convenient option, and by far the most expensive."
Reliable -- something that always works, so you can trust it
"The bus is far less reliable, because it sits in the same traffic as everybody else."
Overpriced -- more expensive than it should be
"I think the first hotel is slightly overpriced for what it offers."
To compare -- to look at two things and see the differences
"The mistake is choosing the taxi without taking one minute to compare."
To make up your mind -- to decide finally, after thinking about it
"I want to give you my honest opinion before you make up your mind."
A downside -- the bad part of something that is otherwise good
"The downside of the second one is the journey in the morning."
Roughly -- approximately, more or less, not exactly
"A taxi costs roughly four times more than a train ticket."
To go for -- to choose one option in the end
"In the end I decided to go for the cheaper one."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every traveller has the same argument at six in the morning: the train or the taxi. The taxi is the most convenient option by a long way, and it is also the most expensive. The train is cheaper, faster and more reliable than both of the others, and the only thing it asks of you is that you carry your own bag.

A taxi costs roughly four times more than a train ticket. On a quiet Sunday it is almost as fast as the train, but on a normal weekday it is far less reliable, because it sits in the same jam as everybody else. On a bad morning the most expensive option is also the slowest one, which is the worst possible combination.

Janderson had to compare two hotels last month, and the argument was the same shape. The first was closer to the office and the rooms were bigger; it was also slightly overpriced. The second was much quieter, the breakfast was far better, and it was a better deal overall. The downside was thirty minutes on a train every morning.

He took a day to make up your mind — his words again, and we will fix the pronoun in class — and in the end he decided to go for the second one, because it was better value for money. Neither hotel was better in general. One was better for him, that week, at that price.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Three shapes cover every comparison in English, and the only technical decision is the length of the adjective.

FormWhat it saysExample
Short adjective + ER + thanOne or two syllables, comparing exactly two things.cheap → "cheaper than"
big → "bigger than"
easy → "easier than"
MORE + long adjective + thanThree syllables or more. The adjective itself never changes."more convenient than"
"more reliable than"
THE + adjective + EST / THE MOSTThree or more things. The word the is obligatory."the cheapest of the three"
"the most expensive of the three"
AS + adjective + ASEqual. With not, the first one is less."as fast as the train"
"not as reliable as the train"
The three irregularsGood, bad and far refuse both rules and you simply learn them.good → better → the best
bad → worse → the worst
far → further → the furthest
Making it stronger or softerOne word in front does all the work. Never use two comparative forms together.much cheaper · far more reliable
slightly more expensive · by far the best
NOT "more cheaper"

The word after a comparison is always than — never that, and never of. That single word is the most common comparison mistake made by Portuguese speakers, and it is a one-second fix.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Somebody asks you to compare two options in English. Choose the best thing to say.

You want to say the train costs less than the taxi:
A "The train is more cheaper than the taxi."
B "The train is much cheaper than the taxi."
C "The train is cheaper that the taxi."
Three options, and one costs more than the other two:
A "It is most expensive of the three."
B "It is the most expensive of the three."
C "It is more expensive of the three."
You want to say the two are equally good:
A "The first one is as good as the second."
B "The first one is so good than the second."
C "The first one is good like the second."
The price is high for what you get. You say:
A "It is very much money for the quality delivered."
B "It is slightly overpriced for what it offers."
C "The price is more high than correct."
A client asks which option you recommend. The best answer is:
A "Both are good options with different characteristics."
B "I would go for the second one. It is better value for money, and the only downside is the wait."
C "It depends of what you prefer."
