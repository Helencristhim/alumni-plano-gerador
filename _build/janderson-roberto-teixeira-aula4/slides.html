Chapter 3: The Code

Than, The Most, As As

Three shapes, and they cover every comparison in English

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The train is cheaper than the taxi."

"The taxi is more convenient than the bus."

"The taxi is the most expensive of the three."

"On Sunday the taxi is almost as fast as the train."

Two of these compare two things. One compares three. One says they are equal. And one adjective changed its ending while another one did not. What decides which?

FormWhat it saysExample
Short adjective + ER + thanOne or two syllables. Comparing exactly two things.cheap → "cheaper than"
big → "bigger than" (double the letter)
easy → "easier than" (Y becomes I)
MORE + long adjective + thanThree syllables or more. The adjective itself never changes."more convenient than"
"more reliable than"
THE + adjective + EST / THE MOSTComparing three or more. The is obligatory."the cheapest of the three"
"the most expensive of the three"
AS + adjective + ASThey are equal. With not, the first one is less."as fast as the train"
"not as reliable as the train"
The three you must simply knowGood, bad and far refuse both rules.good → better → the best
bad → worse → the worst
far → further → the furthest

To make the comparison stronger or softer, put one word in front: much cheaper, far more reliable, slightly more expensive, by far the best.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"The train is more cheaper than the taxi."
"The train is much cheaper than the taxi."
"This hotel is more convenient that the other one."
"This hotel is more convenient than the other one."
"It is most expensive option of the three."
"It is the most expensive option of the three."

English uses one comparative form, never two: either the ending or the word more. To make it stronger, put much, far or slightly in front instead. The word after a comparison is always than, never that and never of. And a superlative always carries the. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

N

J

N

J

Comprehension

About Nina

1. What does Nina say is better about the first hotel?
It is more convenient: ten minutes from the office, and the rooms are bigger.
2. What two things does she prefer about the second hotel?
It is much quieter and the breakfast is far better.
3. Why does she think the train is not a real problem?
Because it is more reliable than the traffic, which matters at seven in the morning.
4. Does she agree that the first hotel is overpriced?
Yes -- she says "it is".
Listening

Three Ways, One Winner

A consumer radio segment. Sound first -- no text.

1. Which option is the cheapest, and roughly how much cheaper than a taxi?
The train, at about a quarter of the price of a taxi.
2. Why is the bus less reliable?
Because it sits in the same traffic as everybody else.
3. When does the report say the taxi is good value for money?
When three people are travelling with heavy luggage.
Listening 2

Before You Decide

A voice message about two hotels. Sound first -- no text.

1. How far is the first hotel from the office?
About ten minutes on foot.
2. What does she say is the downside of the second hotel?
The journey in the morning -- thirty minutes by train.
3. How does she summarise the two options in one sentence?
The first is more convenient, the second is better value for money.
