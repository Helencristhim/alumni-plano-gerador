The Strategic Narrative -- Structuring a Presentation for the Global Head

One idea, twenty minutes, and the courage to cut the rest. Key words: through-line, so what, signpost, land a point, appendix, front-load, granularity, narrative arc, tee up, segue, pay-off, the build. Structure: cleft sentences -- putting the spotlight exactly where the story needs it.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. Most of these name something you have never had a word for, in any language.

Through-line -- the single idea that runs from the first slide to the last
"The through-line is that the growth is real and unpriced."
The so what -- the consequence that makes a fact worth hearing at all
"The number is fine. What is missing from the slide is the so what."
Signpost -- a phrase that tells the room where you are going next
"Two signposts in twenty minutes is enough."
To land a point -- to make a point actually arrive, instead of merely being said
"The exposure point did not land, so I said it again with one number."
Appendix -- the material you keep at the back, for questions rather than for the story
"The market breakdown is real, but it belongs in the appendix."
To front-load -- to put the most important material at the start, not at the end
"Front-load the ask. Nobody reaches slide fourteen with attention left."
Granularity -- the level of detail you deliberately choose for this audience
"The board wants regional granularity. My team needs it broker by broker."
Narrative arc -- the shape a story takes, from tension to resolution
"Every quarterly review has the same narrative arc, and that is fine."
To tee up -- to prepare the ground so that the next point lands easily
"I tee up the exposure with the growth number, so it does not sound like bad news."
To segue -- to move smoothly from one topic into the next
"She segued from the numbers into the risk without anyone noticing the join."
Pay-off -- the moment the audience finally gets what you promised them
"The pay-off is the ask. Everything before it is the case for saying yes."
The build -- the steps that carry an audience to your conclusion
"The build is three slides: what grew, what it cost, what I need."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Count the sentences that have been split in two on purpose.

Denise had twenty-six slides and twenty minutes. The through-line came first, in one sentence: the growth is real, and it has bought a risk that has not been priced. Everything else had to serve it or move to the appendix.

The build was three steps, and each one was written as a cleft so the room could not miss where to look. What drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio. What it cost the region was capacity in the south. What it exposes us to is one bad season. Then the so what, said out loud rather than implied: the question is no longer whether the region grew. It is the exposure, not the growth, that needs a decision. The pay-off was a single ask, front-loaded onto the fourth slide instead of buried at the end.

Nine slides of market granularity went to the back. So did the broker analysis, which was the slide she was proudest of: it teed up a different presentation, and that one was for November. It was only when the deck was down to four slides that the argument became visible.

1. "What drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio." Why is this two clauses instead of one short sentence?
A Splitting it puts a spotlight on whatever follows "was" — the room knows exactly where to look.
B Because "drive" is irregular and needs the extra clause.
C Because it is a question in disguise.
2. "It is the exposure, not the growth, that needs a decision." What does the "not the growth" part add?
A Nothing — it is filler that softens the sentence.
B It names what the room was probably thinking and rules it out, which is the strongest form of the structure.
C It makes the sentence grammatically complete.
3. "It was only when the deck was down to four slides that the argument became visible." Why "it was ... that" rather than "what"?
A Because the spotlight is on a moment in time, and "what" cannot carry time or place.
B Because the sentence is in the past.
C Because "what" can only be used at the start of a paragraph.
4. The broker analysis was the slide she was proudest of. Why did it go to the appendix?
A Because it was not accurate enough for a board.
B Because it did not serve the through-line — it teed up a different presentation, for a different month.
C Because the board had already seen it last quarter.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences

GRAMMAR

Split the sentence in two, and put the spotlight exactly where the story needs it.

PatternWhat goes under the spotlightExample
What ... is / was ...whatever comes after isWhat drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio.
It is / was ... that ...whatever sits between it is and thatIt is the exposure that worries me.
With the contrast addedthe strongest version — name what it is notIt is the exposure, not the growth, that worries me.
Time or placeonly it was ... that works; what cannot do thisIt was only in September that it surfaced.
An actionWhat ... did was + base verb, with no toWhat we did was tighten the limits.
Agreement trapWhat is always singular: What drove the quarter WAS the renewals — never were, even when what follows is plural.
Compared with inversionInversion is a hammer: once per presentation. A cleft is a spotlight: use it whenever the story needs one.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

You have twenty-six slides and twenty minutes. The first thing you decide is:
A The through-line — one sentence that everything else must serve.
B Which market to start with, and then work through them in order.
C How fast you would have to speak to cover all twenty-six.
A good slide does not serve the through-line. You:
A Keep it. It is good work and somebody might appreciate it.
B Move it to the appendix, where it is still available if anyone asks.
C Delete it, so you are not tempted to use it.
You want the spotlight on the corporate portfolio. You say:
A "What drove the quarter were the corporate portfolio."
B "What drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio."
C "It was the corporate portfolio drove the quarter."
You want the spotlight on when something happened. The only structure that works is:
A "It was only in September that the problem surfaced."
B "What the problem surfaced was in September."
C "What was in September that the problem surfaced."
Someone asks for broker-level detail in the middle of a board presentation. You:
A "That is in the appendix and I am happy to go there. What matters for today is the limits decision."
B Open the appendix and go through it slide by slide.
C Say you did not bring that level of detail.
You have stated a fact and the room looks unmoved. What is most likely missing?
A More data behind the fact.
B The so what — the consequence that makes the fact worth their attention.
C A stronger tone of voice.

Documentary
Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates -- Part 3

The final part is the clearest example of a through-line in the whole series: one problem, three steps, one ask. Watch how rarely he adds a fact without immediately saying what follows from it — that is the "so what" test, applied by someone who has never heard the phrase.

Tip: pause after any statistic he gives and try to say the "so what" before he does. You will be right more often than you expect, and that is the skill.
