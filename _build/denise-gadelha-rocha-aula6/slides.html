Which One Do You Actually Build?

Deck A — organised by the data

Brazil. Mexico. Colombia. Argentina. Then claims. Then costs. Then a slide called "Conclusions" that nobody reaches.

Deck B — organised by one idea

"The growth is real, and it has bought us a risk we have not priced." Everything after that slide either supports it or goes in the appendix.

Deck A looks more rigorous. It is not — it just moves the work of finding the point from you to the room. A narrative is not the opposite of rigour; it is what makes rigour arrive.

Chapter 3: The Code

Where the Spotlight Goes

Cleft sentences, and how they carry a narrative

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"What drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio."

"It is the exposure, not the growth, that worries me."

"What I need from you today is a decision on the limits."

"It was only in September that the double counting surfaced."

Every one of these could be a single short sentence. Each has been split into two on purpose. What does the split buy you?

PatternWhat goes under the spotlightExample
What ... is / was ...whatever comes after isWhat drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio.
It is / was ... that ...whatever sits between it is and thatIt is the exposure that worries me.
Adding the contrastthe strongest version: name what it is notIt is the exposure, not the growth, that worries me.
Spotlight on time or placeuse it was ... that, never whatIt was only in September that it surfaced.
Spotlight on an actionWhat ... did was + base verbWhat we did was tighten the limits, not cut volume.
Why it carries a narrativeA cleft answers a question the room has not asked out loud yet. That is exactly what moves a story forward.
Compared with last weekInversion is a hammer: once per presentation. A cleft is a spotlight: use it as often as the story needs.

The Structure

Twenty Minutes, One Idea

The opening you built in lesson one is move one. Here is what follows it.

1 · The through-line, stated

"The growth is real, and it has bought us a risk we have not priced."

2 · The build — three steps, no more

"What drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio. What it cost us was capacity in the south. What it exposes us to is one bad season."

3 · The so what, said out loud

"So the question is not whether we grew. It is whether we can afford to grow the same way again."

4 · The pay-off — the ask

"What I need from you today is a decision on the limits. Everything else is in the appendix."

The test for every slide: does it serve the through-line? If not, it goes in the appendix. Not in the bin — in the appendix.

Cutting the Deck

S
Denise, you have twenty minutes with the board and twenty-six slides. Something has to go. Tell me the through-line first.
D
The growth is real, and it has bought us a risk we have not priced.
S
Good. That is one sentence and I can repeat it. Now, what is the build?
D
Three steps. What drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio. What it cost us was capacity in the south. And what it exposes us to is one bad season.
S
Three slides, then. What are the other twenty-three doing?
D
Nine of them are market granularity. They are good slides, and they answer questions nobody in that room is going to ask.
S
So put them in the appendix and stop mourning them. What about the broker analysis? That one I like.
D
I like it too, and it goes to the back as well. It does not serve the through-line. It tees up a different presentation, and that one is for November.
S
That is the first time I have heard you cut something you were proud of. Send me the four slides tonight.

Listening

The Test Every Slide Has to Pass

A chief of staff who rebuilds board decks for a living. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What is the test he applies to every slide?
Whether it serves the through-line. If it does not, it goes to the appendix.
2. Why does he say the appendix matters so much?
Because it lets people cut without losing anything — the work is still there, it is just no longer in the way.
3. What does he say is the hardest slide to cut?
The one you are proudest of — and it is almost always the one that belongs at the back.
4. What does he say about a deck with no through-line?
It makes the room do the work of finding the point, and a room that has to do that stops trying.
Listening 2

Fewer Words, Not Simpler Ideas

A director who presents in her third language. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What did she believe early in her career about presenting in another language?
That she had to compensate with more words, so that nobody would think the ideas were simple.
2. What did she discover was actually happening?
The extra words were burying the point, and the room read the length as uncertainty.
3. What is the "so what" test she applies to every fact?
She says the fact, then asks herself "so what", and whatever answers that is the sentence that goes on the slide.
4. What does she say about the sentences that survive?
They are shorter than the ones she would write in her own language — and they land harder.

"It is the exposure that worries me, not the growth." (the cleft needs that)
2. "What drove the quarter were the corporate portfolio."
"What drove the quarter was the corporate portfolio." (what takes a singular verb)

"Most people never reach slide fourteen."
4. "Can you explain me the through-line again?"
"Can you explain the through-line to me again?" (explain takes to before the person)

1. Why is every build step written as a cleft?
Because each one answers a question the board has not asked out loud yet, and the cleft puts the answer under the spotlight. Three clefts in a row is a story.
2. Twenty-two slides went to the appendix. What was lost?
Nothing. The work is still there and still yours — it is simply no longer standing between the board and the decision.
