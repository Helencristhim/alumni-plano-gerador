If You Move on That, We Move on This -- The Language of Deal Terms

Three things you can do when somebody puts a condition on the table, and only one of them costs nothing. Key words: an earnout, closing, contingent, a threshold, a clause, binding, a non-disclosure agreement, to walk away from. Structure: the first conditional for deal terms.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An earnout -- part of the price that is only paid if the company hits agreed targets later
"Half the price was an earnout tied to three years of revenue."
Closing -- the day the deal legally completes and the money moves
"If closing slips past June, the whole timetable changes."
Contingent -- depending on something else happening first
"The second payment is contingent on client retention."
A threshold -- the number you have to reach before something is triggered
"If revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays in full."
A clause -- one specific rule inside a contract, written separately
"There is a clause that protects the engineering team for two years."
Binding -- legally forcing both sides to do what the document says
"The term sheet is not binding, except for the confidentiality section."
A non-disclosure agreement -- the contract that stops either side repeating what it learns
"Nothing goes in the data room before the non-disclosure agreement is signed."
To walk away from -- to end the negotiation and accept that there is no deal
"Knowing what you would walk away from is what gives you room to move."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text -- tap Listen to hear each paragraph -- then answer the questions.

There are only three things you can do when somebody puts a condition on the table. You can accept it, you can refuse it, or you can trade. Accepting costs money. Refusing costs information, because the moment you say a flat no the other side stops proposing, and their proposals were the only thing telling you what they actually want. Trading costs nothing, and English has one sentence shape built for it: if you move on the threshold, we will move on the timetable.

The shape is if plus the present simple, then will plus the bare verb. Both halves are about the future, and that is exactly what makes it counterintuitive: the if half stays in the present anyway. If you accept, not if you will accept. This is the single most expensive mistake a Portuguese speaker makes in a negotiation, because Portuguese marks the future in both halves and the direct translation puts will where English will not take it. There is also a quieter version: unless, which simply means if not, and which draws a hard line while sounding like a condition. We will not sign anything unless the clause protects the team is a refusal that nobody in the room will hear as a refusal.

One shape close to it does something different. If revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays in full keeps both halves in the present, and that is not an offer at all — it is a rule that fires by itself, the way a contract clause does. Use it for what is already agreed, never for what you are still negotiating. And the permanent target does not go away just because there is an if in front of it: after if, he, she and it still take the -s. Not if the buyer accept. If the buyer accepts.

The same three moves happen again on paper, and on paper they have their own vocabulary. A payment that arrives only if something else happens first is contingent on it. A document that legally forces both sides to do what it says is binding, and most of a term sheet is not binding at all, apart from the confidentiality section and the non-disclosure agreement both sides have signed weeks before anything else. Closing is the day the deal legally completes and the money moves, and every condition in the document points at that date. If a payment is contingent on a number, the number decides. If it is contingent on a person, you have handed that person the deal.

And there is one condition you write for yourself, before the first meeting. Decide what you will walk away from, and write it down. If you have not decided that, every condition they propose sounds reasonable, because you have nothing to measure it against. If revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays in full is a rule you have already accepted; if you move on the threshold, we will move on the timetable is still an offer. The founders who negotiate well are not the ones who talk the most. They are the ones who know, before the room fills up, which line they will not cross.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The First Conditional for Deal Terms

GRAMMAR

The condition stays in the present. Only the result gets a future.

JobFormExample
An offer, a trade, a condition you proposeIf + present simple, will + bare verbIf you move on price, we will move on timing.
A risk you are flaggingsame shape, result is the consequenceIf closing slips, the timetable will change.
An automatic rule, already agreedIf + present, + presentIf revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays.
A line you will not crossunless = if notWe will not sign unless the clause protects the team.
Softer, more formalwould in the resultIf you moved on it, we would consider it.
The trapwill never follows ifNot if you will accept. Say if you accept.
The permanent targetafter if, he/she/it still takes -sNot if the buyer accept. Say if the buyer accepts.

Never: If you will accept · unless the clause does not protect · if the buyer accept · if closing will slip

Stage 2: Put the Counter-Offer in Order

Order

The buyer has just proposed three conditions. Put your answer in a logical order. Listen first, then put the lines in order.

?"Thank you -- let me take the three conditions one at a time."
?"The closing date works for us, so that one is agreed."
?"On the threshold: if you move from twenty to fourteen, we will accept the three-year period."
?"We will not tie any payment to four named engineers, whatever the price."
?"If you need retention, we will fund a pool for the whole team instead."
?"And before we go further, I should tell you what I would walk away from."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. A buyer has just put three conditions on the table. Accept one, trade the second, and refuse the third by naming what would have to change. Finish by saying what you would walk away from.
