Lesson 20 -- Pre-class

What the Alumni Say -- Who, Which, Whose and Where

Every candidate says he has spoken to alumni. The strong ones can say who, what that person said, and what changed in their plan because of it. Key words: an alumnus, a testimonial, a peer, to network, a sounding board, firsthand, eye-opening, to live up to something, a return on investment, to pay off, a bond, a two-way street. Structure: defining and non-defining relative clauses -- who, which, that, whose and where, the commas, and leaving out the object pronoun.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An alumnus -- a former student of a school or a program; the plural is alumni
"An alumnus from my future class runs a port in Chile."
A testimonial -- a statement about how good something is, written by somebody who used it
"The testimonials on the website are written to sell."
A peer -- a person at the same level as you, in age, job or experience
"For the first time in years, I was in a room full of peers."
To network -- to meet and talk to people who can help you in your career
"I did not go to the residency to network, but I met my best client there."
A sounding board -- a person you test your ideas on before you decide
"For eighteen months he was my sounding board, and I was his."
Firsthand -- coming from your own experience, not from somebody else
"I wanted a firsthand answer, not a brochure."
Eye-opening -- surprising, and teaching you something you did not know
"The case about the family company was eye-opening."
To live up to something -- to be as good as people expected or promised
"The program lived up to what I was promised, but not in the way I expected."
A return on investment -- the benefit you get back compared with the money or time you put in
"I measure the return on investment in people, not in salary."
To pay off -- to bring good results later, after you have spent time, money or effort
"The eighteen months paid off, but not in the way I had planned."
A bond -- a close connection between people who have shared something important
"The residencies created a bond that fifteen years have not broken."
A two-way street -- a situation where both sides have to give, not only take
"A network is a two-way street: you have to give first."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When the panel asks about alumni, you will need to say who somebody is without making three sentences. English does it with relative clauses, and there are two kinds. The first kind tells the listener which one you mean, and it has no commas: the alumni who give first get the most from the network. Without the clause, the sentence loses its meaning. You can use who or that for people, which or that for things, and when the pronoun is the object you can leave it out completely: the peers I met in the residency are now clients.

The second kind only adds extra information, and it goes between commas: the program, which I started at forty-four, was eye-opening. Take it out, and you still know which program. In this kind, that is never possible. For possession, English uses whose, for people and for organizations: a learning team whose members came from six countries. For a place, where: the company where the founder refused to step back.

Two small traps for Brazilian speakers. Do not repeat the object: "the peers who I met them" is wrong. And do not add there after where. In speech, the commas are short pauses; say them, and the listener hears which information is extra.

1. According to the text, when does a relative clause have no commas?
A When it only adds extra information.
B When it tells the listener which one you mean.
C When it uses whose.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "The program, that I started at forty-four, was eye-opening."
B "The program, which I started at forty-four, was eye-opening."
C "The program, who I started at forty-four, was eye-opening."
3. What does the text say about the pronoun in "the peers I met in the residency"?
A It can be left out because it is the object.
B It must always be who.
C It can be left out in every relative clause.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

No commas = which one. Commas = extra information. After a comma, never that.

ToolFormExample
Definingwho / which / that, no commasThe alumni who give first get the most.
Non-definingwho / which, between commasThe program, which I started at forty-four, was eye-opening.
whosepossessiona team whose members came from six countries
wherea placethe company where the founder refused to step back
No pronoundefining clause, pronoun is the objectThe peers I met are now clients.

Never: the program, that I started · an alumna which company builds hospitals · the peers who I met them · the city where I want to study there
