Chapter 3: The Code

When English Turns Itself Around

Put the negative first and the sentence changes shape

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Not only did the new reading survive scrutiny, but it also held up better than the old one."

"Rarely does a consensus collapse all at once."

"No sooner had the archives opened than the arguments began."

"Little did anyone realize how long the dispute had been running."

Four sentences, and in every one the subject has moved. What comes first in each — and what happens to the verb immediately after it?

FormWhat it doesExample
Not only ... but alsoFronts one claim and announces a bigger one behind it. The clause after not only takes question word order."Not only did it survive, but it also held up better."
Rarely · Seldom · Never
· Nowhere		A restrictive adverbial at the front. English then borrows the auxiliary from a question — does, did, has."Rarely does a consensus collapse at once."
No sooner ... than
Hardly / Scarcely ... when		Two events, the second arriving almost on top of the first. No sooner takes than; hardly and scarcely take when."No sooner had it opened than the arguments began."
Little did + subjectRetrospective irony: nobody knew at the time, and the speaker knows now. Almost always about knowing or realizing."Little did anyone realize what it meant."
At no point · Under no
circumstances · Not until		The most formal of the group. Common in argument, in law and in writing that wants no ambiguity at all."At no point was the decision put to a vote."

Inversion is not decoration and it is not old-fashioned. It is how English signals that a claim is about to carry weight — and in a discussion, the person who can use it is heard differently from the person who cannot.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Not only the account survived, but it held up better."
"Not only did the account survive, but it also held up better."
"Rarely a consensus collapses all at once."
"Rarely does a consensus collapse all at once."
"No sooner the archives opened when the arguments began."
"No sooner had the archives opened than the arguments began."

The negative adverbial at the front is a promise: what follows will be question word order, not statement word order. Portuguese has no equivalent obligation, which is why the subject slips back into first place. And keep the pairs straight: no sooner ... than, hardly ... when.

The Historian at the Next Table

R
You read the column? Half my colleagues wrote to complain about it, and not one of them had read past the second paragraph.
H
I read it twice. What struck me was the honesty about the memoirs — the old version did not hold up to scrutiny and everybody had been quoting it for years.
R
That is the part they object to. Nobody minds a new document. What they mind is being told that the old story was received wisdom rather than research.
H
Which it usually is, at that distance. Although I would not go so far as to say the whole account was wrong — only that the tidiness was overstated.
R
No sooner do you say that in a seminar than somebody accuses you of relativism. As if admitting a version is provisional meant admitting it is worthless.
H
In medicine we live with that permanently. The guidance changes and people hear it as an admission that the earlier guidance was a lie.
R
Exactly the same argument, different building. So what would change your mind about the current reading?
H
A document that casts doubt on the timeline. Not an opinion — a date that does not fit.

Listening 1

What the Archive Actually Held

A lecture, three minutes in. Sound first — no text.

1. How many boxes were opened, and over how long?
Four hundred and twelve boxes, over nineteen months.
2. What did the memorandum from 1979 show?
That the argument had been running ten years before anyone in the street noticed.
3. Why does she say the old account survived so long?
Because it was elegant, and nobody had a reason to test it.
Listening 2

Two Historians, One Paragraph

A radio review. Sound first — no text.

1. What does the reviewer accept about the book?
That the research is serious and the archive work is not in dispute.
2. What is her objection?
That the author treats one memorandum as decisive when three others point the other way.
3. What does she say she is not accusing the author of?
Of dishonesty — she says he is simply too fond of his own finding.
