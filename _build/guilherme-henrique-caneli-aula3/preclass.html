Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To convene -- to bring people together for a formal meeting
"GRI convenes people who rarely sit at the same table."
To coalesce around -- to come together and agree on one idea or position
"By the end of the session, the panel had coalesced around one proposal."
An inflection point -- a moment when a trend changes direction
"This auction is an inflection point for rail in the region."
Agenda-setting -- deciding which questions a discussion will focus on
"The first five minutes of a panel are agenda-setting."
A framing device -- a story, image or question used to shape how the audience sees a topic
"The empty highway was the framing device for the whole opening."
A rhetorical pivot -- a planned turn in a speech from one idea to another
"But that is not the whole story was the rhetorical pivot of her talk."
A keynote -- the main speech that opens or defines an event
"The minister’s keynote set expectations too high."
A fireside chat -- an informal on-stage interview between a moderator and one guest
"The CEO preferred a fireside chat to a formal presentation."
To open the floor -- to invite questions or comments from the audience
"After two questions from me, I will open the floor."
A housekeeping note -- practical information for the audience, such as timing, exits or microphones
"One housekeeping note: the session ends at six sharp."
A show of hands -- asking the audience to raise their hands to answer a question
"A quick show of hands: who here has invested in Brazil before?"
To set the tone -- to establish the mood or style of an event from the start
"The first sentence sets the tone for the whole summit."
A curtain-raiser -- a short event or remark that comes before the main one
"The dinner was a curtain-raiser for the roundtable the next morning."
To hand over to -- to give the stage or the discussion to another person
"With that, let me hand over to our first panelist."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Speakers who open conferences for a living use one structure that most people only ever meet in writing. It is called inversion, and it works like a spotlight: it takes a limiting word, puts it at the front of the sentence, and forces the room to wait for the rest.

Compare “Capital has not been this cautious since 2008” with “Not since 2008 has capital been this cautious.” The facts are the same. The second version marks an inflection point. Notice what happens after the limiting phrase: the auxiliary comes before the subject, exactly as in a question. “Rarely do we see every stakeholder at the same table.” “Only by fixing the contracts can we move the money.”

If the verb has no auxiliary, English lends it one: do, does or did. “Not only did the auction close, but it closed early.” Forgetting the auxiliary is the most common mistake: “Rarely we see” sounds like a sentence that stopped halfway.

The last rule is about taste, not grammar. One inversion sets the tone. Two in a row sound like a sermon. Use it once, in the sentence you want remembered, pause after it, and move on to a show of hands, the rhetorical pivot or the moment you hand over to the first panelist.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Inversion for Emphasis

GRAMMAR

Limiting word first, then the auxiliary, then the subject -- and only once per opening.

JobFormExample
A long gap in timeNot since + time + auxiliary + subjectNot since 2008 has capital been this cautious.
One conditionOnly by + -ing + auxiliary + subjectOnly by working together can we close the gap.
How unusualRarely / Seldom + do + subject + verbRarely do we see every stakeholder together.
A first timeNever before + have + subject + participleNever before have so many funds asked.
Two facts at onceNot only + did + subject + verb, butNot only did the auction close, but it closed early.
