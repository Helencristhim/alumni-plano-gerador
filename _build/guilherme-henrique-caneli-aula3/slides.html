The Target

Thirty Seconds That Decide the Room

1. Skip what the screen already says: your name, the agenda, the sponsors.

2. Give the room one idea, and make the key sentence impossible to ignore.

3. Plan the turn and the handover before you walk on stage.

Diagnostic

Open the Summit Now

The producer gives you the microphone: “Doors open in two minutes. Give me your first thirty seconds, exactly as you would say them.”

Do it now, with no preparation. Then say what you would change.

Chapter 3: The Code

The Sentence That Makes the Room Sit Up

Word order you almost never use, and exactly when to use it

Grammar Discovery

What Happened to the Word Order?

"Not since 2008 has infrastructure capital been this cautious."

"Only by aligning the regulator and the investor can we unlock long-term money."

"Rarely do we see every stakeholder at the same table."

"Never before have so many sovereign funds asked about the region."

Say each one in normal word order first. What moved, and what came before the subject? Why would a speaker choose the harder version in the first minute of an opening, and why only once?

JobFormExample
Stress a long gap in timeNot since + time + auxiliary + subjectNot since 2008 has capital been this cautious.
Stress a single conditionOnly by + -ing + auxiliary + subjectOnly by working together can we close the gap.
Stress how unusual it isRarely / Seldom + do/does + subject + verbRarely do we see all stakeholders together.
Stress a first timeNever before + have + subject + past participleNever before have so many funds asked.
Stress two facts at onceNot only + auxiliary + subject + verb, butNot only did the auction close, but it closed early.
Use it onceone inversion per openingTwo in a row sounds like a sermon, not a summit.
The trapnever skip the auxiliaryNot Rarely we see. Say Rarely do we see.

In one line: Limiting word first, then the auxiliary, then the subject -- once, exactly where the room needs to sit up.

Chapter 4: The Rehearsal

Twenty Minutes Before the Doors Open

A rehearsal, two recordings and the speaker brief

Dialogue

The Rehearsal

D
Guilherme, we have twenty minutes before the doors open. Run the first thirty seconds for me, exactly as you will say them.
G
Good morning. My name is Guilherme Caneli, and it is a pleasure to welcome you to the summit on behalf of the whole team...
D
Stop. Your name is already on the screen behind you. What is the one idea you want them to walk out with?
G
That this is an inflection point. Not since the last decade has so much capital been looking at the region.
D
Better. Say it again, and pause after the word region. Now, how do you set the tone without sounding like a keynote?
G
I use a framing device: a show of hands. Who here has signed a thirty-year contract in Latin America? Then I say that rarely do we have the people who signed it and the people who regulate it in one room.
D
I like it. And the rhetorical pivot? You cannot stay optimistic for four minutes.
G
The pivot is the rules. Only by fixing the contracts can we move the money that has been waiting for years.
D
Good. Keep one housekeeping note for the very end, and do not open the floor until after the second question.
G
Agreed. Then I hand over to the first panelist, and we begin.
Comprehension

About Dana

1. How long do they have before the doors open, and what does Dana ask him to do?
Twenty minutes. She asks him to run the first thirty seconds exactly as he will say them.
2. Why does Dana stop him, and what does she want instead?
Because his name is already on the screen. She wants the one idea the audience should walk out with.
3. What does she tell him to do after the word region?
Pause.
4. What are her two instructions at the end?
Keep one housekeeping note for the very end, and do not open the floor until after the second question.
Listening 1 · A Speaking Coach

The Thirty Seconds Nobody Plans

A coach on why most conference openings fail. Sound first — no text.

1. According to the coach, what do most speakers do in the first thirty seconds?
They thank the organizers and sponsors, read the agenda and say their own name, which is already on the screen.
2. Why does he say the first thirty seconds are special?
It is the only moment when every person in the hall is listening at the same time.
3. What two other moments should a speaker plan, and when should the sponsors be thanked?
The turn from good news to the hard question, and the handover. The sponsors should be thanked at the end.
Listening 2 · The Summit

Keep Your Hands Up

A moderator opens a morning session. Sound first — no text.

1. What does the moderator refuse to do, and why?
She refuses to read the agenda, because the audience already has it in their hands.
2. What is the show of hands about, and what does she ask people to do next?
Who has waited more than a year for a regulator to publish a date. Then to keep their hands up if the delay changed their investment decision.
3. What three things will the session cover, and why does it end at eleven?
Contracts, currency and courage. It ends at eleven because the minister has a flight to catch.
