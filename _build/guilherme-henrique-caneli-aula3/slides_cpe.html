The Text

Paragraphs One and Two

Why the Room Still Matters

The prediction was confident and it was wrong. Once a video call could carry a face and a slide deck at no cost, the argument ran, the industry that flies four thousand people to a hotel for three days would quietly die.1 The circuit is larger than it was, the tickets cost more, and the waiting lists are longer.

The explanation people offer first is networking, which is true and shallow.2 A video call is a transaction with an agenda and an end time. A corridor is a place where somebody says the thing they would never put in writing, and where you discover, in four seconds of hesitation, that the deal everybody assumes is closed is not closed.

Adapted from a comment essay on the conference economy · ~600 words · gaps 1 and 2
The Text

Paragraphs Three and Four

Why the Room Still Matters

The second function is harder to admit. A conference is a machine for producing agreement. When an organisation decides to convene four hundred people, and then decides who will deliver the keynote, who will sit for the fireside chat and who will not be invited back, it is doing agenda-setting whether or not it would use the word.3

That is why the opening carries so much weight. A curtain-raiser is not decoration. It is the moment the organisers’ private argument becomes the room’s public one, and a chair who understands the job will set the tone by choosing the hardest version of the question rather than the most agreeable one. A framing device — a date, a number, a single contrast — does most of the work in the first minute, and a rhetorical pivot somewhere around the third moves the room off the comfortable half of the argument.4 The rest of the programme then either answers that question or visibly avoids it, and both are informative.

Adapted from a comment essay on the conference economy · ~600 words · gaps 3 and 4
The Text

Paragraphs Five and Six

Why the Room Still Matters

None of this is free of theatre. A show of hands is a device for manufacturing the appearance of consensus, and an experienced audience knows it. A housekeeping note can be used to eat the two minutes a difficult speaker was going to use.5 The instruments are neutral; what is not neutral is whose hand is on them.

What has genuinely changed is the cost of being wrong. When the sector could coalesce around a view slowly, over years, a bad view was survivable.6 A position formed in a hotel ballroom in March is priced into the market by June, and the people who formed it are under no obligation to revisit it.

Adapted from a comment essay on the conference economy · ~600 words · gaps 5 and 6

Why D is the distractor
D is true, it is on topic, and it would sit comfortably in a conversation about this article. What it never does is answer a reference or complete an argument. That is the whole test: a gapped text is not asking what is true, it is asking what is cohesive.

The trap in (a) and (d)
Both are TRUE statements about corridors. They are still wrong, because the question asks what the sentence is for, not what it contains. In Part 5, at least one option in every item is true and irrelevant.

Why this matters today
The writer ends on what the room EXPOSES, not on what it decides. That is the bridge into the grammar: an inversion does the same job in one sentence -- it puts the thing you want noticed where it cannot be skipped.

Collaborative Task

Who Is in the Room

Four minutes, together

Collaborative task — four minutes. Your organisation has been asked to convene a closed-door session of twenty people on the region’s pipeline. Together, decide: who is in the room, who is deliberately left out, and what single sentence the session exists to test. You have to agree on the sentence before the four minutes are up.

Long Turn

Two Minutes, Uninterrupted

No notes. No apology at the start.

Long turn — two minutes, uninterrupted. You are opening a session at a regional infrastructure forum. Answer this: “What is this sector no longer allowed to say out loud?” Open with a framing device, pivot once, and land on a question the room can disagree with. No notes, and no apology at the start.

Follow-up

Four Questions, No Restarting

Four examiner questions, straight after the two minutes. No restarting, and no preparation between them.
1You opened with a number. Why that number and not the obvious one?
2Say your central claim again, this time with an inversion, and tell me what the inversion bought you.
3Somebody in the room has just told you the opposite is true. Concede one thing to them, out loud, and then hold your position.
4If you had thirty seconds instead of two minutes, which sentence survives?
Debate One

Defend the Side You Disagree With

This house believes that industry conferences are a mechanism for manufacturing consensus, not for testing it.
1Ninety seconds each, alternating. No notes.
2You choose your side, and you have to open with the strongest argument against yourself.
3One inversion per turn, at least. If it sounds forced, it was.
Debate Two

The Side You Were Given

This house believes that a chair who has an opinion should state it in the opening, rather than pretending to neutrality.
1The teacher gives you the side. You do not choose it.
2Before you answer, restate the strongest point the other side has made, in your own words, so that they accept your version of it.
3Only then do you respond. A turn that skips the concession does not count.
