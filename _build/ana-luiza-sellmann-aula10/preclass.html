Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

The Place Nobody Tells You About -- Saying Exactly Which One You Mean

Describing places, people and projects with enough precision that nobody has to ask "which one?". Key words: a hidden gem, a regular, packed, a go-to, down-to-earth, to swear by, a venue, an insight. Structure: relative clauses with who, which, that, where and whose, and what changes when you add two commas.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. Do not translate — the example sentence is the meaning.

A hidden gem -- a very good place that not many people know about
"The bar on the corner is a hidden gem."
A regular -- a person who goes to the same place very often
"The regulars stand at the counter and read the paper."
Packed -- completely full of people, with no space left
"On Saturdays the room is packed."
A go-to -- the one you always choose first, without thinking
"That restaurant is my go-to when I cannot decide."
Down-to-earth -- practical and easy to talk to, never showing off
"She is very down-to-earth and treats everyone the same."
To swear by -- to believe something is so good that you always recommend it
"Everybody in my building swears by that bar."
A venue -- the place where an event or an activation happens
"The venue we booked for November is an old factory."
An insight -- a deep understanding about people that changes what you do
"The insight was that people stay longer in a space that has a story."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are four bakeries on my street, so I always have to explain which one I mean. The one which opens at six is on the corner, and the woman whose name is on the sign still makes the coffee herself. The people who work there have been behind that counter for twenty years. On Saturdays it is packed, and the regulars who stand at the counter are not moving for anybody. Two streets away there is a restaurant where nobody takes a reservation, and that is my go-to when I cannot decide. It is the kind of place that people walk past for years without noticing, which is exactly why the food is still good.

At work the same five words do all the pointing. The venue that we booked for November is an old factory in Barra Funda. The person who signs everything off is called Renata, and she is the only one who can approve a change to the layout. The team that did the Adidas activation already knows the building. And the insight from the last event, which is the part the client actually cares about, was that people stay much longer in a space that has a story. Notice the two commas around that last one: they mean the information is extra news, not the thing that tells you which insight I mean.

1. Why does the writer say "the one which opens at six"?
A Because there is only one bakery on the street.
B Because there are four, and this information says which one.
C Because bakeries always take "which" in English.
2. In "the woman whose name is on the sign", what does "whose" tell you?
A That the writer does not know her name.
B That the sign belongs to the bakery.
C That the name on the sign is hers.
3. What do the two commas around "which is the part the client actually cares about" do?
A They mark extra news you could remove without changing which insight it is.
B They tell you which of several insights the writer means.
C They are there because the sentence is long.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Say Which One, In the Same Sentence

GRAMMAR

Name the thing, then add the information that says which one. Five small words do all the work.

WordPoints atExample
whoa personThe client who approved the brief is in London.
whicha thingA venue which has a story.
thata person or a thing, only when the information says which oneThe team that did the activation.
wherea placeA restaurant where nobody takes a reservation.
whosesomething belonging to somebodyThe woman whose name is on the sign.
Commas change the meaningNo commas = which one: "My colleagues who speak English joined the call" (only some of them). Two commas = extra news: "My colleagues, who speak English, joined the call" (all of them). Never use that between commas.
Dropping it in speechWhen a subject follows, you can leave the word out: "The concept (that) we pitched was approved." Never drop it when it is followed by a verb: "The client who approved it" stays.

Two short sentences ("There is a restaurant. Nobody takes a reservation there.") become one, and you stop losing the thread between them.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are describing places, people and projects. Choose the best thing to say.

There are four bakeries on the street and you want to recommend one of them. You say:
A "Go to the one which opens at six, on the corner."
B "Go to the one who opens at six, on the corner."
C "Go to the one where it opens at six there, on the corner."
You are telling a recruiter who approved your last campaign. You say:
A "The client which approved the concept is in London."
B "The client who approved the concept is in London."
C "The client whose approved the concept is in London."
Everybody on your team joined the call, and you want to add that they all speak English. You say:
A "My colleagues who speak English joined the call."
B "My colleagues, who speak English, joined the call."
C "My colleagues, that speak English, joined the call."
You want to say that the name on the bakery sign belongs to the woman behind the counter. You say:
A "The woman that her name is on the sign."
B "The woman which name is on the sign."
C "The woman whose name is on the sign."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is correcting this one.

A creative recruiter in London asks you to talk her through your work. Speak for about two minutes. Describe two activations from your career: the brand, the venue where each one happened, the people who made the decisions, and the insight that came out of it. Every project needs at least one sentence that says which one you mean, with who, which, that, where or whose. If a sentence breaks in the middle, say "let me start that again" out loud and keep going.
