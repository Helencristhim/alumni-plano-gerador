If This Reform Passes -- Conditional Thinking in Tax Advisory Meetings

Presenting a tax impact analysis with three scenarios: what will happen if the reform passes, and what would happen if it went further. Key words: tax bracket, tax incidence, deferred tax, tax shield, regulatory scenario, penalty clause, tax credit offset, reform rollout. Expressions: run a scenario, flag a risk, trigger a liability, play it safe, worst-case scenario, rule out. Structures: first conditional (real and likely) contrasted with second conditional (hypothetical), plus unless.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Tax bracket -- a range of income that is taxed at one specific rate
"The new law creates a fourth tax bracket for high earners."
Tax incidence -- who really carries the cost of a tax in the end
"The tax incidence falls on the customer, not on the bank."
Deferred tax -- tax that a company will pay in a later period, not now
"The holding recognized a deferred tax of four million."
Tax shield -- a legal item that reduces the tax a company pays
"Interest on the loan works as a tax shield."
Regulatory scenario -- one possible version of the future rules
"We modeled three regulatory scenarios for the client."
Penalty clause -- the part of a contract that sets the fine if a rule is broken
"The contract has a penalty clause for late filing."
Tax credit offset -- using a credit you already have to reduce the tax you owe
"A tax credit offset would cut the final bill by half."
Reform rollout -- the gradual process of putting a reform into practice
"The reform rollout starts in January and ends in 2033."
Run a scenario -- to test one possible future in your numbers
"Let us run a scenario with the higher rate."
Flag a risk -- to make everyone see a risk before it becomes a problem
"I would flag a risk in the second column."
Trigger a liability -- to make a legal obligation to pay start to exist
"That transfer would trigger a liability in Brazil."
Rule out -- to decide that something is not possible and stop considering it
"I would not rule out a delay in the rollout."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Leonardo opens the call with three regulatory scenarios. In the base case, if the reform rollout starts in January, the top tax bracket for insurance products will fall by two points, and the group will recognize a smaller deferred tax. In the second scenario, if the vote slips to the following year, the tax credit offset will arrive too late for this cycle. He also flags a risk: if the company files late under the new rules, the penalty clause will trigger a liability of up to four hundred thousand reais. Then he moves to the scenario nobody wants. If the reform were approved in a much broader form, the tax incidence would move to the final customer, the interest on the loan would stop working as a tax shield, and the whole pricing structure would have to be rebuilt. He does not consider it likely, but he would not rule it out. Unless the board decides otherwise, his recommendation is simple: run the numbers again in September.

1. The text says "if the rollout starts in January, the tax bracket will fall". Why is there no "will" after "if"?
A Because the sentence is about the past.
B Because the condition itself takes the present simple, and only the result takes will.
C Because "start" has no future form.
2. What does the writer suggest by using "if the reform were approved... the incidence would move"?
A That this future is a hypothesis, not something he expects.
B That the reform was already approved last year.
C That the client must act on it this quarter.
3. In the second scenario, what happens to the tax credit offset?
A It is cancelled by the penalty clause.
B It arrives too late to help this cycle.
C It doubles in value.
4. "Unless the board decides otherwise, his recommendation is simple." What does "unless" mean here?
A Because the board decided.
B If the board does not decide otherwise.
C After the board decides.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- First and Second Conditional

GRAMMAR

Ask yourself one question: is this future on the table? If yes, use the first conditional. If it is only a hypothesis, use the second.

FormUseExample
First conditional
If + present simple, will + base verb		A real and likely future. The client can plan around it.If the rollout starts in January, the rate will fall by two points.
Second conditional
If + past simple, would + base verb		A hypothesis: unlikely, or imagined only for analysis.If the incidence moved to the customer, we would rebuild the pricing.
NegativeThe negative can sit in either half, in either conditional.If we do not file on time, the client will pay a fine. / If it were my money, I would not wait.
QuestionThe result half becomes the question.What will happen if the vote slips? / What would you do if you were me?
Never after "if"The condition never takes will or would: "If the reform passes", not "if the reform will pass"; "If I were you", not "if I would be you".
were, not wasIn the second conditional, formal English uses were for every person: If I were advising a foreign bank. If it were only a delay.
unlessunless = if not, and it never takes a second negative: Unless the board approves it, we will not file this quarter.

Quiz
