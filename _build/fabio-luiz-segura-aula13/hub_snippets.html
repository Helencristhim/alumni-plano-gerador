Handling Pushback

Twelve months, one corridor, and how to say no without closing the door. Key words: to push back on, counterargument, assumption, viable, scope, constraint, concession, deal-breaker, counterproposal, to hold your ground. Structure: modal verbs for hedged disagreement and modal perfects -- softer, not weaker.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To push back on -- to disagree with an idea or a request, politely but clearly, and not accept it as it is
"I would push back on the timeline. The rest of the proposal works for us."
A counterargument -- a reason you give against the other side's argument
"Their argument was the size of the bank. My counterargument was the size of the first corridor."
An assumption -- something a person believes is true without checking it
"Their whole timeline rests on one assumption: that every bank needs twelve months."
Viable -- able to work in practice, not just on paper
"It is a beautiful plan. It is not viable with our compliance calendar."
The scope -- exactly what a project includes, and what it does not
"The problem is not the timeline. The problem is the scope: eleven corridors in one phase."
A constraint -- a limit you cannot change, like a rule, a date or a budget
"Our real constraint is the ninety-day compliance review. Nobody can shorten it."
A concession -- something you give up or accept so that the other side can say yes
"We can make a concession on the price if they make one on the scope."
A deal-breaker -- the one condition that ends the negotiation if it is not met
"The price is negotiable. Settlement outside our regulator's rules is a deal-breaker."
A counterproposal -- a different offer you make after you reject the first one
"Rejecting their plan is easy. The counterproposal is what keeps them at the table."
To hold your ground -- to keep your position when the other side pushes, without becoming aggressive
"He pushed three times. I held my ground and kept smiling."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two weeks after a conference, the vendors come back, and most of them come back with the same plan they showed you at the booth. Twelve months, eleven corridors, their settlement model. Saying yes is easy and saying no is easy. The skill is the third option: rejecting the plan while keeping the relationship, and in English that skill lives in a handful of small words.

Listen to how an experienced negotiator says no. That might not be viable for a first corridor. We would need to see the compliance review before we commit. I am not sure that would scale in our market. We could start with one corridor instead. Not one of those sentences is a yes. The modal verb does not make the position weaker; it takes the accusation out of the sentence, so the other side can move without losing face.

The same is true about the past. When something went wrong, the tempting sentence is you should have told us. It is grammatical and it is accurate, and it puts a person on the defensive. The professional version removes the person: this could have been tested at the proof-of-concept stage, or this should have been flagged earlier. The form never changes: modal, then have, then the past participle. Not could been, not could had.

And then there is the part that is not grammar at all. A good pushback comes with a constraint the other side can see, a number, and a counterproposal. Without the counterproposal, you have only said no, and the vendor will send you the same plan next month with a new cover page.

1. According to the text, what does the modal verb do to a sentence that says no?
A It makes the position weaker, so the other side wins.
B It removes the accusation, so the other side can move without losing face.
C It changes the no into a yes.
2. Why does the text prefer "this could have been tested earlier" to "you should have told us"?
A Because "you should have told us" is grammatically wrong.
B Because it is about the future, not the past.
C Because it keeps the criticism but takes the person out of the sentence.
3. What does a good pushback come with, according to the last paragraph?
A A visible constraint, a number, and a counterproposal.
B A strong tone and a clear no.
C A new cover page for the vendor's plan.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Verbs for Hedged Disagreement

GRAMMAR

The modal does not change your position. It removes the accusation.

What you want to doFormExample
Doubt a claimmight / may not + verbThat might not be viable for a first corridor.
Set a conditionwould need to / would have to + verbWe would need to see the numbers first.
Question a resultI am not sure that + would + verbI am not sure that would scale here.
Offer an alternativecould + verbWe could start with one corridor.
Criticize the past, softlycould have / should have + past participleThis could have been tested earlier.
Question formCould we...? / Would it be possible to...?Could we agree on one corridor first?
Negative formmodal + not + verb (never to)It might not be approved this year.

Never: This could been tested · We should had asked · That might not to be viable · You should have told us (it accuses a person)

Stage 2: Put the Email in Order

Order

The vendor sent the same twelve-month plan again. Put your reply in a logical order.

?"Thank you for the call. I take your point that twelve months is common for a full rollout."
?"For a first phase, however, twelve months might not be viable for us."
?"Our compliance review alone takes ninety days, and nobody can shorten it."
?"Our counterproposal: one corridor, conventional settlement, four months."
?"In return, you would get a reference case and a decision date in writing."
?"Could we agree on a thirty-minute call next week to confirm the scope?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A vendor says twelve months is standard for a bank of your size. You say:
A You are wrong. Twelve months is impossible.
B I take your point, but twelve months might not be viable for a first corridor.
C OK, if that is standard, we accept it.
A problem appeared that nobody tested during the proof of concept. You say:
A This could been tested before.
B You should have told us this, it is your fault.
C This could have been tested at the proof-of-concept stage. Let's test it in phase two.
The vendor offers a discount if you sign a three-year exclusive contract, and exclusivity is impossible for you. You say:
A That is a fair offer. However, exclusivity is a deal-breaker for us. We could consider a longer first phase instead.
B We would need to accept the exclusivity, because the discount is good.
C No. We never sign exclusive contracts. Next question.
The vendor accepts your four-month counterproposal and asks what they get in return. You say:
A Nothing, it is a good deal for you already.
B A reference case with our name on it, and a decision date in writing.
C We might to give you a reference case later.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Think of a real vendor or partner proposal you received this year that did not work as it was. Record ninety seconds as if you were answering that vendor now: acknowledge their point, push back with at least two modal verbs, name your real constraint, and finish with a counterproposal that includes a number.

Talk
William Ury — “The walk from ‘no’ to ‘yes’” (TED, 19 min)

The co-author of Getting to Yes explains why the hardest person to negotiate with is usually yourself, and how stepping back from the table — he calls it going to the balcony — lets you hold your ground without escalating.

Tip: every time he describes a moment when somebody pushed back, write down the exact sentence the person used. Count how many of them contain might, would or could.

Podcast
Think Fast, Talk Smart (Stanford GSB) — “Navigating the Nuance: The Art of Disagreeing Without Conflict” with Julia Minson

A Harvard researcher on conflict explains her H.E.A.R. framework, and the first letter is exactly this lesson: Hedging. The other three — emphasizing agreement, acknowledging the other side, reframing — are what Fabio did in the dialogue before every counterproposal.

Tip: listen once for the four letters. Then listen again and copy two hedged sentences she uses word for word — they go straight into your next vendor email.

Julia Dhar — “How to disagree productively and find common ground” (TED on YouTube, 11 min)

A world debating champion and business consultant on three habits that make disagreement useful at work: start from common ground, separate the idea from the person, and be open to being persuaded. She speaks fast, with an Australian accent — good cold listening.

Tip: watch once without subtitles and write the three habits. Then match each one to a line from the dialogue in class — which sentence of Fabio's used which habit?
