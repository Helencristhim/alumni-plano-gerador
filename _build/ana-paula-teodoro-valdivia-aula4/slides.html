Chapter 3: The Code

Will It, or Would It?

Two conditionals, two different messages

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If we sign this as it is, we will lose the cap."

"If the delay passes the threshold, the penalty may apply."

"If the counterparty went bankrupt, we would carry the whole loss."

"If the local law changed, we would have to renegotiate."

Two are open risks, two are remote scenarios. What changes in the verbs?

MessageFormExample
Real risk
(open, avoidable)		if + present, then will / may / couldIf we sign this as it is, we will lose the cap.
Remote scenario
(hypothetical)		if + past, then wouldIf the counterparty went bankrupt, we would carry the loss.
Why it mattersThe tense is the probability. Before any adjective, the manager already knows whether you mean this is going to happen or this could happen in theory.
Three trapsNever "if we will sign" — after if, the present. Never "if the law would change" — after if, the past. And never use would for a risk that is on the table today: it sounds like theory, and nobody acts on theory.

The question that saves you: can this still be avoided this week, or is it a scenario?

Practice

The Habit

The Tense Is the Probability

Before you open a risk, decide which of the two it is. The grammar carries the answer.

Likely → first conditional

if + present · will / may / could
"If we sign this as it is, we will lose the cap."

Remote → second conditional

if + past · would
"If the counterparty went bankrupt, we would carry the loss."

Then add the other two pieces: what we lose, and what you recommend. Likelihood, impact, mitigation — and the first one is already in the verb.

Chapter 4: The Risk Call

Can I Sign on Friday?

Two risks, and they are not the same size

Dialogue

Which One Holds the Signature

D
Ana, quick one. Can I sign the master agreement on Friday?
A
Two risks, and they are not the same size. The first one is real: the liability cap is gone from this draft. If we sign it as it is, we will carry the whole downside.
D
And the second one?
A
The second one is remote. If the local law changed next year, we would have to renegotiate the whole agreement. Low likelihood, high impact.
D
So which one stops me signing on Friday?
A
Only the first. The missing cap is a red flag today; the law is a worst-case scenario. My mitigation is one sentence: put the cap back at twelve months of fees, and you sign on Friday.
D
Good. Send me that sentence, and the fallback if they refuse it, and I will take it to the board.
Comprehension

About David

1. What does David want to do on Friday?
Sign the master agreement.
2. What is the only thing he needs before he signs?
To know which of the two risks should stop the signature.
3. What does he ask for at the end?
The mitigation sentence and the fallback if the other side refuses it, so he can take it to the board.
Listening

The Answer He Cannot Use

A risk manager on what he needs instead. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Which answer can he not use, and why?
"It depends." He still has to sign or not sign on Friday, so it tells him nothing.
2. What are the three things he needs, in order?
How likely it is, what we lose if it happens, and what he should do about it.
3. How long does he give you?
Thirty seconds — three sentences.
4. Which lawyer does he prefer, and why?
The one who commits and may be wrong, over the one who is technically safe and says nothing he can act on. The second protects herself; the first protects the company.
Listening 2

When Everything Is Urgent, Nothing Is

A lawyer on why nobody read her memos. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What did she do for about two years?
She marked everything as high risk — every clause, every draft, every memo.
2. Why did she do it?
She thought she was being careful, and she was protecting herself: nobody could say she had not warned them.
3. What did the manager tell her?
That he had stopped reading her memos, because they all said the same thing.
4. What are her two new habits?
She ranks three risks and says which one should stop the signature; and she keeps "if... would" for what is genuinely remote, so "if... will" gets its weight back.
