Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

The Question You Did Not Expect

A conference in London, an open question, and thirty seconds to answer it. Key expressions: in-house counsel, to draft, compliance, a keynote, a panel, a takeaway, to strike, to touch on, to unpack, a counterpart, off the top of my head, hands-on, to be based in, a follow-up. Structure: present perfect vs past simple — for, since, ever, just, been and gone.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These fourteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

In-house counsel -- a lawyer employed by the company itself, not by a law firm
"Our in-house counsel reviews every contract before it is signed."
To draft -- to write the first version of a document
"I need two days to draft the first version."
Compliance -- the work of making sure a company follows the rules that apply to it
"Compliance is not a department; it is a habit."
A keynote -- the main talk of an event, the one that sets the theme
"The keynote opens the conference at half past nine."
A panel -- a small group of speakers who discuss a topic in front of an audience
"She is on the panel after lunch."
A takeaway -- the one useful point you keep from a talk or a meeting
"My one takeaway from the morning was about cross-border rules."
To strike -- to make a strong impression on somebody
"What struck me was how practical the second speaker was."
To touch on -- to mention a subject briefly, without going into detail
"She only had four minutes, so she could touch on the subject and no more."
To unpack -- to break a complicated idea into parts so that it can be understood
"Could you unpack that for somebody who is not a lawyer?"
A counterpart -- the person who holds the same position as you in another company or country
"My counterpart in Chicago handles the same contracts."
Off the top of my head -- from memory, without checking, right now
"Off the top of my head, I would say about thirty people were there."
Hands-on -- involving direct practical work rather than theory
"The workshop was very hands-on: we drafted a clause together."
To be based in -- to have your main office or home in a particular place
"Our head office is based in Sao Paulo."
A follow-up -- a second contact or meeting that continues the first one
"I will send a follow-up email tomorrow morning."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Last June I went to my first international conference. I had a badge with my name on it, a lanyard, and no idea what to do with either. I have been in the legal team of a media company since 2023, and by then I had drafted a fair number of contracts in English — with a lot of help from a machine, which is a separate problem. So I could follow the sessions. What I could not do was talk about them.

The keynote that morning was excellent and far too short. The speaker touched on cross-border compliance for about four minutes and moved on, and four minutes was exactly the part of my job that nobody ever explains. My one takeaway from the whole day was that question: how do you build a rulebook that works in two countries at once. If somebody had unpacked that on stage, I would have paid for the ticket twice.

Then, in the coffee break, a man I had never met turned to me and asked what had struck me most in the talks. It is not a difficult question. It is not even a legal question. And I went completely blank — not because I did not have an answer, but because I had never practised giving one. I said something about the venue. He said something polite. That was the end of it.

I have thought about that thirty seconds many times since then. The lesson was not about vocabulary. Anybody who can draft a contract has the words. The lesson was that an open question needs a shape, decided in advance: one thing, why it stayed with you, what changes on Monday. Thirty seconds. I have never been caught by that question again, and I have not forgotten the name of the man who asked it, either — he is now a counterpart of mine in London, and we have worked on two deals together.

1. "I have been in the legal team since 2023." What does the present perfect tell you here?
A That he joined in 2023 and is still there now.
B That he worked there in 2023 and then left.
C That he will join the team in 2023.
2. Why does he write "I went to my first international conference" and not "I have gone"?
A Because the conference was abroad.
B Because the sentence names a finished time — last June.
C Because he did not enjoy it.
3. What does he say the real problem was?
A That he did not have the vocabulary.
B That the keynote was too technical.
C That he had never practised giving an answer to an open question.
4. What shape does he recommend for an open question?
A One thing, why it stayed with you, and what changes on Monday.
B A summary of everything you heard during the day.
C A polite comment about the venue.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

Portuguese has one past where English has two. That is the whole difficulty: the choice is not a translation, it is a decision about the timeline. One question settles almost every case — does the sentence name a finished time?

FormWhat it doesExample
have / has
+ past participle		Something that started in the past and is still true now, or an experience with no time attached. The moment is irrelevant — the result is not."I have worked here since 2023."
"I have drafted contracts in English."
past simpleA finished action at a finished time. If the sentence says when — yesterday, last year, in June, two hours ago — it is past simple, always."I drafted it last year."
"The keynote finished twenty minutes ago."
for · sinceFor + a length of time. Since + the point where it started. Both belong to the present perfect. "I work here since 2023" is the single commonest error a Portuguese speaker makes in English."for two years"
"since March 2023"
just · already
yet · ever · never		Recent, earlier than expected, still expected, or at any point in a life. Ever and never are the words of the experience question — and they cannot be built with did."We have just opened an office."
"Have you ever worked with an American client?"
been x goneOne letter apart, opposite meanings. Been = went and came back. Gone = went and is still there."She has been to London." (she is back)
"She has gone to London." (she is there)

One habit worth building this week: every time you say since or for about your own work, stop and check that the verb in front of it is have or has. Nine times out of ten the sentence is about you, it is true right now, and the present perfect is the only tense that says so.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at an international event and somebody has just started talking to you. Choose the version that works.

Somebody asks how long you have been in the legal team. You started in 2023 and you are still there:
A "I am in the legal team since 2023."
B "I have been in the legal team since 2023."
C "I was in the legal team since 2023."
You want to ask about somebody's experience, at any point in their life:
A "Have you ever worked with an American client?"
B "Did you ever work with an American client?"
C "Are you ever working with an American client?"
You do not know the exact number and you do not want to invent one:
A "In the top of my head, six or seven."
B "Off the top of my head, six or seven."
C "For the top of my head, six or seven."
A speaker mentioned your subject for two minutes and then moved on. You want to say that:
A "She unpacked it for two minutes."
B "She only touched on it, unfortunately."
C "She based it in two minutes."
You are asked what struck you most and you genuinely have nothing ready:
A "Nothing, sorry. It was fine."
B "I am still thinking about the second session, actually. What struck you?"
C "I did not understand very well the questions."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Somebody you have just met at an event asks you: "So — what has struck you most today?" Answer in about thirty seconds, using the three moves: name one single thing, say why it stayed with you, and say what you will do differently on Monday because of it. Then turn the question back to them.
