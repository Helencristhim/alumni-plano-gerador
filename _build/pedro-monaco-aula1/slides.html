Chapter 3: The Code

Finished, or Still Open?

Present perfect and past simple — and why Portuguese offers only one of them

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I have worked in compliance for two years."

"I drafted my first contract in English last year."

"Have you been to London before?"

"We have just opened an office in the United States."

Two of these sentences are closed and finished. Two are still open. Which are which, and what in the sentence tells you?

FormWhat it doesExample
have / has
+ past participle		Something that started in the past and is still true now, or an experience with no time attached."I have worked here since 2023."
"I have drafted contracts in English."
past simpleA finished action at a finished time. If the sentence says WHEN, it is past simple."I drafted it last year."
"I went to London in June."
for · sinceFor + how long. Since + the starting point. Both go with the present perfect, never with the past simple."for two years"
"since March"
just · already
yet · ever · never		Recent, expected or unspecified. Ever and never are how you ask and answer about experience."We have just opened an office."
"Have you ever worked with an American client?"
been x goneBeen = went and came back. Gone = went and is still there. One letter apart, opposite meanings."She has been to London." (she is here)
"She has gone to London." (she is there)

Portuguese has one past where English has two, so the choice is not a translation — it is a decision you make about the timeline. The test is quick: does the sentence name a finished time? If yes, past simple. If no, present perfect.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I work in this company since 2023."
"I have worked in this company since 2023."
"I have drafted my first contract in English last year."
"I drafted my first contract in English last year."
"Did you ever work with an American client?"
"Have you ever worked with an American client?"

The first two are the same mistake seen from both sides: since pulls the present perfect, and last year pulls the past simple. The third is the experience question — ever asks whether it has happened at any point in your life, and that question can only be built with have.

Chapter 4: The Coffee Break

Twenty Minutes Between Two Panels

Claire is in-house counsel at an energy group in London

Dialogue

So, What Has Struck You?

C
You have come a long way for a two-day conference. What brought you over?
P
My company is opening an office in the United States, so I have spent six months drafting contracts in English. I wanted to hear people who do it for a living.
C
You are in-house, then. So am I — I have been in-house counsel at an energy group for four years, and I still miss the firm on the quiet days.
P
There are not many quiet days on my side. Compliance, contracts, and whatever the board decides is urgent that week.
C
That sounds familiar. So — what has struck you most so far?
P
Honestly, the keynote. She touched on cross-border compliance for about four minutes and I wanted the whole hour on that.
C
She always does that. If you want it unpacked, the panel after lunch is the one to go to — my counterpart in Chicago is on it.
P
Then I will be there. And I will find you afterwards, if that is all right — I would like to hear how you got from the firm to in-house.
Comprehension

About Claire

1. Where does Claire work, and for how long?
She has been in-house counsel at an energy group for four years.
2. What does she say she misses?
The law firm — on the quiet days.
3. What does she say about the keynote speaker?
That she always touches on a subject briefly and moves on, instead of going into it.
4. What does she recommend, and why?
The panel after lunch, because her counterpart in Chicago is on it.
Listening 1

Before We Start

The chair opens day two of the forum. Sound first — no text.

1. What has changed about the keynote, and why?
It has moved to the main hall at half past nine, because yesterday's room was far too small.
2. What does she say about the panel on compliance in Latin America?
It has already filled up, so anybody who has not registered must do it at the desk.
3. What does she suggest people use the delegate list for?
To find and meet their counterpart in another country — it shows everybody's employer and city.
Listening 2

Thirty Seconds, Three Moves

A senior lawyer on the hardest question at any conference. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say is the hardest question at a conference?
"So, what did you think?" — and it is not a legal question at all.
2. What are the three moves he recommends?
Say what the one thing was, why it stayed with you, and what you would do differently because of it.
3. What does he say to do when you genuinely have nothing?
Be honest, name the session you are still thinking about, and ask what struck them.
