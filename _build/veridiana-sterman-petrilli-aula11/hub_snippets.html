"I can fit you in at twelve thirty."

"Most of my Tuesday patients come in for a check-up."
A day off -- a day when you do not go to work
"Thursday is my day off this month."
To catch up on -- to do the things you did not have time to do before
"I catch up on my emails while the girls are swimming."
These days -- at the present time, compared with how things were before
"These days I am working through lunch."
To take over -- to start doing a job that somebody else was doing before
"I am taking over my assistant's calls while she is away."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When somebody asks "How is your week?", the shortest answer is one word: busy. It is true, and it ends the conversation. The answer that keeps the conversation going has two layers, and English gives each layer its own form of the verb.

The first layer is the routine, the things that happen every week. For this, English uses the present simple: "I see patients from eight to twelve." "The girls finish school at four." "Every Tuesday we go to the club." Timetables use it too: "The practice opens at eight."

The second layer is the exception, the things that are true only now, this week or these days. For this, English uses am, is or are plus -ing: "This week I am working through lunch." "These days my mother is staying with us." "Right now the girls are doing their homework." The small words around the verb help you choose: usually, every Tuesday and always go with the simple form; now, this week and these days go with -ing.

One trap is very Brazilian. In Portuguese, the -ing form can sound natural for something you do every week, so it slips into the routine: every Tuesday I am going. In English, a routine is never -ing. The second trap is a small group of verbs that describe a state, not an action: need, want, know, like, mean. They stay simple even right now: "I need a day off", never I am needing.

1. Which form does English use for a routine, like your normal Tuesday?
A Am, is or are plus -ing, because the routine is happening around the time you speak.
B Either one, because both forms mean exactly the same thing in English.
C The present simple: I see, they finish.
2. Which sentence describes something that is true only this week?
A "I see patients from eight to twelve, from Monday to Friday, all year."
B "This week I am working through lunch."
C "The practice opens at eight and closes at six, like every other week."
3. Why is "I am needing a day off" wrong?
A Because need describes a state, and state verbs stay simple.
B Because a day off is a routine, and routines always take the simple form in English.
C Because need is an irregular verb, so it cannot take the -ing ending at all.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Always, or Right Now?

GRAMMAR

Always true takes the simple form. True for now takes am, is or are plus -ing.

JobFormExample
Routine and habitspresent simpleI usually see patients from eight to twelve.
Fixed timetablespresent simpleThe practice opens at eight.
At this momentam / is / are + -ingRight now they are doing their homework.
Only for nowam / is / are + -ingThese days my mother is staying with us.
Negative and questiondon't / doesn't · am not / isn't / aren'tI don't work on Thursdays. Are you working today?
State verbsneed, want, know, like, meanI need a day off.

Never: every Tuesday I am going · wait, I talk on the phone · I am needing · I am knowing

Quiz

Your patient is waiting and you are fifteen minutes behind. You say:
A "I am late every day because I always have too many patients in the morning."

An American mom at the school asks who takes your girls to school. You say:
A "These days my mother is staying with us, so she drops them off."
B "These days my mother stays with us, so every morning she is dropping them off at the gate."
C "My mother is dropping off them."

A "Today is impossible, my schedule is full of patients all day, so please call another time."
B "My schedule is full, but I can fit you in at twelve thirty."
