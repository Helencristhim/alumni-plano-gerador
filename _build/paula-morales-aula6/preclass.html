Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

What Time Is It? -- My Week, My Schedule

In lesson 5 you learned to ask where. In this lesson you learn to ask when — and to book a meeting in English. Six words from your calendar (schedule, morning, afternoon, appointment, deadline, busy) and the same three little words from lesson 5 (at, on, in) doing another job: now they say time. At the end, the most useful sentence of the day: “I'm busy on Monday. Are you free on Tuesday?” — a no with an open door. This Pre-class is only a first contact: come to class with the ladder at → on → in in your ear, not on the tip of your tongue.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Six words — and all of them already live in your calendar. Listen to each one and repeat it out loud, alone. Two sound traps: schedule is said SKED-jul (with a K, in American English) and busy is said BIZ-y — it is written with a U and nobody says the U, exactly like business, a word you have been saying for years.

Schedule -- the plan of your week, hour by hour
"My schedule is full on Monday." Careful with the false friend: your calendar in English is a schedule. The word agenda does exist in English, but it is the list of topics for a meeting.
Morning -- the part of the day before noon
"I have a call in the morning."
Afternoon -- the part of the day after noon
"The broker visit is in the afternoon."
Appointment -- a meeting with a fixed time
"I have an appointment at three." With ONE person and a fixed time: doctor, dentist, client.
Deadline -- the last day to finish something
"The deadline is on Friday."
Busy -- with no free time
"I'm busy on Monday. Are you free on Tuesday?"

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions. The words in bold are the grammar of this lesson — look at what comes after each at, on and in.

Sexta-feira. O RH da Zurich manda a agenda da primeira semana da Paula.

"Welcome, Paula. Here is your first week. You start on Monday, at nine. The welcome meeting is at nine thirty, in the meeting room. Sofia is busy in the morning — she reads the reports — so she comes at two, in the afternoon."

"On Tuesday you visit a broker with her. You leave at eight. And your first deadline: the report goes to Sofia on Friday, at five. In November we travel to Miami — but that is far. Friday is close."

Paula reads it twice: nine thirty, Monday, November. (Notice: English uses three different words for the three sizes of time — at nine, on Monday, in November. The one that catches everybody is the day: it is on Monday, never in Monday. That single swap is the whole lesson.)

1. Why does it say "at nine thirty" and not "on nine thirty"?
A Because the meeting is important.
B Because nine thirty is a clock time — and a clock time always asks for at.
C Because the sentence is plural.
2. Why does Sofia only arrive at two?
A Because she lives far from the office.
B Because she is busy in the morning — she reads the reports. She comes in the afternoon.
C Because the welcome meeting is at two.
3. It says "on Friday" and "in November". Why two different prepositions?
A Because Friday is a day (it asks for on) and November is a month (it asks for in).
B Because one is already past and the other is not.
C Because on and in are the same — it makes no difference.
4. When is the deadline for Paula's first report?
A In November, before the Miami trip.
B On Friday, at five — "on Friday, at five".
C On Monday, at nine.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Prepositions of time: at (clock time), on (day), in (month)

GRAMMAR

Good news: you already know these three words. In lesson 5 they said where (“on the second floor”, “in the meeting room”, “at the reception”). Today they say when — the same three, with another job. This is not new material: it is material reused.

Think of a ladder. at is the smallest piece of time there is — a point on the clock. on is a whole day. in is the biggest of all — a month, a year, a whole morning. The bigger the piece of time, the more open the word: at → on → in.

The most common mistake here: saying “in Monday”. A day of the week is always on — on Monday. Keep only this: two of the three follow your instinct, and one does not. The one that does not is the day.

The clock, with no drama: at work, Americans read the time as a number. 9:30 is “nine thirty”. 10:15 is “ten fifteen”. You will hear “half past nine” and “a quarter past ten” around you, but you do not have to produce them — only recognize them. And there is no “seventeen hours”: after midday, go back to 1 and add p.m. — 17:00 is five p.m., 14:00 is two p.m.

Why this matters: “I have a meeting” books nothing. “I have a meeting at nine thirty, on Monday” books it. The prepositions are what turn a loose sentence into an appointment — and appointments are what you will negotiate every day at Zurich.

Speaking

And at, on and in come out weak, almost swallowed — the stress of the sentence falls on the hour and the day, not on the preposition.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each real situation — your first week at Zurich, the colleague who wants a slot in your calendar, the broker who calls.

A colleague asks: "Are you free on Monday?" Your Monday is full. You answer:
A "Sorry, sorry... Monday, yes, OK." (saying yes when you cannot)
B "I'm busy on Monday. Are you free on Tuesday?"
C "I'm busy in Monday. Are you free in Tuesday?"
The meeting is at 9:30. The right sentence is:
A "The meeting is in nine thirty."
B "The meeting is at nine thirty."
C "The meeting is on nine thirty."
You want to say that you travel to Miami in November. You say:
A "We travel to Miami in November."
B "We travel to Miami on November."
C "We travel to Miami at November."
The broker asks: "Is the deadline on Saturday?" The deadline is on Friday, at five. You answer:
A "No. Friday five."
B "No, it isn't. The deadline is on Friday, at five."
C "No, it isn't. The deadline is in Friday, on five."
It is 17:00 and you are booking a call with an American colleague. You say:
A "Can we talk at seventeen hours?"
B "Can we talk at five p.m.?"
C "Can we talk in five p.m.?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record your answer. Speak for 60 seconds, with no script. Do not redo it more than twice. This is exactly the exercise you will do in class — and the homework audio afterwards.

Describe your REAL working week in English: what you have on Monday, what time it starts, when you are busy, when you are free, and what your next deadline is. Say five sentences, and each one needs an at, an on or an in. For example: "On Monday I have a meeting at ten. I'm busy in the morning. On Wednesday I'm free. The deadline is on Friday." It does not have to be perfect — if you swap on for in, keep going and carry on speaking. If you get stuck, use the rescue kit from lesson 1.
