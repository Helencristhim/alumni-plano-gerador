Chapter 3: The Code

How Much More?

Grading a comparison instead of just making one

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Rent is far higher there than the salary suggests."

"A ten per cent pay rise buys you slightly less than it sounds."

"Food is nowhere near as expensive as housing."

"There are far fewer flats under that price than there were."

All four sentences compare two things. Three of them also tell you HOW BIG the difference is, and one of them tells you it is small. Which words are doing that work?

FormWhat it doesExample
far · much · a lot · considerablyA BIG difference. These go in front of the comparative, and very never does — that is the single commonest error a Portuguese speaker makes here."far higher" · "much more expensive"
not "very more expensive"
slightly · a bit · a little · marginallyA SMALL difference. Same position, opposite message. Useful when you want to be accurate rather than dramatic."slightly less" · "a bit further out"
not nearly as · nowhere near asA big difference stated the other way round. Nowhere near as is stronger and belongs to speech; not nearly as works everywhere."Food is nowhere near as expensive as housing."
fewer · lessFewer for things you can count, less for things you cannot. And both take far, much or slightly in front of them."far fewer flats"
"far less space"
the ... the ...Two comparatives locked together: change one and the other changes with it. This is the structure the whole reading is built on."The further out you live, the longer you commute."

In Portuguese the same little word grades the plain adjective AND the comparative, so the wrong version arrives whole and at full speed. English splits them: very goes with the plain adjective, and far, much and a lot go with the comparative. Nothing else about this is difficult, and almost everybody gets it wrong under pressure.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Rent in London is very more expensive than in Sao Paulo."
"Rent in London is far more expensive than in Sao Paulo."
"There are far less flats under that price than there were."
"There are far fewer flats under that price than there were."
"The cost of living is more high than the salary suggests."
"The cost of living is much higher than the salary suggests."

The first is the Portuguese pattern arriving whole: very belongs to the plain adjective, and the comparative takes far, much or a lot. The second is countable against uncountable — flats can be counted, so it is fewer. The third builds the comparative with more on a short adjective that already has its own form: higher, never more high.

Listening 1

The Number on the Offer

A Brazilian lawyer on her first six months in London. Sound first — no text.

1. What had she converted, and what had she not?
She had converted the salary, but nothing else — not the tax, not the deposit, not the cost of living.
2. What two things happened to the money before she saw it?
Tax and national insurance took a much larger bite than expected, and then the deposit was six weeks of rent plus the first month in advance.
3. What is her advice to somebody moving?
Work out the disposable income first and ignore the headline salary entirely, because the headline never touches your life.
Listening 2

Space Against Time

A recruiter on the trade-off everybody gets wrong. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say the decisive conversation is about?
Not salary — the trade-off between space and time.
2. What is the difference between twenty and forty minutes further out?
Twenty minutes buys slightly more room and is nothing; forty minutes is nearly seven hours a week, at both ends of the day.
3. What is his advice for the first year?
Take the smaller flat closer in, find out where your life actually happens, and move once you know.
