Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen appear in the text you will read in class, and every one of them is a word you would need in the first week abroad.

Cost of living -- the money you need for the basics where you live
"The cost of living there is far higher than the salary suggests."
A deposit -- money paid up front and returned to you at the end
"The deposit was six weeks of rent, paid before we even saw the keys."
A landlord -- the person who owns the property you rent
"Our landlord wanted three months in advance, which is not unusual there."
To commute -- to travel regularly between home and work
"People commute ninety minutes each way and call it normal."
Take-home pay -- what is left of a salary once tax has been taken off
"His take-home pay was a good deal smaller than he had expected."
Utilities -- the gas, water and electricity you pay for separately
"Utilities add another two hundred a month, and nobody mentions them."
To be priced out -- to be unable to afford to stay somewhere any more
"Whole neighbourhoods have been priced out in a single decade."
A tenancy -- the agreement and the period for which you rent a home
"A twelve-month tenancy is standard, and breaking it early is expensive."
Overheads -- the fixed costs that have to be paid whatever happens
"Our overheads doubled and the revenue did not move at all."
To scrape by -- to survive financially with almost nothing left over
"On that salary you scrape by, and nobody tells you so in advance."
Disposable income -- the money left once the essentials have been paid
"Disposable income there is lower than in Sao Paulo, which surprises people."
A pay rise -- an increase in salary
"A pay rise of ten per cent buys you slightly less than it sounds."
To be worth it -- to justify the cost or the effort involved
"Most people say it is worth it, and most of them are not paying for a family."
A trade-off -- something you accept losing in order to gain something else
"The trade-off is space: you get a city and you lose a room."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Saying that something is more expensive is almost no information at all. The useful sentence says HOW MUCH more, and English does that with a small set of words that sit in front of the comparative. Far, much, a lot and considerably mean the difference is big. Slightly, a bit and a little mean it is small. Rent in London is far higher than in Sao Paulo; a ten per cent pay rise buys you slightly less than it sounds.

The word that does not belong there is very. In Portuguese one single word grades both the plain adjective and the comparative, so very more expensive comes out of a Brazilian mouth at speed and sounds completely wrong to an English ear. Very expensive is fine. Far more expensive is fine. Very more expensive does not exist in English at all.

The same grading works in the negative. Not nearly as and nowhere near as say the difference is big, from the other direction: food is nowhere near as expensive as housing. Note what follows: as ... as, never as ... than. And when you are counting things rather than measuring them, the word changes: far fewer flats, far less space. That distinction is one of the clearest signals of a C1 writer in a TOEFL essay.

Finally there is the structure the whole reading is built on: the further out you live, the longer you commute. Two comparatives locked together, each pulling the other. It is worth practising out loud because it is short, it sounds fluent, and it lets you say a whole trade-off in one sentence — which is exactly what an examiner and a colleague both want from you.

1. Which set of words tells you the difference is BIG?
A slightly, a bit, a little
B far, much, a lot, considerably
C very, really, so
2. Why does the text say "very more expensive" comes out of a Brazilian mouth?
A Because Portuguese has no comparative form.
B Because one single Portuguese word grades the plain adjective and the comparative alike.
C Because very is a formal word in English.
3. Which is correct?
A "Food is nowhere near as expensive than housing."
B "Food is nowhere near so expensive than housing."
C "Food is nowhere near as expensive as housing."
4. When do you use "fewer" rather than "less"?
A When the thing can be counted.
B When the difference is small.
C When the sentence is negative.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Grading a Comparison

GRAMMAR

Making a comparison is B2. Grading it — saying how big the difference is — is where B2 starts sounding like C1, and it is one of the things a TOEFL examiner listens for. The words are few and the positions are fixed.

FormWhat it doesExample
far · much · a lot · considerablyA BIG difference. These go in front of the comparative, and very never does — that is the single commonest error a Portuguese speaker makes here."far higher" · "much more expensive"
not "very more expensive"
slightly · a bit · a little · marginallyA SMALL difference. Same position, opposite message. Useful when you want to be accurate rather than dramatic."slightly less" · "a bit further out"
not nearly as · nowhere near asA big difference stated the other way round. Nowhere near as is stronger and belongs to speech; not nearly as works everywhere."Food is nowhere near as expensive as housing."
fewer · lessFewer for things you can count, less for things you cannot. And both take far, much or slightly in front of them."far fewer flats"
"far less space"
the ... the ...Two comparatives locked together: change one and the other changes with it. This is the structure the whole reading is built on."The further out you live, the longer you commute."

One habit for this week: every time you catch yourself saying more expensive, better or bigger, add one word in front of it before you finish the sentence. Far, much, slightly — any of them. The habit takes about three days and it changes how precise you sound immediately.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are comparing two cities with a colleague who is thinking of moving. Choose the version that works.

You want to say the difference in rent is very large:
A "Rent is very more expensive there."
B "Rent is far more expensive there."
C "Rent is more high there."
You are talking about the number of flats under a certain price:
A "There are far less flats under that price."
B "There are far fewer flats under that price."
C "There are much less flats under that price."
You want to say the difference in food prices is small:
A "Food is nowhere near as expensive as housing."
B "Food is very cheaper than housing."
C "Food is more cheap than housing."
Somebody asks how much a ten per cent rise really helps:
A "It buys you slightly less than it sounds."
B "It buys you very less than it sounds."
C "It buys you a little fewer than it sounds."
You want to describe the trade-off between space and commuting:
A "More far you live, more long you commute."
B "The further out you live, the longer you commute."
C "The more further you live, the more longer you commute."
