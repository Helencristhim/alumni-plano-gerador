Chapter 3: Did, and the Verb Goes Back

One Word Does All the Work

you worked → did you work?  |  he took → did he take?

Grammar Discovery

What Happened to the Verb?

You worked on Saturday. → Did you work on Saturday?
She traveled for eleven hours. → Did she travel for eleven hours?
He took the train. → Did he take the train?
They forgot my name. → Did they forget my name?

Left side and right side are about the same finished day. Look at the verb. What changed?

FormPatternExample
QuestionDid + person + base verbDid you work on Saturday?
Negativeperson + did not + base verbI did not work on Saturday.
Same for everybodyDid I · Did you · Did he · Did she · Did we · Did they
Regular or irregularIt makes no difference: work · take · forget · hear · meet — the base form, always.
CarefulOne past mark is enough. Did you work? — never "Did you worked?"
Grammar Discovery

One Line Has No Did

What did you do on Sunday?
Who did you meet on your first day?
Did you hear about the rain? — Yes, I did.
Was the interview short?

Where does the question word go? And why does the last line have no did?

TypePatternExample
Yes / NoDid + person + base verbDid you sleep on the plane?
Wh-What / Where / When / Who / Why / How + did + person + base verbWhere did you go last March?
Short answersYes, I did. · No, I didn't. — never "Yes, I worked."
With beNo did at all: Was it good? · Were they late? · When was the last time?
CarefulOne verb carries the past, never two: Was it good? — never "Did it was good?"

Chapter 4: Five Minutes With Greg

Today You Are the One Asking

five questions, five minutes, one visitor with a meeting after this

Dialogue

Thursday, Ten O'Clock

Gi
Greg, do you have five minutes? The newsletter asked me for a short interview.
G
Five minutes, yes. After that I have a meeting. Ask your questions.
Gi
First one. When did you arrive, and how long did you travel?
G
I arrived on the ninth of March. Eleven hours in the air, and then four more hours on the road.
Gi
Did you sleep on the plane?
G
No, I didn't. I watched three movies and I read the project report twice.
Gi
Who did you meet on your first day here?
G
Ray, and about forty people. I forgot every name in ten minutes. I remember Ray because he gave me the wrong helmet.
Gi
Did you hear about the rain last week?
G
I heard about it in every e-mail. Did the water go into the lower area?
Gi
It did, on Wednesday. Last question: when was the last time you worked in a tunnel like this one?
G
Four years ago, in Norway. That tunnel was cold and this one is hot. Good answer? Then your five minutes are finished.
Comprehension

About Greg

1. How long did Greg travel, in the air and on the road?
Eleven hours in the air, and four more hours on the road.
2. Did Greg sleep on the plane? What did he do?
No, he didn't. He watched three movies and read the project report twice.
3. Why does Greg remember Ray and nobody else?
Because Ray gave him the wrong helmet.

Five Minutes, No List

Five minutes, a real interview: ask me about my past. Eight questions or more, two of them with a question word, and at least one with was or were instead of did.

No keywords. No stopping. When you ask, the other person talks — and you get time to think.

Survival Card

Five Questions to Keep

"What did you do yesterday?"

"Who did you meet there?"

"Sorry, I didn't hear the question."

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do

I know nine words for asking about the past: interview, question, answer, remember, forget, hear, meet, travel, last time.
I make a question with did and the base verb: "Did you work on Saturday?"
I put the question word before did: "What did you do?", "Who did you meet?"
With the verb be I use was or were and no did: "Was it good?", "When was the last time?"
I asked somebody eight questions about the past, and I understood the answers.
