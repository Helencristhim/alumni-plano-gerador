Lesson 08 -- Pre-class

Running the Room -- Opening, Moving, Closing

Nobody loses a room because their English is imperfect. They lose it because nobody could tell which point they were on. Key words: an opening, a point, a transition, an objection, to clarify, a slot, to summarise, to wrap up, to hand over, a follow-up. Structure: verb + -ing versus verb + to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An opening -- the first thirty seconds, where you say what the meeting is for
"In the opening, say how many points there are, and then stop."
A point -- one of the things you said you were going to cover
"Three points, and the third one is the reason I asked for the slot."
A transition -- the full sentence that moves the room from one point to the next
"A transition is not decoration. It is the only thing telling the room where you are."
An objection -- a reason somebody gives for not agreeing with you
"When an objection comes, repeat it in your own words before you answer."
To clarify -- to say something again, in a way that removes the doubt
"Does anybody want me to clarify anything from the first two points?"
A slot -- the piece of time a meeting gives you, and nothing more
"Your slot is twenty minutes, and it is shorter than it sounds."
To summarise -- to say the long thing again, in two sentences
"Let me summarise before we go on: two points down, one to go."
To wrap up -- to close a meeting by saying what was decided
"Then let me finish, and we will wrap up two minutes early."
To hand over -- to give the floor to the next person, on purpose
"That is everything from me. I am going to hand over to Rita for the numbers."
A follow-up -- the thing somebody has to do after the meeting ends, with a name and a date
"Hand the follow-up to a name and a date, or it does not exist."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Some verbs are followed by -ing and some by to, and there is no logic to find. What decides it is the first verb, and it is a list. The good news is that the list you need in a meeting is short. "I recommend saying how many points there are." "I suggest handing the follow-up to a name and a date." "People avoid using a full transition because it feels mechanical." Recommend, suggest, consider, avoid, keep — all of them take -ing.

The other list takes to. "I plan to finish two minutes early." "I need to have a decision today, not a conversation." "We decided to hold the price." Plan, need, want, decide, expect, agree. Notice that nothing in the second half of the sentence changes anything. Only the first verb decides, and that is why you learn the verbs and not a rule.

There is one place where a real rule exists: after a preposition, English always uses -ing. Repeat the objection before answering it. Say what you decided instead of waiting for somebody to ask. Think for a second before starting to talk. That last one is worth keeping, because rushing an answer is how a good one becomes a long one. And when the slot is nearly over, summarise what was decided, wrap up two minutes early, and hand over cleanly to whoever is next. In an opening you tell the room where you are going. At the end you tell them where you arrived.

1. What decides whether the second verb takes -ing or to?
A Whether the sentence is positive or negative.
B The first verb. It is a list, not a rule.
C The length of the second verb.
2. Which of these is correct?
A "I recommend to say how many points there are."
B "I recommend saying how many points there are."
C "I recommend say how many points there are."
3. The text says there is one place where a real rule exists. Where?
A After a negative verb, where English always uses to.
B After a preposition, where English always uses -ing.
C At the end of a sentence, where either form works.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Verb + -ing or Verb + To

GRAMMAR

The first verb decides, and eleven verbs cover almost everything you say in a meeting.

JobFormExample
These take -ingrecommend, suggest, consider, avoid, keepI recommend saying the number.
These take toplan, need, want, decide, expect, agreeI plan to finish early.

Stage 2: Put the Slot in Order

Order

You have twenty minutes and eight of them belong to the room. Put your slot in order.

?"Today I am going to cover three points, and I plan to finish in ten minutes."
?"First, coverage. It is at nineteen per cent and we expected twenty-two."
?"That is the first point. The second one is different."
?"Does anybody want me to clarify anything before I go on?"
?"Let me summarise: coverage at nineteen, price unchanged, budget needed."
?"Rita is going to send the coverage note on Thursday. We are two minutes early."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You are opening your slot. You say:
A "So, let me start with the coverage numbers."
B "Today I am going to cover three points, and I plan to finish in ten minutes."
C "I recommend to start with the numbers if everybody agrees."
You have finished the first point and you are moving on. You say:
A "Ok. Moving on."
B "That is the first point. The second one is different, and it is about the discount."
C "So."
Somebody objects and you want five seconds. You say:
A "Let me make sure I have that right. You are saying the eighteen months is too long."
B "Sorry, can you repeat? I did not understand."
C "I suggest to move on and come back to that later."
You are closing. You say:
A "That is all from me. Thank you."
B "Let me summarise. Rita sends the coverage note on Thursday, and we are two minutes early."
C "I need having a decision, so please tell me later."

Talk
TED -- Casey Brown, "Know your worth, and then ask for it" (9 min)

Nine minutes that are also a demonstration. Watch what she does structurally: she says how many things she is going to cover, she moves between them with full sentences, and she closes early. The content is about pricing. The lesson is about running the room.

Tip: on the second viewing, ignore what she is saying and write down only her transitions. There are four, and every one of them is a complete sentence.

Video
Harvard Business Review -- "The Explainer: Don’t Just Sell Stuff, Satisfy Needs"

Two minutes, and it does in two minutes what most people fail to do in twenty: one idea, announced, developed and closed. Worth watching again now that you know what to look for in the structure rather than in the argument.

Tip: watch it once, then close your eyes and rebuild it out loud in three sentences. If you can, the structure worked. If you cannot, it is not your English.
