Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and practice the pronunciation. These are the words a formal analysis of a painting cannot live without.

Chiaroscuro -- the strong contrast between light and dark areas in a painting
"It is the chiaroscuro that turns the tavern into a stage."
Tenebrism -- a style in which most of the painting is plunged into deep darkness and a few areas are violently lit
"Tenebrism pushes chiaroscuro to an extreme."
Raking light -- light that falls across a surface at a low, sharp angle
"A beam of raking light cuts across the wall."
Penumbra -- the area of partial shadow between full light and full darkness
"The faces at the table hover in the penumbra."
Foreshortening -- drawing an object at an angle so that it appears to project toward or away from the viewer
"The foreshortening of the arm makes it reach into our space."
Pentimento -- a visible trace of an earlier version that the artist painted over
"X-rays can reveal a pentimento beneath the surface."
Altarpiece -- a work of art placed behind or above the altar in a church
"The canvas was commissioned as part of a chapel, not as an altarpiece."
Commission -- a formal request and payment for an artist to produce a specific work
"It was his first major public commission."
Naturalism -- the depiction of people and objects as they really look, including imperfections
"His naturalism shocked some of his contemporaries."
Stark -- severe, bare and harsh, with no softening
"The contrast is stark: light on one side, darkness on the other."
Unflinching -- looking directly at something difficult without turning away
"Caravaggio gives us an unflinching view of ordinary faces."
Heighten -- to make a feeling or effect stronger
"The darkness heightens the drama of the gesture."
Illuminate -- to light something up, or to make an idea clearer
"It is the light that illuminates the moment of choice."
Provocative -- deliberately causing strong reactions, often discomfort or argument
"What made the painting provocative was its setting."
Pivotal -- of central importance; the point on which everything turns
"The pointing hand marks the pivotal instant."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Every formal analysis eventually faces the same problem: a painting shows everything at once, but a sentence can only say one thing at a time. The writer must decide what the reader should notice first, and English offers a structure designed for exactly that decision. It is this structure that separates a description from an argument.

Consider a plain sentence: the light calls Matthew. It is accurate, but it treats the light as one detail among many. Rewrite it as it is the light that calls Matthew, and the sentence suddenly makes a claim. The frame it is ... that places the light under a spotlight and implies a contrast with something else, perhaps the gesture or the figure of Christ. Grammarians call this a cleft sentence, because the original clause has been split in two in order to isolate its focus.

A second pattern builds suspense instead. What unsettled Caravaggio's critics was not the story but the setting. Here the reader waits through the first half of the sentence, and the focus arrives at the end, where English naturally places new and important information. Critics use this pattern when they want the reader to feel the weight of a conclusion rather than simply receive it.

The most persuasive clefts correct an expectation. It was not piety but naturalism that provoked his contemporaries. A reader who assumed the objection was religious is gently redirected, and the writer gains authority by anticipating a misunderstanding. Similarly, all he needed was a single window emphasizes how little was required to produce so great an effect.

Clefts are powerful precisely because they are marked. A paragraph built entirely from them sounds like a series of announcements, and a cleft that points at something vague, such as what I think is that art is important, only draws attention to its own emptiness. Used once or twice in a paragraph, on the claim that genuinely matters, the structure does what good criticism always does. It tells the reader where to look.

A Deciding what the reader should notice first when a painting shows everything at once.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences

GRAMMAR

A cleft sentence splits one clause into two parts so that one element receives the focus. In analysis, it tells the reader where to look and what to compare it with.

FormWhat it doesExample
It is / was + focus + that / whospotlights one element, often in contrast with anotherIt is the light that calls Matthew.
What + clause + is / was + focusbuilds suspense; the focus arrives at the endWhat unsettled his critics was the setting.
It is not X but Y thatcorrects an expectation the reader probably hasIt was not piety but naturalism that provoked them.
All / The only thing + clause + isemphasizes how little was neededAll he needed was a single window.
The C2 trapusing a cleft on a vague focus or in every sentence. "What I think is that the painting is good" points at nothing.

Tense check: in a past narrative the it-cleft moves to the past as well, and time expressions take that, not when: "It was in 1600 that the canvases were unveiled."
