Grammar Discovery

Which One Says “Not Anymore”?

"I used to get up at five to be at the clinic by six."

"I did not use to travel much, and now I go every chance I get."

"I worked in nutrition for six years."

"Now I get up at eight and I take the dogs out."

The red verbs are habits that repeated and are gone. The blue ones are a block of time with a number on it, and the life you have today. Same past, three different jobs.

JobFormExample
A habit that is overused to + bare verbI used to get up at five.
A state that is overused to + be / haveI used to be a dietitian.
The negative (no d)did not use to + bare verbI did not use to travel much.
The question (no d)Did + subject + use to + bare verb?Did you use to study English as a kid?
A finished block of time, not a habitthe past formI worked in nutrition for six years.
The habit nowthe presentNow I get up at eight.
The storytelling versionwould + bare verbBack then I would drive across town before sunrise.
A different thing entirelybe used to + verb-ing = normal for me nowI am used to working from home.

In one line: a habit that is gone takes used to. A block of time takes the past form. And after did, the d always disappears.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

One Left. One Stayed. One Is You.

An interview, and two people who answered the same question differently

Dialogue

The Profile Interview

S
Okay, I am recording. Start wherever you want — most people start with the job they have now.
B
I run operations at a tech company. But I did not study that. I used to be a dietitian.
S
A dietitian. That is a long way from operations. What did a normal week look like back then?
B
I used to be at the clinic at seven, and I used to see nine or ten people before lunch. I did not use to sit down much.
S
And what changed? People always want a moment.
B
There was not one. I just stopped wanting to go, slowly, over about a year. And then somebody talked me into applying for something I was not qualified for.
S
Do you miss any of it?
B
The people. I used to know the name of everybody’s kid. I do not miss the seven o’clock, and I did not use to think you could miss half of something.
Comprehension

About Sam

1. How does Sam open the interview, and what does he say most people do?
He says he is recording and that she can start wherever she wants, and that most people start with the job they have now.
2. What does Sam say when she tells him she was a dietitian?
That it is a long way from operations, and then he asks what a normal week used to look like.
3. Sam says “people always want a moment”. What is he actually asking for, and does he get it?
He is asking for one dramatic turning point that explains everything. He does not get it — she says there was not one.
Listening 1 · The One Who Left

“There Was Not a Moment. There Was a Parking Lot.”

Eleven years as a nurse, and the week it ended. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say her life used to look like?
For eleven years she was a nurse. She used to get up at four forty, she used to eat standing up, and she used to know exactly what her Tuesday was going to look like in March.
2. What was the whole warning she got, and what does she say people want instead?
Four days in a row when she did not want to get out of the car in the parking lot. People want a story with a dramatic moment in it, and there was not one.
3. What two things does she say are both true at the same time?
She misses being needed that badly, and she does not miss the four forty. She did not use to believe both could be true at once.
Listening 2 · The One Who Stayed

“I Like Who I Am at Six O’Clock.”

Twenty-six years, one job, and an argument nobody makes. Sound first — no text.

1. What did he use to think about staying in the same job?
That it made him boring. For about a decade he was unhappy about it, because everybody around him was pivoting.
2. What did he tell his daughter when she asked if he liked his job?
Something he had not planned: no, but he likes who he is at six o'clock.
3. What is his advice, and what does he say it is NOT?
It is not advice against leaving. It is to be honest about what you are buying: some people buy the work, some buy the six o'clock, and each one costs you the other.
