Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody asks what you did before, and English gives you three different past verbs to choose from. "I worked in nutrition for six years" is a finished block of time: it started, it ended, there is a number on it. "I used to get up at five" is something else entirely: a habit that repeated, and that is over.

The shape is small and it never changes: used to + the bare verb. "I used to be a dietitian." "I used to see ten people before lunch." It carries a whole door closing behind it, which is why it is the verb for a life before a turning point.

The negative and the question are where everybody falls over, and it is always the same letter. In the negative and in the question the d disappears: "I did not use to travel much." "Did you use to study English as a kid?" Not did not used to. The did already carries the past; the verb goes back to being bare, exactly like did you go and never did you went.

Two more things. In a story, Americans swap in would: "Back then I would get up at five and drive across town before the sun came up." Same meaning, and it sounds like a memory instead of a fact. And keep "I used to swim" far away from "I am used to swimming" — the second one is not the past at all. It means it is normal for me now.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Used To, and the D That Disappears

GRAMMAR

A habit that is over takes used to. A finished block of time takes the past form. And after did, it is always use to, never used to.

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

Somebody is interviewing you about your career change. Put the exchange in a logical order.

?"So before all this, what did you actually do?"
?"I used to be a dietitian. I studied nutrition for five years."
?"And what did a normal week look like back then?"
?"I used to be at the clinic at seven, and I did not use to sit down much."
?"What changed? People always want a moment."
?"There was not one. I just stopped wanting to go, slowly, over about a year."
