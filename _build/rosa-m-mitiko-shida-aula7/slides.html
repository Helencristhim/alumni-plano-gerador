Chapter 3: The Code

The Temperature of One Word

must · have to · should · could · might

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Under the contract we must charge the change fee."

"You should call me before you book twelve seats."

"We could keep the ticket open for a year instead."

"It might be the fare class. Let me check and call you back."

Put the four colored words in order, from the strongest to the softest. What exactly changes between them?

WordWhat it saysExample
must · have toObligation, no choice. Must often comes from the rule itself; have to from an outside circumstance. In practice they are interchangeable in most business sentences."We must charge the fee."
"Compliance has to approve it."
should · ought toAdvice and recommendation. Strong enough to be useful, soft enough not to offend."You should call me before you book."
could · mightAn option, or a possibility you are not sure about. This is the polite way to put an idea on the table."We could keep the ticket open."
"It might be the fare class."
must, for a guessMust also means I am almost certain when you are reading evidence, not stating a rule."That must be the old fare — the contract changed in March."
The trap: two negativesmust not = it is forbidden. do not have to = it is optional. They are opposites, not synonyms, and this one is expensive."You must not promise the exception." (forbidden)
"You do not have to copy the office." (optional)

After every one of these words comes the base verb — no to, no -s, no -ing. She must call, never she must to call.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"We must to charge the change fee."
"We must charge the change fee."
"She shoulds call before she books twelve seats."
"She should call before she books twelve seats."
"You do not have to promise the exception before it is approved."
"You must not promise the exception before it is approved."

A modal is followed by the base verb: no to, no -s. And the third pair is the expensive one: must not means it is forbidden, while do not have to means it is optional. Saying the second when you mean the first tells a colleague that the thing you are trying to prevent is simply not required.

Chapter 4: The Client Call

Twelve Seats, Wrong Day

He knows the answer is no. He is calling anyway.

Dialogue

Saying No and Keeping the Client

G
Rosa, it is Gustavo. We booked twelve people on the Tuesday flight and the meeting moved to Thursday. Please tell me there is something you can do.
R
Let me be straight with you first. Under the contract we must charge the change fee on every ticket, and I cannot remove it.
G
That is what I expected. The fee is more than we paid for the tickets, Rosa.
R
I know. So here is what we could do instead. We could leave the tickets open for twelve months, and you use them on any route in the contract. No refund, no new fare, no fee.
G
That would actually work for us. Is it certain?
R
Not yet, and I must not promise it before it is approved. I can escalate the request today with the reason attached, and you should have an answer by Thursday.
G
Fair enough. And what should I do differently next time?
R
One thing only. You should call me before you book more than six seats. There is a grace period on the group fare, and almost nobody knows it exists.
Comprehension

About Gustavo

1. What happened to his booking?
He booked twelve people on the Tuesday flight and the meeting moved to Thursday.
2. Why is the change fee such a problem for him?
Because the fee is more than the company paid for the tickets.
3. What does he want to know besides the solution?
What he should do differently next time.
4. What is his reaction when she says the solution is not certain yet?
He accepts it — he says fair enough.
The Real Thing

The Email After the Call

Email · sent 16:40
Group booking — change to Thursday

Gustavo, following our call, here is where we stand in writing.

The rule. Under the contract we must apply the change fee to each of the twelve tickets. That part I cannot change.

What we could do. The tickets could stay open for twelve months, on any route in the contract, with no fee and no new fare. I have to be clear that this is a request, not a decision: it must be approved, and I must not confirm it before it is.

Next time. You should call me before booking more than six seats. There is a grace period on the group fare that might save you this entire conversation.

1. Why does she write "I must not confirm it" and not "I do not have to confirm it"?
Because it is forbidden until it is approved — do not have to would mean it is simply optional.
2. Which sentence in the email is advice, not a rule, and how do you know?
"You should call me before booking more than six seats" — should is a recommendation, not an obligation.
Listening

The Call You Do Not Want

A voice message from a corporate client. Sound first — no text.

1. What went wrong with the booking?
Twelve people were booked on the Tuesday flight and the meeting moved to Thursday.
2. What does he admit about his own part in it?
That he should have called her before they booked, and he did not.
3. What does he ask for if nothing can be done?
To be told what he should do differently next time.
Listening 2

Three Levels of a Rule

Two minutes on corporate policy. Sound first — no text.

1. What are the three levels she describes?
The rule, the guidelines, and the exception.
2. What are the three conditions for an exception?
It must be requested, it must have a reason attached, and it must be approved by somebody above you.
3. What does she say you must never do, and why?
Promise the exception before it is approved — because that only moves the bad news to somebody else's phone call.
