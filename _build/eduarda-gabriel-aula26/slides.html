Chapter 3 -- The Code

A Number That...

The small words that explain big terms

Grammar Discovery

What Do the Purple Words Have in Common?

"A multiple is a number that shows how many times its profit a company is worth."

"Comparables are companies which are similar to Lane in size and sector."

"A buyer who needs your factories usually pays a premium."

"NorthPack is a company whose factories are close to Lane's."

What does each purple word refer to? Can you remove the part after it?

ForUseExample
Thingsthat / which"a number that shows..."

Placeswhere"the market where Lane sells..."
Neverwhat / extra pronounNot "a number what..." / "the comparables that we used them"
Grammar Rule

Two Facts or One Idea?

Two factsOne idea (same meaning)
"Comparables are companies. They are similar to Lane.""Comparables are companies that are similar to Lane."
"This is the discount rate. We used it.""This is the discount rate (that) we used."
"A premium is extra money. A buyer pays it for control.""A premium is the extra money (that) a buyer pays for control."
"NorthPack is a buyer. Its factories are near Lane's.""NorthPack is a buyer whose factories are near Lane's."
The client has to connect the facts.The client hears one clear definition.

Chapter 4 -- Getting There

The Valuation Call

One conversation, two recordings, one summary

Dialogue

Eduarda and Victor

V
Eduarda, Mark says ninety million is a fair price. Claire says it's too low. I just want to understand the number.
E
Of course. Let me explain our valuation in plain English, without the spreadsheet.
V
Please. My accountant sent me forty pages, and I didn't understand any of them.
E
First, we looked at comparables. Those are companies that are similar to Lane and were sold in the last three years.
V
And what did the buyers pay for them?
E
About nine to ten times their yearly profit. That number is the multiple. Lane makes eleven million a year, so that gives us ninety-nine to a hundred and ten million.
V
So Mark is offering about eight times. Is my company undervalued?
E
At ninety million, yes. And our cash flow model, the one that looks at the money Lane will earn in the future, gives a similar ballpark figure: about a hundred million.
V
But why would Mark pay more than he has to?
E
Because NorthPack is a buyer that needs your factories. A buyer like that usually pays a premium.
Comprehension

About Victor

1. What did Victor's accountant send him?
Forty pages, and he didn't understand any of them.
2. According to Victor, what does Claire think of Mark's offer?
She thinks it is too low.
3. What does Victor ask at the end?
Why Mark would pay more than he has to.

Listening 2 -- The Finance Class

Three Methods

A finance instructor explains three ways to value a company. Sound first -- no text.

1. What are the three common ways to value a company?
Comparables, multiples and discounted cash flow (DCF).
2. What does the discount rate do in a DCF?
It brings future money back to today's value.
3. Why do bankers present a range?
Because no method is perfect.
Real Document

The Valuation Summary

From: Eduarda Gabriel, Houlihan Lokey
To: Victor Lane, Lane Packaging
Re: Lane Packaging -- Valuation Summary

Dear Victor,

Thank you for the call today. Here is the summary that you asked for.

Comparables: three packaging companies that were sold last year, at nine to ten times their profit. At Lane's profit of eleven million dollars, this gives a range of ninety-nine to one hundred and ten million.

Cash flow model: about one hundred million, with a discount rate of ten percent.

Our view: at ninety million, the company is undervalued. NorthPack is a buyer whose factories are close to yours, so a premium is realistic. We propose to go back to Mark with one hundred and five million.

Best regards,
Eduarda

1. What range do the comparables give?
Ninety-nine to one hundred and ten million dollars.
2. Why is a premium realistic?
Because NorthPack's factories are close to Lane's.
3. What number will they take back to Mark?
One hundred and five million.
