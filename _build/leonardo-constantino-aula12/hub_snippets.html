Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The doctoral application asks for a certificate, so Leonardo sits down with a sample paper on a Sunday morning and loses. Not the English: the clock. He reads the first passage the way he reads a contract, from the first word, and only then meets the question. Four minutes are gone before he answers anything. The examiner in the recording is blunt about it. Read the question first, then skim for the main idea for thirty seconds, and only then go looking. When a question names a date, do not read at all: scan for it. And when the options appear, narrow down the options before you choose, because the correct one almost never uses the words of the passage. It paraphrases them, on purpose, and a candidate who is hunting for matching words is being led to the wrong answer. There is a second habit that costs him marks, and it comes from his job. Asked for one reason, he gives three, because all three are true. In a memo that is thorough. In an exam it is a failure to address the question: two of the three sentences are accurate and not relevant. The written part is stranger still. Nobody counts his ideas. The examiner only asks whether the second sentence follows the first, which is what coherent means, and whether the answer is concise rather than long. So he learns five moves and keeps them: state a view, give the evidence, outline the other side, mark its limit, and draw a conclusion. And the grammar? None of it is new. The items are the passive voice from the reform lesson, the reported speech from the difficult calls, the gerund after recommend, and a conditional he has written a hundred times in Portuguese. On the second Sunday he reads the question first, and he finishes the set with two minutes to spare.

1. Why does the examiner tell candidates to read the question before the passage?
A Because the questions are usually easier than the passage.
B Because reading the passage carefully first costs four minutes that the paper does not give you.
C Because the passage is never relevant to the questions.
2. Why is an option that repeats the words of the passage usually wrong?
A Because the correct option paraphrases the passage, so matching words are bait.
B Because the passage and the options are written by different people.
C Because repeated words are always a grammar mistake.
3. "Two of the three sentences are accurate and not relevant." What does this tell us?
A The two sentences contain factual errors about tax.
B They are true, and they answer something the examiner did not ask.
C They are relevant, but they are written in the wrong register.
4. According to the text, what does the examiner measure in the written part?
A How many different ideas the candidate manages to mention.
B How much new grammar the candidate can show off.
C Whether each sentence follows the one before it, and whether the answer stays concise.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- What the Exam Measures

GRAMMAR

This lesson adds no new structure. Every item in a certification paper is built from English you already have: the passive voice, reported speech, gerunds and infinitives, conditionals and relative clauses. What the paper adds is a clock and a question that points at one thing only. The table below is not grammar to learn. It is what each task is measuring, and what to do about it.

TaskWhat it measuresWhat to do
Skimmingthe main ideaThirty seconds. First and last sentence of each paragraph. Ignore every number.
Scanningone specific detailDo not read. Hunt the name, the date or the figure the question gives you.
Inferencewhat is implied, not statedAsk one question: if this sentence is true, what else must be true? The writer implies; you infer.
The paraphrase trapvocabulary, not memoryThe right option almost never repeats the words of the passage. Matching words are bait.
Key word transformationSame meaning, other form — and every form is one you already have. Active to passive voice ("The authority granted the ruling" → "The ruling was granted"). Direct to reported speech ("We will not reopen it," she said → "She said that they would not reopen it"). Advise to recommend, which takes the gerund ("We recommend narrowing down the options"). Two facts to one conditional ("They did not file, so they lost it" → "If they had filed, they would not have lost it").
The written answerFive moves, always in this order: 1 "In my view..." · 2 "This is supported by the fact that..." · 3 "One could argue that..." · 4 "However, it should be noted that..." · 5 "In conclusion, the evidence suggests that...". Move 3 is the one candidates skip, and it is the one that separates a safe pass from a high mark.
ListeningThree frames answer most of the questions: "The speaker implies that...", "The tone suggests that...", "Based on the context, the word most likely means...". If a question asks what is implied, the answer is not said anywhere — listening again will not find it.
The rule under all of itRead the question before the text. A true sentence that does not address the question scores nothing, and in an exam a thorough answer is not a better answer — it is a longer one.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each real exam situation.

A reading question asks for one reason why the group is exposed. You know three. You write:
A All three reasons, in three sentences, because all three are accurate.
B The one reason that is relevant to the question, in one sentence.
C The reason you can explain best, plus a note about the other two.
Two options are left. Option A repeats four words from the passage. Option B says the same idea in different words. You choose:
A Option B, because the correct answer usually paraphrases the passage.
B Option A, because the matching words prove it came from the text.
C Neither, because a repeated word always means the option is a distractor.
A listening question asks what the speaker implies about the deadline. You have listened twice and it is not stated. You:
A Listen a third time until you hear the deadline mentioned.
B Leave the item blank, because the information is missing.
C Infer it: ask what must be true if what she did say is true.
You have fifteen minutes for a written answer of 180 words. Your first move is to:
A Start writing immediately, to be sure you reach the word count.
B Outline the five moves in thirty seconds, then write.
C Write the conclusion first and build the rest around it.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Some people argue that tax rules in emerging markets are too complex for foreign investors to navigate without local legal support. Do you agree? In ninety seconds, answer out loud in five moves: state your view, give the evidence, outline the other side, mark its limit, and draw a conclusion. Use at least three words from today, and keep it concise.
