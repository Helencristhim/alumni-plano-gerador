Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read it in context. These words describe how cultures are placed at the center or at the edge of history, and how they mix.

Vernacular -- belonging to the ordinary, local culture of a place rather than to an official or foreign style
"She painted the vernacular colors of the Brazilian countryside."
Cosmopolitan -- familiar with and influenced by many different countries and cultures
"Her training in Paris was cosmopolitan; her subject was Brazil."
Hybridity -- the mixing of different cultures, styles or traditions into something new
"Critics now describe her work in terms of hybridity rather than imitation."
Emblematic -- representing a particular quality or idea so well that it becomes a symbol of it
"Abaporu became emblematic of Brazilian modernism."
Iconography -- the set of images and symbols typical of a style, culture or artist
"Cacti, suns and giant feet belong to her iconography."
Exoticize -- to present a culture as strange and colorful for the entertainment of outsiders
"European audiences tended to exoticize Latin American art."
Reclaim -- to take back something that was lost, taken or misrepresented
"She reclaimed images that foreign painters had treated as picturesque."
Peripheral -- at the edge of something, and therefore treated as less important
"For decades Brazil was seen as peripheral to the story of modern art."
Provincial -- narrow and unsophisticated, as if far from the center of culture
"Some critics dismissed the city's art scene as provincial."
Disproportionate -- too large or too small in comparison with something else
"The figure has a disproportionate foot and a tiny head."
Centennial -- the hundredth anniversary of an event
"The Modern Art Week coincided with the centennial of independence."
Polemic -- a strong written or spoken attack on an idea, often to provoke debate
"A famous polemic in a newspaper attacked a young painter's exhibition."
Homage -- something done to show respect and admiration for someone
"Later artists paid homage to her palette."
Syncretic -- combining different beliefs, cultures or styles into a single system
"Her work is syncretic, joining Cubist structure and Brazilian subjects."
Manifesto -- a public declaration of the aims and ideas of a group or movement
"The painting inspired a manifesto for a new movement."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Most English sentences are loose. They state their main idea first and then add details, one after another, the way a person naturally speaks. Tarsila became a defining figure of modern art, trained in Paris and admired in São Paulo. The reader knows the point immediately, and everything that follows is supporting information.

A periodic sentence reverses that order. It gathers its details first and delays the main clause until the very end. Trained in Paris, admired in São Paulo and ignored for decades by the museums of the north, Tarsila became a defining figure of modern art. The facts are identical, but the effect is not. The reader has to hold the details in suspension, and when the main clause finally arrives, it lands with more weight. Writers use periodic sentences for conclusions, turning points and the claims they most want remembered.

Periodic sentences almost always rely on parallelism, the repetition of the same grammatical form in a series. In the example above, trained, admired and ignored are all past participles followed by a phrase. That repetition creates rhythm, and rhythm helps the reader keep track of the accumulated details. Correlative structures work in the same way: neither a copy of Europe nor a rejection of it, or not what Paris expected but what neither city had imagined. What follows neither must match what follows nor, and what follows not must match what follows but.

When parallelism breaks, readers feel it even if they cannot name it. She liked to paint, drawing and sculpture sounds clumsy because it mixes an infinitive, a gerund and a noun. The repair is simple: to paint, to draw and to sculpt.

Both techniques lose their power through repetition. A paragraph made entirely of periodic sentences becomes exhausting, and constant tricolons start to sound like a political speech. Used once, at the moment the argument turns, a well-built periodic sentence is one of the most elegant tools an essay writer has.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Periodic Sentences and Parallelism

GRAMMAR

A periodic sentence builds its details first and delivers the main clause last. Parallelism repeats a grammatical form so that the series has rhythm and balance.

FormWhat it doesExample
periodic sentencedelays the main clause for emphasisTrained in Paris, admired at home and ignored abroad, she...
parallel seriessame grammatical form for every itemto paint, to draw and to sculpt
neither... nortwo matching negativesneither a copy of Europe nor a rejection of it
not... butcorrects an expectation with matching partsnot for Paris but for Brazil
tricolonthree parallel elements for rhythmstrange, funny and syncretic
The C2 trapfaulty parallelism (mixing forms in a series) and overuse. Save the periodic sentence for the claim that deserves suspense.

Check a series by reading only the first word of each item aloud. If the forms do not match, the series is not parallel.
