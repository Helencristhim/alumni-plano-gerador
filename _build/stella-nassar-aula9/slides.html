The Same Facts, Two Rhythms

LOOSE

"Tarsila became one of the defining figures of modern art in Latin America. She trained in Paris and was admired in São Paulo, but she was ignored by northern museums for decades."

PERIODIC

"Trained in Paris, admired in São Paulo and ignored for decades by the museums of the north, Tarsila became one of the defining figures of modern art in Latin America."

The first sentence reports. The second one builds, and makes the reader wait for the point.

Chapter 4: The Style Code

The Sentence That Makes You Wait

Periodic sentences and parallelism

Discover the Style

Where Is the Main Clause?

"Trained in Paris, admired in São Paulo and ignored for decades by the museums of the north, Tarsila became a defining figure of modern art."

"She painted not what Paris expected, not what São Paulo remembered, but what neither had imagined."

"Neither a copy of Europe nor a rejection of it, her work was something new."

"If the old map was wrong, if the center was never single, if the edges were never silent, then the story of modern art must be rewritten."

Find the main clause in each sentence. What comes before it, and why do the colored parts sound balanced?

FormWhat it doesExample
periodic sentencedelays the main clause until the end, building suspenseTrained in Paris, admired at home and ignored abroad, she became...
loose sentencestates the main clause first, then adds detailShe became a defining figure, trained in Paris and admired at home.
parallelismrepeats the same grammatical form in a seriestrained in Paris, admired at home, ignored abroad
tricolon / correlative pairsthree parallel items, or not X but Y / neither X nor Ynot what Paris expected, but what neither had imagined
The C2 trapfaulty parallelism and overuse. "She liked to paint, drawing and sculpture" breaks the series. And a paragraph of periodic sentences exhausts the reader: save suspense for the point that deserves it.

From Loose to Periodic

Say the full sentence, then click to compare

1. She became famous. She was born on a farm, studied in Paris and returned to São Paulo. (periodic)
"Born on a farm, trained in Paris and returned to São Paulo, she became famous."
2. Her work was not a copy of Europe. It was not a rejection of Europe either. (neither... nor)
"Her work was neither a copy of Europe nor a rejection of it."
3. The painting is strange, colorful and it provokes people. (parallelism)
"The painting is strange, colorful and provocative."
4. I want to study art history because I grew up in Brazil, I studied English for years and I love museums. (periodic, for an essay)
"Raised in Brazil, fluent in English and at home in museums, I want to study how art travels."
Chapter 5: Voices Across Continents

Dialogue

Mr. Brooks and the Background Question

D
Several colleges ask about your background or community. Have you thought about writing about Brazil?
S
I have, but I worry it will sound like a travel brochure. Beaches, colors, samba.
D
That is a real risk. Admissions readers see a lot of essays that exoticize the writer's own country.
S
So what is the alternative? Pretend I am from nowhere?
D
No. Use it as a way of seeing, not as scenery. What do you notice in an American museum that your classmates here would not?
S
That the Latin American room is always small and always at the end, and that Tarsila is usually labeled as influenced by someone French.
D
Now that is an essay. It is not about where you are from. It is about what where you are from lets you see.
S
Neither a postcard nor an apology, but an argument.
D
When I was in college, my roommate from Lagos wrote something like that, and it was the best essay in our year.
S
Then I will start with the small room at the end of the museum.
Comprehension

About Mr. Brooks

1. What risk does Mr. Brooks agree exists when applicants write about their country?
That the essay exoticizes the writer's own country, as admissions readers often see.
2. What does he suggest Stella use Brazil as, instead of scenery?
As a way of seeing: what she notices in an American museum that her classmates would not.
3. What story does he tell about his college roommate?
His roommate from Lagos wrote an essay like that, and it was the best essay in their year.
Listening 1

The Curator and the Room

A curator introduces a room of Tarsila's paintings in a US museum. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. What mistake does the curator say earlier exhibitions made when showing Tarsila?
They presented her mainly as a student of French painters, as if her importance came from Paris.
2. What does the curator ask visitors to notice in the room?
How she took the structure she learned in Europe and filled it with the colors, plants and figures of Brazil.
3. Why does the curator call the arrangement of the room an argument?
Because placing her at the center, not at the end of a European story, suggests that modern art had more than one center.
Listening 2

The Week of 1922

A historian lectures on the Modern Art Week in São Paulo. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. When and where did the Modern Art Week take place, and what anniversary did it coincide with?
In February 1922, at the Municipal Theater in São Paulo, in the year of the centennial of Brazilian independence.
2. What does the historian say about Tarsila and the week itself?
She was not there; she was in Paris, and she joined the group of modernists only after she returned later that year.
3. Why does the historian say the week mattered even though many people found it chaotic?
Because it announced, publicly and noisily, that Brazilian artists intended to be modern on their own terms.
