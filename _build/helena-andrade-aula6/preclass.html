Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

Navigating ASON Texts -- Cleft Sentences and Relative Clauses in Regulation Language

The vocabulary of maritime law and the two structures that decide which noun a clause is about. Key words: compliance, liability, jurisdiction, amendment, ratification, annex, convention, protocol, provision, enforcement. Structure: cleft sentences and relative clauses with prepositions.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read it inside a real sentence. These are the words the exam text will use.

Compliance -- the state of doing what a rule or a convention requires
"The vessel was found to be in compliance with the provision."
Liability -- legal responsibility for damage, loss or injury
"It is the flag state that bears liability for the certificate it issued."
Jurisdiction -- the legal authority a state has over a place, a ship or a person
"The vessel comes under the jurisdiction of the state whose flag she flies."
Amendment -- an official change made to a text that is already in force
"The amendment entered into force eighteen months after adoption."
Ratification -- the formal act by which a state accepts to be bound by a treaty
"Ratification by thirty states was required before the annex applied."
Annex -- a section added to a convention, carrying its detailed technical rules
"The technical requirements are set out in the annex, not in the articles."
Convention -- a binding international agreement between states, such as SOLAS
"The convention sets out the responsibilities of flag states."
Protocol -- a later instrument that adds to or modifies an existing convention
"The protocol of 1988 replaced the survey regime of the original text."
Provision -- one specific clause or requirement inside a legal text
"Enforcement of the provision falls to the port state."
Enforcement -- the act of making sure a rule is actually obeyed, with consequences if not
"Enforcement is what separates a ratified convention from a real one."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

What makes a convention law on a particular ship is ratification by the state whose flag she flies. It is for that reason that two identical vessels at the same berth may be subject to different provisions.

The classification society to which the owner had contracted the surveys held an authorisation from one flag state only. The port authority in whose waters she lay was acting for a state which had ratified both instruments. It was not the surveyor who was at fault, and what produced the detention was an amendment that one registry had accepted and the other had not.

1. It was not the surveyor who was at fault. What does this structure add?
A Nothing. It is a longer way of saying the surveyor was not at fault.
B It names the surveyor in order to exclude him, and implies that somebody else is meant.
C It means the writer is unsure who was at fault.
2. The port authority in whose waters she lay was acting for a state which had ratified both instruments. Who had ratified both?
A The port authority.
B The state the authority was acting for.
C The vessel's own flag state.
3. Why is it the state whose flag she flies and not the state which flag she flies?
A Because whose shows possession, and it works for states as well as for people.
B Because which can never introduce a relative clause.
C Because a state is treated as a person in legal English.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Clefts and Relative Clauses -- Controlling Where the Reader Looks

GRAMMAR

A cleft says look here and nowhere else. A relative clause says this one, not the other one. In a legal text those two instructions are not decoration: they are the meaning.

FormWhat it doesExample
It + be + X + who / thatthe it-cleft: it names X and excludes everything elseIt was not the surveyor who was at fault.
What + clause + be + Xthe wh-cleft: it delays X to the end, where the emphasis fallsWhat produced the detention was an amendment.
preposition + which / whom / whoseformal relative clause: the preposition moves to the front with the pronounthe society to which the owner had contracted the surveys
whose + nounpossession inside the clause; it can belong to a thing, not only to a personthe state whose flag she flies
The reading trapthe relative clause does NOT always attach to the nearest noun. The port authority in whose waters she lay was acting for a state which had ratified both -- which points at the state, not at the waters and not at the authority. Every wrong option re-attaches the clause to the wrong noun.

Both structures exist to control where the reader looks. The cleft says look here and nowhere else; the relative clause says this one, not the other one. In a legal text those two instructions are the whole meaning.
