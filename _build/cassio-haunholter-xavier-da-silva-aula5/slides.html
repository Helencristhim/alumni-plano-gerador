Chapter 3: The Code

Slightly, Considerably, By Far

How English measures a difference

Grammar Discovery

How Big Is the Difference?

"A steel frame is considerably faster to erect than a concrete one."

"Concrete is slightly heavier on the foundations."

"Precast is by far the quickest option for the external walls."

"Masonry is nowhere near as durable as concrete in this environment."

The words in blue are the comparison. The words in red are the size of the comparison, and they are what an engineer is actually listening for. Try reading each sentence without the red word and notice how little is left.

JobFormExample
A small differenceslightly / marginally / a little + comparativeConcrete is slightly heavier on the foundations.
A large differenceconsiderably / significantly / far / much + comparativeSteel is considerably faster to erect.
The clear winnerby far + the + superlativePrecast is by far the quickest option.
Two things that are equalas + adjective + asPanels are as durable as masonry here.
A difference in the other directionnot as + adjective + as / nowhere near asMasonry is nowhere near as durable as concrete.
The trapnever more + -er, and never the most + -estNot more cheaper. Say cheaper. Not the most fastest. Say the fastest.

In one line: slightly = small, considerably or far = large, by far the + superlative = no contest, as ... as = equal.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Design Meeting, at Real Speed

One discussion, three recordings, one comparison sheet

Dialogue

Why Did You Choose Concrete?

D
I looked at the general arrangement this morning. Why did you go for concrete on the main frame? I would have expected steel.
C
Mainly the load. The process equipment on the first floor is far heavier than a normal industrial load, and the client may add more later.
D
Fair. But a steel frame would be considerably faster to erect. You are losing weeks on site.
C
We are. That is the trade-off. But at an eighteen-metre span, the difference is much smaller than it looks, and the foundation cost is nearly the same either way.
D
And the external walls? Are you doing masonry?
C
No. Precast panels on three faces. They are by far the quickest option, and the durability in a humid process area is significantly better than block.
D
That makes sense. What about the east face, next to the loading bay?
C
Metal cladding there, because the client wants to be able to open that wall in five years. It is not as durable, but it is the only one we can remove.
Comprehension

About Dana

1. What did Dana expect to see on the main frame?
Steel, not concrete.
2. What argument does Dana make in favour of steel?
That a steel frame would be considerably faster to erect, so the project loses weeks on site.
3. What does Dana ask about after the external walls?
The east face, next to the loading bay.
Listening 1 · The Structural Lead

The Question I Ask Before the Material

A structural lead explains how he decides between concrete and steel. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say decides the choice more often than cost?
The load and the span, and whether the client will change the equipment later.
2. At what span does he say steel usually wins?
Beyond about twenty-five metres.
3. What does he say about the word cheaper?
That it means nothing until you say cheaper over what, because the frame is a small part of the total.
Listening 2 · The Precast Specialist

Why Panels Won on That Project

A specialist describes a decision between precast panels and block masonry. Sound first — no text.

1. How much time did the panels save on that project?
About seven weeks.
2. What did the client care about more than speed, in the end?
Durability in a humid process area, where block was performing badly.
3. What does she say is the real disadvantage of panels?
They are much harder to change later, so every opening has to be decided early.
Listening 3 · The Design Review

How to Lose a Technical Argument

A design manager on the way engineers present a comparison badly. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say is the most common mistake in a comparison?
Presenting a list of advantages for both options and no recommendation.
2. What three things does she want in the first thirty seconds?
The recommendation, the one reason behind it, and the thing you are giving up.
3. What does she say about the word better?
That it is not an argument unless you say better at what, and by how much.

Read the sheet as an argument, not a table. One line is the recommendation, one line is the single reason, and one line is what you are giving up. Every other line only exists to support those three.
1. Which line is the single reason for the recommendation?
The equipment load: option A carries the future load with no change, while B would need oversizing.
2. Which line is the thing being given up, and how would you say it out loud?
The trade-off: we are accepting about four weeks on site in exchange for future load capacity.
3. How would you say the cost line with the right degree word?
Option B is only slightly cheaper on the frame, and the total cost is nearly the same.
