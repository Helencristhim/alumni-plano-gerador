Handling Objections

The committee read your readout and sent back three objections. Key words: objection, concern, address, valid, sticking point, counter, common ground, caveat, soften, take on board. Structure: concessive connectors (although, even though, though, despite, in spite of, however, while).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Objection -- a reason someone gives for not agreeing with a plan
"The memo lists three objections, not three refusals."
Concern -- a worry about a risk or a result, said out loud
"Their first concern is the size, not the price."
To address -- to deal with a question or a worry directly, not around it
"Address the size, and the rest of the deal follows."
Valid -- reasonable and based on facts, so you cannot just refuse it
"The concentration point is valid, and I will say so first."
Sticking point -- the one issue that stops an agreement from closing
"The sticking point is the concentration, not the fee."
To counter -- to answer an argument with an argument of your own
"He countered with the five-year cost of the package."
Common ground -- the part of the question that both sides already agree on
"Start from common ground: nobody wants this deal to die."
Caveat -- a condition you attach to something you accept
"We accept the fee, with one caveat: the grace period stays."
To soften -- to make a message less hard, without changing what it says
"Soften the wording, but do not soften the number."
To take on board -- to accept a point and let it change what you do next
"We took the concentration point on board and asked for a cap."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The committee sent the memo back at eleven, and it carried three objections. The first was the size of the exposure. Although the price was the best on the table, two members were not comfortable with one hundred and eighty million placed with a single bank. The second was the arrangement fee. Even though the tenor is longer and the grace period is new, one percent still looks expensive next to the zero point seven five we paid last year. The third was timing: despite the pressure from the desk, some members wanted to wait for the quarterly numbers. The chair was clear about which one decides. It is the sticking point of the concentration, not the fee. So the plan for Wednesday is simple. I say that the concern about the size is valid and that I have taken it on board. However, I do not stop there: I counter with a cap written into the contract and a second bank for forty of the amount. I address the fee last, and I accept it with one caveat, which is that the grace period stays in writing. I will soften the wording, not the number, and I will open from the common ground: nobody in that room wants this deal to die.

1. "Although the price was the best on the table, two members were not comfortable." Why is there no "but" in the second half?
A Because the second half is a question, and questions never take "but".
B Because "although" already carries the contrast, so one turning word is enough.
C Because "but" can only be used at the beginning of a sentence.
2. The text says "despite the pressure from the desk". Why not "despite the desk pressured them"?
A Because "despite" takes a noun or an -ing form, never a subject with a verb.
B Because "despite" can only be used in the past.
C Because "despite" needs a comma before the subject.
3. "I have taken it on board. However, I do not stop there." Which sentence is also correct?
A "However I have taken it on board, I do not stop there."
B "I have taken it on board however I do not stop there."
C "Although I have taken it on board, I do not stop there."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Concessive Connectors

GRAMMAR

A good answer to an objection has two halves that pull in opposite directions. The connector is what turns the sentence, and each connector needs a different thing after it.

ConnectorWhat comes after itExample
although · though · even thoughA subject and a verb, inside ONE sentence. Even though is the strongest of the three.Although the fee is high, we accept it.
despite · in spite ofA noun, a pronoun or an -ing form. Never a subject with a verb.Despite the fee, we accept it. Despite paying more, we accept it.
however · neverthelessA new sentence (or a semicolon) before it, and a comma after it. It is an adverb, not a conjunction.The fee is high. However, the package is the best.
while · whereasA subject and a verb, to put two facts side by side.While the fee is higher, the tenor is two years longer.
where the concession sitsFirst, with a comma: Although the fee is high, we accept it. Or second, with no comma: We accept it although the fee is high. Three errors to avoid: "although... but", "despite the fee is high", "however the fee is high, we accept it".

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best option for each moment in the committee room.

A member says the fee is too high. What do you say first?
A "That is not a real problem, and I will explain why."
B "You are right that it is more than last year. However, the five-year cost is lower."
C "Although the fee is higher, but the package is the best on the table."
You want to say that the exposure is large and that the contract can still cap it. Which sentence is correct?
A "Despite the exposure is large, the contract caps it."
B "Despite the large exposure, the contract caps it."
C "Despite of the exposure be large, the contract caps it."
Three objections came back, and only one decides the deal. What do you call that one?
A The caveat.
B The common ground.
C The sticking point.
You accept the fee, but only if the grace period stays in writing. How do you say it?
A "We accept the fee, with one caveat: the grace period stays in writing."
B "We accept the fee, with one common ground: the grace period stays in writing."
C "We soften the fee, and the grace period stays in writing."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Think of one objection somebody made to your work this month: a committee, a regulator, a client or another team. Answer it out loud in two halves. First say which part of the objection is valid and that you have taken it on board. Then turn, with although, even though or however, and give your strongest fact. Finish with the condition you can offer and the next step. Take your time.
