The World Beyond Work -- A Story With Two Layers

The five o’clock alarm, the tennis, the school run, four or five trips a year. Key words: to wind down, to carve out time, a knack, to keep something up, to burn out, to take something up, to be hooked, a getaway, the off-season, to make up for something, to be second nature, a ritual. Structure: past perfect and past perfect continuous.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To wind down -- to let yourself slow down after a long stretch of effort
"It takes me about an hour to wind down after the girls are asleep."
To carve out time -- expression: to create time for something by protecting it from everything else
"You do not find an hour. You carve it out and defend it."
A knack -- a natural ability to do something well, without much effort
"My older daughter has a knack for languages that I certainly did not have."
To keep something up -- to go on doing something regularly, over a long period
"I have kept the five o’clock start up since the army, which is over fifteen years."
To burn out -- to become exhausted by effort you kept up for too long
"I do not think you burn out from working hard. You burn out from never stopping."
To take something up -- to start doing an activity or a hobby
"I took tennis up again about four years ago and I play twice a week."
To be hooked -- to reach the point where you cannot easily stop doing something you enjoy
"She swam once at the club and she was hooked."
A getaway -- a short trip taken to get away, usually a few days
"We do two long trips and about three getaways a year."
The off-season -- the period when the busiest part of the year stops
"Nothing at the plant moves in the off-season, so that is when we travel."
To make up for something -- to do something that compensates for something else that was missed
"There is no making up for a Tuesday you were not there. You just do not miss the next one."
To be second nature -- expression: so familiar that you do it without thinking about it
"After fifteen years the early start is second nature. Sunday included."
A ritual -- a small thing you do the same way every time, for what it means rather than what it achieves
"The school run is not logistics. It is a ritual, and I do not give it up."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A parent gets to the pool forty minutes late and the race is over. Told in the plain past, that is a list: I was on the phone, I arrived, she swam. Told properly, it has a second layer. By the time I got to the pool, she had already swum her race. The had plus the past participle puts one event behind the other and tells you, in the verb, which came first. Nothing else in the sentence has to carry the order.

The other half of the pair does a different job. We had been driving for three hours when the rain started. Here the earlier thing was not finished — it was still going on and ran into the moment. That is the past perfect continuous: had been plus a verb with -ing, and it is the natural form with for and since. She had been asking to go back all winter says how long, up to the point where the story turns.

Three practical notes. The negative works the same way: I had not played tennis for eleven years, she had never swum anywhere without a wall. State verbs stay simple even for duration: we had had the car for two days, never had been having. And the whole structure lives with a small family of time words — by the time, already, never, before, when. A Portuguese speaker can tell the same story in the plain past and be understood perfectly. It simply arrives flat, because everything in it happened at the same distance from the listener.

1. Why is "By the time I got to the pool, she had already swum" better than "I arrived and she swam"?
A Because it is more formal.
B Because the verb itself carries the order, so nothing else has to.
C Because "swim" cannot be used in the simple past.
2. What is the difference between "had swum" and "had been swimming"?
A The first is finished before the moment; the second was still going on into it.
B The first is formal and the second is informal.
C There is no difference.
3. Which is correct with a state verb?
A We had been having the car for two days.
B We had had the car for two days.
C We were having the car for two days.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Perfect and Past Perfect Continuous

GRAMMAR

A story has two layers. The lower one says what had already happened, and what had been going on.

JobFormExample
Finished before another past momenthad + past participleShe had already swum her race.
Still going on up to that momenthad been + verb-ingWe had been driving for three hours.
How long, up to a past momenthad been + -ing + for / sinceShe had been asking all winter.
Negative and neverhad not / had never + participleI had never seen her swim a whole race.
Common time wordsby the time, already, never, when, beforeBy the time we arrived, it had finished.
State verbsbe, have, know: no -ing even for durationWe had had the car for two days.
The trapthe plain past is not enough when order mattersNot when we arrived she already swam. Say she had already swum.

Never: when we arrived she already swam · we were driving for three hours when the rain started · I had never play tennis before · we had been having the car for two days · by the time we got there she has already swum.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You arrived after the race had finished. You say:
A "When I arrived she already swam her race."
B "By the time I arrived she had already swum her race."
C "When I arrived she was swimming her race already."
You want to say how long you had been driving before it started raining. You say:
A "We were driving for three hours when the rain started."
B "We had been driving for three hours when the rain started."
C "We drove three hours and then the rain started."
You want to say you had not done something for eleven years. You say:
A "I had not played tennis for eleven years."
B "I had never play tennis for eleven years."
C "I was not playing tennis since eleven years."
Somebody asks why you travel in the off-season. The strongest answer is:
A "Because it is cheaper at that time of year."
B "Because the plant is quiet then, and it is the only window that is ours."
C "We travel when it is possible, more or less."
