Getting Around -- Asking for Information on the Street

A corner in a city you do not know, a dead phone, and a stranger with thirty seconds. Key words: a block, an intersection, to head, downtown, a detour, within walking distance, a transit card, a fare, to transfer, to get off, to miss, around the corner. Structure: indirect questions and polite requests.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A block -- the distance from one street corner to the next
"It is three blocks north, on your right."
An intersection -- the place where two streets cross each other
"Turn left at the second intersection."
To head -- to go in a particular direction
"Head north for two blocks and you will see it."
Downtown -- the central business area of an American city
"This train goes downtown. The other platform goes out."
A detour -- a longer way round, when the direct way is closed
"There is a detour this week because of the construction."
Within walking distance -- close enough to reach on foot
"The hospital is within walking distance of the apartment."
A transit card -- a card you load with money to use buses and trains
"Buy a transit card at the machine. It is cheaper than paying each time."
A fare -- the money you pay for one journey
"The fare is two dollars and fifty cents, whatever the distance."
To transfer -- to change from one line to another and keep going
"Take the red line and transfer at Fullerton."
To get off -- to leave a bus or a train
"Get off at the third stop, right after the bridge."
To miss -- to fail to catch something, or to go past it without noticing
"I missed my stop because I was reading."
Around the corner -- very close, just past the next corner
"The pharmacy is right around the corner from here."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways to ask a stranger for help, and the difference between them is not politeness for its own sake. Where is the station? is correct English. Excuse me, could you tell me where the station is? is the one that makes a person in a hurry actually stop.

Look at what happens inside. In the direct question the verb jumps in front of the subject: where is the station. The moment you put a wrapper in front, that inversion disappears and the words go back to normal order: could you tell me where the station is. It is the same with yes-or-no questions, which take if: does this bus stop at the museum becomes do you know if this bus stops at the museum -- and notice that does vanished and the -s went back onto the verb.

The third form is for asking somebody to do something rather than tell you something: would you mind + the -ing form. Would you mind repeating that. Not to repeat. This is the single most useful sentence in the language for anybody living abroad, because it asks for a repetition without saying anything about your English.

And then the answer, which is the other half of the problem. Americans give distance in blocks, not metres, and direction in compass points: head north two blocks, turn at the second intersection, it is around the corner from the pharmacy. On the train they will tell you where to transfer, where to get off, what the fare is, and whether there is a detour. None of that is difficult vocabulary. It only sounds fast.

2. "Do you know if this bus stops at the museum?" What happened to does?

B It moved to the end of the sentence.

3. Why does the text call "would you mind repeating that" the most useful sentence for somebody living abroad?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions and Polite Requests

GRAMMAR

Put a wrapper in front and the question inside goes back to being an ordinary sentence: no inversion, and no do, does or did.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You stop a stranger on a corner in Chicago. Your first sentence is:
A Where is the station, please?
B Excuse me, could you tell me where the nearest station is?
C Sorry, my English is bad. Station?
You did not catch the answer. You say:
A Would you mind to repeat that?
B Sorry, I do not understand English very well.
C Would you mind repeating that a little more slowly?
You want to know whether the bus stops at the hospital. You ask:
A Do you know if this bus stops at the hospital?
B Do you know if this bus does stop at the hospital?
C Do you know where is the hospital stop?
You have gone two stops too far. You say:
A I lost my stop. Where I go now?
B I think I missed my stop. Could you tell me where I should get off?
C This train is wrong. I need to return.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

You have missed your stop, your phone is dead, you are late for something that matters, and the person you stop is in a hurry. In two minutes, find out where you are, how to get back, how long it will take, and whether it costs anything extra. Every question wrapped, none with inversion inside.
