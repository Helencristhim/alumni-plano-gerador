Chapter 4: If and What If

Turning a Claim Into a Condition

Six words that make a supplier stop selling and start planning

Grammar Discovery

What Happens After “If”?

"If the integration runs past sixteen weeks, what happens to the fee?"

"If the volume triples next year, the onboarding team will need two more people."

"That could work for the first corridor, but it might not survive the audit."

"What would you need from my side, and what should we agree before the pilot?"

In the green lines, look at the verb straight after if — and at what is missing there. In the red ones, nobody is promising anything yet.

JobFormExample
A real possibility, and its consequenceIf + present simple, will + verbIf the volume triples, we will need two more people.
A real possibility, as a questionIf + present simple, questionIf the integration runs past sixteen weeks, what happens to the fee?
Possible, not certaincould · might + bare verbThat could work for the first corridor. It might not survive the audit.
Hypothetical, and therefore politewould + bare verbWhat would you need from my side?
A recommendationshould + bare verbWe should agree the exit terms before the pilot.
The trapno will straight after ifNot if the volume will triple. Say if the volume triples.
The other trapno to after a modalNot it could to work. Say it could work.

In one line: a claim you cannot turn into an "if" sentence is a claim the supplier has not tested either.

Grammar Practice

Which Form, and Why?

Say it first, then click to check

“If the integration ___runs past sixteen weeks, what happens to the fee? (run — after if, no will)”
“If the volume triples, we ___will need two more people. (need — the consequence)”
“That ___could work for the first corridor. (work — possible, not certain)”
“What ___would you need from my side? (you / need — hypothetical, and polite)”
“If the regulator ___changes the reporting rule in March, who absorbs the work? (change — after if)”
“We ___should agree the exit terms before the pilot. (agree — a recommendation)”

Chapter 5: Real Speed

Both Sides of the Table

The buyer, the seller, and the page between them

Listening 1 · The Buyer

The Four Questions, Every Time

Somebody who has sat through two hundred vendor pitches. Sound first — no text.

1. What are his first two questions?
What exactly happens on day one if they say yes, meaning the first two weeks rather than the vision. And which existing client is closest to them in size and regulation, and whether he can call them.
2. What is his third question, and why does he ask it?
What breaks first when the volume triples. He says every system breaks somewhere and he would rather hear it from the vendor than find it himself.
3. Which question does he call the most useful in the room, and why?
What the vendor would need from his side that they have not asked for yet, because it moves them from selling to planning.
Listening 2 · The Seller

What Your Questions Tell Him About You

Somebody who sells to banks for a living, being unusually direct. Sound first — no text.

1. What happens on his side when a bank asks for a reference client in the same regulatory environment?
He knows the deal is real, and he takes it to his head of delivery that evening.
2. What does he do when a bank asks only about price in the first meeting, and why?
He quotes high, because price is the only thing they have given him to compete on.
3. What does he admit about the question on what breaks at three times the volume?
That half of his competitors cannot answer it at all.
Listening 3 · The Pattern

Where the Decision Actually Goes Wrong

A short piece on what the reviews of failed procurement always find. Sound first — no text.

1. Where does the piece say the decision goes wrong?
At the point where the buying side accepts a claim without turning it into a condition.
2. What two examples of unquestioned claims does it give?
Integration typically takes twelve weeks, and the platform scales. Nobody asks what would make it twenty, or scales to what.
3. What does the piece say the fix is not, and what is it?
It is not scepticism and it is not more meetings. It is one habit: turning every claim into a sentence beginning with if.
Real Document

The Page They Hand You

NORTHGATE PAYMENTS · PROPOSAL
prepared for Bradesco · Global Trade and Finance
SolutionSettlement layer for cross-border corridors
IntegrationTypically 12 weeks via documented API
OnboardingComplete within one month of go-live
CapacityScales to meet demand
Proof of concept6 weeks, 2 corridors, at our cost
Return on investment18 months
Illustrative document, written for this lesson. Three of these six lines cannot be checked as written. Find them.
1. Which three lines cannot be checked as written?
"Typically 12 weeks" (typically describes the good case only), "Scales to meet demand" (to what, and what happens at the limit) and "18 months" (a promise built on assumptions that are not shown).
2. Turn the integration line into a question beginning with if.
"If the integration runs past sixteen weeks, what happens to the fee, and who covers the extra work?"
3. Turn the capacity line into a question beginning with if.
"If our volume triples in year two, what breaks first, and at what point do we have to move to a different plan?"

Rebuild

Role-Play 1 of 3 — Guided

The Vendor Meeting

Scenario: A fintech is pitching a trade finance solution to your division. Listen to the pitch, then ask the four questions: day one, a reference client, what breaks at three times the volume, and what they would need from you that they have not asked for.

Keyword chips:

What happens on day one if we say yes? Which client is closest to us? If the volume triples, what breaks first? What would you need from my side?
Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

When the Answer Is “It Depends”

Scenario: The same meeting, but every answer you get is vague. Ask again, politely, and turn each vague answer into something you can write down: a number, a date, or a name. Do not move on until you have one of the three.

Keyword chips:

Could you give me a number for that? What would have to be true for that? If that slips, who absorbs it? Can we put that in the document?
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Two Minutes, No Keywords

Scenario: A new vendor, a new page, two minutes. Say what your requirements are before they pitch, then question the three claims that cannot be checked. Every question in the form of a condition. Finish by saying what you would need in writing before a second meeting.

No keywords, no notes, two minutes.
