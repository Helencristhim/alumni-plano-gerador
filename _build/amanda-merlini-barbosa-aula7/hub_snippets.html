Stage 1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Four words for the change, four for what happens to the work. Listen to each one and read it in context.

career shift -- a change of direction inside a professional life
"A career shift is not a promotion: it is a different road."
scope of practice -- the areas of law a person actually works in
"My scope of practice used to be contracts. Now half of it is regulatory."
steep learning curve -- a period when a lot has to be learned very fast
"It is a steep learning curve, and that is exactly why they wanted somebody internal."
to take on -- to accept a new responsibility and add it to what you already have
"I took on the environmental file before it had a name."
to phase out -- to stop doing something slowly, over a period, instead of all at once
"We are phasing out the routine contracts over three months."
to hand over -- to pass a matter, with everything it needs, to the person who continues it
"I will hand over the supply contracts with a template and a short note."
previously -- before now, in an earlier stage of the story
"Previously, the environmental work sat with an outside firm."
at the time -- back then, when the situation was different from now
"At the time, nobody in the company had heard of a carbon credit."

Stage 3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

Every professional who changes direction has to tell the same story twice a week for about a year, and in English that story needs three expressions that look almost identical and mean completely different things.

The first is used to plus an infinitive, and it describes a habit or a state that is over. "I used to spend two days a week on supply contracts." The sentence carries its own contrast: if the habit still existed, you would not say it this way. Two details are worth remembering. In the question and the negative, the d disappears: "Did you use to work with land law?" and "I did not use to work with it." And there is no present version of this expression: for a habit you still have, the present simple does the job.

The second is be used to, followed by a noun or an -ing form, and it means something is already familiar. "I am used to long documents." There is no effort left in it. It is not about the past at all: it is about how the thing feels to you now.

The third is get used to, and it is the process of the second one happening. "I am getting used to reading technical annexes." Not comfortable yet, but moving. This is the one that describes a steep learning curve honestly, without pretending the work is easy and without sounding lost.

Put together, they tell the whole arc. What you used to do, what you are used to, and what you are getting used to. Add the words for the mechanics of the change — to take on, to phase out, to hand over, previously, at the time — and a year of professional life fits into two minutes.

1. What happens to the d in the question and the negative of "used to"?

Stage 4: Grammar Tip -- used to, be used to, get used to

GRAMMAR

Three expressions, almost the same words. What separates them is what comes after, and what each one is about.

FormMeaningExample
used to + infinitivea habit or a state in the past, now overI used to spend two days a week on contracts.
be used to + noun / -ingalready familiar: no effort leftI am used to reading long documents.
get used to + noun / -ingbecoming familiar: still in progressI am getting used to the annexes.
Question and negativethe d disappears: "Did you use to...?" and "I did not use to..."
No present formfor a habit you still have, use the present simple: "I spend half my week on regulatory work", never "I use to spend".
The pronunciation"used to" is two syllables with a hard s: YOOS-tuh. The ordinary verb ("I used your template") has a soft s and a clear d.

Quick test before you speak: is this the past, something already easy, or something becoming easy?
