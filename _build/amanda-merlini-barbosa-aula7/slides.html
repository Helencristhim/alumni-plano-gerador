Chapter 3: The Handover

What Leaves Your Desk

A handover conversation with the lawyer who takes it, line by line

Dialogue

Handing Over the Old Work

D
So these are all mine now? That folder is bigger than my first year here.
A
Not all at once. We are phasing out my side of it over three months, and you get the template first.
D
All right. How much of your week did this take?
A
I used to spend two days a week on supply contracts. Now it is closer to half a day, and next quarter it will be none.
D
Two days. And the disputes? Am I taking those too?
A
No, the disputes stay with me. You are used to reading a contract, but you have not seen how these particular buyers behave when they stop paying.
D
Fair. And what is taking the two days now?
A
Land, permits and a thirty-year concession. Previously that work sat with an outside firm, and at the time nobody here thought we would ever do it ourselves.
D
Honestly, that sounds harder than what you are giving me. Is it?
A
It is a steep learning curve, and I am still getting used to reading technical annexes. But it is the direction the work is going, so I would rather learn it now.
Comprehension

About the Handover

1. What is Diego's first reaction, and how is he reassured?
The folder is bigger than his first year. He is told it is not all at once: three months, with the template first.
2. Why do the disputes stay where they are?
Because he is used to reading contracts, but he has not seen how these particular buyers behave when they stop paying.
3. Where did the environmental work sit before, and what was thought about it at the time?
With an outside firm, and nobody in the company thought they would ever do it in-house.
Chapter 4: The Code

Three Look-Alikes, Three Meanings

used to · be used to · get used to

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I used to spend two days a week on supply contracts."

"I am used to reading long contracts."

"I am getting used to reading technical annexes."

"I did not use to work with land law at all."

One is about the past. One is about something already easy. One is about something becoming easy. Which is which?

FormMeaningExample
used to + infinitivea habit or a state in the past that is over nowI used to spend two days a week on contracts.
be used to + noun or -ingit is already familiar: no effort left in itI am used to reading long contracts.
get used to + noun or -ingthe process of becoming familiar: not finished yetI am getting used to the annexes.
Question and negativethe d disappears: "Did you use to work with land law?" and "I did not use to work with it."
Only the pastthere is no present form of this used to. For a present habit, use the present simple: "I spend half my week on regulatory work."
Why it matters herea career that changed direction is told with all three: what you used to do, what you are used to, and what you are getting used to.

Sound Check

Say It Clearly

used to, said as two syllables · the sentences of this lesson

Why this one is hard for a Portuguese speaker

The habit: reading the spelling and saying three separate words, with a clear d and a long to.

In English: the whole thing is two syllables and the s is hard, like in use the noun. Think "YOOS-tuh", glued to the verb that follows.

The pair worth keeping: "I used to send it" and "I used your template" are spelled the same and sound different. Say both, twice.

Chapter 5: Real Speed

The Decision and the Precedent

What the board decided, and what it is like on the other side of the move

Listening 1

The Board Approved It

Karin calls with a good problem. Sound first, no text.

1. What changed on Tuesday, and what does it mean for the job?
The board approved the project, so the environmental work stops being a side file and becomes part of the job description.
2. What is Karin's suggestion about the routine supply contracts and about the disputes?
The routine contracts go to the team with a template and a short note; the disputes stay, because nobody else has the history.
3. Why does she say the board wanted Amanda and not an outside firm?
Precisely because it is a steep learning curve and she did not use to work with land law — they wanted somebody internal who learns it.
Listening 2

Somebody Who Did It Twelve Years In

A lawyer describes changing area after twelve years. Sound first, no text.

1. How does he describe the first year, and why does he say it is not a complaint?
Humiliating, meant as a technical description: he used to be the person who knew the answer, and became the person asking what a word meant.
2. What happens to the old knowledge, according to him?
It does not disappear, it changes function: he reads every new contract with twelve years of disputes behind his eyes.
3. What is the third part, the one nobody tells you?
You never stop feeling new; you get used to feeling new. When that stops being uncomfortable, the move is finished.
Real Document

The Handover Note

HANDOVER NOTE · LEGAL DEPARTMENT
internal page · sample data
Routine supply contracts (template exists)moves to the team · 3 months
Renewals with existing buyersmoves to the team · immediately
Open disputes (3 files, one abroad)stays — history not transferable
Employment mattersoutside firm, unchanged
Concession and permits (new)stays — growing
Weekly time previously on contracts2 days → half a day → none
Sample data for this lesson. Nothing here is a real department record.
1. Two lines move immediately and one moves over three months. Why the difference?
The renewals follow an existing template, so they can move at once. The routine contracts still need the template and a note, so they are phased out.
2. Read the last line out loud as a full sentence, using used to.
Example: "I used to spend two days a week on contracts; now it is half a day, and next quarter it will be none."
3. Which line would be a mistake to hand over, and why?
The open disputes: the history of how those buyers behave is not in any file, so it cannot be transferred with a template.
