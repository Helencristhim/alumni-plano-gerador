Lesson 14 · The Description

The Person Who Decides

Every meeting starts with somebody explaining who is in the room. Doing that in English needs one small word, and choosing the wrong one changes who you are talking about.

Chapter 1: The Description

Describe One Person You Work With

One colleague, in two sentences. Not their job title -- what they are actually like to work with.

Where You Start

Which One, or Extra Information

Which one

"The client who signed last week is coming." -- there are several clients

Extra information

"Mr Prado, who signed last week, is coming." -- there is only one Mr Prado

The difference

Two commas, and the meaning of the sentence changes

This is the structure that lets you say exactly which person, which fund, which meeting, without starting a new sentence for each one. Without it every description becomes three short sentences and everybody stops listening after the second.

Chapter 3: The Code

Who, Which and That

One small word, and two commas that change the meaning

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The client who signed last week is coming on Thursday."

"Mr Prado, who signed last week, is coming on Thursday."

"This is the fund which pays quarterly."

"The report I sent yesterday has the numbers."

The first two sentences look almost identical and mean different things. One tells you which client; the other adds a fact about a client you already know. What is doing that work?

FormWhat it saysExample
who for people, which for thingsThe small word replaces the noun and starts the description."the client who signed"
"the fund which pays quarterly"
that for bothInformal and extremely common in speech. It works for people and things, but only without commas."the client that signed"
"the fund that pays"
No commas: which oneThe description identifies the person or thing. Remove it and nobody knows who you mean."The client who signed last week is coming."
Two commas: extra informationYou already know who it is. The description is a bonus, and the sentence survives without it."Mr Prado, who signed last week, is coming."
Never use that here.
Dropping the wordWhen the description has its own subject, you can leave who, which or that out entirely. Very common in speech."the report I sent yesterday"
= the report that I sent yesterday

One test for the commas: read the sentence without the description. If you still know who or what it is about, the commas belong there. If you do not, they do not.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"The client which signed last week is coming."
"The client who signed last week is coming."
"Mr Prado, that signed last week, is coming."
"Mr Prado, who signed last week, is coming."
"The report that I sent it yesterday has the numbers."
"The report that I sent yesterday has the numbers."

Who is for people and which for things. That replaces either one, but never after a comma. And the small word already stands for the noun, so the sentence does not need it as well — the report that I sent, never the report that I sent it. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Comprehension

About Sonia

1. Who decides, according to Sonia?
The woman who sits in the middle.
2. How does she describe the woman's questions?
Thorough.
3. Who else is coming in?
The woman's brother, who is easygoing, and a lawyer they bring to everything.
4. What is her warning?
That the woman comes across as cold and is not, so he should not fill the silence.
Listening

The One Who Decides

A short radio segment. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does the speaker say is the most common mistake before a meeting?
Preparing for the person who invited you rather than the person who decides.
2. How does she say you can usually identify the decision maker?
They speak least, and everybody looks at them before answering.
3. What does she recommend doing in the first two minutes?
Working out who the questions are really for, and answering to that person.
Listening 2

Three People and One Lawyer

A voice message before a meeting. Sound first -- no text.

1. Who is the person who signs?
The sister, who has signed everything for eleven years.
2. What does he say about the brother?
That he is easygoing and asks the friendliest questions, and that they are not the ones that matter.
3. What does he say about the lawyer?
That she says almost nothing, and that the two questions she asks are always the difficult ones.

The Habit

One Person, Out Loud

Before the next class, describe one real person out loud each day in two sentences, and force one who or which into each description. Colleagues, clients, neighbours, anybody.

Two short sentences about a person are easy. One longer sentence that joins them is what makes you sound fluent, and it costs one word.
