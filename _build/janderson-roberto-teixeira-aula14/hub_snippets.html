Describing People and Things Precisely -- Who, Which and Two Commas

How to say exactly which person or which thing you mean, in one sentence instead of three. Key expressions: straightforward, demanding, down to earth, to get on with, sharp, easygoing, to come across as, thorough, to stand out, blunt. Structure: defining and non-defining relative clauses.

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These ten stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

Straightforward -- simple, clear and easy to deal with
"He is straightforward, which makes everything faster."
Demanding -- needing a lot of time, attention or effort
"She is demanding, but she is never unfair about it."
Down to earth -- practical and sensible, with no pretension
"He is completely down to earth for somebody with that much money."
To get on with -- to have a good relationship with somebody
"I get on with everybody in that team except one person."
Sharp -- quick to understand and to notice things
"She is very sharp, so do not send her half an answer."
Easygoing -- relaxed and not easily upset
"The brother is easygoing and the sister decides everything."
To come across as -- to give a particular impression, correct or not
"He comes across as cold and he is not."
Thorough -- careful and complete, leaving nothing out
"Her questions are thorough, so prepare the second page too."
To stand out -- to be clearly different or better than the rest
"One thing stood out in that meeting, and it was not the numbers."
Blunt -- saying what you think directly, sometimes too directly
"He is blunt, which people either like immediately or never."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before any meeting that matters, somebody spends two minutes explaining who will be in the room, and those two minutes decide more than the first half hour does. There is usually one person who decides, one who signs, and one who asks the difficult questions, and they are almost never the same person.

In the family Janderson deals with, the sister decides. She is sharp, her questions are thorough, and she comes across as cold in the first ten minutes and is not. The brother, who is easygoing and completely down to earth, asks the friendliest questions in the room, and none of them are the ones that matter. There is also a lawyer they bring to everything, who says almost nothing and whose two questions are always the hardest.

Describing that in Portuguese takes Janderson about fifteen seconds. Describing it in English used to take him three separate sentences, one per person, and by the third one the other side had stopped listening. That is not a vocabulary problem. It is one small word, repeated: the one who decides, the lawyer that they bring, the report I sent yesterday.

The commas do the rest. The client who signed last week is coming tells you which client, out of several. Mr Prado, who signed last week, is coming tells you something extra about a man you already know. Same words, two commas, two different sentences — and in a room where somebody is deciding about money, that difference is not decoration.

1. "The client who signed last week is coming." What is the description doing here?
A Adding an extra fact about a client we already know.
B Telling you which client, out of several.
C Replacing the client's name.
2. Why is it "the lawyer that they bring" and not "the lawyer, that they bring"?
A Because that never follows a comma.
B Because lawyers are things, not people.
C Because the sentence is informal.
3. According to the text, what was Janderson's problem in English?
A He did not know enough vocabulary to describe people.
B He needed three separate sentences where one would do, and people stopped listening.
C He described the wrong people.
4. Who asks the hardest questions, according to the second paragraph?
A The brother.
B The lawyer, who says almost nothing.
C The sister's assistant.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

One small word joins two sentences into one, and two commas decide whether the description identifies somebody or simply adds a fact about them.

FormWhat it saysExample
who / whichWho for people, which for things."the client who signed"
"the fund which pays quarterly"
thatWorks for both, and it is what people actually say. Never after a comma."the client that signed"
No commas = which oneThe description identifies. Remove it and the sentence stops making sense."The client who signed last week is coming."
Two commas = extra informationYou already know who it is. The sentence survives without the description."Mr Prado, who signed last week, is coming."
Dropping the wordIf the description has its own subject, the relative word can disappear."the report I sent yesterday"
where and whoseFor places and for possession."the room where we met"
"the client whose brother signs"

The comma test, in five seconds: read the sentence without the description. If you still know who it is about, the commas belong. If you do not, they do not.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are describing people before a meeting. Choose the best thing to say.

You want to say which client, out of several:
A The client which signed last week is coming.
B The client who signed last week is coming.
C The client, who signed last week, is coming.
You are adding a fact about a man everybody knows:
A Mr Prado, that signed last week, is coming.
B Mr Prado, who signed last week, is coming.
C Mr Prado who signed last week is coming.
You are talking about a document:
A The report that I sent it yesterday.
B The report I sent yesterday.
C The report which I sent it yesterday.
You want to warn a colleague about a first impression:
A She comes across as cold, and she is not.
B She passes an impression of cold person.
C She seems to be cold but this is not the reality of the fact.
You want to say who you should be talking to:
A Who is the person that decides here?
B Who is the person which decide here?
C Who is the person, that decides here?
