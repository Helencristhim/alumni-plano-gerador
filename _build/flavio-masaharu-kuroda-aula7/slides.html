Chapter 3: The Code

What Always Happens

The structure of every process description in English

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"When a file arrives, the system checks the fields."

"If a field is missing, the system flags the invoice."

"Once the team approves it, the two systems sync."

"As soon as the run fails, one alert goes to one person."

Look at the red verbs on both sides of each sentence. Not one of them is in the future, and every sentence is about something that happens every single time.

JobFormExample
Something that always happenswhen / if + present, presentWhen a file arrives, the system checks it.
Never the future after these wordswhen, if, once, as soon as, after, before, untilNot when a file will arrive
Order of the stepsfirst, then, after that, finallyFirst the file arrives. Then it is checked.
One step ends, the next startsonce / as soon asOnce it is approved, the systems sync.
The clause can come secondthen no commaThe system checks it when a file arrives.
Third person keeps its -son both sidesOnce the team approves it, the run starts.
No then after whenthe comma does that jobNot When it arrives, then the system checks
When vs ifwhen = it always happens · if = it may notWhen the file arrives · If a field is missing

In one line: describing a process is present on both sides, and there is no future in it anywhere.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Explanation, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one page

Dialogue

Explaining It to Ingrid

I
I am not technical, and I am going to stop you the moment I stop understanding. How does it actually work?
F
Five steps. When a supplier file arrives, that triggers the process. Nobody presses anything.
I
And if the file is wrong? Because half of them are.
F
Then step two. The system reads nine fields, and if one of them is missing it flags the invoice instead of rejecting it. A person decides, not the system.
I
Which person? That matters more to me than the software.
F
Whoever you name. As soon as an invoice is flagged, one alert goes to that one person, with the invoice number in it. Step three: once they approve it, the two systems sync.
I
Is that immediate?
F
No, and I would rather be honest about it. It runs once a night, in one batch. If the run fails, we roll back and nothing half-finished reaches your ERP. Step five is the only one people ask about twice: every invoice can be traced from the supplier to the payment.
Comprehension

About Ingrid

1. What does Ingrid say in her first line, and what is she warning him about?
That she is not technical and will stop him the moment she stops understanding. She is warning him that jargon will not survive this call.
2. Which of her questions is not about the software at all?
Which person decides when an invoice is flagged. She says it matters more to her than the software, because it is her team's time.
3. He answers is that immediate with a plain no. Why does that answer help him?
Because a yes would be found out at the first month-end. Saying no once a night, with the reason, makes everything else he says easier to believe.
Listening 1 · No Jargon

The Test She Uses on Every Sentence

A solutions engineer on explaining a system without opening it. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the test she applies to every sentence she says?
Whether the person listening could repeat it to somebody else an hour later. If they could not, the sentence is hers, not theirs.
2. What does she say about people who use a lot of technical words?
That the ones who understand a system best use the fewest technical words, and that jargon is usually a place to hide.
3. How many steps does she say an explanation should have, and why?
Five at most, one verb each, because that is how many somebody can hold in their head while they are also deciding whether to trust you.
Listening 2 · From the Buyer

What He Actually Needs to Understand

A finance director on the demos he sits through. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say he never needs to know, and what does he need instead?
He never needs to know how it works. He needs to know what happens when it does not work, and who finds out first.
2. What does he say about a vendor who says everything is in real time?
That he stops listening, because almost nothing is, and the claim tells him the person is repeating a sentence rather than describing a system.
3. What does he say is the question he asks that ends most demos?
Show me what my team sees on a bad morning. He says most people have no answer, because the demo was built for a good morning.
Real Document

The Page You Would Draw on a Napkin

HOW IT WORKS — ONE PAGE
five steps · one verb each
Step 1A supplier file arrives and triggers the run
Step 2Nine fields are read; a missing one is flagged
Step 3One alert goes to one named person
Step 4Once it is approved, the systems sync overnight
Step 5Every invoice can be traced end to end
If the run failsThe batch is rolled back, nothing half-finished
Six lines, one verb each, and no word that needs a second explanation. The last line is not a step: it is the answer to the only question a finance director really asks, which is what happens on a bad morning. Notice that step 3 names a person and not a system, and that step 4 admits it is overnight rather than immediate.
1. Why does the note say the last line is not a step?
Because it does not happen in the normal run. It is what happens when the run fails, and it answers the question the buyer cares about most.
2. Step 3 names a person rather than a system. Why does that matter to the buyer?
Because a system that flags something without naming who acts on it just moves the problem. Naming the person is what turns a feature into a process they can run.
3. Step 4 says overnight instead of immediate. What does the honesty buy you?
It is found out at the first month-end anyway. Saying it once, early, makes the other four steps believable.
