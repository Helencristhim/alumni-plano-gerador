Lesson 12 -- Pre-class

The Live Wire -- Second Simulation, Unknown Evaluator

What a second evaluator is really scoring, and why the same answer given twice is the evidence. Key words: a scorecard, a red flag, a deal-breaker, to vet, due diligence, a reference check, to sound somebody out, a gut call, consistency, to hold up, a calibration meeting, to score. No new grammar: this is the second full simulation, with an evaluator you have not met.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words a headhunter uses on the phone. You will hear them far more often than you say them, so the target here is recognition at speed.

A scorecard -- the sheet a panel fills in while you are talking
“Every serious process has a scorecard, even when nobody shows it to you.”
A red flag -- the one thing that stops a process, however good the rest is
“For that panel, blaming a previous employer was the red flag.”
A deal-breaker -- the condition that ends the conversation if it is not met
“Relocation was never a deal-breaker for me, and I said so early.”
To vet -- to check somebody carefully before a decision
“They vet the final two candidates with people who are not in the room.”
Due diligence -- the checking a buyer or an employer does before committing
“Their due diligence took three weeks, and mine took two calls.”
A reference check -- the call a company makes to somebody you used to work with
“The reference check is where a good interview is confirmed or lost.”
To sound somebody out -- to ask informally, before anything is official
“The chairman sounded me out over coffee before the formal round.”
A gut call -- a decision made on instinct rather than on the sheet
“Half of these decisions are a gut call, and the sheet is there to slow that down.”
Consistency -- giving the same answer and the same numbers every time
“Consistency is what a second interviewer is really measuring.”
To hold up -- to stay true when somebody checks it
“My story held up, because it was the same story in both rounds.”
A calibration meeting -- the meeting where a panel compares its scores
“In the calibration meeting they discovered they had scored me for different things.”
To score -- to give a mark against a criterion
“They score structure, evidence and recovery, not accent.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The second round is where a process is usually lost, and not for the reason people think. The evaluator has the scorecard from the first conversation, and what they are testing is consistency: the same figures, the same story, the same ownership of the bad year.

I used to prepare new material for a second interview. It was the wrong instinct. Now I give the same three numbers, slowly, and let them write. If the first answer held up, saying it again is not lazy: it is the evidence.

What raises a red flag is easy to list, because it is always the same three things: blaming somebody else, a story that moves between rounds, and an answer that never ends. And at the end, when they ask what would make me say no to them, I give a real deal-breaker, because the polite answer tells them nothing.

1. What is the second evaluator mainly testing?
A Whether you have new material.
B Whether your story and your figures are the same as in the first round.
C Whether your accent has improved.
2. Why is repeating the same answer not lazy?
A Because interviewers do not remember the first round.
B Because the repetition is itself the evidence that the story holds up.
C Because there is no time to prepare anything else.
3. Which three things raise a red flag?
A Blaming somebody else, a story that moves, and an answer that never ends.
B Numbers, dates and names.
C Asking what they are scoring.
4. What is wrong with saying "there is nothing that would make me say no"?
A Nothing — it shows commitment.
B It suggests you have not thought about it, or that you would say anything.
C It is too specific.

Stage 1.4: What Holds Up in a Second Round

GRAMMAR

Nothing new to learn here: this is the checklist of what a second evaluator scores, and the five sentences that carry it.

OpeningWhat followsExample
Same figure, same wordsConsistency is a score line, not a style choiceIt is the same answer I gave in the first round: thirty-one per cent, and the reason has not changed.
Ask what is being scoredAn indirect question keeps it collaborativeCould I ask what you will be scoring today?
Name a real deal-breakerA true limit is more convincing than enthusiasmI would say no to this if the reporting line went through somebody else.
Recover out loudOne bad answer is normal; the recovery is the scoreLet me pick up the thread: I was on the second reason.
The trapDo not prepare NEW material for a second round. Prepare the SAME material, said more slowly.
Do not overdo itOne or two indirect questions sound senior. Five in a row sound uncertain. Mix them with direct questions once the room is warm.

This is the form you will use for the three closing questions — the part of the interview you control completely.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The last five minutes are yours. Choose the version that gives the panel a sentence to repeat about you.

A second evaluator asks a question you already answered in round one. You:
A Give the same answer, with the same figures.
B Give a new example, so it sounds fresh.
C Say that you covered it in the first round.
At the start of a final round, the most useful question you can ask is:
A "How many candidates are left?"
B "Could I ask what you will be scoring today?"
C "Is this interview going to be difficult?"
"What would make you say no to us?" The strongest answer is:
A "Nothing, I am completely committed."
B A real limit, named specifically, with the reason behind it.
C "It is too early for me to say."
One answer goes badly in the middle of the interview. The score depends on:
A Whether you apologise for it.
B What you do in the next two minutes.
C Whether you mention it again at the end.
Which of these is a red flag for almost every panel?
A Saying you do not know a figure and offering to send it.
B Explaining a bad year by blaming a previous employer.
C Asking what the next steps are.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is correcting this one, and there is no score.

Record the three figures you will give in a second interview: the margin, the cash conversion and the headcount, with the reason behind each one. One take, sixty seconds. Then wait a day, do not listen to it, and record the same three again from memory. Compare only one thing: did any figure or any reason change between the two takes? That difference is what a second evaluator is looking for.
