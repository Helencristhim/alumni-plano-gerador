Eleven Seconds to Three

D
Before I show you anything: what do you think has changed since the first session?
F
Honestly, not a great deal. I still hesitate, and I still notice every mistake I make.
D
Right. So here is the baseline. First recording: eleven seconds to your first word, and two pauses over twenty seconds. Last week: three seconds, and the longest pause was four.
F
That is not what it feels like from the inside.
D
It never is. Your standard has risen faster than your performance, so you are gauging yourself against something that keeps moving. That is not a plateau, it is arithmetic.

Comprehension

About Diane

1. Why does she ask her question before showing him anything?
So that his impression is on the table before the measurement contradicts it.
2. What are the two numbers she compares, and how did they change?
Seconds to the first word, from eleven to three; and the longest pause, from over twenty seconds to four.
3. What does she identify as the blind spot, and what does she suggest doing about it?
Only two answers in eight opened with a conclusion. She would sharpen exactly that between now and lesson nineteen.
Listening

Feeling Worse While Performing Better

A coach on the shape of the curve. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does improvement feel visible at the start and invisible later?
Early on the problems being fixed are obvious. Later what improves is speed of retrieval and recovery, which nobody experiences from the inside.
2. What is happening to the learner's own standard at the same time?
It is rising — they notice things they would not have noticed a month earlier — so they feel worse while performing better.
3. What does he say the answer is, and what is it not?
It is not trying harder. It is measuring something specific against a baseline, because feeling is a terrible instrument and a stopwatch is a good one.
Listening 2

Not More Practice. Narrower Practice

What deliberate practice actually means. Sound first — no text.

1. What three conditions does she give for deliberate practice?
Isolating the part you are worst at, repeating only that under slightly harder conditions, and feedback that arrives immediately.
2. What is the pianist example, and what is the equivalent for interview practice?
A pianist repeats the four bars that break, not the whole piece. The equivalent is the four seconds after a hard question, twenty times, with immediate feedback.
3. How does she describe it, and how much more efficient does she say it is?
Boring and uncomfortable — and roughly ten times more efficient than a pleasant hour of general conversation.
