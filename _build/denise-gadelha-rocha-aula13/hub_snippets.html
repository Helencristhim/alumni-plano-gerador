The Complete Reported Voice -- Relaying Decisions, Instructions and Conversations with Authority

What happens to a decision on its way from Milan to Bogotá. Key words: cascade, minutes, relay, verbatim, directive, convey, brief, attribute, read-out, water down, walk back, circulate. Structure: reported speech in full -- tenses, pronouns, time markers, indirect questions, and the reporting verbs that carry a judgement.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words for the part of your job that has no name yet: carrying a decision from the room where it was taken to the people who have to act on it.

To cascade -- to pass a decision down through each layer, so every level hears a version it can use
"We cascade it on Monday, region by region, and I take the country leads first."
Minutes -- the written record of what a meeting decided and who is accountable for each action
"The minutes say what we decided. They do not say why we decided it."
To relay -- to pass a message on from the person who gave it to the people who need it
"I am not the author of this decision. I am the person who relays it."
Verbatim -- word for word, exactly as it was said
"I will not repeat it verbatim. His sentence was built for an Italian committee."
Directive -- an instruction from above that is not open to negotiation
"Milan sent a directive, not a proposal, and I will not present it as one."
To convey -- to get the meaning across, not only the words
"The words travelled. What I failed to convey was the reasoning behind them."
To brief -- to give people the information they need before they have to act on it
"I briefed the three country leads separately before the group call."
To attribute -- to name the person a statement or a decision came from
"Do not attribute the timing to me. It was decided in Milan and it should be heard that way."
Read-out -- a short summary of what was said in a meeting, for the people who were not in the room
"You will have my read-out within the hour, and the minutes on Thursday."
To water down -- to weaken a message as you pass it on, usually to make it easier to deliver
"If I water it down for you, your team hears a suggestion and plans around a deadline that is not moving."
To walk back -- to soften or partly withdraw something you have already said in public
"I would rather deliver it once and hold it than walk it back next week."
To circulate -- to say openly that something is true, especially when it is not convenient
"The minutes are circulated on Thursday, once every lead has been briefed."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Pay attention to the verbs used to report what Milan said, and to what each one does.

Milan decided on Tuesday. Denise was not in the room, and neither was anybody who reports to her, which is the ordinary condition of her job. She had the minutes by Wednesday morning and a call with Rafael at nine. He wanted one thing before she started: was this a directive, or a proposal his team could still push on? She told him it was a directive, and that she was relaying it rather than opening it.

What Cornelli had actually said was that the pricing floor was final and that Group would review it in March. Denise did not repeat him verbatim — his sentence had been built for an Italian committee, not for a Colombian sales floor. She conveyed the force of it instead, and she chose her verb with care. He had not suggested that they hold the price. He had insisted, and she said so.

Two things she refused to do. She did not water down the message so that the call would go more easily, because a team that hears a suggestion plans around a deadline that is not moving. And she did not let Rafael attribute the timeline to her: Cornelli had acknowledged that the date was a Group constraint, and Rafael's leads deserved to know whose constraint it was. The read-out went out that afternoon. Before it did, she added the line most read-outs are missing: what the meeting did not decide.

1. In the room Cornelli said "the pricing floor is final". In her report it becomes "the pricing floor was final". Why does the verb move?
A After a reporting verb in the past, the tense goes one step back: is becomes was, will becomes would.
B Because she is no longer sure that it is final.
C Because the past tense is more polite in a corporate register.
2. Why does she deliberately avoid repeating Cornelli verbatim?
A Because she cannot remember his exact words.
B Because his sentence was built for an Italian committee. Repeating the words is not the same as conveying the force, and it is the force Rafael has to act on.
C Because quoting a superior directly is considered rude in English.
3. "He had not suggested. He had insisted." What is she choosing when she chooses between those two verbs?
A How the decision will be received: suggested tells the team the door is ajar and somebody will push on it, insisted tells them it is closed.
B Only a matter of style. Both verbs report the same fact and the effect is the same.
C Which tense the rest of the sentence has to take.
4. Why does she add a line about what the meeting did not decide?
A To show the region that Milan does not always have the answers.
B Because the minutes require a section for unresolved items.
C Because every gap she leaves gets filled by somebody's assumption, and three countries would otherwise invent three different answers and work from them.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech, in Full

GRAMMAR

Three things move together — the tense, the pronoun and the time marker. And then there is the verb you choose, which is not grammar at all.

MoveWhat happensExample
Tenses go one step backis → was · decided → had decided · will → would · can → could · must → had to"The floor is final." → He said the floor was final.
Pronouns move with the speakerthe I in the room becomes he in your report; you becomes we or they; my becomes his"I will send you my numbers." → He said he would send us his numbers.
Time and place move tootoday → that day · tomorrow → the following day · next week → the following week · here → there · this → that"We review it next week." → He said they would review it the following week.
Reported questions lose the inversionstatement word order, and no do / does / did. The wh- word stays at the front"What do you think?" → He asked me what I thought.
Yes-or-no questions take if / whetherwhether is the register of a board room; if is fine in speech"Have you briefed them?" → They asked whether we had briefed them.
say vs tellsay (that) takes no person; tell always takes a person. Never he said meHe said the floor was final. / He told me the floor was final.
No backshift when it is still truea standing rule may keep the present tense — but choose it deliberately, do not simply forget the moveHe said the mandate runs to December. (and it still does)
Patterns of the judgement verbseach one takes its own structureinsist that we hold (base form) · urge somebody to hold · warn somebody that · deny holding · acknowledge / concede that
Why it matters here"He said we should wait", "he suggested we wait", "he insisted we wait", "he conceded we should wait", "he claimed we should wait". Same meeting, same words, five different teams by Monday. The reporting verb is an editorial decision, and it belongs to the person relaying it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

Rafael asks whether the pricing decision is still open. Milan issued it as final. The strongest opening is:
A "It is a directive. Milan decided on Tuesday, and I am relaying it, not opening it."
B "I think they more or less decided it, but let us see how it develops."
C "They said maybe we should hold the price for now."
On Tuesday Cornelli said "the floor is final". On Thursday you report it to your team. You say:
A "He said me the floor was final."
B "He said the floor was final." (or "He told me the floor was final.")
C "He told the floor was final."
In the meeting he asked you: "What do you think of the forecast?" You report it as:
A "He asked me what did I think of the forecast."
B "He asked me what I thought of the forecast."
C "He asked me what do I think of the forecast."
Milan pushed hard for the delay and refused every regional exception. Reporting that to your team, the honest verb is:
A "He suggested that we delay it."
B "He insisted that we delay it."
C "He mentioned a possible delay."
Your team will not like the deadline. Which of these costs you the most?
A Delivering it at full strength even though the call becomes uncomfortable.
B Watering it down so the call goes well — your team then hears a suggestion and plans around a deadline that is not moving.
C Attributing the decision to Milan so that nobody argues with you personally.
Your country leads were not in the Milan meeting and have to act today. The minutes arrive on Thursday. You send:
A Nothing, until the minutes have been approved and circulated.
B A read-out the same day — a short summary of what was said and what was left open, for the people who were not in the room.
C The full recording of the meeting, so nobody can accuse you of editing it.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

Take a decision you had to pass on to your team in the last month. In ninety seconds: report what was said, using a verb that matches the force it actually had; report one question you were asked; and name what was left undecided. Then answer the harder question out loud: did you choose that verb because it was true, or because it made the call easier?
