Lesson 13 -- The Complete Reported Voice

Series
Suits -- Season 4, Episode 1

Almost nobody in this firm ever hears a decision first-hand. Somebody walks out of a partner's office and tells somebody else what was said, and the sentence quietly changes shape on the way. Watch one episode only for that: every time a character reports a conversation you did not see, ask what verb they chose and what it does to the person listening.

Tip: write down three reported sentences and the verb used in each — told, insisted, made it clear, hinted. Then rewrite one of them with a softer verb and decide what would have happened next in the story.

Podcast
Think Fast, Talk Smart -- the episodes on communicating change and delivering hard messages

This is the closest thing there is to training for the position you sit in: the person who did not take the decision but has to make three countries act on it. The Stanford guests are mostly people who have delivered unpopular instructions and survived them, and they are unusually specific about the wording that holds and the wording that invites a negotiation you cannot win.

Tip: listen for the moment a guest reports what somebody else said. Note the reporting verb, then ask yourself whether you would have used a softer one in Spanish. That gap is what this lesson is about.

YouTube
Nancy Duarte -- The Common Structure of the Greatest Communicators (TEDxEast)

Duarte spends the talk doing what you will do on Thursday: taking something a person said in one room and re-presenting it to a room that was not there. Watch how much of her work is quotation — she reports other people's sentences constantly, and each time she frames them before she delivers them, so the audience knows how to receive the words before it hears them.

Tip: notice that she never drops a quotation in cold. There is always a verb and a frame first. Steal that habit for your read-out: one sentence of frame, then what was said.
