Holding the Floor

Politics, opinion, and staying in a conversation you disagree with. Key words: an election, a candidate, inflation, a policy, trade, a tariff, the opposition, corruption, from where I stand, that is a tough one, I see where you are coming from, to bring up a topic. Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An election -- the day a country chooses who governs it, and the campaign around it
"Nobody in this sector signs a long contract in the month before an election."
A candidate -- a person asking people to vote for them in an election
"I do not follow the candidates. I follow what they say about trade."
Inflation -- prices going up across the whole economy, year after year
"With inflation this high, a customer who pays after the harvest is paying less than he owes."
A policy -- the line a government or a company decides to follow on a subject
"A change in trade policy reaches a farm about ten months later."
Trade -- buying and selling between countries, in both directions
"Half of what we sell depends on trade decisions made a long way from here."
A tariff -- a tax a country charges on something brought in from abroad
"If the tariff on that input went up, the price would reach the farm at planting, not now."
The opposition -- the parties that are not in government and argue against it
"The opposition promised to review the rule, which is not the same as changing it."
Corruption -- using public power dishonestly, for private money or advantage
"Every country argues about corruption. The interesting part is what each one counts as normal."
From where I stand -- a way of saying that this is your view, not the only truth
"From where I stand, the delay hurts the small grower far more than the big one."
That is a tough one -- an honest way of saying a question is difficult, while you think about it
"That is a tough one. Give me a second and I will tell you what I actually think."
I see where you are coming from -- a way of saying you understand how the other person reached their view, without agreeing
"I see where you are coming from, and from where I stand it looks a little different."
To bring up a topic -- to introduce a subject into a conversation that was about something else
"He brought up the election over dessert, which is exactly when it is hardest to answer well."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody at a company dinner decides to bring up a topic nobody was ready for: an election, a candidate, or what the opposition has promised. The safest first move is not an opinion. It is a shape of sentence. If the government changed the tariff, the price would reach the farm at planting. Look at the verb in the first half. It is in the past, and yet nothing in that sentence is about the past. The past here does not mark time. It marks distance from reality: it says this has not happened, and probably will not.

That is the whole difference from last week. If the harvest is late, the payments will move is a real possibility, so it stays in the present and takes will. If the harvest failed completely, we would lose the season is imagined, so it steps back into the past and takes would. And with the verb to be, careful English uses were for every person: If I were more fluent, I would say a lot more at that table. The same shape gives advice without giving orders: If I were you, I would ask what he means by that.

The rest is not grammar, it is manners, and it is what keeps you at the table. That is a tough one buys three seconds and costs nothing. From where I stand turns a claim about the world into a claim about you, which is much harder to argue with. I see where you are coming from says you followed the reasoning without saying you agree. And the best question in any disagreement is itself a second conditional: what would have to change for you to see it differently? One warning for a Portuguese speaker: never put would in the if-half. Not if the government would change it, but if the government changed it. Get that right, and trade, inflation and even corruption become subjects you can hold a position on instead of subjects you avoid.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Second Conditional

GRAMMAR

The past tense here means unreal, not earlier.

JobFormExample
Something imagined, unlikely or untrueif + past simple, ... would + base verbIf the government changed the tariff, the price would reach the farm later.
Why the pastit marks distance from reality, not timeNothing in the sentence happened. The past says: this is not the case.
The verb to bewere for every person, in careful EnglishIf I were more fluent · if he were in my position
AdviceIf I were you, I would ...If I were you, I would ask him what he means by that.
QuestionWhat would you do if ... ?What would you do if the harvest failed?
First or second?real possibility = first · imagined = secondIf it rains, we will wait · If it rained for a month, we would lose the season.
The trapnever would inside the if-halfNot if the government would change it. Say if the government changed it.

Never: if the government would change the tariff · if I would be more fluent · what would you do if the harvest fails completely? · I understand from where you come.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A guest asks a hard political question and you need two seconds. You say:
A "I prefer not to answer this question."
B "That is a tough one. Give me a second and I will tell you what I actually think."
C "It is very difficult, you know, the politics."
You want to imagine a change that has not happened. You say:
A "If the government changed the tariff, the price would reach the farm at planting."
B "If the government would change the tariff, the price would reach the farm."
C "If the government change the tariff, the price will reach the farm."
Somebody says something about your country that you do not agree with. You say:
A "No, that is wrong. You do not know the country."
B "Yes, exactly, you are completely right."
C "I see where you are coming from. From where I stand it looks a little different, and here is why."
You want to turn a shouting match into a conversation. You ask:
A "What would have to change for you to see it differently?"
B "Why do you think that? It makes no sense."
C "Can we change the subject, please?"
