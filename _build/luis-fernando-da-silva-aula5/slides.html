Lesson 5 · Holding the Floor

Holding the Floor

Somebody says something about your country or your industry that you do not agree with. Staying in that conversation is a skill, and it has a grammar.

Chapter 1: First Words

What Are You Following Right Now?

First, from last week: give me two sentences starting with if about what could go wrong this season.

Now the new one. What is going on in the news that actually affects your customers? Not politics in general — the part that reaches a farm.

Today’s Goal

Disagree, and Stay at the Table

1. The Words

an election, a policy, inflation, trade, a tariff, the opposition, corruption...

2. One Structure

if it were different, this would happen — the safest way to argue

3. The Move

say where you stand, ask how they got there, then answer

The aim is not to win. It is to still be talking five minutes later, which at a company dinner is worth far more than being right.

One Question

If You Could Change One Rule

If you could change one rule that affects agriculture in this country, which one would you change, and what would happen?

Then the harder half: who would be worse off if you did?

Chapter 3: The Code

If It Were Different

The past tense that is not about the past

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"If the government changed the tariff, the price would reach the farm at planting."

"If I were more fluent, I would say a lot more at that table."

"What would you do if the harvest failed completely?"

"If I were you, I would ask what would have to change for them to see it differently."

Every blue half is in the past. Now ask yourself the strange question: is a single one of these sentences about the past?

JobFormExample
Something imagined, unlikely or untrueif + past simple, ... would + base verbIf the government changed the tariff, the price would reach the farm later.
Why the pastthe past marks DISTANCE from reality, not timeNothing in these sentences happened. The past says: this is not the case.
The verb to bewere for every person, in careful EnglishIf I were more fluent · if he were in my position
AdviceIf I were you, I would ...If I were you, I would ask him what he means by that.
QuestionWhat would you do if ... ?What would you do if the harvest failed?
First or second?real possibility = first · imagined = secondIf it rains, we will wait · If it rained for a month, we would lose the season.
The trapnever would inside the if-halfNot if the government would change it. Say if the government changed it.

In one line: the past tense here means unreal, not earlier.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Conversation That Could Go Wrong

One dialogue, two recordings, one briefing note

Dialogue

Dessert, and Somebody Brings It Up

M
I have to ask, because I read about it on the plane. What is actually going on with agricultural policy here? From outside it looks impossible to plan anything.
L
That is a tough one. It is less impossible than it looks from outside, but the timing is brutal. A policy changes in March and a farm feels it in January.
M
See, that is the part nobody writes about. Where I work, everybody argues about whether a rule is right. Nobody asks when it lands.
L
Exactly. If the government changed a tariff tomorrow, the grower would not pay it tomorrow. He would pay it at planting and feel it at harvest, ten months later.
M
And with inflation where it is, ten months is a different currency. Honestly, I would find that unmanageable.
L
You get used to it. From where I stand, the delay is harder on the small grower than on the big one, because the big one can wait and the small one cannot.
M
I would have said the opposite, actually. Bigger operations carry more debt, so I would expect them to be the fragile ones.
L
I see where you are coming from, and on paper you are right. In my portfolio it is the other way around, and I think it is because the big ones can move a payment and the small ones cannot.
M
That is worth knowing. I spend my life reading reports about this and I have never once seen that written down.
L
Nobody writes it down. Let me ask you the same question back. If you could change one trade rule tomorrow, which one would you change?
Comprehension

About Meg

1. Why does Meg bring the subject up, and how does the situation look from where she is?
She read about it on the plane. From outside, she says, it looks impossible to plan anything.
2. What does Meg say people argue about where she works, and what does nobody ask?
Everybody argues about whether a rule is right. Nobody asks when it lands.
3. Meg disagrees with one thing. What is her position, and what reason does she give?
She would have expected bigger operations to be the fragile ones, because they carry more debt.
Listening 1 · The Reporter

Without Ruining the Evening

A political reporter of twenty-two years on how to talk politics at a dinner. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say his answer is not, and what does he say it is?
It is not a clever technique. It is being genuinely curious about how the other person got to their position.
2. Where does he say political positions come from?
Not from nowhere. From something that happened to the person, to their business, or to the town they grew up in.
3. What sentence does he offer, and what does he say the phrase "from where I stand" does?
"What would have to change for you to see it differently." He says "from where I stand" turns a statement about the world into a statement about you, which is harder to argue with and more interesting to hear.
Listening 2 · The Trade Economist

Ten Months Before Anybody Feels It

An agricultural trade economist on why policy arguments go nowhere. Sound first — no text.

1. If a government changed a tariff tomorrow, when would the farmer feel it, and when would he pay for it?
He would feel it at planting, which might be four months later, and pay for it at harvest, another six months after that.
2. What does she say is the whole difficulty of her job?
She is asked to explain today a decision whose effect nobody will see for a year.
3. Which question does she ask instead of arguing about whether a policy is good, and what happens when she does?
If this rule came into force in March, who would have to change what they are doing, and by when. She says somebody always answers that one and the room stops shouting.
Real Document

The Note They Read on the Plane

MARKET NOTE · AGRICULTURAL INPUTS
circulated to visiting investors, one page, no recommendation

Editorial note: this page contains dates and figures only. Where a view is expressed it is labelled as a view, and readers are asked to treat the two differently. The most useful question for a visitor to ask on the ground is not whether the policy is right, but who would have to change what they are doing, and by when.
1. According to this note, how long would it take for a tariff decision to reach a grower in cash?
About ten months: roughly four to planting and a further six to harvest.
2. Two lines on the card say almost nothing certain. Which are they, and why does the note keep them anyway?
"Decision expected before the next election, date not fixed" and "Opposition position: review promised, no figure given". They matter because the absence of a date and of a figure is itself information.
3. What question does the editorial note recommend, and why is it better than arguing about the policy?
Who would have to change what they are doing, and by when. It is better because it turns an argument that has no end into dates and numbers, which can be checked.
