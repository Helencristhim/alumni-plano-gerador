The Inbox: Read It, Then Decide

Three routine emails, without any real data, to sort by urgency and by type of answer. Key words: request, issue, priority, deadline, pending item, approval, policy update, follow-up. Structure: the present simple for routine -- I need to approve, not I needed to approve.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Three messages arrive before nine o'clock, and they are not the same kind of message. The first one is a request: a manager in Argentina asks for an approval. The second one is an issue: shared services found a problem in the contract of a new joiner. The third one is a policy update from headquarters, and it ends with four letters that change everything: FYI, for your information.

The first question is not "what do I answer?" but "what kind of message is this?". A request needs a decision. An issue needs an action, sometimes from another team. A policy update usually needs nothing at all, except the two minutes it takes to read it.

The second question is the deadline. The manager in Argentina writes "as soon as possible", and everybody writes that. The manager in Chile writes that the person leaves on the thirtieth. Only one of those two is a real date. When you answer, you give a real date back: "I get back to you by end of day."

Every morning you go through the inbox, you decide what your priority is, and you leave some messages open. Those are pending items. Once a week you follow up on them, because a pending item that nobody looks at becomes a problem in three weeks. Some of them are on hold, and that is different: they are paused because something else has to happen first, usually a budget confirmation.

Notice the tense in the sentences above. All of this happens every day, so it lives in the present: I go through, I decide, I follow up. The past belongs to what finished: last Friday I approved the position in Chile. When you say "I needed to approve it", a manager reads a closed story, and your real message, which is about today, does not arrive.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The present simple for your routine

GRAMMAR

What happens every day goes in the present simple. The past is only for a finished moment. This is the tense that carries almost everything you say about your work.

FormWhat it doesExample
I / you / we / they + verbroutine, habit, what is always trueI go through my inbox every morning.
he / she / it + verb + sthe same, with -s at the endShe sends the file every Monday.
negative: do not / does not + verbthe -s moves to doesHe does not answer before ten.
past simple: verb + ed (or irregular)one finished moment, with a time wordLast Friday I approved the position.
Watch outusing the past for something that is still true. "I needed to approve" means the need is over; say I need to approve.

Quick test before you speak: does this happen every day, or did it happen once? Every day, present. Once, past.
