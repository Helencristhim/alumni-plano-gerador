Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

How Much Is It? -- Numbers, Prices and Quantities

You work with numbers all day long. Policy, budget, limit, claim — you spot a wrong number in two seconds in your own language. And still, when the client says “thirty” in English, you cannot tell whether it was 13 or 30. Here is what nobody told you: the American cannot tell either. That is why “One-three or three-zero?” exists — a sentence native speakers use because their language is ambiguous, not because they are bad at it. In this lesson: two questions (how much for what you cannot count, how many for what you can), some and any, and six words from your desk (price, cost, budget, available, expensive, bill). This Pre-class is a first contact: come to class with the numbers in your ear, not on the tip of your tongue.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Six words — and you use all six at your desk every day. Listen to each one and repeat it out loud, alone. Two sound traps: available has four syllables and the stress falls on the second (a-VAI-la-bol) — it is the longest word today, go slowly — and budget is BUD-jet, stress on the first syllable and the U of but, never a French "bu-DJET".

Price -- how much money something is
"What's the price?" Say prais, rhyming with nice. It is the label, the number you read.
Cost -- to be a certain price
"How much does it cost?" Price is the label; cost is the verb. Same information, two roads: "The price is 180" = "It costs 180".
Budget -- the money you have for something
"What's the budget for the factory?" Stress on the first syllable: BUD-jet. It is the limit agreed in advance — what you can spend, not what has been spent.
Available -- free and ready to be used
"Are you available on Thursday?" In lesson 6 you learned "Are you free?" — this is the same thing, in a tie. It is the third pair like this you have seen: can/could, want/would like, and now free/available.
Expensive -- with a high price
"That's expensive." Stress on the second syllable: ex-PEN-sive. Two words that settle any shop window — and any policy quote.
Bill -- the paper that says what you have to pay
"Can I have the bill, please?" It is the paper the hotel gives you at check-out — do not mix it up with account, which is the client account and the bank account.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions. The words in bold are the grammar of this lesson — look at what comes right after each how much and each how many.

Eleven in the morning, check-out at the Miami hotel. Paula looks at the bill before going to the airport.

"How much is the room?" — one hundred and eighty dollars a night. "How many nights?" — two. Three hundred and sixty. "How much does breakfast cost?" — twenty-two each, forty-four in total. "That's expensive."

And then she sees it: Minibar — $18.00. She did not open the minibar. In her own language she would ask without thinking twice. In English, her hand is already reaching for the card — paying eighteen dollars feels cheaper than having the conversation. So she breathes and says: "I have a question about the bill. What is the eighteen dollars? I didn't use the minibar." The receptionist looks, types, and removes it. Eighteen dollars that came back because she opened her mouth.

In the taxi, the broker calls: "How much is the budget for the factory?" Paula has the number, but he speaks too fast and his own number goes flying past: sixty or sixteen? She does not guess. "Sorry — six-zero, or one-six?" — "Six-zero. Sixty thousand." — "Perfect. I have some questions, but I don't have any time right now. Are you available at four?" (Notice what she did not do: she did not apologize, and she did not guess. In insurance, a wrong number is not embarrassment — it is somebody's factory.)

1. Why does it say "How many nights?" and "How much is the room?"
A Because much is used in hotels and many in airports.
B Because you can count nights on your fingers (one, two) — and you cannot count money. Countable = many; uncountable = much.
C Because nights ends in -s and room does not.
2. Why does "How much does breakfast cost?" have the does, when "How much is the room?" does not?
A Because the first has a real verb (cost) and needs the helper — the same does as lesson 8. In the second, is is already the verb.
B Because breakfast is plural in English.
C Because does shows up whenever the sentence is about money.
3. Paula says "I have some questions, but I don't have any time". Why is one some and the other any?
A Because some is for good things and any for bad things.
B Because questions can be counted and time cannot.
C Because the first sentence is affirmative (some) and the second is negative (any). Negatives and questions ask for any.
4. The broker speaks fast and Paula cannot tell whether it was 60 or 16. She asks "six-zero, or one-six?". What does that show?
A That her English is still weak and she needs to train her ear more.
B That she is doing her job. In insurance a wrong number is money — the professional confirms, and the guesser causes the problem.
C That she should have asked him to email it and avoided the conversation.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- how much / how many, some / any

GRAMMAR

Here English asks you for two words where one would do — and this time, unlike lesson 9, it makes things harder, not easier. But the choice between them is one question only, and you already know how to answer it.

The question that decidesInglêsWhen you use it
Can you count it on your fingers? → manyHow many nights? · How many meetings?How many nights? How many meetings?
You cannot? → muchHow much is it? · How much money?How much is it? How much money?
After many, always pluralnights · meetings · brokers · peopleThe -s is never missing (people is already plural).
Tem verbo? Entra o doesHow much does it cost? · How much is it?With a verb (cost) you need the helper; with is, you do not.
Afirmativa → someI have some questions.Affirmative — the questions exist.
Negative and question → anyI don't have any time. · Do you have any time?I don't have any time. / Do you have any time?

The rule, in five words: countable = many, uncountable = much. Money is never countable — you count notes, not money. Nor is time — you count hours. So: how much money, how much time, but how many hours.

The most common mistake here: “How much costs the room?”. In English the does steps in and the verb goes to the end: “How much does the room cost?”. And it is not new: it is the same does as “Where does the flight arrive?”, from lesson 8. You already have that piece.

Mistake number 2: “I don't have some time”. In the negative some does not go in — it is any. One exception, and it is the polite one: when you are asking for or offering something (and not really asking a question), some comes back: “Could I have some information?”, “Would you like some coffee?”. They are not questions: they are requests dressed as questions — the same trick as could in lesson 9. Do not freeze on this: if any comes out, nobody notices.

And now the part that is not grammar — and that is the whole lesson. Thirteen and thirty. Fifteen and fifty. Eighteen and eighty. The difference is one sound, and it sits at the end of the word — where nobody is paying attention any more. There is a rule that helps: the teens throw the stress to the end (thir-TEEN) and the tens throw it to the front (THIR-ty). It works most of the time — and not always: in fast speech it disappears, including for people born speaking English.

That is why the sentence you take away from this lesson exists: “Sorry — one-three, or three-zero?” It is not a beginner's sentence. It is what one American asks another, because their language is ambiguous — not because they are bad at it. Why this matters: you have been spotting a wrong number on a policy in two seconds for twenty years. That has not gone away — it is only slower. Numbers are the last thing to become automatic in any language. Not the hardest: the last. You are not behind — you are exactly where everybody is at this point.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Record 90 seconds explaining to a new colleague at Zurich: how many meetings you have a week, how many brokers you look after, and how much a business trip costs, more or less. Use how many, how much, some and any, and at least three of the new words. For example: "I have some meetings every week -- about twelve. I don't have any meetings on Friday. I work with fourteen brokers. A trip to Miami costs about four thousand dollars, and that's expensive." One rule only in this recording: say the numbers out loud, in full — no skipping the number and mumbling "about twelve". Numbers are the last thing to become automatic in any language, and the only way to speed that up is your mouth saying fourteen out loud until it sounds normal. Nobody is listening. Get it wrong as much as you like.
