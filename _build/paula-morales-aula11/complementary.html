Lesson 11 -- How Much Is It? Numbers, Prices and Quantities

Series
Superstore · Season 1, Episode 1 · Prime Video — included in the subscription · 21 min

An American comedy set entirely inside a huge store — which is why it is today's lesson disguised as a series. Every scene has a price tag, a shelf with a quantity, and a customer asking how much something costs. The words you have just learned (price, cost, expensive) are not the subject: they are the scenery. And better still: it is workplace humour, grown-ups complaining about managers and targets — the same fauna as your office, only in English. Twenty-one minutes per episode, which is less than your commute.

Tip: watch it with subtitles, with no guilt — the goal is not to understand everything (American comedy is fast and jokes about US supermarket life). Do one thing only, and it is task 3 of the homework: pick any 5 minutes, switch the subtitles to English for that stretch, and hunt for one number — a price, a quantity, any one. Write the whole sentence down and bring it to the next class. If you hear a “thirteen” and have to rewind the subtitle to know whether it was 13 or 30: perfect, that was exactly the point of the lesson.

Podcast
Espresso English — “10 useful phrases for talking about price” (with Shayna, American teacher, ~4 min, free)

Four and a half minutes, and they are exactly the words of today's lesson: price, cost, expensive. Look at the title: phrases, not rules. Shayna does not explain grammar here — she gives you ten ready-made sentences to use, and that is what you need. People who freeze do not freeze for lack of a rule: they freeze because the sentence does not come. So instead of one more explanation, here are ten sentences already built for you to steal and start using.

Tip: listen twice — it is four minutes, it fits in the coffee queue. The first time, just listen. The second time, do the task that counts: pause at each sentence and say your version out loud, with a real number from your work (“That's expensive” about a real quote, “How much does it cost?” about a real policy). A borrowed sentence only becomes yours when you put your own number inside it. Open and free, no sign-up: the player is on the page itself.

Vanessa is American, speaks slowly on purpose and has been with you since lesson 8 — only this time the subject is yours. Big numbers. You work in large risk insurance: your numbers have six or seven digits, and they are exactly the ones nobody teaches. Every course gives you 1 to 100 and stops. Then a four-and-a-half-million factory turns up and you freeze — not because it is hard, but because nobody ever made you say it out loud. These eighteen minutes are the only ones this week that speak the language of your actual work.

Tip: watch with the video open and your mouth working — every time she says a number, say it along with her, even if you trip over it. The goal is not to understand (you understand numbers better than anyone), it is to get your mouth through the movement without freezing. And do one thing afterwards: take three real figures from policies you saw this week and say all three out loud, in English, alone in the kitchen. If it comes out tangled, repeat it. Those three numbers are worth more than the whole video, because they are yours. Open channel, no sign-up.
