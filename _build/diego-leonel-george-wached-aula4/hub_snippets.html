The Pharmacy Aisle -- Reading a Label and Asking at the Counter

An American pharmacy at nine in the evening: the aisle, the label, the counter. Key words: a prescription, a refill, a dosage, an aisle, an expiration date, generic, to apply, sunscreen, a pharmacist, a copay, a label, to run out of. Structure: modals of obligation and advice -- must, have to, should, can.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A prescription -- a written order from a doctor for a medicine
"You need a prescription for the stronger one."
A refill -- another supply of the same medicine you were already prescribed
"I am here to pick up a refill for my blood pressure tablets."
A dosage -- how much of a medicine to take, and how often
"The dosage is one tablet twice a day, with food."
An aisle -- a long narrow passage between the shelves in a shop
"Sunscreen is in aisle four, on the left."
An expiration date -- the day after which a product should not be used
"Check the expiration date before you apply it."
Generic -- sold under its chemical name, without a brand
"The generic is the same molecule and it costs half as much."
To apply -- to put a cream or an ointment on the skin
"Apply a thin layer to the affected area twice a day."
Sunscreen -- a cream that protects the skin from the sun
"Broad-spectrum sunscreen, thirty or higher, every morning."
A pharmacist -- the professional who prepares and hands over medicines
"Ask the pharmacist. She is behind the counter at the back."
A copay -- the part of the price the patient pays when insurance covers the rest
"Your copay is fifteen dollars. The plan covers the rest."
A label -- the printed information stuck to a medicine package
"Everything you need is on the label, in the same order every time."
To run out of -- to use all of something, so that none is left
"I ran out of the cream on Sunday and the clinic was closed."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every over-the-counter box sold in the United States carries the same label, with the same seven headings, in the same order. That is why a customer who learns to read one box can read every box. And the whole thing is written in four little verbs.

Under Warnings, the strongest ones live. You must not use a hydrocortisone cream on broken skin or on the face: that is a ban, and it does not leave you a choice. You should stop and ask a doctor if the skin becomes more irritated, or if the symptoms last more than seven days: that is advice, and it leaves the decision with you. The two feel similar in translation and they are not the same instruction.

Under Directions come the practical ones. Adults can apply a thin layer three or four times a day: permission. Under two years old, you have to ask a doctor first: an obligation that comes from outside you. And the sentence that traps everybody: you do not have to cover the area with a bandage. That is not a ban. It is a free choice.

The rest is practical vocabulary. The dosage is on the box, the expiration date is on the tube, the generic is on the same shelf for half the price, the copay is what you pay at the till, and the pharmacist is the person you ask when you ran out of your prescription on a Sunday.

1. The label says you do not have to cover the area with a bandage. What does that mean?
A It is forbidden to use a bandage.
B You may use a bandage or not -- it is your choice.
C You should always use a bandage.
2. Which of these is a ban, and not advice?
A You should stop if the skin becomes more irritated.
B You must not use it on the face.
C You can apply it three times a day.
3. Why does have to appear instead of must in "under two years old, you have to ask a doctor first"?
A Because must is impolite in English.
B Because have to has no negative form.
C Because the obligation comes from outside -- a rule of the product, not the speaker's own decision.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Must, Have To, Should, Can

GRAMMAR

Four verbs, four different strengths. The difference is not politeness -- it is how much choice you are left with.

JobFormExample
A rule, written downmust + base verbThe label must list every active ingredient.
A prohibitionmust not + base verbYou must not use it on broken skin.
An obligation from outsidehave to / need to + base verbYou have to ask the pharmacist.
No obligation at alldo not have to / do not need toYou do not have to take it with food.
Adviceshould / should not + base verbYou should stop if it gets worse.
Permission or possibilitycan / cannot + base verbYou can buy it without a prescription.
In the pastcould / had toI could not find it, so I had to ask.

Never: you should to stop · she shoulds ask · you must not take it with food (when you mean it is optional) · I can not find it yesterday · you have must ask the pharmacist

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You are in an American pharmacy and you cannot find the sunscreen. You say:
A Where is the sunscreen, please you tell me?
B Excuse me, could you tell me which aisle this is in?
C I want sunscreen. Bring it.
You want to say that covering the area with a bandage is optional. You say:
A You must not cover it with a bandage.
B You do not have to cover it with a bandage.
C You cannot cover it with a bandage.
You want to give advice, not an order, about stopping the cream. You say:
A You should stop if the skin becomes more irritated.
B You must stop if the skin becomes more irritated.
C You should to stop if the skin becomes more irritated.
You finished your prescription over the weekend. At the counter you say:
A My medicine is finished and the doctor is closed.
B I ran out of it on Sunday. What should I do?
C I have no more of the medicine. You must give me.
