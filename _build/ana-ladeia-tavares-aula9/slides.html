Chapter 3: The Code

Two Forms, Two Instructions

The verbs where -ing and to are not interchangeable

Grammar Discovery

What Changes in Each Pair?

"He stopped to read the clause."  /  "He stopped reading the clause."

"I regret to tell you we lost."  /  "I regret telling you we lost."

"Remember to send the note."  /  "I remember sending the note."

"Try to reach him."  /  "Try calling the other number."

In every pair, the -ing half is about something that has already happened or was already going on. The to half looks forward: the purpose, the next action, the news that is still coming.

Verb+ to+ -ing
stoppause in order to do it — he stopped to readend an activity — he stopped reading
regretbad news you are about to give — I regret to tell yousomething you wish you had not done — I regret telling you
remember / forgetthe task ahead — remember to send itthe memory behind — I remember sending it
trymake the effort — try to reach himexperiment with a method — try calling instead
go onmove to the next thing — she went on to saycontinue the same thing — she went on talking
meanintend — I meant to call youinvolve as a consequence — it means starting again
Fixed patternsdecide / agree / refuse / manage toavoid / advise against / look forward to / put off -ing

In one line: -ing looks backwards at what already happened; to looks forwards at what comes next.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Advice, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one advice note

Dialogue

The Conversation Nobody Teaches You

V
I have decided. I want to take them to court, and I do not want anybody talking me out of it this time.
A
Then let me not try to change your mind. Let me tell you what each course of action costs, and you decide. There are three, and only two are viable.
V
Go on. And do not soften it. Everybody has been softening it for four months.
A
I regret to tell you that your prospects at trial are about sixty per cent. Sixty is good. It is also four in ten that you lose, and if you lose you pay their costs as well as your own.
V
And the second option? The one where I sit in a room with them again.
A
Eight weeks instead of two years, and the downside is that you will probably take less money. I am not going to advise against going to court. I am going to ask you to weigh up two years of your life against the difference.
V
You are the first person who has put it that way. Everybody else kept telling me what they would do.
A
Take the week. And if you have second thoughts on Thursday, remember to call me rather than putting it off until Monday.
Comprehension

About Victor

1. What does Victor say he has decided, and what does he ask nobody to do?
He has decided to take them to court, and he asks nobody to talk him out of it this time.
2. What does Victor say about the last four months?
That everybody has been softening it. He asks explicitly not to have it softened again.
3. What does Victor say everybody else had been doing, and why is that useless to somebody who has to decide?
They kept telling him what they would do in his place. It is useless because it answers a question about them rather than giving him the cost of each option, which is the only thing he can decide with.
Listening 1 · The Solicitor

Advising Somebody Who Wants to Fight

A solicitor on the hardest conversation in the job. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say young lawyers do wrong when a client wants to fight?
They try to talk the client out of it, which makes the client dig in. He says the job is not to change the decision but to make sure the client is deciding with the real numbers.
2. What does he say about the phrase "if I were you"?
That he has banned it in his own team. The client is not him, does not have his money and does not have his two years, so the sentence answers a question nobody asked.
3. What does he say the client always remembers afterwards?
Not the advice. Whether they were told the downside before they decided, or only afterwards.
Listening 2 · The Client Who Went Through With It

Two Years, and I Would Do It Again

Somebody who did go all the way to a hearing. Sound first — no text.

1. What was she told before she started, and what does she say about that advice now?
She was told it would take about a year and that her prospects were good but not certain. She says the advice was honest and the year was two, and she does not hold the estimate against anybody.
2. What does she say she stopped doing during those two years, and what does she wish she had done instead?
She stopped talking about anything else, with anybody. She wishes she had kept one part of her life where the case did not exist.
3. She says she would do it again. What is the condition she attaches?
That it was never about the money. She says anybody going through with this for the money should settle, and that the people who last are the ones who need the finding itself.
Listening 3 · Radio Report

What People Actually Regret

A radio report on the research into regret and decision-making. Sound first — no text.

1. What does the research find about the two kinds of regret?
In the short term people regret what they did. Over years, they regret what they did not do, and that second kind is more persistent and harder to argue with.
2. What does the report say about people who put a decision off?
That delay feels like keeping the options open and is usually the option being chosen. The report says most decisions that are postponed long enough are made by the calendar.
3. What does the researcher at the end say she tells people who are weighing something up?
To write down what the downside actually is, in one sentence. She says most people are frightened of something they have never made themselves name, and naming it usually makes it smaller.

The Note That Proves He Knew

I have not advised against either of the two viable options, and I have said so to the client in those words. The purpose of this note is that in eighteen months, whatever happens, there is a record that he chose with the real numbers in front of him and not with mine.
1. Why is option three not viable, according to the note?
Because there is a time limit in March, so doing nothing is not really an option that stays open.
2. Say Downside 2 back as a full sentence using the right verb form.
Accepting mediation would mean taking less money and giving up any public finding.
3. According to the last paragraph, why does this note exist at all?
So that in eighteen months there is a record that the client decided with the real numbers in front of him, and not with the lawyer's preference.

Chapter 6: Your Turn

The Same Advice, Three Times

Guided > Semi-free > Free

Role-Play 1 of 3 — Guided

Victor Asks Again

Scenario: The client asks the three questions again: what his chances are, what the other option costs him, and what you would do. Answer all three without telling him what to choose, and finish by asking him for one thing.

Keyword chips:

I regret to tell you would mean giving up weigh it up remember to call me
Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Now You Are the One Deciding

Scenario: Change chairs. You are the client now, and the lawyer opposite keeps telling you what he would do in your place. Ask for what you actually need: the numbers, the downside of each option, and the deadline. Do not accept a single sentence that begins with "if I were you".

Keywords:

what are my prospects what is the downside is that viable what would going to court mean
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Three Minutes, No Notes

Scenario: Give the advice, from nothing. Lay out the three options, give the prospects, name the downside of each one, say which is not viable and why, refuse to say what you would do, and end with the one thing you need from the client and by when.

No keywords, no notes, three minutes. If a sentence comes out wrong, finish it anyway.
