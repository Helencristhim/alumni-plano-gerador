Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Stop to Think or Stop Thinking? -- Telling Somebody What Their Options Cost

"I regret to tell you" and "I regret telling you" are opposite sentences. One is bad news for the client; the other is a confession. Key words: to weigh up, a course of action, to advise against, prospects, to commit to, daunting, to put off, viable, to talk somebody out of, second thoughts, a downside, to go through with. Structure: gerunds and infinitives with meaning change.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To weigh up -- to consider the good and the bad sides of something carefully, before deciding
"Take a week and weigh it up. Nobody makes this decision well in an afternoon."
A course of action -- the particular path somebody decides to take out of several possible ones
"There are three courses of action open to you, and two of them end in the same place."
To advise against -- to tell somebody clearly that they should not do something
"I would advise against going to court, and I will put that in writing."
Prospects -- the realistic chances of something happening, judged from where you are now
"Your prospects are good, and good is not the same as certain."
To commit to -- to bind yourself to a decision, so that going back on it costs you something
"Do not commit to a date until you have seen their evidence."
Daunting -- big and difficult enough to make somebody hesitate before starting
"The paperwork is daunting rather than difficult, and those are different problems."
To put off -- to delay something you do not want to do, and to keep delaying it
"She put off reading the contract for two years, and then read it in four minutes."
Viable -- good enough to actually work in practice, whether or not it is the best option
"Only one of the three is viable, and it is not the one you like."
To talk somebody out of -- to persuade somebody not to do something they had decided to do
"Two people talked her out of signing, and one of them was right."
Second thoughts -- doubts that arrive after you have already decided
"Second thoughts are not a reason to change course. They are a reason to check the reasons."
A downside -- the part of a decision that costs you something, even when the decision is right
"There is a downside to every course of action here. Choose the one you can live with."
To go through with -- to do the difficult thing you said you would do, even when you no longer want to
"Half the people who file never go through with it, and the other half never regret it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A client walks in having decided. He wants to go to court, and he does not want anybody talking him out of it. The lawyer does not try to change his mind. She tells him what each course of action costs, and then she says the sentence that this whole lesson is about: I regret to tell you that your prospects at trial are about sixty per cent.

Change one word in that sentence and it becomes a different sentence entirely. I regret telling you would mean that she wishes she had kept the news from him. The -ing form looks backwards, at something that already happened; the to form looks forwards, at what is coming next. The same split runs through a small group of verbs and it is not optional. He stopped to read the clause means he paused in order to read it. He stopped reading it means he was reading and gave up. Remember to send the note is an instruction; I remember sending it is a memory.

Some verbs then take one form and never the other, and there is no logic to learn, only a list: you advise against going, you put off reading, you look forward to hearing — all with -ing; and you decide to, agree to, refuse to, manage to. One more that matters in advice: accepting the offer would mean giving up the credit is a consequence, while I meant to call you is an intention. Getting this wrong does not sound foreign. It says something you did not say.

1. "I regret to tell you that we lost." What is the speaker sorry about?
A The news itself, which is being delivered right now.
B Having told the person, which she now wishes she had not done.
C Both readings are possible; only the tone decides.
2. "He stopped reading the clause." What happened?
A He paused whatever he was doing in order to read the clause.
B He was reading the clause and gave up before the end.
C He never started reading it.
3. According to the text, why does this matter more than an accent?
A Because native speakers notice it immediately.
B Because getting it wrong does not sound foreign; it says something you did not say.
C Because these verbs are more common than any others.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Gerunds and Infinitives With Meaning Change

GRAMMAR

-ing looks backwards at what already happened; to looks forwards at what comes next.

Verb+ to+ -ing
stoppause in order to do it — he stopped to readend an activity — he stopped reading
regretbad news you are about to give — I regret to tell yousomething you wish you had not done — I regret telling you
remember / forgetthe task ahead — remember to send itthe memory behind — I remember sending it
trymake the effort — try to reach himexperiment with a method — try calling instead
go onmove to the next thing — she went on to saycontinue the same thing — she went on talking
meanintend — I meant to call youinvolve as a consequence — it means starting again
Fixed patternsdecide / agree / refuse / manage toavoid / advise against / look forward to / put off -ing

Never: I would advise against to go · she put off to read it · I look forward to hear from you · he avoided to answer · I regret telling you, when you mean the news.

Stage 2: Put the Advice in Order

Order

Six moves in a conversation with somebody who has to decide. Put them in a logical order.

?"Let me not try to change your mind. Let me tell you what each option costs."
?"There are three courses of action, and only two of them are viable."
?"I regret to tell you that your prospects at trial are about sixty per cent."
?"Accepting mediation would mean taking less money and giving up a public finding."
?"I am not going to advise against either of them. That decision is yours."
?"Take the week, and remember to call me rather than putting it off until Monday."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You have to give a client bad news about the odds. You say:
A "I regret telling you that your prospects are about sixty per cent."
B "I regret to tell you that your prospects are about sixty per cent."
C "I regret for telling you that your prospects are about sixty per cent."
You want to say that the other option costs him the public finding. You say:
A "Accepting mediation would mean giving up a public finding."
B "Accepting mediation would mean to give up a public finding."
C "To accept mediation would mean give up a public finding."
You want to tell him clearly not to go to court. You say:
A "I would advise against to go to court."
B "I would advise you against go to court."
C "I would advise against going to court."
He asks you to remind him of a task for tomorrow. You say:
A "Remember to send the undertaking before six."
B "Remember sending the undertaking before six."
C "Remember of sending the undertaking before six."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Give the advice, in two minutes. Lay out the three options, give the prospects, name the downside of each one, say which is not viable and why, refuse to say what you would do, and end with the one thing you need from the client and by when. If a sentence comes out wrong, finish it anyway.
