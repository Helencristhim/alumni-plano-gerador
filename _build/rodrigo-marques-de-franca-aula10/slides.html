Chapter 4: The Order of Events

Which One Came First?

Grammar Discovery

Two Things Happened. Which Came First?

"He had been working on the memoir for six years when a cousin wrote to him."

"His father had left in April, and the family had moved out before the winter he describes."

"I was reading the memoir on a plane when I realized I had met one of the people in it."

"By the time I reached chapter nine, the narrator had already lied to me twice."

Every sentence has a moment in the blue part — a point in the past. The red verbs are what was already going on, or what had already happened, when you arrived at that point.

JobFormExample
The events, in a linepast simpleA cousin wrote to him. He kept the chapter.
What was already going onwas / were + -ingI was reading the memoir when I realized...
What had happened beforehad + past participleHis father had left in April.
How long it had been going onhad been + -ingHe had been working on it for six years.
Marking the point in the pastwhen · by the time · before · alreadyBy the time I reached chapter nine...
Once the order is clearpast simple is enoughAfter I had finished the book, I wrote to him.
The trapsno continuous with know, remember, believeNot I was knowing. Not when I arrived he already left.

In one line: past simple is the line of events; past continuous is the background around one of them; past perfect is anything that already happened before that point.

Rewrite the Order

Same Events, Told Backwards

Say the rewritten sentence, then click to compare

1. "The father left in April. The son wrote the scene twenty years later." (start from the scene)
"When he wrote the scene, his father had already left, twenty years earlier."
2. "The cousin sent the photograph. He was working on the memoir." (one sentence, both tenses)
"He had been working on the memoir for six years when the cousin sent the photograph."
3. "I read chapter nine. The narrator lied to me twice before that." (by the time)
"By the time I reached chapter nine, the narrator had already lied to me twice."
4. "The witness told the story forty times. Then the statement was taken." (by the time)
"By the time the statement was taken, the witness had told the story forty times."

Listening 1 · A Novelist

I Never Tell It in Order

Somebody explaining why the beginning is the worst place to begin. Sound first — no text.

1. What is her reason for not starting at the beginning, and what happens to readers if she does?
The reason is practical, not literary: the reader would have to carry forty pages of backstory before anything is at stake. They put the book down at page nine and are polite about it later.
2. Where does she start instead, and when does she go back?
In the middle of a bad afternoon — a woman sitting in a car outside her own house. She goes back only once the reader already wants to know why.
3. What does she say her editor tells her, and how does she answer it?
That she overuses the device. She agrees that her editor is right, and says she will keep doing it, because the alternative is a book nobody finishes.
Listening 2 · Taking a Statement

I Start With the Boring Parts

Somebody explaining how he opens a statement three years after the fact. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he ask about first, instead of the event?
The day around it: what you were doing that morning, what the weather was like, who else was in the building, whether you had eaten.
2. What are the two things that happen when he does this?
The ordinary detail brings the rest back, because memory is stored next to the boring parts. And it shows him which sections are remembered and which are reconstructed.
3. Which witness does he prefer, and why?
The one who says he cannot remember whether the door was open, rather than the one who is certain about everything. That distinction is worth more to him than confidence is.

Describe It Without the Ending

Scenario: Somebody at dinner asks what you are reading. Start with one scene, not with the plot. Say what was going on around it, and what had already happened before the book begins. Do not give away the ending.

Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Take the Statement, Three Years Later

Scenario: You are taking a statement about a meeting that happened three years ago. Open with the day around it, the way the second speaker does. Ask what the witness was doing, who else was there, and what had already happened by then. End by naming which parts sounded remembered and which sounded reconstructed.

Keywords:

what were you doing had you already an account
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Three Minutes, Out of Order

Scenario: Tell a real episode from your own past — a case, a trip, a decision — the way the novelist would. Start in the middle, at the moment things were already going wrong. Only then go back to what had led to it. Finish in the aftermath, and say what you can see now that you could not see then.

No keywords, no notes, three minutes.

Five Phrases for Any Story

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can tell a story out of order and still make the sequence clear.
I use had + participle for the event that came earlier, not past simple for everything.
I use was / were + -ing for what was already going on around an event.
I never say I was knowing, and I do not put had on both verbs.
I know the words: protagonist, backstory, a twist, the aftermath, hindsight, to gloss over.
