Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

The Book You Are Reading -- Narrative and Memory

No good story is told in the order it happened. Key words: a protagonist, backstory, a twist, to unravel, the aftermath, vivid, to build up to, to look back on, a memoir, an account, hindsight, to gloss over. Structure: narrative tenses -- past perfect and past continuous.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A protagonist -- the person a story follows, and whose choices drive it
"The protagonist is not the man on the cover."
Backstory -- everything that happened before the story begins
"Almost none of the backstory is given to you at the start."
A twist -- a turn that changes how you read everything before it
"The twist in chapter nine changes how you read the first half."
To unravel -- to come apart slowly, one thread at a time
"Everything unravels from there, and nobody can tell what was built."
The aftermath -- the period right after something serious has happened
"The aftermath rewrites the event, and it takes up half the book."
Vivid -- so clear and detailed that you seem to see it
"The memory was vivid, complete and, in every checkable respect, false."
To build up to -- to move gradually toward a moment the reader can feel coming
"The whole first half builds up to a conversation that lasts two pages."
To look back on -- to consider something from the present, long after it happened
"We rebuild a scene every time we look back on it."
A memoir -- a book in which somebody tells one part of their own life
"He had been working on the memoir for six years."
An account -- one person's telling of what happened, from where they stood
"By the author's own account, the scene never happened."
Hindsight -- the understanding of an event that you only have afterwards
"In hindsight, the twist had been in front of me all along."
To gloss over -- to pass over something quickly so as not to deal with it
"He glosses over the year that mattered most."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The scene everybody remembers takes four pages and never happened. The author had been working on the memoir for six years when a cousin sent him a photograph of the kitchen he describes. His father had left in April. The family had moved out of that house eighteen months before the winter afternoon he puts himself in, aged nine, in the doorway. The memory was vivid, complete and, in every checkable respect, false.

He kept the chapter and added four pages explaining what the photograph showed, and the twist is that the second version is the more truthful book. Anybody who takes a statement three years after the fact knows the pattern from the other side. The witness is trying hard to be honest, was standing in the right place, and has told the story forty times since. The aftermath rewrites the event. Every time we look back on a scene we rebuild it, borrowing detail from photographs and from other people's accounts, and almost nobody ever finds the receipt.

What he did not do is gloss over the gap. In hindsight, he says, the chapter had been a reconstruction from the first draft, and he simply had not known it. An account that says where the certainty ends is worth more than one that sounds seamless, in a courtroom as much as on a page. It is the seamless version that unravels, and it takes everything around it with it.

1. "He had been working on the memoir for six years when a cousin sent him a photograph." What does the tense tell you?
A He finished the memoir six years before the photograph arrived.
B The work had already been going on for six years at the moment the photograph arrived, and it was still going on.
C He works on the memoir now and has done so for six years.
2. Why "had left" and "had moved" rather than "left" and "moved"?
A Both happened before the winter afternoon the chapter describes, and past perfect is what marks the earlier event.
B Because the writer is not certain that they happened.
C Because they happened a very long time ago.
3. What is the text's argument about an account that sounds seamless?
A It is the most persuasive and therefore the most useful.
B It proves the person is lying.
C It is the one that unravels, because nobody can tell afterwards which parts were remembered and which were built.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Narrative Tenses

GRAMMAR

Past simple is the line of events. The other two tenses tell the listener where in time you are standing.

JobFormExample
The events, in a linepast simpleA cousin sent the photograph.
What was already going onwas / were + -ingI was reading it when the call came.
What had happened beforehad + past participleHis father had left in April.
How long, up to that pointhad been + -ingHe had been working on it for six years.
Marking the point in the pastwhen · by the time · before · alreadyBy the time I reached chapter nine...
Question and negativehad you...? · had not / hadn'tHad you read it before? He hadn't known.
Once the order is clearpast simple is enoughAfter I had finished it, I wrote to him.

Never: when I arrived he already left · I was knowing the ending · after I had finished, I had written · while I read the book, my phone rang · I was believing it

Stage 2: Put the Story in Order

Order

Put the events in the order they actually happened -- not the order the book tells them in.

?"His father left the house in April 1973."
?"The family had moved out of that house by the following winter."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A "When I arrived, they already decided."
B "By the time I arrived, they had already decided."
C "When I was arriving, they were already deciding."
Somebody asks what you were doing when the amendment came in. You say:
A "I was reviewing the draft when it arrived."
B "I reviewed the draft when it was arriving."
C "I had been reviewing the draft when it had arrived."
At dinner, somebody asks about the book you finished last week. You say:
A "It is about a man. Then this happens, then this, then this."
B "There is a twist two thirds of the way in, and in hindsight it had been in front of me from the start."
C "I was knowing the ending before chapter three."
You are describing a memory you are not certain about. You say:

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Tell a real episode from your own past in two minutes, but do not start at the beginning. Open at the moment things were already going wrong, then go back to what had led to it, and finish in the aftermath with what you can see now that you could not see then.
