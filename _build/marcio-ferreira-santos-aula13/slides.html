Chapter 3: The Code

The Past That Did Not Happen

if we had tested · we would have caught it

Grammar Discovery

Read and Notice

"If we had tested the second market, we would have caught the blind spot."

"We should have piloted in one region first."

"The discount could have worked in a different category."

"Finance must have seen the weekly data before we did."

All four sentences are about the past. What comes right after the modal, every single time? And what happens inside the if clause?

StructureRuleExample
Third conditionalIf + had + participle, then would have + participle. The past did not happen.If we had tested it, we would have caught it.
Never "would" after ifThe if clause takes had, never would have. Same rule as the other conditionals.never if we would have tested
Regretshould have + participle: the right thing, not done.We should have piloted first.
Possibilitycould have + participle: it was possible, and it did not happen.It could have worked elsewhere.
Deductionmust have + participle: you are almost certain about the past. Cannot have + participle is the opposite. In speech all of these contract: should've, could've, must've.

One line to remember: after every modal, HAVE plus the participle.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If we would have tested the second market, we would have caught it."
"If we had tested the second market, we would have caught it."
"We should piloted in one region first."
"We should have piloted in one region first."
"If we had more data at the time, the result would have been different."
"If we had had more data at the time, the result would have been different."

Inside if, use had. After the modal, never drop the have.

Chapter 4: The Post-Mortem Meeting

Blameless, Not Painless

We talk about decisions, not people.

Dialogue

The Post-Mortem

O
Let us keep this blameless. In hindsight, what would you have done differently?
M
I would have piloted in one region first. I signed off on four.
O
And if you had piloted in one, what would that have changed?
M
We would have caught the blind spot in March instead of June. Regional pricing was never in the model.
O
So was the model the root cause?
M
No. The model did what we asked it to do. The root cause was the data, and leaving the second market out was an oversight, not a decision.
O
Fair. Anything that surprised you?
M
The discount backfired. Volume rose and margin fell, and I could have seen that coming. Today I do not sign off on a rollout without a pilot.
Comprehension

About Olivia

1. What kind of meeting does Olivia say this is?
A blameless one. She asks him to keep it blameless before anybody speaks.
2. What does she want to know after his first answer?
What piloting in one region would have changed.
3. What is her last question about?
Whether anything surprised him.
Listening

Two Ground Rules Before We Start

A manager opens a post-mortem. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What is rule one, and why does she give it?
Talk about decisions, not people. A decision made with the information available in March is reasonable even if it looks foolish in June.
2. Who has to answer the question, according to rule two?
Everybody, including her. If only the junior people answer, the meeting is theater.
3. In her example, what is the symptom and what is the root cause?
The discount that backfired is the symptom. The root cause is the reason nobody caught it for three months.
Listening 2

The Question That Ends Interviews

An interview coach, unscripted. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What are the two fatal answers candidates give?
Saying nothing comes to mind, which reads as dishonest or incurious, and confessing a disaster and then apologizing for four minutes, which reads as fragile.
2. What are the three sentences of a senior answer?
Name the decision without defending it, say what you would have done differently, and say what you do differently now.
3. Why does the last sentence have to be in the present?
Because an answer that stays in the past describes a wound. Ending in the present describes a lesson.
