Chapter 4: The Word That Binds

Four Modals, Four Jobs

Duty, right, prohibition, and no duty at all

Grammar Discovery

What Does Each One Actually Do?

"The supplier must notify the buyer within five business days."

"The buyer is entitled to inspect the records at any reasonable time."

"Neither party may assign this agreement without prior written consent."

"The seller is not required to replace goods damaged in transit."

Four clauses, four different jobs. Two of them are easy to name. The other two are the ones that end up in front of an arbitrator, and the red words are the whole reason why.

JobFormExample
Dutymust · shall · is required toThe supplier must notify the buyer.
Rightis entitled to · mayThe buyer is entitled to inspect.
Prohibitionmay not · shall not · neither party mayNeither party may assign without consent.
No duty at allis not required to · need notThe seller is not required to replace them.
The trapno duty is NOT a prohibitionnot required to replace ≠ may not replace

In the pasthad to · was entitled to · was not allowed toThey were not allowed to assign it.

In one line: read the modal first, then the subject, then the exception. Most of a clause becomes readable in one pass.

Name the Job

Duty, Right, Prohibition, or No Duty?

Say the word, then click to compare

1. "The parties shall negotiate in good faith before commencing arbitration."
Duty. Shall here creates an obligation on both parties, and the obligation is to negotiate first.
2. "The buyer may terminate on thirty days written notice."
Right. May gives the buyer a choice, and the notice period is the condition attached to it.
3. "The supplier shall not subcontract any part of the services."
Prohibition. Shall not forbids it outright, with no exception carved out.
4. "The seller is not required to provide replacement goods."
No duty. The seller has no obligation to provide them, and is not forbidden from doing so either.

Listening 1 · In-House Counsel

I Always Start at the Back

An in-house lawyer describing how she actually reads a contract. Sound first — no text.

1. Which three clauses does she read first, and why those?
The governing law, the termination clause and the liability cap. Those three tell her what happens on the worst day, and the front of the contract only matters if that day arrives.
2. What does she do with the modal verbs, and how long does it take?
She reads every sentence with a modal in it and marks them in two colours: one for what her side owes, one for what the other side owes. It takes twenty minutes.
3. Which clause does she say she looks hardest for, and what does she admit about it?
The clause that removes a duty without granting a permission. She admits that everybody misreads it, including her, twice.
Listening 2 · A Drafting Teacher

One Word, Four Jobs

Somebody who has been teaching contract drafting for eleven years. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say the real problem with shall is?
Not that it is evil, but that one word is asked to do four different jobs in the same document, and no reader can tell which job it is doing without stopping to think.
2. What does he say about must, and how long does the objection last?
That must is not aggressive, only unfamiliar, and that unfamiliar wears off in about a week.
3. What actually convinces a sceptical partner, according to him?
Not the style guide. Opening their own contract, finding the fourth or fifth shall on the page, and asking them out loud whether that sentence creates a duty or describes the future. Half the time they cannot answer.
