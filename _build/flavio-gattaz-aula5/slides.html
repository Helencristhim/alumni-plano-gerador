Lesson 5 · The Opening

Tell Me About Yourself

Six words, no question mark, and the answer decides the next twenty-nine minutes.

Warm-Up

You Have the Why. Now Who Is Saying It?

Last time you made the case for the program. The interview does not open with that. It opens with six words that are not even a question, and most candidates spend ninety seconds on the wrong part of the answer.

Two minutes, starting now: tell me about yourself. I will time it and say nothing.

Today’s Goal

Two Minutes, Backwards

1. The Words

a trajectory, a turning point, a mandate, credentials...

2. One Structure

putting the important part at the front of the sentence

3. Your Answer

now, then the turning point, then where it goes

Most candidates tell the story forwards, from the degree to today, and arrive at the interesting part with thirty seconds left. Start where you are. The past is the explanation, not the introduction.

One Question

One Sentence. What Do You Actually Run?

Say it out loud, in one sentence, as though somebody had just doubted it.

Then the second one: what is the single thing that connects everything you have done since you left university?

Chapter 3: The Code

Putting the Important Part at the Front

The same facts, and a completely different weight

Grammar Discovery

What Happened to the Second Sentence?

"I run a construction company." → "What I run is a construction company."

"The management side interests me." → "The part that interests me is the management side."

"2011 changed everything." → "It was 2011 that changed everything."

"I want to close a gap." → "All I want is to close a gap."

Each second sentence has been broken in two. Something opens it — what, the part that, it, all — and a small is or was holds the two halves apart. Everything after that little verb is the part you want the listener to keep.

JobFormExample
Foreground a thingWhat + subject + verb + is ...What I run is a construction company.
Foreground a part of itThe part / the thing that ... is ...The part that interests me is the management side.
Foreground a time or a personIt is / it was ... that / who ...It was 2011 that changed everything.
Limit it to one thingAll + subject + verb + is ...All I want is to close a gap.
Foreground an actionWhat ... did was + base formWhat I did was take on the commercial side.
Agreementwhat + singular verb, even before a pluralWhat I need is two more engineers.
The trapnever a question word orderNot What do I run is a company.

In one line: break the sentence in two and put the half you care about after the verb to be.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Opening, at Full Speed

One interview, three recordings, one form

Dialogue

Thirty Minutes, Starting Now

M
Thanks for making the time. I have your form here, so let us not repeat it. Tell me about yourself.
F
What I run is a construction company in São Paulo, four projects at a time, and I have been doing it with my uncle since my father died. My mandate is everything outside the site.
M
You trained as an engineer. When did that stop being the job?
F
It was 2011 that changed it. I stepped up before anybody offered me the title, and I took on the commercial side because nobody else was going to.
M
That is a real turning point. Is there a through-line in all of it, or did it just happen to you?
F
There is one. I have always run things that other people build. The part that interests me is the decision, not the drawing.
M
Good. And what do you want out of this program? In one sentence, if you can.
F
All I want is to make the decisions I already make with something better than instinct behind them. Can I ask what the cohort looks like this year?
Comprehension

About Meredith

1. How does Meredith open, and what is she asking for without saying it?
She thanks him for the time, says she has his form in front of her and suggests they do not repeat it. She is asking him not to recite the resume.
2. What does she pick up on after his first answer, and what does she want to know about it?
That he trained as an engineer, and she wants to know when that stopped being the job.
3. What is the last thing she asks for, and what limit does she put on it?
What he wants out of the program, and she asks him to do it in one sentence if he can.
Listening 1 · A Candidate

Two Minutes That Work

A candidate answering the opening question, recorded in a practice session. Sound first — no text.

1. Where does he start his answer, and what does he leave out at the beginning?
He starts with what he runs today, in one sentence. He leaves out the degree and the early jobs, and says he will come back to them if she wants.
2. What does he name as the turning point, and how does he describe what changed?
The year the plant nearly closed. He stepped up before anybody offered him the job, and everything after that year was a different role.
3. What does he say the through-line is, and how does he close?
That he has always been the person who fixes the thing nobody wants to own. He closes by handing the question back and asking what she would like him to go deeper on.
Listening 2 · An Interview Coach

It Is Not a Question, It Is a Test

A coach on what the opening line is actually measuring. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say tell me about yourself is really testing?
Whether you can decide what matters. It is an open space, and what you put in it is the answer.
2. What does she say about the first ninety seconds, and what mistake do most candidates make in them?
That the room decides in the first ninety seconds how much of the rest it is going to believe. Most candidates spend them on the degree and the first job, which nobody asked about.
3. What does she say about candidates who apologize for their English?
That the apology costs more than the mistake. Nobody expects perfect English; they expect a person who keeps going.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

Being Interviewed in Your Second Language

A radio report on what interviewers actually notice, and what they do not. Sound first — no text.

1. What did the study compare, and what surprised the researchers?
Recordings of candidates interviewed in a second language, rated by people who did the hiring. Grammatical mistakes had almost no effect on the ratings.
2. What two things did lower the ratings?
Apologizing for your English, and rambling: answers that ran past two minutes without arriving anywhere.
3. What does the reporter say about pauses?
That a pause before answering was read as thinking, not as difficulty, and that in several recordings it made the candidate sound more senior.
