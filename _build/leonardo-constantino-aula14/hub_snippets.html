Vocabulary

Synthesize -- to combine several sources or ideas into one clear whole

Reconcile -- to make two positions that seem to conflict fit together
"We have to reconcile the board's timeline with the authority's."
Transition -- to move an audience from one part or topic to the next
"Before I transition to the second scenario, let me sum up the first."
Demonstrate -- to show clearly, with evidence, that something is true
"The grid demonstrates that your passive voice is now consistent."
Anticipate -- to expect something before it happens, and prepare for it
"Anticipate the two questions the board always asks, and answer them first."
Distinguish -- to see and show the difference between two things
"You have to distinguish between what the law says and what the authority does."
Command -- full control of something you have learned
"Your command of the language is strongest when you stop translating."
Bring together -- to put separate pieces of work into one document or position
"This note brings together thirteen months of advice into one position."
Draw on -- to use experience or knowledge you already have
"Draw on the calls you have already handled, not on theory."
Pull it all together -- to make everything you have learned work at the same time
"Thursday is the day you pull it all together in front of a board."
Sum up -- to give the whole position in one short statement
"Let me sum up the position in one sentence before we take questions."
Hit your stride -- to reach the point where something stops costing you effort
"You hit your stride around the tenth session, and the recordings show it."
Come full circle -- to end where you started, but able to do what you could not do then
"You have come full circle: lesson one was an introduction, lesson fifteen is a presentation."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Thirteen lessons ago Leonardo could describe his work in English. On Thursday he has to defend it, for twelve minutes, in front of six people who will interrupt him. The board pack he will present brings together a year of advice into a single position, and the last thing he does before the meeting is synthesize the three scenarios into one recommendation that fits in one sentence. Nothing in that document uses English he does not have. The group has been a client since 2019, the structure was approved that year and has not been reviewed since, and the committee which drafted the second phase has already suggested postponing it. The authority said that it would issue guidance before the end of the year and has not issued it. If the phase is implemented, the burden rises. If it were postponed, the restructuring would still be worth doing. The team recommends restructuring now and advised the client to avoid signing anything new before the decision. Eight structures, one page, and every one of them was built in an earlier lesson. What is left is not grammar. It is speed. Under pressure two habits come back: a closed date pulls the present perfect into the past simple, and reported speech loses its backshift. So he keeps a short list of what he has to monitor, he learns to anticipate the two questions the board always asks, and he practises the one move that separates a strong candidate from a nervous one: repair yourself in three words and keep going. He also has to distinguish, out loud and every time, between what the law requires and what the tax authority does in practice, because the exposure sits in the second one. When he can do all of that at once, without checking, that is what an examiner calls command of the language. He will not learn anything new on Thursday. He will pull it all together.

C Because a board pack is written in simpler English than a technical note.
2. According to the text, what comes back under pressure?
A A closed date pulling the present perfect into the past simple, and reported speech losing its backshift.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Eight Structures, One Turn

GRAMMAR

This lesson adds no new structure. It puts the eight forms of the whole program into a single extended turn and asks a different question: not "do you know it?" but "does it survive an interruption?" The table below is your readiness map. The last column is what to watch on Thursday.

StructureWhat it does in a board packWhat to watch
Present perfect vs past simple"We have advised the group since 2019" (still running) vs "we reviewed it in March" (finished).A closed date forces the past simple. Never "we have advised them last March".
Modals of obligation and advice"The group should transition", "the officer has to sign".Advice is not a promise. Keep "should" where you mean advice.
First and second conditionalReal: "If it is implemented, the burden will rise." Hypothetical: "If it were postponed, it would still be worth doing."No "would" inside the if-clause.
Expanded modals and hedging"It may be challenged", "it could take two years", "it ought to arrive this year".Hedging disappears when the question is hostile. That is when you need it most.
Passive voice"The structure was approved", "the guidance has not been issued".The participle is not optional: "has not been issued", never "has not been issue".
Relative clauses"The committee that drafted the phase", "the entity which holds the licence".One clause per sentence. A second one is usually a full stop.
Reported speech"The authority said that it would issue guidance."Backshift: said + would, not said + will. This is the first thing lost at speed.
Gerunds and infinitives"We recommend restructuring", "we advised them to avoid signing".recommend + -ing; advise someone + to. Never "recommend to do".
DeliveryThree habits decide whether a listener hears content or effort: linking ("I've_already sent it"), weak forms ("the cost of the delay" — the small words shrink), and sentence stress ("The client asked us, not the partner"). And one repair move: "Let me rephrase that" in three words, then keep going.
