Lesson 13 -- Pre-class

The Negotiation Room -- Salary, Title and Scope

Asking about the range before naming a number, saying the number as a region, and attaching the condition to the yes. Key words: a package, base salary, variable pay, equity, a sign-on bonus, benefits, market rate, a counter-offer, a ceiling, a range, scope creep, to walk away. Structure: would and could for hypothetical negotiation, plus provided for the condition.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These twelve are for disagreeing with somebody more senior than you, in a language that is not yours — firmly, and never defensively.

A package -- everything they pay you, not only the salary
“The package is the number that matters: salary, bonus and what they pay into a pension.”
Base salary -- the fixed part, paid every month
“My base salary is the part I would not want to move backwards.”
Variable pay -- the part that depends on results
“I am comfortable with variable pay if the targets are agreed in writing.”
Equity -- a share of the company itself, instead of cash now
“Equity is interesting to me, although I would want to understand the vesting.”
A sign-on bonus -- a one-off payment for joining, often to replace what you lose
“A sign-on bonus would cover the bonus I am leaving behind.”
Benefits -- what they provide beyond money: health cover, pension, a car
“The benefits are worth more than they look, and nobody asks about them.”
Market rate -- what the same role pays in the same market today
“The market rate for a group CFO in that sector is the number I would start from.”
A counter-offer -- the number you propose after their first one
“My counter-offer moved the base by eight per cent and the scope by a lot more.”
A ceiling -- the highest number they are able to reach
“If the ceiling is below that, I would rather know today than in three weeks.”
A range -- the distance between their lowest and highest number
“Could I ask what range this role was signed off at?”
Scope creep -- the job quietly growing after you accepted it
“Scope creep is not a salary problem, and it is the one I would fix before signing.”
To walk away -- to say no and end the conversation, politely
“Being able to walk away is what makes the rest of the conversation calm.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The offer conversation is the one nobody rehearses, and it is the shortest one in the process. The first rule is that I do not name a number before I know the range. Once a number leaves my mouth it becomes a ceiling, and no company has ever paid me more than I asked for.

So I ask. Could I ask what the role was signed off at? Then I say my number as a region: I would be looking for the upper half. And the reason always lives in the job, never in my life: the scope includes a shared service centre that was not in the original description.

If the base is fixed, the rest is not. I would be comfortable with the number, provided two things are in the contract: the reporting line to the board, and a review of the scope at six months, because scope creep is what makes people leave inside two years. It costs them nothing, and it changes the job.

1. Why does he avoid naming a number first?
A Because it is rude to talk about money early.
B Because the number he says becomes the ceiling.
C Because the recruiter decides the salary alone.
2. What kind of reason does he give for asking for more?
A One that lives in the job, like the scope.
B One that lives in his personal life, like a mortgage.
C No reason at all, so it sounds confident.
3. When the base is fixed, what does he negotiate?
A Nothing — he accepts and moves on.
B The reporting line and a scope review, in the contract.
C A bigger sign-on bonus every year.
4. Why does he want the scope review in writing?
A Because scope creep is what makes people leave inside two years.
B Because it is normal in every contract.
C Because it replaces the bonus.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Asking Without Demanding

GRAMMAR

One word carries this whole conversation: would. It turns a demand into a position, and it leaves the other side somewhere to go.

FormMeaningExample
would be + -ingNames a number without demanding itI would be looking for something in the region of four hundred and fifty.
Could I ask...?Gets information without pressingCould I ask what range the role was signed off at?
would be comfortable withAccepts a condition, calmlyI would be comfortable with a larger variable part.
provided / as long asAttaches your condition to the yes
The trapAfter provided and as long as, use the present, not would: provided the targets are agreed, never would be agreed.
Why it matters in an interview"I used to find that difficult, and I am used to it now" answers half the hostile questions in any process. It shows change without claiming to have been perfect — which is precisely what a panel is listening for.

The three together make a complete arc in one breath: what you used to do, what you are used to now, and how long it took to get used to it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The question has an accusation inside it. Choose the answer that is not defensive.

"What are your salary expectations?" The strongest first move is:
A Give a number immediately, to sound confident.
B Ask what range the role was signed off at.
C Say that money is not important to you.
You want the upper half of the range. The reason you give is:
A The cost of living has gone up.
B The scope includes a shared service centre that was not in the description.
C Another company is interested in you.
They say the number is final. You:
A Accept and say nothing else.
B Say yes, and negotiate the reporting line and a scope review in the contract.
C Ask again for more money in a week.
Which sentence attaches a condition correctly?
A That would work for me, provided the targets are agreed in writing.
B That would work for me, provided the targets would be agreed in writing.
C That would work for me, despite of the targets.
Why is naming your number as a region better than a single figure?
A It sounds less prepared.
B It leaves the conversation open instead of setting a ceiling.
C It hides what you actually want.

Reflection
