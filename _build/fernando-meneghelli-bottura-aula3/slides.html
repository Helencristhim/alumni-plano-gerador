Chapter 3: The Code

What They Must Have Meant

Three verbs for a conclusion about something already finished

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"He must have wanted a number, not a principle."

"She might have been testing how I handle a follow-up."

"I should have asked her to repeat the last part."

"It cannot have been the vocabulary — I know those words."

Same structure, four different meanings. What is doing the work — the time, or how sure the speaker is?

FormMeaningExample
must have + participleI am almost certain this is what happenedHe must have wanted a number.
might / may / could haveIt is possible, and I am not claiming moreShe might have been testing me.
cannot have + participleI am almost certain this is NOT what happenedIt cannot have been the vocabulary.
should have + participleThe right thing to do, and I did not do itI should have asked her to repeat.
The shape never changesmodal + have + past participle. The modal never takes an s, and have never becomes had. Continuous: modal + have been + -ing, for something in progress at that moment.
Why it matters in an interviewThis is how you talk about a room you have already left — a decision, a conversation, an interview — without pretending to certainty you do not have. They must have been looking for a turnaround profile sounds like judgment. They did not like me sounds like a wound.

In speech all of these contract to the same sound: must've, might've, should've. That is why they are easy to read and hard to hear — and why they are on the listening list today.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"He must had wanted a number."
"He must have wanted a number."
"I should have went back to the second part."
"I should have gone back to the second part."
"She might had been testing me with that follow-up."
"She might have been testing me with that follow-up."

The modal is always followed by have, never by had. And what comes after have is the participle: gone, not went.

Practice

Sure, Possible, or Regret?

Say it first, then click to check

"The room went quiet, so he ___must have expected a longer answer." (expect — almost certain)
"I do not know why she stopped me. She ___might have wanted the short version." (want — possible)
"I ___should have paraphrased the question before answering it." (paraphrase — regret)
"It ___cannot have been the accent, because I understood the first half." (be — almost certainly not)
"He kept probing, so he ___must have been looking for the order, not the list." (look — almost certain, in progress)
"I ___could have asked him to repeat, and nobody would have minded." (ask — a possibility I did not take)
The Move

Three Ways to Buy Three Seconds

1. Ask for it again

"Sorry, could you repeat the last part?"

2. Say it back

"So, to paraphrase, you want the order of the decisions."

3. Take the time

"Bear with me for a second — there are two things in that question."

Nobody has ever lost a role for asking a question to be repeated. People lose roles for answering a question nobody asked.

Chapter 4: In the Room

The Long Question

Sixty words of context and eight words of instruction

Dialogue

Anneke Asks a Sixty-Word Question

A
Before we get into the transformation, and I know most of it is public, I want to understand how you operate rather than what you delivered. Nine entities, three of them loss making, a target already announced. Talk me through the order.
F
Sorry, bear with me — could you repeat the last part?
A
Of course. Not what you cut. How you decided the order of it.
F
So, to paraphrase: you want the sequence and the reason behind it, not the list of what went. Is that the gist?
A
That is exactly what I am driving at. Take your time.
F
Then let me talk you through it in three steps. First the entity where the loss was structural, because that one was not going to fix itself.
A
And the two you left until last? I want to drill down on that specifically.
F
I will unpack that in a second, but let me clarify one thing first, and then I will circle back to your question.
Comprehension

About Anneke

1. What does Anneke say she wants to understand, and what does she say she does not need?
She wants to understand how he operates, not what he delivered — because the outcome is already public.
2. When he asks her to repeat, what does she do?
She repeats it immediately and in fewer words: not what he cut, but how he decided the order.
3. Which part of his answer does she want to drill down on?
The two entities he left until last.
Listening

Where the Question Actually Is

A search consultant on what panels are really doing. Sound first — no text.

1. Where in a long question is the actual question, and what is everything before it?
The question is usually the last eight or nine words. Everything before it is context — she calls it scenery.
2. What single habit does she tell candidates to train?
Not to listen for words, but to listen for the verb at the end — tell me, walk me through, how do you decide — because that verb is the instruction.
3. What does she say she has never written down, and what does she write down instead?
She has never written down that a candidate asked for a repeat. She does write down when somebody answers confidently and completely off target.
Listening 2

One Question, Sixty Words

A panel member, at full speed. Find the instruction. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the instruction at the end — what exactly does he want you to do?
To talk him through how the order was decided: what went first, what was deliberately left until last, and why.
2. What does he say he is NOT interested in, and why?
He is not interested in the outcome, because the outcome can be read. He wants the way the candidate operates.
3. Which three facts does he give as context before the instruction?
Nine entities, three of them loss making when the candidate arrived, and a board that had already announced a target externally.

The Sheet

What the Panel Has on the Table

Interview Guide — Do Not Circulate
CFO — Second Round
45 minutes
4 questions · 2 probes each

Q1. Talk me through the order of your decisions.

Listening for: sequence and reasoning, not a list

Q2. What did you deliberately leave until last?

Listening for: a real trade-off, named out loud

Q3. How do you know within a month who will work out?

Listening for: criteria, not personality

Q4. How do you make sure you are understood across two languages?

Listening for: a method, not a reassurance

Note to panel: ask one unscripted follow-up after each answer. A candidate who asks us to repeat or paraphrase the question back is not marked down for it.

1. For question 1, what is the panel listening for — and what would fail?
The sequence and the reasoning. A list of what was cut would fail.
2. What does the note tell the panel to do after every answer?
To ask one unscripted follow-up.
3. What does the note say about a candidate who asks for a question to be repeated?
That the candidate is not marked down for it.
