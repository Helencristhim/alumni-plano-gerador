The Executive Pen -- The Word Before the Idea

Twenty minutes after the roundtable, the press statement has to say exactly what changed, and exactly how firm every commitment is. Key words: A joint statement, A press statement, A holding statement, An embargo, On background, On the record, Material information, Disclosure obligations, Forward guidance, Boilerplate, A key message, Stakeholder alignment, A spokesperson, A lede. Structure: formal linking devices and be to, be supposed to and have to in executive writing.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A joint statement -- an official statement released together by several organizations at the end of a meeting
"The joint statement listed three commitments and no dates."
A press statement -- a short official text an organization gives to journalists
"The press statement went out twenty minutes after the panel."
A holding statement -- a short first statement released while the full facts are still being checked
"We sent a holding statement and promised details by noon."
An embargo -- a request that journalists do not publish information before a set time
"The release is under embargo until six o’clock."
On background -- describes information a journalist may use but not attribute to the speaker by name
"The details of the deal were shared on background."
On the record -- describes information a journalist may publish and attribute to the speaker by name
"The minister’s quote was on the record."
Material information -- information that could change an investor’s decision
"A delay in the auction is material information for the funds."
Disclosure obligations -- the legal duty to make certain information public
"Listed operators have strict disclosure obligations."
Forward guidance -- what an organization says publicly about what it expects to do in the future
"The ministry gave no forward guidance on the timeline."
Boilerplate -- standard text repeated at the end of every release, such as the paragraph about the organization
"Nobody reads the boilerplate, but it has to be there."
A key message -- the one idea that every sentence of a communication should support
"The key message was that the funds and the ministry are still talking."
Stakeholder alignment -- the situation in which the main parties agree on the same direction
"Stakeholder alignment took longer than the engineering."
A spokesperson -- the person officially chosen to speak for an organization
"The fund’s spokesperson declined to comment."
A lede -- the first sentence of a news story or press release, which gives the most important fact
"The lede should tell the investor what changed today."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An investor reading your release in her second language needs two things from every sentence: the fact, and its relationship to the fact before it. Good executive writing gives her the relationship first, with a small word that arrives before the idea.

Whereas announces a contrast between two positions: “The funds want arbitration abroad, whereas the ministry prefers local courts.” Albeit limits what you have just said, and it takes a short phrase, not a full sentence: “Investment rose, albeit from a low base.” Notwithstanding concedes a problem and moves on: “Notwithstanding the delay, the auction will proceed.” In light of gives a cause, and a noun must follow it: “In light of the floods, the dates were revised.”

The second job is to describe commitments precisely, because a commitment in a press statement can become material information. Be to announces an official plan: “The ministry is to present a draft by March.” Was supposed to reports a plan that did not happen: “The review was supposed to come out in June.” Have to states an obligation: “Operators have to comply within ninety days.”

Put the three together and a reader who was not in the room can follow the whole story from the lede to the boilerplate. Mix them up, and the same reader will call your spokesperson to ask what you really meant.

1. According to the text, what should good executive writing give the reader first?
A The name of the organization.
B The relationship between the facts, with a small word before the idea.
C A list of all the participants.
2. What must follow in light of?
A A noun.
B A full sentence with that.
C An adjective.
3. What does was supposed to report?
A An official plan for the future.
B A legal obligation.
C A plan that did not happen.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Formal Connectors and Commitments

GRAMMAR

The connector tells the reader what is coming; be to, was supposed to and have to say how firm the commitment is.

JobFormExample
Contrastwhereas + clauseFunds want arbitration abroad, whereas the ministry does not.
Limitalbeit + short phraseInvestment rose, albeit from a low base.
Concede and continuenotwithstanding + nounNotwithstanding the delay, the auction will proceed.
Causein light of + nounIn light of the floods, the dates changed.
Official planbe to + verbThe ministry is to present a draft by March.
Plan that failed / obligationwas supposed to + verb / have to + verbIt was supposed to come out in June. Operators have to comply.

Never: in light of that the floods happened · notwithstanding of the delay · was supposed to came out · gave a declaration to the press

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Your first sentence should tell the investor:
A "Where the event took place."
B "What changed today."
C "How many people attended."
You want to limit a positive fact in writing. You write:
A "Investment rose, albeit from a low base."
B "Investment rose, albeit it was low before."
C "Investment rose, albeit of a low base."
A report did not come out on the date everyone expected. You write:
A "The report is to come out in June."
B "The report has to come out in June."
C "The report was supposed to come out in June."
A journalist asks for figures a fund shared privately. You say:
A "Those were shared on background, so we cannot put them on the record."
B "Those are on the record, sorry."
C "Those figures are boilerplate."
