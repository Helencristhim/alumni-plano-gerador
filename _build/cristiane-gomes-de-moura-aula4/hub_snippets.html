Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

What I Love to Do -- Your Week in General, and Your Week Right Now

"I watch films" is a fact on a form. "I am watching a series about a small town" is a conversation. Key words: a series, an episode, a thriller, a plot, subtitles, to recommend, an audience, to enjoy, a weekend, to join. Structure: present continuous (am / is / are + verb-ing) for now and for these weeks, and the contrast with the present simple you already use for habits.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A series -- a story told in many parts, one after another, over weeks or years
"At the moment I am watching a series about a small town."
An episode -- one single part of a series, usually forty minutes long
"I am on episode six, and I am sending messages at one in the morning."
A thriller -- a film or a series that keeps you nervous until the last minute
"This month the club is watching thrillers, and the room is fuller than usual."
A plot -- what happens in a story, and in what order it happens
"Two women in the second row are arguing about the plot before it even starts."
Subtitles -- the words at the bottom of the screen that show what people are saying
"Put the subtitles on, in the same language as the film. You are not cheating."
To recommend -- to tell somebody that something is good and they should try it
"My daughter recommended it, and I ignored her for two months."
An audience -- all the people who sit together and watch the same thing
"Half the audience is learning English, so they asked for subtitles."
To enjoy -- to like something while it is happening, not afterwards
"They are not agreeing, and they are enjoying that more than the film."
A weekend -- Saturday and Sunday, when most people do not work
"I usually watch films on the weekend, but tonight I am watching one at the cinema."
To join -- to start doing something together with a group that already exists
"You can join at any time. Nobody asks you why you stopped coming."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

You already have one present tense, and it does a very specific job: it describes your life in general. "I watch films on the weekend. I never watch anything before nine. My son joins me about once a month." Every one of those is true this year, last year and probably next year. The verb stands alone, and that is all it needs.

Now say what is on your screen tonight, and the sentence grows a second half: "At the moment I am watching a series about a small town. This week the film club is showing thrillers. Two people in the second row are arguing about the plot." Two parts, always: a form of to be — am, is or are — and the verb with -ing on the end. Neither half survives on its own. Not I watching, and not I am watch.

Negatives and questions do not need any new word: the not slides in after to be, and the question just turns the first two words around. "I am not enjoying the plot." "Are you enjoying it?" "What are you watching at the moment?" And the words around the sentence usually tell you which tense to reach for before you even choose: usually, always, never, every week pull the simple; right now, at the moment, this month, tonight pull the -ing. One warning: liking is not something anybody can watch you doing, so it stays simple. Say I like it, and never I am liking it. Then hand the question back: "And you? What are you watching at the moment?"

1. Why is "I watch films on the weekend" in the simple present?
A Because films are not actions.
B Because it describes your life in general, not what is on your screen tonight.
C Because the sentence has fewer than eight words.
2. The text says neither half survives on its own. What does that mean?
A You need both am, is or are AND the -ing. Not "I watching", and not "I am watch".
B You have to say the sentence twice.
C The two halves have to be in different sentences.
3. Why does the text warn you against "I am liking it"?
A Because like is an irregular verb.
B Because it is too informal for a conversation.
C Because liking is not something anybody can watch you doing, so it stays in the simple present.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Two Presents, and How to Tell Them Apart

GRAMMAR

The verb alone is your life in general. Am, is or are plus -ing is your life this week.

JobFormExample
A habit, every weekpresent simple — the verb aloneI watch films on the weekend. She joins me once a month.
Happening now, or these weeksam / is / are + verb-ingAt the moment I am watching a series.
Negativeam not / is not / are not + verb-ingI am not enjoying the plot.
QuestionAm / Is / Are + subject + verb-ingAre you enjoying it?
Question word in frontWhat / Why + am/is/are + subject + verb-ingWhat are you watching at the moment?
Short answerYes, I am. / No, she is not."Is he watching it alone?" — "Yes, he is."
The words that give it awayusually, always, never, every week → simple · right now, at the moment, this month, tonight → -ingI usually watch at home, but tonight I am watching one at the cinema.
Verbs that stay simplelike, love, hate, want, know, understandNot I am liking it. Say I like it.
In speechI am becomes I’m · is not becomes isn’t · are not becomes aren’tI’m watching it now. The plot isn’t working. They aren’t agreeing.

Never: I watching a series · I am watch a series · They not are agreeing · What you are watching? · I am liking it

Lesson 4 — What I Love to Do

Series
Behind Her Eyes -- six episodes

A British thriller with something under it that is not quite of this world, which is exactly the corner you said you like. Six episodes, so it ends before it can lose you, and the plot is the whole point — people talk about the last ten minutes for years. The English is clear and the sentences are short, because most of the tension is in what nobody says.

Tip: English subtitles on. After each episode, say two sentences out loud: one about what happens in general in the series, and one that starts with "At the moment I am...". That is today’s grammar, practised on something you actually want to watch.
