Chapter 4: One Step Back

What the Verb Does on the Way Out

Every tense takes one step backwards, and two words change with it

Grammar Discovery

What Changed on the Way Out?

"We are on track."  →  She said they were on track.

"I will send it on Friday."  →  He said he would send it on Friday.

"Can you escalate this?"  →  She asked me to escalate it.

"What do you think?"  →  He asked what I thought.

The blue words are what came out of somebody's mouth. The red ones are the same thing, one step back.

Chapter 5: Real Speed

Two Recordings, One Set of Minutes

What was said, and what survived the writing

Listening 1 · A Minute-Taker

Accurate Is Not the Same as Exact

Thirty years of writing down what other people said. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does he say verbatim minutes are a bad idea?
Because people do not speak in finished sentences, so the exact words make an intelligent person look confused, and the reader ends up judging the speaker instead of the decision.
2. What is the one thing he says he never paraphrases?
A condition. If somebody agrees with a caveat, the caveat goes in as close to the original words as possible.
3. What does he do at the end of every meeting, and why?
He reads the decisions back out loud before anybody leaves — because a correction in the room takes ten seconds, and a correction by email takes three days and an argument.
Listening 2 · Science Radio

What Happens to a Message on the Fifth Retelling

A short piece on a hundred-year-old experiment that still runs today. Sound first — no text.

1. What did the original experiment ask people to do?
Read a story once and retell it from memory, then have the next person retell that version, and so on down a chain.
2. What two things happened to the story by the end of the chain?
It got much shorter, and the parts that did not fit what the listeners expected either disappeared or were changed into something familiar.
3. What does the piece say happens to caveats in an organisation?
They are the first thing to go: a warning with a condition on it arrives at the top as a clean recommendation, and nobody removed it deliberately.
Real Document

Said, and Written

MINUTES · QUARTERLY REVIEW
circulated by J. Piffer · corrections close Thursday
“We are on track for the quarter.”
→ R. Keller said the team were on track for the quarter.
“I will send the final numbers on Friday.”
→ M. Duarte said he would send the final numbers on Friday.
“Can you escalate this before the board meeting?”
→ R. Keller asked J. Piffer to escalate it before the board meeting.
“Yes — but only if the April file is closed first.”
→ M. Duarte agreed, with one caveat: only if the April file is closed first.
Three of these were paraphrased. The fourth was kept almost word for word, because it is a condition, and a condition that gets tidied up stops being a condition.
1. Which line is a request rather than a statement, and how is it written?
The third one. It becomes "asked J. Piffer to escalate it" — asked plus a person plus to plus the verb.
2. What happened to "will" in the second line?
It became "would" — one step back, like every other verb in the column.
3. According to the note, why was the fourth line kept almost word for word?
Because it is a condition, and a condition that gets tidied up stops being a condition.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I move the verb one step back when I report what somebody said, and I leave it alone when the thing is still true.
I use "told me" with a person and "said that" without one, and I never write "said me".
I report a question without inversion: "she asked what I thought", not "what did I think".
I attribute every statement to a person, and I keep a condition close to the original words.
I know the words: minutes, to paraphrase, to misquote, a caveat, to water something down, to attribute something to somebody, verbatim, to read between the lines, a takeaway, to circulate.
