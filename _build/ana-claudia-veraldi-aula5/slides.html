Chapter 3: The Code

The Order, and the Ending

a scruffy little brown mutt · exhausting or exhausted

Grammar Discovery

Could You Swap Them Around?

"She is a scruffy little brown mutt."

"He sleeps in a big old wooden dog house."

"Walking all three of them is exhausting."

"By eight in the morning I am exhausted."

The orange ones follow a fixed order that nobody ever taught you. The blue ones are the same verb: one says what causes the feeling, the other says who feels it.

PointWhat it meansExample
The orderopinion → size → age → shape → color → origin → material, then the noun.a scruffy little brown mutt
Two or threeReal speech stacks two or three. Five sounds like a shopping list.a big old wooden dog house
No commas, no andBefore the noun they simply line up.a big old dog, not a big and old dog
-ing adjectiveDescribes the thing that causes the feeling.The walk is exhausting.
-ed adjectiveDescribes who feels it — people and animals both.I am exhausted. She was frightened.
Both at onceThe two forms sit happily in one sentence.The storm was frightening, so she was frightened.
The trapNever the -ing form for your own feeling.I am exhausting after that walk.

In one line: opinion comes first, material comes last — and -ing is the cause, -ed is the feeling.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"She is a brown little scruffy mutt."
"She is a scruffy little brown mutt."
"He sleeps in a wooden big old dog house."
"He sleeps in a big old wooden dog house."
"I am exhausting after that walk."
"I am exhausted after that walk."
"The thunder last night was frightened."
"The thunder last night was frightening."

The first two are the same error: Portuguese puts the adjective after the noun and in almost any order, English fixes the order and puts them all in front — opinion first, material last. The last two are the other error: -ing names what causes the feeling, -ed names who has it. Say "I am exhausting" to a colleague and you have just told them you wear people out.

Chapter 4: Many Englishes

Same Dogs, Different Accents

A call, a woman in Italy and a man in the United States

Dialogue

Something Just Walked Across Your Camera

G
Something just walked across your camera. Was that a dog? How many do you have?
A
Three. All rescues, all mutts, and not one of them was planned.
G
Three! What are they like?
A
One is a big boisterous young thing who never stops moving. One is a docile old lady who sleeps through everything. And the third is a small scruffy brown one who was the runt of the litter.
G
Are they friendly? I only ask because my sister’s dog is wary of new people and it took me two whole years.
A
The little one is wary of everybody. The other two are so clingy it is embarrassing. I fuss over them far too much, honestly.
G
Nothing wrong with that. Do the three of them get along?
A
Most of the time. There is an old argument between the two youngest that nobody has ever solved, so we let sleeping dogs lie.
Comprehension

About Greg

1. How did Greg find out she has dogs?
One of them walked across her camera during the call.
2. Why does Greg ask whether the dogs are friendly?
Because his sister’s dog is wary of new people, and it took him two whole years to win her over.
3. Greg says "Nothing wrong with that." What is he answering, and what does it tell you about him?
She apologized for fussing over her dogs too much, and he waves it away. For him this is normal, welcome small talk — not a private subject he needs permission to enter.
Listening

The Dog Outside the Bakery

A woman in Italy on the stray nobody wanted. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Where and how did she first see the dog, and why did nobody in the village want her?
Outside the bakery, in the rain — a small scruffy grey stray. Nobody wanted her because she was the runt of the litter and everyone assumed she would not survive the winter.
2. How did the dog behave in the first two months, and what changed?
She was wary of everybody, her husband included, and would not come near the kitchen. Then one morning she decided they were hers, and she has been unbearably clingy ever since.
3. What does her mother say about her?
That she fusses over that dog more than she ever fussed over her brothers — and she admits her mother is right.
Listening 2

One Perfect Dog, Split in Two

A man in the United States on his two opposite dogs. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Which one is easier, according to him?
Neither — for completely opposite reasons.
2. He uses exhausting and exhausted in the same breath. Who or what is each one about?
Taking Cooper out is exhausting — the walk is the cause. He is exhausted — he is the one who feels it. And Cooper is only getting started.
3. What is the problem with the docile one, and when does she perk up?
She barely moves, so he has to talk her into every single walk. She only perks up when she hears the leash, and even then it takes about a minute.
