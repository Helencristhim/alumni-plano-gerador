My Dogs -- Routine and Personality

The dogs, their routine and their personalities. Key words: a rescue, a stray, a mutt, scruffy, clingy, boisterous, docile, wary, to fuss over, to perk up, the runt of the litter, to let sleeping dogs lie. Structure: adjective order before the noun, and -ed against -ing adjectives.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A rescue -- an animal taken from the street or a shelter and given a home
"All three of mine are rescues. Not one of them was planned."
A stray -- an animal that lives on the streets with no owner
"She was a stray sitting outside the bakery in the rain."
A mutt -- a dog of mixed breed, with no pedigree at all
"She is a mutt, and she is smarter than any pedigree I have met."
Scruffy -- untidy and messy-looking, often in a way people find charming
"A small scruffy grey thing with one ear up and one down."
Clingy -- always wanting to be close to you, never leaving your side
"If I sit down, she is on my feet. She is unbearably clingy."
Boisterous -- noisy, full of energy and hard to calm down
"He is a big boisterous young thing who never stops moving."
Docile -- calm, gentle and easy to handle, never aggressive
"She is nine, completely docile, and sleeps through everything."
Wary -- careful and slow to trust, especially with strangers
"For two months she was wary of everybody, my husband included."
To fuss over -- to give somebody or something a lot of loving attention
"My mother says I fuss over that dog far too much."
To perk up -- to suddenly become lively and interested again
"She only perks up when she hears the leash."
The runt of the litter -- the smallest and weakest puppy of the group
"Nobody wanted her, because she was the runt of the litter."
To let sleeping dogs lie -- to leave a delicate matter alone instead of stirring up trouble
"Nobody has ever solved that argument, so we let sleeping dogs lie."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ana has three dogs, and all three of them are rescues. The oldest is a docile old black and white mutt who sleeps through everything, including thunder. The youngest is a big boisterous young thing who has never walked calmly anywhere in his life. And the third is a scruffy little brown mutt who arrived as a stray and was the runt of the litter.

The morning walk with all three is exhausting. By eight o'clock Ana is exhausted and they are only getting started. The little one is still wary of visitors; the other two are so clingy that she cannot cross the kitchen alone. She knows she fusses over them far too much.

The old one barely moves during the day and only perks up when she hears the leash. There is an old argument between the two youngest that nobody has ever solved, so the house has learned to let sleeping dogs lie.

1. The text says "a scruffy little brown mutt". Why is that the order, and not "a brown little scruffy mutt"?
A Because "scruffy" is the longest word.
B Because English fixes the order: opinion, then size, then color.
C Because the dog is female.
2. The walk is "exhausting" and Ana is "exhausted". Why the two different endings?
A The -ing form names what causes the feeling; the -ed form names who feels it.
B The -ing form is present and the -ed form is past.
C They mean exactly the same thing, and both are correct.
3. "The house has learned to let sleeping dogs lie." What does this actually mean here?
A The dogs are allowed to sleep on the furniture.
B Nobody wakes a dog that is sleeping.
C Nobody brings up the old argument, because raising it would only cause trouble.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Order, and the Ending

GRAMMAR

Where each adjective goes, and which ending belongs to whom.

PointWhat it meansExample
The orderopinion → size → age → shape → color → origin → material, then the nouna scruffy little brown mutt
Two or threeReal speech stacks two or three, not sevena big old wooden dog house
No commas, no andBefore the noun the adjectives simply line upa big old dog, not a big and old dog
-ing adjectiveThe thing that causes the feelingThe walk is exhausting.
-ed adjectiveWhoever feels it, people and animals alikeI am exhausted. She was frightened.
Both at onceThe two forms sit happily in one sentenceThe storm was frightening, so she was frightened.

Never: a brown little scruffy mutt · a wooden big old dog house · I am exhausting after that walk · the thunder was frightened.

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

A colleague sees a dog cross Ana's camera. Put the exchange in a logical order.

?"I saw a dog walk across your camera just now. How many do you have?"
?"Three. All rescues, all mutts, and not one of them was planned."
?"Three! What are they like?"
?"One is a big boisterous young thing who never stops moving."
?"Are they friendly?"
?"The little one is wary of everybody, and the other two are so clingy it is embarrassing."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

A colleague asks what your smallest dog looks like. You answer:
A "She is a brown little scruffy mutt."
B "She is a scruffy little brown mutt."
C "She is a mutt little scruffy brown."
You come back from a two-hour walk with all three of them. You say:
A "That was exhausting. I am exhausted."
B "That was exhausted. I am exhausting."
C "I am exhausting after that walk."
You are meeting somebody else's dog for the first time. The polite question is:
A "Does it bite?"
B "Is she friendly? Can I say hello to her?"
C "Is that dog dangerous or not?"
Two of your dogs have an old quarrel that nobody can fix. You say:
A "Nobody has ever solved it, so we let sleeping dogs lie."
B "Nobody has ever solved it, so we fuss over them."
C "Nobody has ever solved it, so they perk up."
