The Post-mortem -- What We Would Have Done

A manual control on your map did not run for three days, and nobody lost money. Key words: post-mortem, hindsight, near miss, overlook, lapse, blameless, trace back, workaround, timeline, complacency. Structure: third conditional and past modals (would have, should have, could have, might have).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Post-mortem -- a review you hold after something goes wrong, to learn from it
"The post-mortem is on Tuesday, and the note goes to the committee first."
Hindsight -- the clear view of a mistake that you only have afterwards
"It is easy in hindsight. It was not obvious on the day."
Near miss -- something that almost went wrong, and did no damage this time
"Call it a near miss out loud, or the committee will file it and forget it."
To overlook -- to fail to notice something that was there all along
"The out-of-office was in every inbox, and everybody overlooked it."
Lapse -- a short break in a routine, when a step is simply not done
"One lapse on a Monday morning, and the number ran for three days."
Blameless -- looking at the system that failed, not at the person to punish
"This review is blameless by design: the step failed, not Camila."
To trace back -- to follow a problem to the step where it started
"We traced it back to a single line in an out-of-office message."
Workaround -- a temporary way around a problem, until the real fix arrives
"We could have used the automated alert as a workaround that week."
Timeline -- the ordered list of what happened, and at what time
"Put the timeline on the table first. Opinions come after it."
Complacency -- the comfortable feeling that stops you checking, because nothing has gone wrong yet
"A quarter of good luck is how complacency starts."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The post-mortem is on Tuesday and it is blameless by design: we look at the step that failed, never at the person to punish. The timeline is short. On Monday the daily exposure check was not run, because the owner was on a plane and the deputy had never been told. The out-of-office message was in every inbox and everybody overlooked it. The number passed the limit on Tuesday, and a trader noticed it on Wednesday afternoon, by accident. If the deputy had been told on the Friday, the check would have run on time and there would have been no breach at all. If the alert had covered the manual controls as well, we would have seen it on Monday afternoon. We should have marked that control as manual on the map we sent in June, and we should have named a deputy on the same line. We could have used the automated alert as a workaround while the check was down, and nobody thought of it, because nobody knew the check was down. We might have caught it a day earlier, but I would not promise that. It is easy to say in hindsight; it was not obvious on the day. We traced the whole thing back to one lapse: a message nobody read. And we are calling this what it is — a near miss. The market moved back on Thursday and we lost nothing, which is exactly why it is dangerous: a quarter of good luck is how complacency starts.

1. "If the deputy had been told, the check would have run on time." Did the check run on Monday?
A Yes, it ran, because the deputy was told on the Friday.
B No. The sentence describes a past that did not happen and the result it would have had.
C We do not know; the sentence is about next Monday.
2. The text says "We should have marked that control as manual". What does "should have" add here?
A It says the marking was done, and done correctly.
B It says the marking is a plan for next month.
C It says it was the right thing to do, it was not done, and the writer is taking that on his own desk.
3. Why does the text say "we might have caught it a day earlier" instead of "we would have caught it"?
A Because it was possible but not certain, and the writer cannot prove it.
B Because "might have" is the polite form of "would have" in a formal note.
C Because "would have" can only be used in questions.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past That Did Not Happen

GRAMMAR

A post-mortem is written almost entirely in a past that never happened. English has one structure for the result — the third conditional — and three past modals for the tone.

FormWhat it doesExample
If + had + past participle, ... would have + past participleA condition in the past that did NOT happen, and the result it would have had.If the deputy had been told, the check would have run.
where would livesOnly in the result half. The if half takes had and nothing else.If we had known... — never "if we would have known".
should haveThe right thing, not done. It carries criticism, so point it at your own desk.We should have named a deputy on that line.
could haveIt was possible and it was not done. No blame in it.We could have used the alert as a workaround.
might haveA possible result, not a certain one. The honest choice when you do not know.We might have caught it a day earlier.
two trapsAfter had comes the past participle, not the simple past: known (not "knew"), run (not "ran"), seen (not "saw"). And in speech these contract to would've, should've, could've: it sounds like "of", and the word is always have.

Order

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best option for each moment in the review.

A member asks what would have happened if the deputy had been told. What do you say?
A "If the deputy would have been told, the check would have run."
B "If the deputy had been told, the check would have run on Monday."
C "If the deputy had been told, the check would run on Monday."
You want to own a mistake that belongs to your own desk, without naming anybody. What do you say?
A "We should have marked that control as manual, and we did not."
B "The analyst should have run the check, and she did not."
C "We should of marked that control as manual."
The chair asks whether you could have caught it on Tuesday. You believe it was possible, but you cannot prove it. What do you say?
A "We would have caught it on Tuesday, without any doubt."
B "We might have caught it on Tuesday, but I would not promise that."
C "We should have caught it on Tuesday, and that is a fact."
Nothing was lost, and the committee wants to move on. How do you name the event so that it is taken seriously?
A "It was a small issue and it is closed."
B "This was a near miss, and a quarter of good luck is how complacency starts."
C "It was a lapse of one person, and she has been spoken to."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Think of one thing that went wrong on your desk this quarter — it does not have to be serious, a near miss is better. Run a short post-mortem out loud, with no names in it. Give the timeline first, then the step that failed, then one counterfactual you could defend in a meeting ("If ... had ..., we would have ..."), then one thing that was only possible ("We might have ..."), and finish with the change and a date. Take your time.
