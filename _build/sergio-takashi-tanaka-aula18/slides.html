Chapter 3: The Code

The Past That Did Not Happen

would have · should have · could have · might have

Grammar Discovery

Read and Notice

"If the deputy had been told, the check would have run on Monday."

"If the alert had covered manual controls, we would have seen it on Monday afternoon."

"We should have marked that control as manual on the map."

"We could have used the alert as a workaround, and we might have caught it a day earlier."

Look at the orange words. Did the check run on Monday — yes or no?

FormWhat it doesExample
If + had + past participle, ... would have + past participleA past that did NOT happen, and the result it would have hadIf the deputy had been told, the check would have run.
where would livesONLY in the result half. The if half takes had, never would haveIf we had known, we would have stopped it. Never "if we would have known".
should have + past participleThe right thing, not done: criticism or regret. Carries blameWe should have named a deputy on that line.
could have + past participleIt was possible and we did not do it. No blameWe could have used the alert as a workaround.
might have + past participleA possible result, not a certain one. The honest one when you do not knowWe might have caught it a day earlier.
the sound of itIn speech these contract: would've, should've, could've. It sounds like "of" and it is have. Never write "should of". The past participle is irregular in half of these: known, seen, run, caught, told.

One structure, three tones. Should have points a finger. Would have only states a result. Might have admits you do not know.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If we would have known, we would have stopped it on Monday."
"If we had known, we would have stopped it on Monday."
"If the deputy had knew, the check would have ran."
"If the deputy had known, the check would have run."
"We should of marked that control as manual."
"We should have marked that control as manual."

The if half takes had; would waits in the other half. After had comes the past participle: known, not "knew"; run, not "ran". And "of" is only a sound — the word is have.

Listening

Marina on the Night Before

A voice note before the committee session. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does Marina say about naming people in the room?
Keep it blameless in the words, not only in the intention. The moment a name sits in the same sentence as the word failure, the room watches the person instead of the system, and he will never get the honest version of anything again. Say the step failed, never say Camila failed.
2. Which counterfactual does she tell him to use, and which one to avoid?
Use the provable one: if the alert had covered manual controls, we would have seen it on Monday afternoon. Avoid "we would have caught it in an hour", because he does not know that and somebody in the room will ask him how.
3. How does Marina say the post-mortem has to end?
With a change, not with hindsight: the two changes, a date on each one, and his name on both. And he should call it a near miss out loud, or the committee will file it and forget it.
Listening 2

Paulo on Three Sentences

A colleague from the credit desk. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. According to Paulo, what does "should have" add that "would have" does not?
Blame. "Should have" is criticism — fine when it points at your own desk, a bad idea when it points at somebody else's. "Would have" states only a result, a consequence, which is why the auditors like it.
2. When does Paulo say "might have" is the right choice?
When you do not know. "Might have" says it was possible, not certain, and that is the sentence that keeps him credible.
3. Which two mistakes does Paulo warn him about?
Putting "would" in the if half — it is "if we had known", never "if we would have known". And writing "would of", which is only how "would have" sounds when it is said fast.
