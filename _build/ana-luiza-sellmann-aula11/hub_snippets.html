Enjoy Working, Want to Work -- The Two Shapes After a Verb

Talking about goals, plans and the decisions you keep moving to next year. Key words: eventually, stubborn, on a whim, to hesitate, a commitment, reluctant, the scope, a revision. Structure: verbs followed by -ing (enjoy, avoid, keep, consider, suggest) against verbs followed by to (want, decide, agree, offer, refuse), plus stop, remember and try, which take both and change meaning.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

I have a friend who spent two years deciding to travel. He kept planning the trip and then finding a reason to wait, until his sister refused to listen to his excuses and offered to look after his flat. Last week he finally decided to do it. He asked for three months off and his boss agreed to keep his job open. When he told me, I said it sounded like a decision made on a whim, and he said no: he had stopped hesitating, which is a different thing. I would love to do something like that, but I have too many commitments here, so I keep saying next year. He asked whether I had considered asking for one month instead of three. I had not, and I might try talking to my director about it.

The same two shapes run through my whole working week. The client has asked to add two more cities, which means the scope has changed, and they expect us to absorb it. My colleague suggested offering one more revision and asking them to pay for the extra cities. He also told me to avoid agreeing to anything in writing before the numbers were clear, and not to promise a date. Notice that suggest and avoid take the -ing shape, while ask, expect and promise take to. There is no logic to work out: the first verb carries the shape, and you learn it by hearing it.

1. Why is "he kept finding a reason to wait" not "he kept to find"?
A Because "keep" is one of the verbs that always takes the -ing shape.
B Because the action was repeated, and repeated actions always take -ing.
C Because there is another "to" later in the sentence.
2. What is the difference between "he stopped hesitating" and "he stopped to hesitate"?
A Nothing — both mean the hesitation ended.
B The first means the hesitating ended; the second would mean he paused in order to hesitate.
C The second one is simply a grammar mistake and means nothing.
3. According to the text, how do you learn which verbs take which shape?
A By working out the logic behind the two groups.
B By choosing the -ing shape whenever the sentence is long.
C By hearing the pairs often, because the first verb carries the shape.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The First Verb Decides

GRAMMAR

There is no logic to work out here. The first verb carries the shape, and the list is the rule.

ShapeVerbsExample
verb + -ingenjoy, avoid, keep, consider, finish, suggest, recommend, practice, imagine, mind"I avoid looking at the website until March."
verb + towant, need, plan, decide, hope, expect, agree, manage, offer, refuse"She agreed to keep my job open."
NegativeThe "not" goes before the second verb: "I decided not to wait." / "He suggested not saying anything yet."
suggest and recommendThese two never take to + verb. "I suggest offering one more revision" is right; "I suggest to offer" is not English. This is the most common mistake in a client email.
stop, remember, tryBoth shapes, different meanings. "I stopped waiting" (the waiting ended) against "I stopped to wait" (I paused in order to wait). "I remember sending it" (I have the memory) against "I remembered to send it" (I did not forget). "I tried talking to her" (I did it, as an experiment) against "I tried to talk to her" (I attempted it, and it was hard).

In Portuguese the second verb never changes shape, so your ear has nothing to copy. The fix is repetition, not reasoning: say the pairs out loud until anything else sounds strange.

Quiz

A "I suggest to offer one more revision."
B "I suggest offering one more revision."

A "I am considering asking for a month off."
