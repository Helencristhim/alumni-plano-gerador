Chapter 4: The Code

Three Pasts, One Story

What happened, what had happened before it, and what was running at the time

Grammar Discovery

Put These Four on a Timeline

"She presented to the clinic in March."

"The lesion had changed eighteen months earlier."

"While she was waiting for the appointment, the lesion began to bleed."

"By the time she was seen, the tumour had metastasised."

All four are past. Two of the red verbs have two parts, and those two are the ones that happened before the rest. One of the blue phrases is not a point in time at all — it is something that was still running.

JobFormExample
The main line of the storypast simpleShe presented in March.
Earlier than that linehad + past participleThe lesion had changed eighteen months earlier.
Still running at the timewas / were + -ingWhile she was waiting, it began to bleed.
Background + interruptionwas -ing + past simpleShe was travelling when the referral arrived.
Time words for the earlier pastby then, by the time, before, earlier, alreadyBy the time she was seen, it had spread.
Negative and questionhad not + participle / had + subjectShe had not attended the appointment.
The traporder in the VERB, not only in the adverbNot she came in March and the mole changed before.

In one line: past simple for the story, had + participle for what came before it, and was + -ing for what was still going on.

Listening 1 · A Resident

Presenting the Case at Morning Report

A resident presenting this case to the team. Sound first — no text.

1. How does she open the presentation, and what three facts does the first sentence carry?
Age, sex and the reason for presentation: a fifty-four year old woman with a pigmented lesion on the left shoulder.
2. What had already happened before the patient ever came to the clinic?
The lesion had changed eighteen months earlier, she had shown it to a pharmacist, and she had been told it was probably nothing.
3. What does she say the case is really about?
The delay in presentation, not the surgery. She says the tumour was ordinary and the eighteen months were not.
Listening 2 · An Editor

What Makes a Case Worth Reporting

A journal editor on the case reports that get published. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say a case report is NOT?
A rare diagnosis on its own. He says rarity is the weakest reason to publish, because it teaches the reader nothing they can use.
2. What does he say about the chronology?
That most rejected reports lose the order of events, and that if he cannot tell what happened before what, he cannot judge the decisions.
3. What does he say authors should do when a patient was lost to follow-up?
Say so plainly, with the number of months. He says hiding it is what makes a reviewer stop trusting the rest.
