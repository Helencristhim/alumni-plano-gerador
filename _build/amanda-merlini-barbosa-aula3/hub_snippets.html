Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Running Your Part of the Meeting

Ten minutes on the agenda, and everything that happens inside them: open it, hold the thread, raise a concern and close with who does what by when. Key words: agenda, action item, to oversee, to liaise with, to raise a concern, minutes, to follow up on, to wrap up. Structure: present perfect continuous for the work in progress, present simple for the job itself.

Stage 1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Four words for the structure of a meeting, four for what you do inside it. Listen to each one and read it in context.

agenda -- the list of points a meeting will cover, in order
"There are three items on the agenda, and the third one is mine."
action item -- one task the meeting produced, with an owner and a date
"Three action items: I send the draft, Paul confirms the volume, we meet on Monday."
minutes -- the written record of what a meeting decided
"Nobody has been taking the minutes, so we decided the same thing twice."
to oversee -- to be responsible for work that other people do
"I oversee the legal department, but I do not draft every contract myself."
to liaise with -- to keep regular contact with another team so that both know what the other is doing
"I liaise with the commercial team every day, not once a month."
to raise a concern -- to say out loud that something worries you, before it becomes a problem
"I would like to raise a concern about the deadline, not about the price."
to follow up on -- to come back to something later and check that it moved
"I will follow up on the notice on Friday if nothing arrives."
to wrap up -- to close a meeting or a subject with a short summary
"Let me wrap up: three action items and one date."

Stage 3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

In a meeting held in a second language, the hardest moment is not the difficult question. It is the ordinary one: so, where are we on this? The answer has to do two things at once, and most people do only one.

The first thing is the job. What your department does is permanent: it does not change because it is Wednesday. "I oversee the legal department." "I liaise with the commercial team every day." These sit in the present simple, and they answer the question who are you in this room.

The second thing is the week, and it needs a different tense. "We have been reviewing the contract since Friday." "The vessel has been waiting at the port for six days." Work that started earlier, is still going, and is the reason the item is on the agenda at all. That is what have been plus -ing is for, and it is the sentence that buys you the room, because it explains why there is no answer yet without sounding like an excuse.

Then the item has to close. A meeting that ends in agreement and not in action items produces nothing: in three weeks nobody remembers who owned what, and the minutes are the only proof. So the last thirty seconds belong to whoever is willing to say the words. "Let me wrap up. Two action items: I send the notice today, and you confirm the date by Friday. I will follow up on the answer myself."

Nothing in that sentence is difficult English. What is difficult is deciding that closing the item is your job, and not the job of whoever is most senior in the room.

Stage 4: Grammar Tip -- present perfect continuous

GRAMMAR

Two things get confused in every status update: the job and the week. The job takes the present simple. The week takes have or has been plus -ing.

FormUseExample
Present simplethe job: true every weekI oversee the department.
Present perfect continuous
have / has been + ing		work that started earlier and is still goingWe have been reviewing it since Friday.
Negative and questionhave not been + ing / have you been + ingWe have not been waiting. Has the team been calling the agent?
Result or activity"I have read the contract" = finished, you know what is in it. "I have been reading the contract" = still in the middle, and that is why there is no answer yet.
Never continuousknow, want, need, understand, mean. Say "I have known this buyer for years", never "I have been knowing".
Watch outthe present continuous alone does not carry "since". Say "we have been reviewing it since Friday", never "we are reviewing it since Friday".

Quick test before you speak: is this the job, or is this the week?

Stage 8: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Real situations from your week. Only one option is both correct and natural.

A We are calling the agent since Monday.
B We have been calling the agent twice a day since Monday.
C We call the agent twice a day since Monday.
D We have been called the agent twice a day since Monday.
2. A colleague asks what you do on a normal week. Which answer is correct?
A I have been overseeing the legal department.
B I oversee the legal department and I liaise with the commercial team.
C I am overseeing the legal department since 2021.
D I have been knowing the legal department for four years.
3. The conversation has drifted and there are ten minutes left. What do you say?
A This is not the subject of the meeting.
B Can we come back to that after the agenda? I would like to close item three first.
C Sorry, but we do not have time for your point today.
D I prefer to talk about my item, if you do not mind.
4. The meeting is ending and nothing has been written down. What is the best last sentence?
A So, I think we all agree, right?
B Good meeting. I will send an email about everything later.
C Let me wrap up. Two action items: I send the notice today, and you confirm the date by Friday.
D If nobody has anything else, we can finish here.

Film
Margin Call (2011)

Almost the whole film is one long meeting: a technical person has to explain a complicated problem to a director who does not understand it, in a room with a hard stop. That is the skill of this lesson, at feature length.

Tip: watch the boardroom scene with English subtitles and count how many times somebody says "speak to me as you would to a young child". Then notice what changes in the explanation after that line.
