Lesson 23 -- Pre-class

The Follow-up That Works -- Calling the Customer Who Went Quiet

How to contact a customer who stopped answering: show you understand, ask what has changed, and agree on a date. Key words: to go quiet, to chase, a nudge, to circle back, a gentle reminder, top of mind, on hold, to revisit, a breakup email, a change in priorities. Structure: present perfect in follow-ups (I haven't heard back, have you had a chance, has anything changed).

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When a customer goes quiet, a good follow-up talks about the time between the last contact and now. That is why it uses the present perfect: I haven't heard back from you since the demo. We do not say the exact time. We say that the situation is still open.

The most useful question in a follow-up is also in the present perfect: have you had a chance to look at the table? It is polite, because it does not say the customer forgot. And the second question is even better: has anything changed on your side?

To show that you understand, you can say: I know you've been busy. The project has been on hold since September. All these sentences connect the past to today.

When you say the exact day, you change to the past simple: I sent the table on Monday. Brazilian speakers often say I have sent it on Monday, or I didn't hear from you since the demo. Both are wrong in English.

1. Which sentence is correct?
A "I didn't hear from you since our call last month, so I am writing again."
B "I haven't heard from you since our call."
C "I have sent you the table on Monday."
2. Why is "Has anything changed on your side?" a good follow-up question?
A Because it tells the customer that they made a mistake when they forgot to write back to you.
B Because it uses the past simple, which is more polite in all business emails.
C Because it asks about the situation now, not about why they did not answer.
3. When do you use the past simple in a follow-up?
A When you say the exact day or time, like on Monday.
B When the situation is still open and has not finished yet, like the silence of a customer.
C Never: a follow-up only uses the present perfect.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Present Perfect in a Follow-up

GRAMMAR

Have / has + past participle. No exact time, and still connected to now.

FormUseExample
haven't + participle + sinceno answer until nowI haven't heard back since the demo.
Have you had a chance to...?ask politely about progressHave you had a chance to read it?
Has anything changed?ask about the situation nowHas anything changed on your side?
you've been + adjectiveshow you understandI know you've been busy.
past simple + exact timea finished momentI sent it on Monday.

Never: I have sent it on Monday · I didn't hear from you since · Did you have a chance yet?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Giulia has not answered for two weeks. The best first sentence is:
A "Why didn't you answer my emails?"
B "I haven't heard back since our call, so I wanted to check if anything has changed."
C "I didn't hear from you since our call."
You want to know if she read the table. You ask:
A "Did you read the table yet or not, because I sent it to you two weeks ago?"
B "Have you had a chance to look at the table?"
C "You have read the table on Monday?"
The project is on hold. The best answer is:
A "I understand. Can we circle back after your budget review?"
B "Okay, so I will call you every day until the project starts again, if that is okay for you."
C "Okay, we lost."
After four messages with no answer, you write:
A "This is my last message about this for now. If things change, I am here."
B "I have sent you four emails last month and you have not answered any of them, which is not very professional."
C "Hello? Are you there?"
