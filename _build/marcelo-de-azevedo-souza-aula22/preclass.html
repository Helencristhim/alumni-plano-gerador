Lesson 22 -- Pre-class

Navigating Opinions -- Agreeing, Disagreeing and Debating

Read a travel column, disagree politely and measure the difference: the more..., the less..., nowhere near as ... as, far more. New vocabulary: to play devil's advocate, to see eye to eye, a fair point, to beg to differ, to sit on the fence, a sweeping generalization, a counterargument, to concede, to back up a claim, a bone of contention, to agree to disagree, to come around to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To play devil's advocate -- to argue the other side on purpose, to test an idea
"Let me play devil's advocate for a minute."
To see eye to eye -- to agree completely with someone
"We do not always see eye to eye on politics."
A fair point -- a reasonable idea that you accept, even if you disagree
"That is a fair point. I had not thought of that."
To beg to differ -- a polite way to say that you disagree
"I am sorry, but I beg to differ."
To sit on the fence -- to avoid choosing a side in an argument
"You cannot sit on the fence forever."
A sweeping generalization -- a statement that is too general to be true for everyone
"Saying that all tourists are rude is a sweeping generalization."
A counterargument -- a reason given against another argument
"The best counterargument is a real example."
To concede -- to admit that something is true, often after arguing
"I concede that the money helps the village."
To back up a claim -- to give facts or examples that prove what you say
"Can you back up that claim with an example?"
A bone of contention -- the subject that people keep arguing about
"Money was always the bone of contention."
To agree to disagree -- to accept that you will not agree, and stay friends
"Let us agree to disagree and order dessert."
To come around to -- to slowly change your mind and accept an idea
"It took him a year, but he came around to my view."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Fiona Reid has written a column about sharing quiet places. She admits that the more people hear about a place, the less quiet it becomes, and that the guesthouses above Theth are nowhere near as cheap as they were. That is a fair point. But then she decides to play devil's advocate. The village, she writes, is considerably more alive than it was, and the slower visitors travel, the more they spend locally. She refuses to sit on the fence, and she concedes only one thing: some places are much better off without a crowd. Notice the shapes. The more ..., the less ... shows two things changing together. Nowhere near as ... as shows a big difference in the negative. And far, much and considerably make a difference bigger.

1. What does "the more people hear about a place, the less quiet it becomes" show?
A That two things change together: more visitors, less quiet.
B That the place is now completely silent.
C That nobody visits the place any more, because people only hear about it and never actually go there.
2. What does "nowhere near as cheap as they were" mean?
A The guesthouses are a little cheaper than before, especially if you book early in the season.
B The guesthouses are much more expensive than before.
C The guesthouses cost exactly the same.
3. Which word makes the difference BIG in "considerably more alive"?
A considerably
B more
C alive, because it is the adjective that comes at the very end of the phrase

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives for Argument

GRAMMAR

In a debate, the size of the difference is the argument.

FormYou use it forExample
the + comparative, the + comparativetwo things that change togetherThe more people come, the less quiet it is.
nowhere near as / not nearly as ... asa big difference, in the negativeIt is nowhere near as cheap as it was.
far / much / considerably + comparativea big differencefar more walkers
slightly / a bit + comparativea small differenceslightly busier than last year

Key point: in the double comparative, both halves need the: "The more you travel, the less you judge". And never put very before a comparative: say "far more crowded", never "very more crowded".

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best response for each situation.

A friend makes a good point that you had not considered. How do you react?
A "That is a fair point. I had not thought of that."
B "You are wrong."
C "I sit on the fence about your point, because I never really choose a side in any conversation."
You want to disagree with your boss, politely. What do you say?
A "No. That is a bad idea."
B "With respect, I beg to differ."
C "I beg you to differ."
Prices rose as more tourists came. Which sentence links the two ideas?
A "More tourists came, more higher the prices."
B "The more tourists came, the higher the prices went."
C "The tourists came very more, and the prices went very higher than before in the whole valley."
The conversation is going in circles at dinner. How do you end it kindly?
A "I think we will have to agree to disagree."
B "Stop talking."
C "Let us agree to agree, even if we still disagree about almost everything we have said tonight."
