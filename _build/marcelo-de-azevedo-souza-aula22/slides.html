Chapter 4: The Code

Measuring the Difference

the more... the less · nowhere near as · far more

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

Every sentence compares two things. How does the highlighted part measure the difference?

A. "The more people hear about a quiet place, the less quiet it becomes."

B. "The guesthouses are nowhere near as cheap as they were."

C. "The valley sees far more walkers than it did."

D. "The village is considerably more alive than it was."

Grammar Rule

How Big Is the Difference?

FormYou use it forExample
the + comparative ..., the + comparativetwo things that change togetherThe more people come, the less quiet it is.
nowhere near as / not nearly as + adjective + asa big difference, said in the negativeIt is nowhere near as cheap as it was.
far / much / considerably + comparativea big differencefar more walkers, much better off
slightly / a bit + comparativea small differenceIt is slightly busier than last year.

Key idea: in a debate, the size of the difference is the argument. "A bit more expensive" and "far more expensive" are two different opinions. And in the double comparative, both halves need the: "The more you travel, the less you judge", never "More you travel, less you judge".

Dialogue

Debating with Fiona

F
So, Marcelo, you read my column. Be honest. Do we see eye to eye, or are you going to play devil's advocate?
M
A bit of both. You make a fair point about the money. The village is far more alive than it was. But the more people go there, the less special it feels.
F
That is a sweeping generalization, though. Some places are much better off with visitors than without them. Can you back that up?
M
I can. There is a beach I loved for years. The more hotels opened, the harder it was for local families to pay the rent. It is nowhere near as quiet as it was.
F
All right, I concede that one. But I still beg to differ on keeping places secret. A secret helps the people who already know it, not the people who live there.
M
Then I think we have to agree to disagree. Although, give me one more dinner and I might come around to your view.
Did You Catch It?

About Fiona

1. What does Fiona ask Marcelo at the start?
Whether they see eye to eye, or whether he is going to play devil's advocate.
2. Why does Fiona call his first point a sweeping generalization?
Because some places are much better off with visitors than without them.
3. What does Fiona still disagree with at the end?
Keeping places secret, because a secret helps the people who already know it, not the people who live there.

0:000:00

Find the part you agree with and say it out loud.
2. Why should you avoid words like always and never?

Not to win, but to leave the table still friends.

Self-Assessment

What I Learned

I use "the more..., the less..." for two things that change together.
I use "nowhere near as ... as" for a big difference.
I make a difference bigger or smaller with far, much, considerably or slightly.
I can concede a point and still disagree politely.
I can end an argument and stay friends: "Let us agree to disagree."
