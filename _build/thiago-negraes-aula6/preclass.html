Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Comparing in English depends on one thing only: how long the adjective is. Short adjectives take an ending. The second plan is cheaper than the first, and this shop is the cheapest in the street. Long adjectives keep their shape and borrow two words instead. That model is more expensive than this one, and it is the most expensive in the catalogue. You choose one system or the other, never both, and more cheaper is the single most common mistake a Brazilian speaker makes here, because in Portuguese the comparative always needs an extra word. Two adjectives refuse both systems and have forms of their own: good becomes better and the best, and bad becomes worse and the worst.

1. Why do we say "more expensive" and not "expensiver"?
A Because expensive is about money.
B Because it is a long adjective, so it takes more instead of an ending.
C Because it is an irregular adjective.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "This plan is more cheaper than that one."
B "This plan is cheaper than that one."
C "This plan is more cheap that that one."
3. What is the superlative of "bad"?
A the baddest
B the most bad
C the worst

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and superlatives

GRAMMAR

The length of the adjective decides the form. Two things take a comparative; one against all takes a superlative.

AdjectiveTwo thingsOne against all
cheap, big, longcheaper thanthe cheapest
expensive, reliablemore expensive thanthe most expensive
goodbetter thanthe best
badworse thanthe worst

Spelling: one syllable ending in one vowel plus one consonant doubles the consonant (big → bigger). An adjective ending in -y turns it into -i (easy → easier). And the word after the comparative is always than, never that.
