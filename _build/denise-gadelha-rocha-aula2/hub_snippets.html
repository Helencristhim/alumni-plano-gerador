Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words that turn a job title into a scope.

To spearhead -- to lead something new from the front, before anyone else moves
"I spearhead commercial operations across Latin America."
To oversee -- to be in charge of work that other people carry out
"I oversee a cross-functional team of forty people."
Accountability -- being the one person who answers for a result
"The accountability for the region sits with me, not with the team."
Scope -- the limits of what a role or a project actually covers
"Argentina came into my scope when I took the directorship."
Stakeholder -- anyone whose interests are affected by what you decide
"Every stakeholder in the region reads that report."
Deliverable -- a concrete result someone is expecting from you, by a date
"There are three deliverables before the board meets."
Alignment -- agreement between people on direction and priorities
"Half my job is alignment between Milan and the regional teams."
Cross-functional -- involving people from several different areas of the company
"It was a cross-functional project: commercial, claims and finance."
Counterpart -- the person who holds your job in another country or company
"I speak to my counterparts in Colombia and Mexico every week."
To leverage -- to use something you already have to get more out of it
"We leveraged the broker network we built in Mexico."
To liaise with -- to keep contact and coordinate between two groups
"I liaise with Milan on everything above the local limits."
To drive -- to make something happen and keep it moving forward
"The corporate team drove the result, and I want that said out loud."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the profile and answer the questions. Notice which sentences describe things that never happened.

Denise spearheads commercial operations across Latin America and oversees a cross-functional team of forty. Her scope covers four markets. Above the local limits she liaises with the group head office in Milan; below them, the accountability is hers alone. She works with three counterparts in the region, and every stakeholder — brokers, regional teams, the global function — reads the same numbers differently.

She came up through the technical side of the business, which is unusual for the role. If she had stayed there, she would never have built the broker network that now drives the region. If the group had not leveraged that network last year, the corporate result would have been flat. She says the hardest part of the job is not the numbers but the alignment: agreeing on one set of deliverables across four markets takes longer than producing them. If someone had told her how much English the directorship would require, she might have started preparing a year earlier.

1. "If she had stayed there, she would never have built the broker network." What does this tell you?
A She did not stay, and the network exists because of that.
B She stayed, and the network never got built.
C She may still decide to stay in the future.
2. Why is the condition written as "if she had stayed" and not "if she would have stayed"?
A Both are correct; "had" is simply shorter.
B After "if" the verb takes the past perfect. "Would" belongs on the other side of the sentence, never after "if".
C Because the subject is "she" and not "I".
3. "She might have started preparing a year earlier." Why "might" and not "would"?
A "Might" leaves room for doubt — it is a possible outcome, not a certain one.
B "Might" is the polite form of "would" in professional writing.
C "Would" cannot be used with the verb "start".
4. Why does the text say the accountability "is hers alone" rather than "is shared with the team"?
A Because she does not trust her team.
B Because responsibility can be shared, but accountability sits with one person — the one who answers for the result.
C Because the team members are in other countries.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Third Conditional

GRAMMAR

The tense for a past that never happened — and the one senior people use to show judgement.

PartShapeExample
The conditionIf + had + past participleIf I had stayed in operations...
The resultwould / could / might + have + past participle...I would not have learned the commercial side.
What it meansNeither part happened. The past is closed — you are reading it, not regretting it.
Choosing the modalwould = you are sure · could = it was possible · might = perhaps, you are not sure
Either order worksThe result can come first, with no comma.I would never have built that network if I had stayed there.
In speechAlmost everything contracts: If I'd stayed, I wouldn't have learned. Hearing the contraction is half the listening battle.
The one trapNever If I would have known. "Would" never follows "if." The condition takes had; the result takes would have — one on each side.

Documentary
Inside Bill's Brain: Decoding Bill Gates -- Part 1

Three hours of one person explaining how he decides things. Ignore the technology and listen to the structure: he names the scope of a problem before he touches the detail, every single time. That is the habit this lesson is trying to build in you, in English.

Tip: watch with English subtitles. Every time he describes a decision he did not take, write the sentence down — you will collect five or six third conditionals in an hour without trying.
