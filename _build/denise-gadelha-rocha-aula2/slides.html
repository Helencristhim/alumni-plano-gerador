Chapter 3: The Code

The Road You Did Not Take

Talking about a past that never happened

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If I had stayed in operations, I would not have learned the commercial side."

"If we had not spearheaded that review, we would have missed the alignment problem."

"If they had told me the scope earlier, I could have planned the deliverables differently."

"If I had not taken this role, I might never have needed English at this level."

Did any of these things actually happen? So what are the two verb shapes that make it possible to say them?

PartShapeExample
The conditionIf + had + past participleIf I had stayed in operations...
The resultwould / could / might + have + past participle...I would not have learned the commercial side.
What it meansNeither part happened. The past is closed — you are reading it, not changing it. That is why leaders use it: it signals judgement, not regret.
Choosing the modalwould = certain · could = it was possible · might = perhaps, you are not sure
In speechNative speakers contract almost all of it: If I'd stayed, I wouldn't have learned. Understanding the contraction is half the listening.
The one trapNever If I would have known. Would never goes after IF. The condition takes had, the result takes would have — one on each side.
Practice

Complete the Sentence

Say it first, then click to check

"If we had involved the claims team earlier, the review ___would have been genuinely cross-functional." (be)

The Structure

Three Layers, Thirty Seconds

"Tell us a bit about yourself" is the question you can prepare once and use for the rest of your career.

1 · The scope

"I spearhead commercial operations across Latin America for an Italian insurance group."

2 · The shape of the job

"I oversee a cross-functional team, I liaise with Milan on anything above our local limits, and I report to our global head."

3 · The turn that got you here

"If I had stayed on the technical side, I would never have built the broker network — and that network is what drives the region today."

Scope → shape → the turn. Never a job title on its own.

Comprehension

About Victor

1. Why does he want to hear it from her instead of the org chart?
He is writing the regional leadership profiles and the org chart does not show where a role really stops.
2. What surprises him about her background?
She came from the technical side, while most people in her seat came from sales.
3. Which sentence does he say he was looking for?
The one about the broker network — that if she had stayed on the technical side, she would never have built it.
Listening

What a Bad Introduction Sounds Like

He has heard three thousand of them. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What is the first mistake he describes?
Listing tasks instead of naming scope — a list of activities, with no shape.
2. What is the second one?
Starting with an apology, usually about the person's English.
3. What does he say about the size of the team?
It is the least interesting number — what a director decides matters more than how many people report in.
4. What does he ask candidates to say in one sentence?
What breaks if they leave tomorrow.
Listening 2

The Turn That Made the Career

A director looking back at one decision. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What was the job she turned down?
A bigger title at head office, with a team of two hundred.
2. Why did she take the smaller role instead?
It had real accountability for a market, rather than a large team and no decisions.
3. What does she say she would not have learned?
How to lose money — and how to explain it to a board without losing the room.
4. What is her advice about telling this story?
Tell it as judgement, not as luck — say what you chose and why, not what happened to you.

Defining turnone decision, told as judgement — "if I had..., I would have..."
1. Which line is the hardest to fill in, and why is that the important one?
"What breaks if you leave" — it forces you to name accountability instead of activity, which is exactly what separates a director from a manager.
2. Why does the last line ask for a decision and not an achievement?
An achievement can be luck. A decision, told with "if I had...", shows judgement — and judgement is what the profile is really assessing.
