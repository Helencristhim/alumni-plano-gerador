Lesson 16 -- Pre-class

Ethics on the Site -- The Rules and the Gray Areas

Every company has a code of conduct, and the hard decisions are the ones it does not cover. Key words: a code of conduct, a conflict of interest, a kickback, to disclose something, a gray area, a loophole, to cut corners, to bend the rules, compliance, a whistleblower, integrity, to turn a blind eye to something. Structure: modals of obligation and prohibition -- must, have to, mustn't, don't have to, be required to, be not allowed to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A code of conduct -- the written rules a company expects every employee to follow
"Every new engineer signs our code of conduct on the first day, before he visits a site."
A conflict of interest -- a situation where your personal interest could influence a decision you make at work
"My cousin's company was bidding for the job, so I had a conflict of interest and stayed out of the decision."
A kickback -- an illegal payment to somebody who helped you win a contract
"In our sector the most common kickback is not cash. It is a favor paid back later."
To disclose something -- to tell the company about something officially, before anybody asks
"I disclosed the invitation the same afternoon, in writing."
A gray area -- a situation where it is not clear what is right, or what the rule says
"A weekend at a supplier's beach house is a gray area. Our code did not mention it."
A loophole -- a gap in the rules that lets people avoid them without breaking them
"The rule said cash and gifts. Invitations were a loophole, so we closed it."
To cut corners -- to do a job badly or unsafely to save time or money
"Skipping the second coat of waterproofing is cutting corners, even if nobody will ever see it."
To bend the rules -- to break a rule a little, usually saying it is only this once
"Once you bend the rules for one client, every client knows the rules can bend."
Compliance -- following the laws and the rules that apply to your company, and being able to prove it
"The bank checked our compliance with the safety rules before it approved the loan."
A whistleblower -- a person who reports something wrong or illegal that is happening where they work
"Our code says nobody is allowed to punish a whistleblower, and I have to make sure that is true."
Integrity -- being honest and doing the right thing, even when nobody is watching
"A client can check our concrete. What he cannot check is our integrity, so he has to trust it."
To turn a blind eye to something -- to pretend you have not noticed something wrong
"The foreman saw the missing harness and turned a blind eye to it. That is the day we changed the rule."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A code of conduct is written in the language of rules, and English has more than one way of saying that something is a rule. Must is the strongest and the most personal: the company, or the person speaking, makes the rule. "Every engineer must wear a harness above two meters." Have to is used when the rule comes from outside — the law, the city, the client: "We have to get a new permit for every extra floor." Must is never followed by to, and it has no past form. Yesterday's obligation is always had to: "Last year we had to close the site for a week."

The two negatives are where most Brazilian speakers make mistakes, because Portuguese uses one expression for both. Mustn't is a prohibition: there is no choice. "You mustn't accept cash from a supplier." Don't have to is the opposite of a rule: it means you are free to decide. "You don't have to report a coffee after a meeting." You can report it if you want — nobody requires it. Mix these two up in an interview and you say the opposite of what you mean.

Written codes prefer longer, more formal forms. Employees are required to disclose a conflict of interest. Nobody is allowed to punish a whistleblower. Suppliers are not permitted to offer gifts. In conversation, the short forms are more natural; in a document, the long ones sound more official.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Obligation and Prohibition

GRAMMAR

Mustn't closes the door; don't have to opens it. And must has no past -- use had to.

JobFormExample
Obligation (your rule)must + plain verbEverybody must wear a harness.
Obligation (outside rule)have to + verb; past had toWe have to get a permit.
Prohibitionmustn't / are not allowed toYou mustn't accept cash.
No obligationdon't have toYou don't have to ask me first.
Formal obligationbe required to + verbEmployees are required to disclose it.
Formal prohibitionbe not allowed to / not permitted toSuppliers are not permitted to offer gifts.

Never: you must to wear · we musted close it · nobody is allowed punish · you don't have to accept cash (when you mean it is forbidden).
