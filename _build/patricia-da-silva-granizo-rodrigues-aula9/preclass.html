Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Projecting Forward

The ten terms of opening a site in the United States -- four of them legal -- and the four ways English talks about the future. Key words: requisition, ramp-up, hiring freeze, jurisdiction, compliance, at-will employment, exempt employee, non-compete, payroll, to phase. Structure: will, going to, present continuous and future perfect.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read it inside a real sentence. Four of these are legal terms with no exact equivalent in Brazilian labour law.

Requisition -- the formal request to open a position, with approval and a budget line
"Nothing is a real role until the requisition is approved."
Ramp-up -- the period in which activity increases towards full capacity
"The ramp-up in Austin is six months, not six weeks."
Hiring freeze -- a decision to stop opening new positions for a period
"A hiring freeze announced in December is a resignation letter in January."
Jurisdiction -- the area in which a particular set of laws applies
"Texas and California are two jurisdictions, not two offices."
Compliance -- following the rules and laws that apply to you
"Compliance is not a department here, it is a column in the hiring plan."
At-will employment -- the US rule that either side may end the employment at any time, with exceptions
"At-will employment is the biggest single difference from Brazilian labour law."
Exempt employee -- a US category of salaried employee who is not entitled to overtime pay
"Classifying somebody as exempt when they are not is an expensive mistake."
Non-compete -- an agreement limiting where somebody may work after leaving
"A non-compete that is normal in Texas may be unenforceable in California."
Payroll -- the list of employees and the money paid to them
"We will be running two payrolls in two jurisdictions by June."
To phase -- to introduce something in stages instead of all at once
"We are phasing the openings: Austin first, then Sacramento."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

We are opening the Austin site in March and Sacramento in September; both dates are fixed. We are going to phase the openings rather than run them together, because we are not going to have two payrolls and one payroll specialist twice in the same year.

I will send the job descriptions to counsel by Thursday. By June we will have hired eight of the eleven roles, and by September we will have completed the certification for all of them. The one thing I will not promise is full capacity in six weeks. When we have the requisitions, we will post them -- not before.

1. We are opening the Austin site in March. Why not we will open?
A Because the present continuous is more formal in a plan.
B Because the date is a fixed arrangement, and will would make it sound like a decision just taken.
C Because open cannot be used with will.
2. By June we will have hired eight of the eleven. What does this form say?
A That the hiring will start in June.
B That the hiring will be finished before June arrives.
C That eight people will be hired during the month of June.
3. When we have the requisitions, we will post them. Why have and not will have?
A Because English does not use a future form after when in this structure.
B Because the requisitions already exist.
C Because will cannot appear twice in one sentence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Four Futures

GRAMMAR

English distributes the future across four forms, and each one tells the listener something different about how settled the thing is. Using will for everything is understandable and costs you precision.

FormWhat it doesExample
willa decision made in the moment, a prediction, or a promiseI will have the plan by Thursday.
going toa plan already decided before you spoke, or evidence in front of youWe are going to open Texas first.
present continuousa fixed arrangement, usually with a time attachedWe are opening the Austin site in March.
future perfect (will have + participle)finished BEFORE a point in the futureBy June we will have hired eleven people.
the trapI will send it tomorrow for something already arranged. If it is in the diary, English uses the present continuous: I am sending it tomorrow. Will makes it sound like you have just decided.

And one structural rule that catches everybody: after when, as soon as, before and until, English uses the present for a future event. When we have the requisitions, never when we will have.
