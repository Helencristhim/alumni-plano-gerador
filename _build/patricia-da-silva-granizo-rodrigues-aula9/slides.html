Twenty Minutes With US Counsel

Dialogue

The Legal Call

C
Curt Delaney, employment counsel. I have got twenty minutes. You are opening Austin and Sacramento, is that right?
P
That is right. We are opening Austin in March and Sacramento in September. Eleven roles in the first ramp-up, mostly field engineers.
C
Then you are dealing with two different worlds, and I would not use the same paperwork in both. Start with the easy one. In Texas, employment is at-will and stays at-will unless you write something that changes it.
P
And in California?
C
At-will as well, on paper. In practice California gives the employee a great deal more, and a non-compete that is routine in Texas is generally unenforceable there. Do not copy the Texas contract across.
P
Understood. What is the mistake you see most often with field engineers?
C
Classification. Somebody decides an engineer is exempt because they are salaried, and two years later there is a claim for unpaid overtime with interest. Send me the job descriptions before you post anything.
P
I will have them with you by Thursday. And by June we will have hired eight of the eleven, so I would rather be wrong now than in June.
Comprehension

About Curt

1. What does Curt say about at-will employment in the two states?
That in Texas employment is at-will and stays at-will unless something written changes it, and that California is at-will on paper but in practice gives the employee a great deal more.
2. What does he warn her about copying?
The Texas contract. He says a non-compete that is routine in Texas is generally unenforceable in California, so the paperwork should not be copied across.
3. What does he say is the mistake he sees most often, and what does he ask for?
Classification -- deciding an engineer is exempt because they are salaried, which leads to a claim for unpaid overtime with interest two years later. He asks for the job descriptions before anything is posted.

Chapter 4: The Code

Four Futures, and the One You Overuse

When an American expects will, going to, the continuous, or the future perfect

Grammar Discovery

We Are Opening Austin in March

"I will have the job descriptions with you by Thursday."

"We are going to open Texas before California."

"We are opening the Austin site in March."

"By June we will have hired eight of the eleven."

All four are about the future. One is a decision made right now, one a plan already taken, one a fixed arrangement, one a finish line. Which is which?

FormWhat it doesExample
willa decision made in the moment, a prediction, or a promiseI will have the plan by Thursday.
going toa plan already decided before you spoke, or evidence in front of youWe are going to open Texas first.
present continuousa fixed arrangement, usually with a time attachedWe are opening the Austin site in March.
future perfect (will have + participle)finished BEFORE a point in the futureBy June we will have hired eleven people.
the trapI will send it tomorrow for something already arranged. If it is in the diary, English uses the present continuous: I am sending it tomorrow. Will makes it sound like you have just decided.

Portuguese speakers reach for will for everything, and it works -- but it makes every arranged thing sound as if you had just thought of it. In a hiring plan that costs you credibility.

Chapter 5: The Ear

Two People Who Have Done This Before

A US employment lawyer and an HR director who opened a site in Texas

Listening 1

Fifty States, Fifty Rule Books

An employment lawyer, briefing an international team. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What is the mistake he says people from outside the US make?
Thinking of the United States as one jurisdiction, when it is fifty, plus the federal layer and cities with their own rules.
2. What three examples does he give of rules that vary?
Notice periods, which mostly do not exist by statute; non-competes, routine in Texas and generally unenforceable in California; and pay transparency, where some states require a salary range in the advertisement and others forbid asking what somebody earned before.
3. What does he tell every new client to do?
Not to build one process, but one process plus a compliance column, with the state at the top of every page.
Listening 2

What the Ramp-Up Actually Cost Us

An HR director who opened a US site two years ago. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What did they tell the board, and what actually happened?
They said the site would be at full capacity in six weeks, and it took seven months.
2. Where did the time actually go?
Not in recruiting -- nine of eleven roles were filled inside two months. None of the nine could work unaccompanied for another four months, because the certification is site specific and training slots are booked a quarter ahead.
3. What would she do differently, and why?
Say seven months at the start and take the argument in week one, when it costs an uncomfortable meeting, rather than in month four, when it costs her credibility.
