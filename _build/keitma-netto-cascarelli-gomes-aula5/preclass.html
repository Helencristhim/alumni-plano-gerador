Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A spike -- a sudden sharp increase that does not last
"There is a spike in June and then it goes back to normal."
A dip -- a short fall before the number comes back up
"That is a dip in April, not a trend."
A driver -- the thing that makes a number move
"The main driver is the new contract in the south region."
To account for -- to be the reason for a part of a total
"One contract accounts for eighty per cent of the increase."
An outlier -- a number so far from the others that it needs its own explanation
"June is an outlier, and every other month is in line."
A forecast -- the number you expect before the period is over
"The forecast has not changed, because this is a one-off."
A budget -- the number you agreed to spend before the year started
"That line came in eighteen per cent above budget."
Seasonality -- the pattern that repeats at the same time every year
"Part of it is seasonality, since we see the same thing every June."
To be in line with -- to be as close to the expected number as you wanted
"Every other month is in line with the budget."
To overstate -- to show a number as bigger than it really is
"If we book it twice, we overstate the cost by half a million."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a common belief, in every finance team in the world, that the only two answers to a hard question are a confident one and a silent one. The confident one is dangerous, because it is remembered when it turns out to be wrong. The silent one is worse, because it is remembered every time. What almost nobody is taught is that English has a third answer, and it is not vague at all — it is precise about something else. It is precise about how sure you are.

The tools are four small verbs, and they sit on a scale. Must be is the top: almost certain, because no other explanation is left standing. It must be the new contract, because nothing else changed. Could be and may be sit in the middle: possible, and you have a reason to think so. It could be freight, because the fuel index moved in the same month. Might be is weaker still: possible, but you would have to check. And cannot be is the floor — not doubt, but the opposite of it: you have evidence against the idea. It cannot be a posting error, because every entry traced back correctly.

The shape is simple and there are two traps in it. First, these verbs are followed by a bare verb, with no to and no -s: it must be, never it must to be. To talk about the past, add have plus the participle: it must have been posted twice. Second, the opposite of must be is cannot be, not must not be — must not in English is an order, not a deduction. And one habit matters more than the grammar: every one of these sentences is followed by a reason. The verb says how sure you are; the because after it is the only part that makes anybody believe you.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- How Sure Are You

GRAMMAR

Four verbs on a scale, and a reason after every one of them.

WordHow sure you areExample
must bealmost certain — no other explanation is leftIt must be the new contract, because nothing else changed.
could be · may bepossible, and you have a reason to think soIt could be freight, because the fuel index moved.
might bepossible, but weaker — you would have to checkIt might be seasonality, but I would have to check.
cannot beruled out — you have evidence against itIt cannot be a posting error, because every entry traced back.
the shapemodal + bare verb: no to, no -sNot it must to be. Say it must be.
about the pastmodal + have + past participleIt must have been posted twice.
the trapthe opposite of must be is cannot be, never must not beNot it must not be currency. Say it cannot be currency.
the one that travels with itthe spike happens in June, on the fifth, and you send it to somebodyI sent the explanation to Mark on Friday.

Never: it must to be the contract · it must not be currency · I sent it for Mark · the spike happened at June

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Nothing else changed that month and you are almost certain. You say:
A It must to be the new contract.
B It must be the new contract, because nothing else changed.
C It is for sure the new contract, one hundred per cent.
You have evidence against one explanation. You say:
A It must not be a posting error.
B It cannot be a posting error, because every entry traced back correctly.
C It is not possible to be a posting error, I checked.
You suspect something happened last month, and you are almost sure. You say:
A It must have been posted twice.
B It must be posted twice last month.
C It must to have been posted twice.
Somebody pushes back and asks whether you are sure. The strongest answer is:
A Yes, definitely, I am sure.
B What I am sure about is the total. To be certain about the split, I would need one more day.
C I do not know, sorry.
