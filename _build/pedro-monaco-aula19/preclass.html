Lesson 19 -- Pre-class

The Difficult Conversation

A deadline was missed, the client wants it to go away, and you have to tell the truth and hold your position. Key expressions: to break the news, to beat around the bush, to soften the blow, a heads-up, to own up to, to pass the buck, to point fingers, damage control, a worst-case scenario, a silver lining, an automatic renewal, to stand your ground, in good conscience, to put things right. Structure: past modals -- should have, must have, can't have, might have, could have.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to the moment something goes wrong: how to say it, who takes the blame, and what happens next.

To break the news -- to tell somebody about something bad that has happened
"I'd rather break the news myself than let her hear it from the distributor."
To beat around the bush -- to avoid saying the important thing directly
"Don't beat around the bush: tell me what went wrong."
To soften the blow -- to make bad news less painful for the person who hears it
"There is no way to soften the blow, so I'm going to say it in the first sentence."
A heads-up -- an early warning that something is going to happen
"Thanks for the heads-up; I'll call the client before the end of the day."
To own up to -- to admit that you did something wrong
"If it was our mistake, we will own up to it."
To pass the buck -- to make somebody else responsible for a problem that is yours
"The worst thing legal can do now is pass the buck to the sales team."
To point fingers -- to blame other people for something that went wrong
"I'm not here to point fingers; I'm here to fix the contract."
Damage control -- action taken to make a bad situation less harmful
"The mistake is done; now it is a question of damage control."
A worst-case scenario -- the worst result that could realistically happen
"In the worst-case scenario, we are tied to this distributor for one more year."
A silver lining -- something good that comes out of a bad situation
"The silver lining is that we can renegotiate the price now."
An automatic renewal -- a contract clause that extends the contract unless somebody cancels it in time
"Nobody noticed the automatic renewal until the contract had already renewed."
To stand your ground -- to refuse to change your position when somebody pressures you
"If the client asks you to backdate the letter, stand your ground."
In good conscience -- honestly, without feeling that you are doing something wrong
"I can't sign that in good conscience."
To put things right -- to correct a mistake or a bad situation
"We made the mistake, and we are going to put things right."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

On Monday morning, Pedro discovers that a distribution agreement renewed automatically for another year. The notice to end it should have gone out ninety days earlier, and it never did. Before he calls the client, he tries to understand what happened.

The head of sales is sure that the email went out: somebody on my team must have sent it. That is a deduction — she has no proof, but she feels certain. Pedro has checked every inbox, so he answers with a stronger deduction in the other direction: they can't have sent it; there is no trace of it anywhere. Then he adds a possibility: it might have been discussed in a meeting, but nobody wrote it down.

When it is time to talk about responsibility, Pedro chooses should have for his own side: legal should have put the date in the shared calendar. He avoids you should have told us, even though it is partly true, because it sounds like an accusation. And he uses could have for the option that existed and was not used: we could have set an alert months ago.

All of these forms follow the same pattern: modal + have + past participle. What changes is the meaning: should have criticizes, must have and can't have deduce, might have and could have open a possibility.

1. Why does the head of sales say somebody must have sent it?
A Because she has proof that the email was sent, and she can show it to Pedro.
B Because she feels certain, even without proof.
C Because she is criticizing her team.
2. Which sentence is a criticism?
A Legal should have put the date in the shared calendar.
B It might have been discussed in a meeting.
C They can't have sent it; there is no trace of it anywhere in the inboxes.
3. Why does Pedro avoid you should have told us?
A Because it is grammatically wrong in a business email or in a conversation with a client.
B Because it is not true at all.
C Because it sounds like an accusation.
4. What pattern do all these forms follow?
A Modal + have + past participle.
B Modal + had + the base form of the verb, as in the past perfect.
C Modal + past simple.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Modals

GRAMMAR

Your first language uses almost the same verb for criticism and for deduction about the past. English separates them, and the difference matters in a difficult conversation.

PatternWhen to use itExample
should have + past participleCriticism or regret: the right thing that did not happen.We should have sent the notice in March.
shouldn't have + past participleCriticism of something that happened and was a mistake.She shouldn't have signed it without a review.
must have + past participleA confident deduction about the past.The email must have gone to the old address.
can't have + past participleA deduction that something was impossible.He can't have read the clause; it took him two minutes.
might / could have + past participleA possibility about the past: maybe.It might have been lost in the handover.
could have + past participleAn option that existed but was not used.You could have asked us before you signed.

One habit for this week: in speech, should have sounds like SHUD-uv and must have like MUS-tuv. In writing, it is always have, never of.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Something has gone wrong with a client. Choose the version a professional would actually use.

1. You start the call with the client:
A So, how are things? Well, there is a small detail about the contract that we need to discuss at some point.
B I'm afraid I have bad news, and I'd rather tell you now.
C You should have read the contract.
2. You take responsibility for your team:
A Legal should have put the date in the calendar.
B Legal should put the date in the calendar last year, before the renewal date.
C Legal must put the date in the calendar.
3. You are almost sure the email went to the wrong person:
A It should have gone to the wrong address.
B It must have gone to the wrong address.
C It must go to the wrong address, according to the logs we checked this morning.
4. The client asks you to backdate a document. You refuse politely:
A Of course, I'll change it.
B I can't do that, because if anybody finds out, we will all lose our jobs and the company will be sued.
C I can't do that in good conscience, but here is what I can do.
5. You want to say that the client had another option:
A You could have asked us before you signed.
B You could ask us before you signed it last month.
C You can't have asked us before you signed, as far as I remember from the emails.
