Chapter 3: The Code

Blaming, Guessing, Owning It

should have, must have, can't have, might have

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"We should have sent the notice in March."

"The email must have gone to the old address."

"She can't have read the clause; she signed it in two minutes."

"It might have been lost in the handover."

All four orange parts talk about the past. In which one is the speaker criticizing? In which ones is the speaker guessing, and how sure is the speaker each time?

FormWhat it doesExample
should have + past participleCRITICISM or REGRET: the right thing to do in the past, which did not happen."We should have sent the notice in March."
shouldn't have + past participleCriticism of something that DID happen and was a mistake."She shouldn't have signed it without a review."
must have + past participleA confident DEDUCTION about the past, based on evidence."The email must have gone to the old address."
can't have + past participleA deduction that something was IMPOSSIBLE in the past."He can't have read the clause; it took him two minutes."
might / could have + past participleA POSSIBILITY about the past: maybe, but you are not sure."It might have been lost in the handover."
could have + past participleAn option that EXISTED but was not used, often a criticism."You could have asked us before you signed."

Your first language uses almost the same verb for criticism and for deduction. English separates them: should have blames, must have guesses.

Meaning

One Mistake, Four Sentences

The same missing notice, seen from four different angles

"We should have put the date in the calendar."

Taking responsibility. The speaker criticizes his own side.

"You should have told us about the renewal."

Blaming. True or not, the other person hears an accusation.

"Somebody must have forgotten to send it."

A confident guess. The speaker has evidence, but no proof.

"It might have gone to the wrong address."

A possibility. The speaker is not sure at all.

Chapter 4: The Conversation

The Notice That Never Went Out

Nora Collins, head of sales, and a contract that renewed by itself

Dialogue

Whose Fault Is It?

N
Pedro, you said it was urgent. Please don't beat around the bush: what happened?
P
I wanted to break the news myself. The notice to end the distribution agreement should have gone out ninety days before the renewal date, and it didn't.
N
That can't be right. Somebody on my team must have sent it. We talked about it in February.
P
We have checked every inbox. It might have been discussed, but it was never sent. Because of the automatic renewal, the contract renewed last week for another year.
N
So whose fault is it? I'm not going to let my team take the blame for a legal deadline.
P
I'm not here to point fingers or pass the buck. Legal should have put the date in the shared calendar, and we will own up to that.
N
Then fix it. Tell them we sent it by email in March. Nobody can prove that we didn't.
P
I can't do that in good conscience, and I'm going to stand my ground on it. The worst-case scenario is one more year, and the silver lining is that we can renegotiate the price now.
Comprehension

About Nora

1. What does Nora ask Pedro not to do?
To beat around the bush.
2. Who does she think must have sent the notice?
Somebody on her team.
3. What is she not going to let happen to her team?
She is not going to let them take the blame for a legal deadline.
4. What does she ask Pedro to tell the distributor?
That they sent the notice by email in March.
Listening 1

Clients Forgive the News

A senior partner on the first call after a missed deadline. Sound first — no text.

1. According to her, what do clients not forgive?
Finding out late.
2. Who does she call first when a deadline is missed?
The client, before the internal meeting and before anybody writes a report.
3. Why does she never speculate in front of the client?
Because guessing sounds like an excuse.
Listening 2

Ten Minutes Too Long

A lawyer remembers the worst conversation of his career. Sound first — no text.

1. What news did he have to give, and to whom?
That they had lost an appeal; he had to tell the owner of a family business.
2. What did the client tell him after ten minutes?
That he should have told her in the first minute.
3. What does he do now when he has bad news?
He says it first, in one sentence, and then he stops talking.
