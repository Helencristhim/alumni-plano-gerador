Chapter 4: The Count That Is Still Running

When the Story Has No End Date

The shape English uses for a company that is still going

Grammar Discovery

Two of These Close. Two Do Not.

"She has run the same company for twelve years."

"She went home that night and wrote one line."

"Two firms have made offers, and she has turned both down."

"Vantia has not been sold yet, and nobody has ever bought a company for its address."

The red sentence names the night it happened, and the story closes. The green ones name no date at all, because the count is still running — and tomorrow it could go up.

JobFormExample
Something that started before and is still truehas / have + past participle + for or sinceDataside has operated for fifteen years.
A count that can still go uphas / have + past participleWe have delivered a hundred and eighty projects.
A life experience, with no dateever · neverWe have never lost a client to a competitor.
News, with the result still herejust · already · yetWe have just signed the term sheet.
Finished, and the time is namedpast simple + a closed timeWe opened the Recife office in 2018.
The trapa named time kills the present perfectNot Last year we have grown. Say Last year we grew.
The other trapsince never takes the present simpleNot I am CEO since 2009. Say I have been CEO since 2009.
The permanent targetthe company is an it, so it takes hasNot Dataside have grown. Say Dataside has grown.

In one line: name the date and the sentence closes. Leave the date out and you are still counting.

Chapter 5: Real Speed

The Story, Told Out Loud

Two recordings and one page

Listening 1 · Sixty Seconds

A Founder Who Has Learned the Sentence

Sixty seconds on stage at an investor event. Sound first — no text.

1. How long has the company been operating, and how many people work there?
Nine years, and a hundred and ten people.
2. What number does he give as the proof, and what does he say about it?
Two hundred and forty deployments, and he says the number goes up every month, which is why he never puts a final figure on a slide.
3. What does he say he is not going to talk about, and why?
The technology, because he says nobody has ever written a cheque because of an architecture diagram.
Listening 2 · An Advisor

What a Track Record Actually Proves

An investor relations advisor on the numbers founders choose to show. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say a track record proves, and what does she say it does not prove?
It proves the company can repeat what it has already done. It does not prove the company can do something it has never done.
2. What is the first question she says every investor asks a consultancy?
Whether it is scalable — and she says it usually comes inside the first hour.
3. What does she advise founders to do with that question?
Answer it before it is asked, with a number, because a founder who waits for the question looks like a founder who has not thought about it.

Every line on this page is a count that is still running. None of them carries a date, because a date would close it. The one number a buyer will ask for and this page does not give is the one you should be ready to say out loud.
1. Which line on the page names a closed year, and which one leaves the date out on purpose?
The founding line names 2009, which is closed. The operating line leaves the date out: operating for fifteen years is still running.
2. Why does the note say a date would close the line?
Because naming a time turns the sentence into the past simple, and the point of the page is that every number can still go up.
3. What is the one number the page does not give?
Revenue — and the note says you should be ready to say it out loud.
