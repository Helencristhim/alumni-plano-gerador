Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Have You Ever -- The Convention Conversation

Ever, never, already and yet, in the twenty minutes between two sessions. Key expressions: to network, to introduce, to represent, to coordinate, to negotiate, to handle, client base, small talk, icebreaker, name badge, to run into, to be in touch. Structure: the present perfect with ever, never, already and yet -- and where each of the four words goes in the sentence.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

To network -- to meet people in your industry and build useful professional relationships
"I have never been very good at networking, but I am learning."
To introduce -- to tell people who somebody is, so that they can start talking
"Let me introduce you to the person who handles Chile."
To represent -- to speak or act officially for a company or a country
"I represent the commercial team for Brazil at this event."
To coordinate -- to organize different people or parts so that they work together
"She coordinates the four country teams from one office."
To negotiate -- to discuss something until both sides agree on the terms
"We have never negotiated a contract this size before."
To handle -- to deal with a task, a problem or a person successfully
"I have never handled the operations side of a route."
Client base -- all the clients a company or a person works with
"Our client base in Chile has grown since the new route opened."
Small talk -- light, easy conversation with somebody you do not know well
"Small talk is not the point. It is the door to the point."
Icebreaker -- something you say or do to start a conversation with strangers
"The badge is the fastest icebreaker in the building."
Name badge -- the card with your name that you wear at an event
"Blue on the name badge means commercial."
To run into -- to meet somebody by chance
"I ran into an old client at the coffee table."
To be in touch -- to stay in contact with somebody over time
"We have been in touch since the last convention."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

She has never enjoyed this part. The session ends, ninety people stand up, and there are twenty minutes of small talk before the next one. In Portuguese she does this without thinking. In English she takes a coffee, looks at her phone, and waits for somebody she already knows to walk past.

This year she decides to try what the column suggested. She looks for a name badge she does not recognize, and she uses the colour as an icebreaker: green means operations, and she has never worked with that team. That is not a weakness. It is a question she genuinely wants to ask.

The conversation is easier than she expected. She asks whether he has ever coordinated a route launch, and he talks for four minutes. She says she has never handled the operations side and that she has always wanted to understand it. He asks about her client base, and she answers with a number and two names. Somewhere in the middle they discover that they both represent the company at the same event in March.

Then the part that costs nothing and that almost nobody does. Before she walks away, she says what happens next. She has not met his director yet, and he offers to introduce her. She asks him to send the launch timeline, and he asks for her numbers. They have not negotiated anything and they have not signed anything, but they are going to be in touch, and in this industry that is how everything starts. The next time she runs into him, they will not be strangers.

1. Why does the text say "she has never enjoyed this part" and not "she never enjoyed this part"?
A Because it is about her whole life up to now, and it is still true.
B Because it happened once, last year.
C Because the sentence names a closed moment.
2. Where does never go in an English sentence?
A Before have or has.
B Between have or has and the verb.
C At the end of the sentence.
3. "She has not met his director yet." What does yet tell us?
A That she will never meet him.
B That she met him a long time ago.
C That it has not happened up to now, and it is still expected.
4. According to the text, what does she do before she walks away?
A She says what happens next.
B She hands over a business card and leaves.
C She introduces him to her own director.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Ever, Never, Already and Yet

GRAMMAR

Four small words that carry a whole conversation with a stranger. Three of them sit in the middle of the sentence and one of them sits at the end -- and getting that position right is most of the work.

FormWhat it saysExample
everAsks about your whole life up to now. It belongs to questions, not to normal statements."Have you ever worked in Chile?"
NOT "I have ever worked in Chile."
neverZero times, ever. It is already negative, so you never add not as well."I have never worked in Chile."
NOT "I have not never worked in Chile."
alreadyIt happened, and sooner than the other person expected. Goes between have and the verb."I have already met two of your colleagues."
yetIt has not happened up to now, and it is still expected. Questions and negatives only, and it goes at the end."I have not met your director yet."
"Has the client confirmed it yet?"
Where each one sitsever, never and already go between the auxiliary and the verb. yet goes at the end of the sentence, always.have ever worked · have never worked · have already worked · have not worked yet

A useful habit for the corridor: when the honest answer is never, add what you have done instead. "I have never handled operations, but I have worked with the team on two launches." That turns an admission into a conversation.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are in the corridor at the regional convention, with a coffee in your hand. Choose the best thing to say.

1. Somebody is standing alone with a green badge. You open with:
A Have you ever been to one of these before?
B Have you ever be to one of these before?
C You have ever been to one of these before?
2. He asks whether you have worked with operations. You have not. You say:
A I have not never worked with operations.
B I never have worked with operations.
C I have never worked with operations, but I would like to understand it better.
3. He asks whether you have met the regional director. You have not, and you expect to. You say:
A I have not met him yet.
B I have yet not met him.
C I did not meet him yet.
4. You met two of his colleagues earlier this morning. You say:
A I have met already two of his colleagues.
B I have already met two of your colleagues this morning.
C I already have met two of your colleagues this morning.
5. The break is nearly over and the conversation was good. You close with:
A Nice to meet you. (and you walk away)
B Send me your figures, and let us stay in touch after this week.
C I have ever enjoyed talking to you.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is scoring it.

Think of one real person you would like to talk to at the next convention, and record the first two minutes of that conversation, in about ninety seconds. Open with an icebreaker, ask one question with "have you ever", say one thing you have never done and one thing you have, and finish with a concrete next step. Keep every sentence short enough to finish.
