Lesson 15 -- Pre-class

Phrasal Verbs as Executive Language -- The Words Between the Words

The spoken register of an American working day: the two-word verbs that people say in the room, next to the long formal verbs that documents write. Key words: to bring up, to come up with, to sign off on, to run into, to walk through, to fall through, to hold off, to weigh in. Structure: phrasal verbs -- separable (bring it up), inseparable (run into it), three-part (come up with it) and the ones that take no object at all (the contract fell through).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each verb and read the example. Notice that the stress falls on the second word.

To bring up -- to put a topic on the table in a meeting or a conversation
"I would rather bring up the delay myself than wait for them to find it."
To come up with -- to produce an idea or a solution that did not exist before
"The team came up with a workaround in two days."
To sign off on -- to give the final approval to something
"The VP signs off on the budget at the end of the quarter."
To run into -- to meet a problem or a person without planning it
"We ran into a licensing issue two weeks before the launch."
To walk through -- to explain something to somebody step by step
"Let me walk you through the first ninety days."
To fall through -- to fail to happen after it was already planned or agreed
"The vendor contract fell through in the first quarter."
To hold off -- to wait before doing something you have already decided to do
"We are holding off on the migration until the audit closes."
To weigh in -- to give your opinion in a discussion where other people are deciding
"Ray weighs in on anything that touches safety."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

A recruiter writes a short note before the panel. Dana opens, and she will bring up cost in the first five minutes. Ray weighs in on anything that touches safety. The VP signs off on the offer at the end. If the candidate runs into a number she does not have, the advice is simple: hold off, and send the real figure later instead of trying to come up with one in the room. Nothing in that note is difficult English, and yet many strong readers would never write it that way. They would say "raise the cost issue", "approve the offer", "encounter a problem", "generate a figure". Those verbs are not wrong -- they are written English, and they belong in a report. In a conversation, English prefers the two-word verb. Notice where the object goes, because that is the whole grammar of tonight. Some of these verbs open up: you can bring up the delay or bring the delay up, and if the object is a pronoun it can only go in the middle, so it is bring it up and never bring up it. The same happens with walk through: you walk somebody through a plan, so it is "let me walk you through it". Other verbs never open up at all. You run into a problem and you run into it -- the two words stay together, pronoun or not. Three-part verbs are even stricter: you come up with a solution, you come up with it, and nothing ever goes inside that group. And a few take no object at all, which is why the vendor contract fell through and Ray simply weighed in. Say the sentence with the word it and your ear will tell you which type you are holding.

1. Why does the text say that "raise the cost issue" and "approve the offer" are not wrong?
A Because they are old forms that native speakers no longer understand.
B Because they are written English, and they belong in a report. In conversation, English prefers the two-word verb.
C Because they mean something completely different from the phrasal verbs.
2. According to the text, why is "bring up it" impossible?
A Because with a separable verb a pronoun can only go in the middle: bring it up.
B Because "bring up" never takes an object of any kind.
C Because "up" has to be the last word of the sentence.
3. What is different about "come up with" compared with "bring up"?
A It takes no object, so nothing can follow it.
B Its object can go in the middle, exactly like a separable verb.
C It is a three-part verb: the three words stay together and the object always follows all of them.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Phrasal Verbs

GRAMMAR

Four types, and where the object goes in each one.

TypeWhere the object goesExample
Separable
bring up, walk through		After the particle or in the middle. A pronoun goes only in the middle."bring up the delay" / "bring the delay up" / "bring it up"
"walk me through the plan"
Inseparable
run into		Always after both words, pronoun included."run into a problem" / "run into it"
Three-part
come up with, sign off on, hold off on, weigh in on		The three words stay glued, and the object follows all of them."come up with a solution" / "come up with it"
"sign off on the budget"
No object
fall through, weigh in, hold off		Nothing follows the verb at all."The contract fell through." / "Ray weighed in."
The test, and the registerReplace the object with it and say it out loud: if "bring it up" sounds right, the verb is separable; if only "run into it" works, it is not. And remember the register: the long single verb (raise, approve, encounter) is what you write, the two-word verb is what you say.
