The Pitch Decoded

Three phrases you will hear forty times on an exhibition floor, and none of them means what it sounds like. Key words: interoperability, compliance burden, settlement, counterparty, middleware, friction, throughput, token-based, to roll out, to sign off on. Structure: "used to", for saying how things worked before.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Interoperability -- the ability of two different systems to work together without a translator in between
"Interoperability is the whole question when a bank runs three systems from three decades."
A compliance burden -- the extra work a rule puts on you before you can do the actual job
"The compliance burden is where most optimistic timelines quietly die."
Settlement -- the moment the money actually moves and the transaction is finished
"The instruction leaves in a second. Settlement still takes two days."
A counterparty -- the institution on the other side of the transaction
"If the counterparty has to change anything, the project is twice as long."
Middleware -- software that sits between two systems and passes messages between them
"Their middleware translates between formats. Which formats, exactly?"
Friction -- anything that makes a transaction slower or more expensive than it needs to be
"Friction covers two days of delay and one extra click. Ask which one they mean."
Throughput -- how many transactions a system can handle in a given time
"Four thousand a second is a throughput figure, and a figure can be tested."
Token-based -- built so that value moves as a record on a network, not as a message to a bank
"Token-based or not, they are all selling against the same two-day delay."
To roll out -- to make something available to everybody, step by step, after the pilot
"We will roll out in two corridors first, and only then in the rest."
To sign off on -- to give the final approval that lets something go ahead
"I can recommend it. The business unit signs off on it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Half of what you will say at a technology conference is a comparison between how something worked before and how it works now, and English has a structure built for exactly that. Used to plus the bare verb says that something was a habit or a state, and that it has stopped — without you having to say when, or having to explain that it ended. Banks used to send short coded messages. Compliance teams used to check everything by telephone. We used to reconcile by hand. In every one of those sentences, the listener knows the practice is over, and nobody had to add a date.

Compare it with the past simple, which is for one finished event with a time attached: last year we tested two providers. That is one thing that happened once. We used to test every provider ourselves is a habit that lasted years and then stopped. The two are not interchangeable, and choosing the wrong one changes the story you are telling about your own institution.

Two mechanical details. In the negative and in the question, the d disappears, because did is already carrying the past: did not use to, did you use to — never did not used to. And there is a lookalike that means something completely different: be used to, which means to be accustomed to something, and which takes a noun or a verb with -ing. I am used to meetings in English, or I am used to working in English — never I am used to work. One sentence says a habit ended; the other says you are comfortable with something now. In a meeting, saying the wrong one turns a story about progress into a statement about yourself.

1. What does "used to" tell the listener, according to the text?
A That something happened once, at a particular time.
B That something was a habit or a state and has stopped, without anybody giving a date.
C That somebody is comfortable with something now.
2. What happens to the d in the negative and in questions?
A It disappears, because did is already carrying the past: "did not use to".
B It stays, and did is added in front: "did not used to".
C The whole structure changes to the past simple.
3. What does "I am used to working in English" mean?
A That I worked in English in the past and stopped.
B That I am accustomed to it now.
C That I will work in English from now on.

Stage 2: Put the Demo Questions in Order

Order

A supplier has just finished a two-minute demo. Put your questions in the order that gets you the most.

?"That was clear, thank you. Can I ask four things before we run out of time?"
?"You said the middleware translates between formats. Which formats, exactly?"
?"What is your throughput at peak, in transactions per second?"
?"Compliant with which regulation, in which jurisdiction, and as of what date?"
?"Which counterparty have you done that with, and can I speak to them?"
?"And last one: what did you take out of this demo to make it run smoothly?"

Quiz

A That sounds very interesting.
