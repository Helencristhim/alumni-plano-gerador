Staying Sharp After Day Two

The second half of a big conference: fast accents, a tired brain, and how to stay in the conversation. Key words: cognitive load, attention span, conference fatigue, side conversation, corridor talk, off the record, to stay in the loop, to manage expectations, to pick up where we left off, to buy time. Structure: the past continuous for getting back into a conversation.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Cognitive load -- the amount of mental effort your brain is using at one moment
"After four meetings in a second language, my cognitive load was very high."
An attention span -- the length of time a person can concentrate on one thing
"By the last panel of the day, my attention span was about ten minutes."
Conference fatigue -- the tiredness you feel after several long days of meetings, panels and networking
"Conference fatigue usually hits me on the afternoon of day two."
A side conversation -- a short private conversation between two people while a bigger meeting or event continues
"The most useful information came from a side conversation after the panel."
Corridor talk -- informal conversations in the halls of a conference, between the official sessions
"Corridor talk is where you hear what people really think about a new regulation."
Off the record -- said privately, and not meant to be repeated or published officially
"Off the record, she said the new rules will probably be delayed."
To stay in the loop -- to keep receiving the latest information about something
"Please copy me on those emails so I can stay in the loop."
To manage expectations -- to explain clearly what is and is not possible, so people are not disappointed later
"I need to manage expectations: we will not decide anything before March."
To pick up where we left off -- to continue a conversation from the exact point where it stopped
"I have to run now, but let's pick up where we left off tomorrow at nine."
To buy time -- to say or do something that gives you a few more seconds to think
"Repeating the question is a polite way to buy time before you answer."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every conference has a curve. On day one, your energy is high, but your ears are still adjusting. By the afternoon of day two, the problem is different: cognitive load. You have been in meetings since nine, your attention span is short, and conference fatigue is real.

This is exactly when the best information arrives, in corridor talk and quick side conversations. So what do you do when somebody speaks fast and you lose the thread? You do not pretend. You show where you lost it, and the past continuous is the perfect tool: I was following you until you mentioned Brussels. Or, after an interruption: You were saying that the banks are not ready.

The past continuous, was or were + verb-ing, describes an action in progress at a moment in the past. The short action that stops it is in the past simple: I was reading a message when you asked. To go back, ask: What were you saying about the fees?

One small trap: verbs like understand and know do not usually take -ing. Not I was understanding, but I was with you until... Clear recovery phrases help you buy time, and they show the other person that you are careful, not lost.

1. According to the text, what is the main problem on the afternoon of day two?
A The ears are still adjusting to the accents and the speed of the room, so the listening is slow all day.
B There is no useful information left.
C Cognitive load: the brain is full after many meetings.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A I was follow you until you mentioned the regulator in Brussels.
B I was following you until you mentioned Brussels.
C I following you until you mentioned Brussels.
3. After a phone call, you want to go back to her point about fees. You ask:
A What were you saying about the fees?
B What you were saying about the fees and the pricing model for next year?
C What did you were saying about the fees?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past Continuous for Recovery

GRAMMAR

Show the exact point where the conversation was when you lost it.

UseFormExample
Action in progress in the pastwas / were + verb-ingI was following you. · She was explaining the rules.
Interrupted by a short actionpast continuous + when / until + past simpleI was reading a message when you asked.
Asking to go backWhat were you saying about...?What were you saying about the testing phase?
Restarting after a breakWe were talking about...Where were we? We were talking about the deadline.
State verbsno -ing with understand, knowI was with you until your second point.

Never: I was follow you · We discussing the timeline · What you were saying? · I was understanding

Stage 2: Get Back into the Conversation

Order

A conversation in the corridor is interrupted by a phone call. Put it in a logical order, from the first sentence to the next step.

?"You were saying that the enforcement gets softer."
?"Sorry about that. Where were we?"
?"Right. Let's pick up where we left off tomorrow at nine."
?"Off the record, half of the banks in Europe are not ready for the new rules."
?"That's a lot to process. Are you saying that the deadline could move?"
?"Not move, but the enforcement gets softer. Sorry, I have to take this call."

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen, then record yourself. You will get a word-by-word score. Take a two-second pause before you start: a calm start sounds confident.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that works best on the afternoon of day two.

A banker speaks very fast and you understand only half. You say:
A Yes, yes, I understand, of course, that is a very good point about the regulation in Europe.
B What?
C I may have caught only part of that. Could you hit the key point again?
Your phone rang in the middle of her explanation. After the call, you say:
A Sorry. Can you say everything again from the beginning?
B Sorry about that. You were saying that the testing takes longer?
C Sorry, so what did you were explaining before my call about the tests?
Your director wants a decision this week, but the vendor needs a month of tests. You say:
A Let me manage expectations: we will not have a decision this week.
B No problem, we can decide this week.
C I think maybe we can try to decide something soon, but I am not sure, because there are many tests and many people involved.
You need two seconds to think before answering a hard question. You say:
A Hmm, hmm, hmm, well, you know, it depends.
B That's a lot to process. Give me a second.
C I need to think about this question for a long time, so maybe I can answer it by email next week.
