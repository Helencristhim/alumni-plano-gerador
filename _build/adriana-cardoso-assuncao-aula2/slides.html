Two of these words are traps in a meeting. Turnover in human resources means people leaving; in finance the same word means the money a company makes in a year. And a rate is always a proportion, never a total: if you say the rate went up by two hundred people, nobody understands you. Say the number went up by two hundred, or the rate went up by two points.

What the Average Is Hiding

The Headline Rate

What the Average Is Hiding

Every quarter the same slide opens the meeting: one turnover rate for the whole company. This quarter it is fourteen point two percent, higher than last quarter and higher than the market benchmark of eleven point five. The room reacts to the headline, somebody asks whether salaries are the problem, and the conversation is over before it starts. The trouble is that a single rate for eight hundred people is an average, and an average is the easiest place in a report to hide bad news.

Break the same number down and it says something else. Operations is at nineteen percent and the corporate offices are at six, so the gap between the two is wider than the gap between the company and the market. Absenteeism has been steady for four quarters, which means people are not sick; they are leaving. And overtime tells you why: the two sites that pay the most extra hours are the same two sites that lose the most people. The headline rate is not wrong. It is just not the number that tells you what to do on Monday.

Chapter 4: The Code

Two Numbers, One Sentence

Higher than, much worse than, the highest in three years

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Have in Common?

Turnover is higher than it was last quarter.
Operations is much worse than the corporate offices.
This is the highest rate we have had in three years.
Absenteeism is as steady as it was four quarters ago.
Click to reveal the rule
Two things: comparative — short word + -er than (higher, wider, steadier), long word + more ... than (more expensive). One against all the others: superlative — the + -est (the highest) or the most (the most expensive), always with a period or a group: in three years, of all our sites. Irregular pair you will use every week: good / better / the best, bad / worse / the worst. To say two things are equal: as ... as. And to make the difference sound big, put much or far in front: much worse, far higher.

The benchmark says eleven point five. We are three points above it. That is a bad number.

The number is right, and I am not going to argue with it. But which companies are inside that benchmark? Half of them are in a much tighter labor market than ours.

The vice president looks at the headline rate and asks whether salaries are the problem.

  • Do not answer yes or no. Offer the breakdown first.
  • One comparative sentence is enough to move the room.

You have to say that operations is at nineteen percent and the offices are at six.

  • Two numbers, one comparison. Use than.
  • Points, not percent, when you say the difference.

Somebody says your turnover is terrible compared to the market.

  • Ask which companies are inside that benchmark.
  • You can agree with the number and disagree with the conclusion.

You want to say this is the worst quarter in three years, without sounding defensive.

  • The superlative needs a period: in three years.
  • Follow it immediately with what you are going to do.

The finance director asks how much the extra hours cost.

  • One point four million dollars.
  • Say the decimal as point, never comma.
