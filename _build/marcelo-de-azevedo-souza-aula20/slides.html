Chapter 4: The Code

Participle Clauses

Two ideas, one sentence

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Sitting with the map, I marked three rest days."

"Having ridden all day, I could barely speak."

"Written in a hurry, the early notes say almost nothing."

"A route sketched in pencil is still a plan."

No and, no because, no which — and the subject is never repeated.

ShapeIt meansExample
-ing clauseat the same time as the main verbSitting with the map, I marked the days.
-ing clausethe reason (because ...)Knowing that road, I took the long way.
having + participlefinished before the main verbHaving ridden all day, I could barely speak.
past participlepassive: it was done to the thingWritten in a hurry, the notes say nothing.
reduced relativea shorter "that is / that was"a route sketched in pencil

One rule decides everything: the participle clause and the main clause must share the same subject.

Language Focus

Before or At the Same Time?

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

✗ WRONG

"Having ridden all day, the hotel was a relief."

✓ RIGHT

"Having ridden all day, I found the hotel a relief."

✗ WRONG

"Reading fifteen notebooks, I finally understood the pattern."

✓ RIGHT

"Having read fifteen notebooks, I finally understood the pattern."

Ask who did it. The hotel did not ride, so the sentence needs I. And when the first action is already finished, use having plus the participle, not a plain -ing.

Dialogue

Taking Stock with Marit

M
Twenty rides together, on and off. Sitting here with this map, do you ever stop and take stock of all of it?
M
I did last month, for the first time. Having read fifteen notebooks in order, I could see exactly where I had plateaued.
M
That is brave. Most people count the milestones and never go looking for the blind spots.
M
Mine was rest. Every trip that fell through, fell through for exactly the same reason. It is a hard-won lesson.
M
Well, with eleven countries under your belt, you have already laid the groundwork. Now you build on it instead of starting again from nothing.
M
That is exactly how it feels. Sketched in pencil and left loose after the first week, the new route finally fell into place last night.

Money. She has never written down what a trip actually costs, so she is surprised every year, and twice a trip fell through in March because of it.

Listening 2

The Twenty-Year Question

A host on how to tell whether you are still improving. Read the questions first, then listen.

0:000:00
1. What does the host say most of us do long before we stop practicing?
We stop improving. Having reached the level where everything feels comfortable, we quietly stop learning and call it experience.
2. Why does he insist on taking stock on paper rather than in your head?
Because memory is generous and paper is not.
3. What does he say twenty trips or twenty years actually are?
Not a finish line. Groundwork, hard-won and quietly laid, for whatever you decide to do next.

Self-Assessment

What I Learned

I can join two ideas with an -ing clause: "Sitting with the map, I marked the days".
I use "having" plus a past participle for what was finished first.
I use a past participle alone for a passive idea: "Written in a hurry, the notes say nothing".
I keep the same subject in both halves, so nothing dangles.
I can take stock out loud: milestones, blind spots and what comes next.
