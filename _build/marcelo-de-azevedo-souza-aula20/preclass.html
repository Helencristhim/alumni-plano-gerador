Lesson 20 -- Pre-class

The Road So Far -- Taking Stock and Building the Next Trip

Closing the first twenty lessons: read an honest audit of fifteen years on the road and learn to put two ideas in one sentence with participle clauses. New vocabulary: to take stock, a milestone, to have come a long way, a stepping stone, to lay the groundwork, to consolidate, to build on, a blind spot, to plateau, hard-won, to have under your belt, to fall into place.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To take stock -- to stop and look carefully at where you are
"Every few years I sit down and take stock."
A milestone -- a point that marks real progress on a long road
"Twenty rides is a milestone worth marking."
To have come a long way -- to have improved a great deal since the start
"Reading those notebooks, you can see he has come a long way."
A stepping stone -- something small that carries you to the next stage
"That first tour was a stepping stone, not a destination."
To lay the groundwork -- to do the quiet work that makes later success possible
"Those winters laid the groundwork for everything after."
To consolidate -- to make what you already know firmer and more solid
"This season is for consolidating, not for adding."
To build on -- to use what you already have as the base for more
"Now we build on what is already there."
A blind spot -- the one thing you keep failing to notice about yourself
"Rest days were my blind spot for a decade."
To plateau -- to stop improving and stay at the same level
"I plateaued somewhere around year eight."
Hard-won -- gained only after a great deal of effort
"It is a hard-won lesson, and I paid for it twice."
To have under your belt -- to have already done something and kept it for good
"With eleven countries under my belt, I plan differently."
To fall into place -- to start to make sense and work out well
"By the third evening, the whole route fell into place."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Marcelo spent an evening taking stock. Laid out on the kitchen table were fifteen notebooks, one for each season. Written in a hurry at the end of enormous days, the first three record kilometers and nothing else. Comparing them year by year, he could see exactly where he had plateaued. Having learned enough to be comfortable, he had quietly stopped learning at around year eight. There was also a blind spot: every trip that fell through, fell through for the same reason. The trips that worked were the ones with a rest day built in — a hard-won lesson. Sitting with the map last week, he stopped drawing lines between cities and started marking the days he will not ride. The new route, sketched in pencil and left deliberately loose, finally fell into place. Notice the shapes. An -ing clause happens at the same time as the main verb. Having plus a past participle happened before it. And a past participle on its own is passive: somebody else did it.

1. Why does the text say "Comparing them year by year, he could see ..." instead of "He compared them and he could see ..."?
A A participle clause joins the two ideas in one sentence, with no "and" and no repeated subject.
B Because comparing happened long after he could see the pattern.
C Because the notebooks compared themselves.
2. What does "Having learned enough to be comfortable, he had quietly stopped learning" tell you about the order of events?
A The two things happened at exactly the same moment.
B The learning was finished first, and only then did he stop.
C He stopped first and learned afterwards.
3. Why is it "Written in a hurry" and not "Writing in a hurry"?
A The notebooks did not write. Somebody wrote them, so the clause is passive.
B Both forms are always interchangeable in English.
C Because the action is still going on today.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Participle Clauses

GRAMMAR

Two ideas, one sentence. Ask yourself: before, at the same time, or done to it?

ShapeIt meansExample
-ing clauseat the same time as the main verbSitting with the map, I marked three rest days.
-ing clausethe reason (because ...)Knowing that road, I took the long way.
having + past participlefinished before the main verbHaving ridden all day, I could barely speak.
past participle alonepassive: it was done to the thingWritten in a hurry, the notes say nothing.
reduced relativea shorter "that is" or "that was"a route sketched in pencil

Key point: the participle clause and the main clause must share the same subject. "Having ridden all day, the hotel was a relief" is wrong, because the hotel did not ride. Say "Having ridden all day, I found the hotel a relief".

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best response for each situation.

You are looking at a map and, while you do it, you mark the rest days. How do you say it in one sentence?
A "Sitting with the map, I marked the rest days."
B "Sat with the map, I marked the rest days."
C "Having sat with the map, I sit and mark the rest days."
You finished reading fifteen notebooks, and only then did you understand the pattern. Which sentence is right?
A "Reading fifteen notebooks, I understood the pattern."
B "Having read fifteen notebooks, I understood the pattern."
C "Read fifteen notebooks, I understood the pattern."
The notes were written by you, in a hurry, years ago. How do you describe them now?
A "Writing in a hurry, the notes say almost nothing."
B "Written in a hurry, the notes say almost nothing."
C "Having written in a hurry, the notes say almost nothing."
A friend says: "Having ridden all day, the hotel was a relief." What is wrong with it?
A Nothing at all. The sentence is correct.
B The hotel did not ride. Both halves need the same subject: "I found the hotel a relief".
C It should start with "Have ridden" instead.
