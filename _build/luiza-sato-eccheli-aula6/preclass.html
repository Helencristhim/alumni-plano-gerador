Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

How the TOEFL Reads You -- Inference and Deduction

The new TOEFL reading section rewards inference, not word hunting. Learn the modals that say what must be true. Key words: To skim, To scan, To infer, An inference, A passage, A distractor, A context clue, To eliminate, Adaptive, A module, To pace yourself, To second-guess, A hunch. Structure: modals of deduction in the present and in the past.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To skim -- to read something quickly to get the general idea, without reading every word
"Skim the passage first, and then read the questions."
To scan -- to look quickly through a text to find one particular piece of information
"Scan the email for the date of the meeting."
To infer -- to reach an idea from what a text suggests, even if it does not say it directly
"From the last paragraph, you can infer that the author disagrees."
An inference -- an idea you reach from the information you have, not from a direct statement
"The question asks for an inference, not for a fact."
A passage -- a short part of a text, used for reading questions in a test
"The academic passage is shorter in the new version of the test."
A distractor -- a wrong answer in a multiple-choice question that is designed to look right
"The distractor used the same words as the text, but changed the meaning."
A context clue -- a word or a phrase near an unknown word that helps you understand it
"Use the context clues instead of stopping on the word."
To eliminate -- to remove something because it is wrong or not needed
"Eliminate two options first, and the choice becomes easier."
Adaptive -- describes a test that changes its next questions based on your earlier answers
"The reading section is adaptive, so the second part depends on the first."
A module -- one separate part of a test or a course
"Your score in the first module decides the level of the second module."
To pace yourself -- to control your speed so that you have enough time or energy until the end
"Pace yourself, or you will run out of time in the last passage."
To second-guess -- to doubt a decision you have already made, and change it without a good reason
"Do not second-guess your first answer unless the text proves it wrong."
A hunch -- a feeling that something is true, without clear proof
"Check your hunch against the text before you choose."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Reading tests are full of questions that ask what must be true, and the answer is rarely a sentence you can point to. It is a deduction. English has a small group of modal verbs for exactly that job, and they tell your listener how sure you are.

When you are almost certain that something is true now, use must: “The group rooms are booked online, so there must be a booking website.” When you are almost certain it is not true, the opposite is not mustn’t, which means prohibition. It is can’t: “The library closes at nine, so students can’t be there at ten.”

When something is only possible, use might, may or could: “That option could be a distractor.” For the past, keep the modal and add have + past participle: “She left three questions blank, so she must have run out of time,” and “He finished in ten minutes, so he can’t have read the whole passage.”

This is also how you should think during the test. When you infer, you are not guessing: you are saying what must be true if the text is true. When you eliminate an option, you are saying it can’t be supported. And when you want to change an answer because of a hunch, ask yourself whether you are deducing, or only second-guessing.

1. According to the text, what is the opposite of must for deduction?
A mustn’t
B can’t
C shouldn’t
2. How do you talk about a deduction in the past?
A Keep the modal and add have + past participle.
B Use the modal with a past verb, like must ran.
C Use did before the modal.
3. What does the text say you should ask yourself before changing an answer?
A Whether the option is longer.
B Whether the tutor would agree.
C Whether you are deducing or only second-guessing.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Deduction

GRAMMAR

Must for almost yes, can’t for almost no, might for maybe; add have + participle for the past.

JobFormExample
Almost certain yes (now)must + verbThere must be a website.
Almost certain no (now)can’t + verbThey can’t be there.
Possible (now)might / may / could + verbIt could be a distractor.
Almost certain yes (past)must have + participleShe must have run out of time.
Almost certain no (past)can’t have + participleHe can’t have read it.
Possible (past)might have + participleHe might have had a hunch.

Never: he mustn’t be at home (for deduction) · she must to have studied · he might has made a mistake · they must read it yesterday
