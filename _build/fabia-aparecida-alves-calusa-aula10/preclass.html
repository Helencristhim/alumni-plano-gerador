Stage 1.1: Words for a disagreement

Vocabulary

All eight are neutral. None of them accuses anybody — that is exactly why they work. Tap Listen and say each one out loud.

Dispute — a disagreement about whether something is right
"There is one figure in dispute, not the whole report."
Discrepancy — when two numbers that should match do not
"There is a discrepancy of four thousand between the two files."
Reconcile — to make two sets of numbers agree, line by line
"We reconciled both files this morning."
Query — a formal question about one specific item
"They have one query on the payroll line."
Back up — to show the evidence that proves your figure
"Every line is backed up by the source document."
Concede — to accept that the other side is right on one point
"I will concede the date, but not the amount."
Cross-check — to check one source against another
"We cross-checked it against the bank statement."
Overstate — to report a figure as bigger than it really is
"They believe the cost was overstated by four thousand."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

She has just come off the call and is telling her own team about it. Read it once, then answer.

Quick one before you all go. I have just come off the call with the client.

He said his file and ours did not match on the warehouse line — a discrepancy of about four thousand. He asked whether we could resend the breakdown, and he wanted to know where the four thousand came from.

I told him it was an invoice that had landed after his export, and that we would send it across today.

I conceded one thing: he said the description on that line was unclear, and he was right, so we are changing that wording everywhere.

What I need from you: cross-check both versions against the invoices and come back to me before the end of the day. Not tomorrow morning. Today.
Why "he asked whether we could resend" and not "he asked can you resend"?
A Because "whether" is more polite than "can".
B Because a reported question loses its question shape and the verb steps back: can → could.
C Because the request was made in writing rather than out loud.
Why does she report the concession to her team at all?
A Because it changes what the team does — the wording has to be fixed in every report from now on.
B To show the team that she was polite with the client.
C Because the client asked her to pass the apology on.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Reporting takes the verb one step back.

Statements → said. is → was · will → would · have → had · can → could. "The figure is wrong" → He said the figure was wrong.

Yes/no questions → asked whether. "Can you resend it?" → He asked whether we could resend it. The question mark disappears with the question shape.

Wh- questions → asked / wanted to know + the wh- word. "Where does it come from?" → He wanted to know where it came from — never "where did it come from".

Said or told? told takes a person straight after it: he told me. said does not: he said the figure was wrong, or he said to me. "He said me" does not exist.

This is not decoration. "He said the figure was wrong" records his position on the record, without you putting your name under it.

Stage 2: Build the line

Order

Put the reply to the client in the order that works.

?"Thank you for flagging it."
?"Could you confirm which version you are looking at?"
?"You are right that the description was unclear."
?"The amount is backed up by the invoice, which I am attaching."
?"We will reconcile both files and come back to you today."

Stage 3: Say it out loud

Speaking

The first one buys you the ten seconds you need to think in English. Say all three calmly, without speeding up.

Thank you for flagging it. Could you confirm which version you are looking at?
You are right that the description was unclear.
He asked whether we could resend the breakdown.

Stage 4: When they push back

Quiz
A client says a figure is wrong and you are almost certain it is right. What comes out of your mouth first?
A "No, that figure is correct. It was cross-checked."
B "Thank you for flagging it. Could you confirm which version you are looking at?"
C "I am so sorry. I will check it and get back to you."
He is right about a small point and wrong about the big one. What do you do?
A Concede the small point plainly, then back up the big one with the document.
B Say nothing about the small point so that the big one looks stronger.
C Concede both, to keep the relationship comfortable.
Telling your team what he asked:
A "He asked where did the four thousand come from."
B "He wanted to know where the four thousand came from."
C "He said me where comes the four thousand."
