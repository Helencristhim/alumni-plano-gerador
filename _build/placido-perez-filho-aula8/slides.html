Chapter 4: The Code

How Long, or How Many?

Two forms of the present perfect

Grammar Discovery

How Long, or How Many?

"I’ve been studying English for about forty years."

"This week I’ve read three book summaries."

"She has been learning Japanese since she retired."

"He has reached two important milestones this year."

Blue: the activity continues, and the question is how long? Red: the result, and the question is how many? or what? Both connect the past to now.

JobFormExample
Duration, still goinghave been + -ing + for / sinceI’ve been studying for forty years.
Result, or a numberhave + past participleI’ve read three summaries this week.
Ask about timeHow long have you been + -ing?How long have you been learning?
Ask about resultsHow many... have you + past participle?How many books have you read?
State verbssimple only: know, like, understand, haveI’ve known her for years.
The trapno present simple for a duration up to nowNot I study English for forty years. Say I’ve been studying.

In one line: how long = have been + -ing; how many / what result = have + past participle.

Listening 1 · A Researcher

What We Know Now

A British researcher talks about older language learners in a radio interview. Sound first — no text.

1. How long has she been studying older learners?
For about fifteen years.
2. What surprised her most?
How well older learners retain words when they use them in conversation.
3. What is the one thing older learners usually need more of?
Repetition, and more time.
Listening 2 · A Retired Engineer

Spanish at Sixty-Seven

A retired engineer from India talks about learning Spanish after he retired. Sound first — no text.

1. Why did he start learning Spanish?
Because his daughter married a man from Spain and he wanted to talk to the family.
2. How long has he been learning?
For two years.
3. What milestone has he reached?
He has had a full conversation with his son-in-law’s parents.
Listening 3 · The Plateau

When Progress Stops

An Australian language coach explains the intermediate plateau and how to get past it. Sound first — no text.

1. When does the plateau usually happen?
At the intermediate level, after the first few years.
2. Why does it happen, according to him?
Because learners keep doing the same things that worked at the beginning.
3. What are his two pieces of advice?
Change the input, and set a new, concrete goal.
