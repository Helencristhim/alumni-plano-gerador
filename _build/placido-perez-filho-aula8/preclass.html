Lesson 08 -- Pre-class

Learning After Sixty -- The Long Road

What research says about adults who learn languages. Key words: a myth, to retain, consistency, a plateau, to brush up on, to keep at it, proficiency, a milestone, exposure, to memorize, cognitive, a mindset. Structure: present perfect simple and continuous.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A myth -- an idea that many people believe but that is not true
"It’s a myth that adults can’t learn languages."
To retain -- to keep information in your memory
"Older learners retain new words well if they use them."
Consistency -- doing something in the same way, regularly, over time
"Consistency matters more than talent."
A plateau -- a period when you stop making progress for a while
"Many learners reach a plateau at the intermediate level."
To brush up on -- to practice something you learned before so that you get better again
"I’m brushing up on my grammar before the trip."
To keep at it -- to continue working hard at something difficult
"It’s slow, but if you keep at it, you improve."
Proficiency -- a high level of skill in something
"My goal is full proficiency, not just fluency."
A milestone -- an important moment or step in a long process
"Watching a film without subtitles was a milestone for me."
Exposure -- the time you spend in contact with something, like a language
"The more exposure you get, the faster you understand."
To memorize -- to learn something so well that you can remember it exactly
"I don’t memorize lists; I learn words in sentences."
Cognitive -- connected with thinking, memory and understanding
"Learning a language is good cognitive exercise."
A mindset -- a way of thinking that influences how you act
"A learner’s mindset is more important than age."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ana is seventy. She has been learning English for five years, and this year she has read twelve books in English. She says that age is a myth: what matters is consistency and a good mindset.

Last year she reached a plateau. Instead of trying to memorize lists, she changed her routine and got more exposure: series, podcasts and real books. She kept at it, and she retains words much better now.

Her latest milestone? A film without subtitles. Now she is brushing up on her grammar. Her goal is proficiency, and she says it is great cognitive exercise.

1. “She has been learning English for five years.” Is she still learning?
A Yes.
B No, she stopped.
C We don’t know.
2. What did Ana do when she reached a plateau?
A She stopped for a year.
B She changed her routine and got more exposure.
C She memorized more lists.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A “This year she has been reading twelve books.”
B “This year she has read twelve books.”
C “This year she reads twelve books.”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect -- Simple or Continuous?

GRAMMAR

Both connect the past to now. The continuous answers how long and shows the activity. The simple answers how many or what, and shows the result.

JobFormExample
Duration, still goinghave been + -ing + for / sinceI’ve been studying for forty years.
Result, or a numberhave + past participleI’ve read three summaries this week.
Ask about timeHow long have you been + -ing?How long have you been learning?
Ask about resultsHow many... have you + past participle?How many books have you read?
State verbssimple only: know, like, understand, haveI’ve known her for years.
The trapno present simple for a duration up to nowNot I study English for forty years. Say I’ve been studying.

Never: I study English for forty years · I’ve been reading three books · I’ve been knowing · since two years
