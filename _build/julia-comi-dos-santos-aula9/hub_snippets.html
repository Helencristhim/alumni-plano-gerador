Stage 1.1: Words for Advice

Vocabulary

Tap Listen, then say the word out loud. Eight words, two minutes.

Advice — what somebody tells you to do, when they want to help
"She gave me some good advice about the interview."
Recommend — to say that something is good, so somebody else tries it
"I recommend the small company. You learn more."
Network — the people you know who can help you professionally
"Half of the internships come from your network."
Visa — the official permission you need to enter another country
"You should apply for the visa three months before."
Appointment — a time you booked to meet somebody
"I have an appointment at the consulate on Monday."
Contact — to write to somebody or call them
"You could contact them again next week."
Organise — to put things in order so you can find them
"You should organise your documents in one folder."
Warn — to tell somebody about a problem before it happens
"She warned me that the queue is always long."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the message. Then answer the questions.

My cousin gave me a lot of advice about the trip, and most of it was good. She said I must book the visa appointment now, because it takes six weeks. That one is not advice, she said, that is a deadline. Then she said I should organise all my documents in one folder, so I do not lose anything at the consulate. She recommended a card that works abroad, and she warned me not to take much cash. She also said I could contact two people she knows there — her network, not mine, but she says that is how it works. They might not answer. But they might.
Why does the cousin say "must" about the visa appointment?
A Because it takes six weeks — it is a deadline, not advice.
B Because the consulate is far away.
Why is it "I should organise" and not "I should to organise"?
A Because after a modal the verb never takes "to".
B Because organise is irregular.
How sure is she that the two contacts will answer?
A She is sure they will.
B Not sure at all — they might, they might not.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Advice is a scale, not a rule

Choose the strength first. Then the sentence is easy.

must — no choice. "You must have a valid passport."
should — my advice. "You should apply three months before."
could — one option. "You could contact them again."
might — maybe. "They might answer tomorrow."
− You should not wait until December. (short: shouldn’t)
? Should I contact them again? (no do, no does)

After all of them the verb never changes: no to, no s, no -ed. And must sounds like an order in English — with friends, use should.

Stage 1.5: Complete the sentence

Practice

Write must, should, could or might. Choose the strength first.

"You have a valid passport."
"I think you book it this week."
"You always contact them again."
"They answer tomorrow."

Stage 3: Say it out loud

Speaking

Tap Listen, then Record. "Should" is short and quiet — the stress falls on the verb after it.

I think you should book it this week.
You could always contact them again.
I see what you mean, but I would rather do it the other way.

Stage 4: In the conversation

Quiz
A friend asks what to do about a late reply. Which sounds best?
A You must write to them right now.
B I think you should contact them again.
Which sentence is correct?
A She should contact them again.
B She shoulds to contact them again.
Somebody gives you advice you do not agree with. What do you say?
A No, that is wrong.
B I see what you mean, but I would rather do it the other way.

Stage 5: Advice for somebody real

Reflection
Write four pieces of advice first, each with a different strength: one "must" about something with a deadline, one "should" about the internship hunt, one "could" for somebody who is stuck, and one "might" about something you are not sure of. Then turn the paper over and record 30 seconds giving them to a real person you know — without reading.
