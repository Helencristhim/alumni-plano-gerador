Lesson 17 · Precision Is Credibility

Numbers, Data and Evidence

At a public hearing, one wrong number can destroy an hour of good arguments. Two percent is not two percentage points, and a few is not few. Today you present numbers the way a committee trusts them.

Chapter 3: The Code

Most, a Few, Roughly

Quantifiers and approximate numbers

Grammar Discovery

How Much, How Many, How Sure?

"Most investors look at the rules first."

"Only a few concessions were cancelled."

"Roughly a third of the budget goes to maintenance."

"Very little evidence supports that idea."

Look at the nouns after each blue part: investors, concessions, budget, evidence. Which ones can you count? Why a few with concessions but little with evidence? And what does roughly tell the audience?

JobFormExample
Countable nouns (investors, projects)many / a few / few / a number ofA few concessions were cancelled.
Uncountable nouns (money, evidence, data)much / a little / little / a large amount ofVery little evidence supports it.
A part of a specific groupmost of the / half of the / a third of the + nounMost of the users were happy.
Approximate numbersroughly / around / just over / just under / almostJust over two thousand users answered.
Percent or percentage pointsfrom 4% to 6% = two percentage points (a 50% increase)The rate rose by two percentage points.
The trapfew = almost none (negative); a few = some (positive)Few funds came (bad). A few funds came (good).

In one line: count it or not, all or part, exact or approximate: the small word gives the number its weight.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Numbers, at Real Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one page

Dialogue

The Numbers for the Hearing

M
I have the numbers for the hearing. Do you want the long version or the short one?
A
The short one, please. The committee will not read a hundred pages.
M
Okay. The port handles roughly a third of the state's exports, and traffic has doubled in ten years.
A
Good. And how many users answered the survey?
M
Just over two thousand. A majority said the service has improved, but only a few said the tariff is fair.
A
So we have strong evidence on quality, and very little on price.
M
Exactly. And be careful: the tariff rose by two percentage points, not two percent.
A
Good point. A mistake like that could destroy our credibility at the hearing.

Listening 1 · The Statistician

Numbers for People Who Hate Numbers

A statistician explains how to present data to a committee of non-experts. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does she recommend rounding numbers?
Because nobody remembers 33.7%, but everybody remembers roughly a third.
2. What should every number have, according to her?
A comparison.
3. How many numbers should a slide have?
One.
Listening 2 · The Expert Witness

When Lawyers Misuse Numbers

An economist who works as an expert witness talks about evidence in court. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the problem with the survey in his example?
The sample was very small: only forty people.
2. What did the other lawyer say about the tariff?
That it had gone up by fifty percent.
3. What is his advice to lawyers?
Always ask how big the sample is and what the number is compared with.
Business Radio

The Infrastructure Gap

A short radio report on a new study about infrastructure investment. Sound first — no text.

1. How much do most countries in the region invest in infrastructure, according to the study?
Roughly two percent of their GDP, or just under.
2. How much should they invest, according to the authors?
Around five percent.
3. Where does a majority of the private money go?
To energy and telecommunications.
