Lesson 5 · Plans and Priorities

What You Are Not Going to Do

Any plan can list what you will build. The plan that survives a board is the one that names, out loud, what you decided to give up.

One Question

What Are You Giving Up?

Name one good project you are not going to do next quarter. Say what it is, in one sentence.

Then the hard half: say why, in one more sentence, without apologising for it.

Chapter 3: The Code

Already Decided

The form a plan is written in

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"We are going to put the whole budget behind the workshop programme."

"We are not going to do the trade fair this year."

"Are you going to postpone the packaging change?"

"I am meeting the distributors on Friday."

Three red parts share the same three words, and those three words say something about how firm the plan is. The blue one is different: it already has a day on the calendar.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Plan, Under Questioning

One conversation, three recordings, one page

Dialogue

The Plan Meets the Question

D
So, next quarter. What is the priority?
M
One priority. We are going to put the whole budget behind the workshop programme.
D
The whole budget. So what are you giving up?
M
The trade fair. We are not going to do it this year, and we are going to postpone the packaging change to the second half. That is a trade-off, and I want to name it as one.
D
Good. And what does the timeline look like?
M
The proposal goes out in the first week of October. The rollout starts in November. The first milestone is thirty per cent coverage by the end of January.
D
And if January comes and you are at fifteen?
M
Then we stop and I come back here. What I need today is the budget, because the deadline for the contracts is the fifteenth. Are you going to approve it today, or do you need something else from me first?
Comprehension

About Diane

1. What is her first question, and what does she ask immediately after hearing the answer?
She asks what the priority is. Then, as soon as she hears that the whole budget goes to one programme, she asks what he is giving up.
2. Does she say anywhere that the plan is wrong? What does she do instead?
No. She never argues with the plan. She asks what it costs, what the dates are, and what happens if the first milestone is missed.
3. What does she want to know about January, and why would somebody in her chair ask that?
She asks what happens if coverage is at fifteen instead of thirty. She asks it to find out whether he has already thought about the bad case.
Listening 1 · A Director

Three Points, and One Approval

A marketing director opening a planning presentation. Sound first — no text.

1. What is her priority, and what is she giving up for it?
The whole budget goes behind the workshop programme. She is giving up the trade fair this year, and calls it a trade-off.
2. What are the three dates in her timeline?
The proposal goes out in the first week of October, the rollout starts in November, and the first milestone is thirty per cent coverage by the end of January.
3. What is the one approval she asks for, and why does she need it now?
The budget. She needs it because the deadline for the distributor contracts is the fifteenth.
Listening 2 · A Podcast

The Plan That Survives

A business podcast on why most plans do not last the year. Sound first — no text.

1. What small thing does he say the good planning meetings all do at the start?
They say what they are not going to do.
2. What happens to the plans that skip it?
The room nods, the plan is approved, and eight months later somebody works out that three of the five things never had a chance.
3. What does naming the trade-off change about the conversation?
The room stops arguing about whether the plan is optimistic and starts arguing about whether the trade-off is the right one, which he says is the argument you actually want.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

Volume Today, or Growth Later

A business radio report on where lubricant companies put their money. Sound first — no text.

1. What are the two places a company can put its money, according to the report?
Where the volume already is, which is the big distributors, or where the growth is going to come from, which is the small independent workshop.
2. What happened to the companies that chose the workshop, and to the ones that chose the distributor?
The workshop group had two very slow years and then a very good third one. The distributor group had three comfortable years and is now finding that somebody else owns the conversation at the counter.
3. What does the report say about how long it took to know who was right?
Three years, which it points out is longer than most marketing directors stay in the job.

If coverage is below twenty per cent in January, the programme stops and comes back to this board. That line is on the page so that nobody has to invent it in February.
1. Two things are being given up on this page. What are they, and are they the same kind of decision?
The trade fair and the packaging change. They are not the same: the trade fair is dropped for the year, and the packaging change is only postponed to later.
2. What has to happen before the fifteenth, and why?
The budget has to be approved, because the fifteenth is the deadline for the distributor contracts.
3. What does the note at the bottom protect the team from?
From inventing a decision in February under pressure. The stopping rule is agreed in advance, in writing.
