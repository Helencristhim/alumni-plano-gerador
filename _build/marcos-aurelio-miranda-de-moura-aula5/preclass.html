Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

Plans and Priorities -- What You Are Not Going to Do

Any plan can list what you will build. The plan that survives a board is the one that names what you decided to give up. Key words: a priority, a timeline, a deadline, a proposal, a milestone, a budget, a trade-off, to approve, to postpone, upcoming. Structure: going to for decided plans.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A priority -- the thing that comes before everything else, when you cannot do all of them
"If three things are the priority, then nothing is the priority."
A timeline -- the order of the dates: what happens first, what happens after, and when
"The timeline is simple. October, November, and the first result in January."
A deadline -- the date after which something is late, whatever the reason
"The deadline for the distributor contracts is the fifteenth."
A proposal -- a written plan you send to somebody, asking them to agree to it
"The proposal goes out to the distributors in the first week of October."
A milestone -- a point on the way that proves the plan is working, with a date and a number
"The first milestone is thirty per cent coverage by the end of January."
A budget -- the money a team is allowed to spend inside a period
"We are going to put the whole budget behind one programme this year."
A trade-off -- what you give up in order to get something else, and you always give up something
"That is a trade-off, and I want to name it as a trade-off."
To approve -- to say officially that something can go ahead
"The board approves the budget, and the rest is my problem."
To postpone -- to move something to a later date, without cancelling it
"We are going to postpone the packaging change to the second half of the year."
Upcoming -- coming soon, and already on the calendar
"The upcoming board is in November, so the proposal has to be ready in October."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A plan is a set of decisions, and English has a form for a decision that is already taken. "We are going to put the whole budget behind the workshop programme." Three words, going to, and everybody in the room understands that this is settled, not an idea. The negative works the same way and it is the half most people skip: "We are not going to do the trade fair this year." The question inverts the first word: "Are you going to approve the budget today?"

There is a second form near it, and the difference is small but useful. When something already has a day on the calendar, English often uses the present continuous instead: "I am meeting the distributors on Friday." Both are about the future. Going to is the decision; the continuous is the appointment. Nobody will misunderstand you if you mix them, but the person listening hears the difference, and in a planning meeting that is the difference between a plan and a diary.

Now the part that has nothing to do with grammar and everything to do with being believed. Every plan lists what it is going to do. The plan that survives the year also says what it is not going to do. "We are going to postpone the packaging change." "That is a trade-off." Naming it takes thirty seconds and it changes what the room argues about: not whether your timeline is optimistic, but whether the trade-off is the right one. And when you close, close with the ask: the proposal is out in October, the first milestone is in January, the deadline is the fifteenth, and this is what I need approved today.

1. What does "going to" tell the room about a plan?
A That it is one possible idea among several.
B That the decision is already taken and settled.
C That it is happening at this exact moment.
2. What is the difference between "we are going to meet the distributors" and "I am meeting the distributors on Friday"?
A The first is the decision. The second already has a day on the calendar.
B The second one is about the past.

3. According to the text, what changes when you name the trade-off out loud?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Going To

GRAMMAR

Going to is the form of a decision you have already taken, and a board hears the difference.

JobFormExample
A decision already takenam / is / are + going to + bare verbWe are going to postpone it.
Negativeam / is / are not going toWe are not going to do the trade fair.
QuestionAm / Is / Are + subject + going toAre you going to approve the budget?
Already on the calendaram / is / are + verb-ing, with a dayI am meeting the distributors on Friday.
Whennext quarter, in October, by the end of JanuaryThe rollout starts in November.
In speechgoing to runs together as one unitWe are going to do it.
Closing the plansay the ask out loud"This is what I need approved today."

Never: we going to put the budget · I am going to presenting the proposal · we are not going do the trade fair · you are going to approve it today? · we will postpone it, I think.

Quiz

A "The trade fair. We are not going to do it this year, and that is a trade-off."
