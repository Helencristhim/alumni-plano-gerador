Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. All twelve are about something that has not happened yet. These twelve stay here in your material.

Outlook -- a general view of what the coming period looks like
"The outlook for the next two years is better than anybody was saying in January."
Trajectory -- the direction something is already travelling in
"You do not have to predict anything. Just look at the trajectory of the last decade."
Forecast -- a prediction built on the evidence you have now
"A forecast is not a promise, and the good ones say so out loud."
Inevitable -- certain to happen, whatever anybody does about it
"The change was inevitable. What surprised everybody was the speed."
Uncertain -- not settled yet, and possibly not settled for years
"The next eighteen months are genuinely uncertain, and anybody who says otherwise is guessing."
To envision -- to picture something clearly before it exists
"I find it easy to envision the trip and much harder to envision the year after it."
Momentum -- the force that keeps a change moving on its own
"Once a habit has momentum, it costs nothing to keep and everything to restart."
Scenario -- one possible version of how things could turn out
"There is a scenario where none of this matters, and it is not the unlikely one."
Optimistic -- expecting the better of the possible outcomes
"I am optimistic about the decade and cautious about the year."
Cautious -- careful, because the risk is not worth the speed
"He is cautious with predictions and generous with everything else."
Foreseeable -- near enough in the future to plan around
"For the foreseeable future we are staying exactly where we are."
To pan out -- to develop in the way somebody was hoping
"Two of the three plans will pan out, and nobody knows which two."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ask ten people what happens next and you will get ten answers of the same length. Ask them how sure they are and the answers separate immediately. That second question is the one English builds into the verb, and it is the one most learners never answer.

Look at what changes. Growth will slow. That is a flat claim, and the listener has no idea whether you would bet on it. Growth will probably have slowed by June. Now there is a likelihood and a deadline. I will still be getting used to it in December. Now the action is still running at the moment you name. She might well have changed her mind. Strong possibility, already finished. Four sentences, four different degrees of certainty, and not one of them needed a different tense.

The pattern underneath is simple enough to hold in your head at a table. Pick the modal that matches how sure you are, and then pick the shape: have plus a past participle if the thing is finished by that point, be plus -ing if it is still going on. Everything after the modal stays bare. No -s, no past tense, ever.

There is a reason this matters more in English than at home. In Portuguese the voice carries most of the doubt: the same words, said two ways, are two different claims. English puts it in the words instead, which means it survives an email, a bad connection and a room where three people are talking at once. Anything you were carrying in your tone alone arrives flattened.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Stacking the Future

GRAMMAR

One verb phrase, two decisions: a modal that says how likely, and a shape that says whether the action is finished by then or still running. Everything after the modal stays bare.

FormWhat it doesExample
will probably have + past participleLikely, and finished by the point you name."I will probably have finished by June."
will still be + verb-ingIn progress at that future moment. Still admits it is taking longer than planned."I will still be getting used to it in December."
might well have + past participleStrong possibility, already completed. Well makes might heavier, not lighter."She might well have changed her mind."
could well be + verb-ingStrong possibility, in progress at that point."They could well be dealing with that by 2040."
The trapAfter a modal the verb is bare. No -s, no past tense, no exceptions.NOT "I will probably finished"
NOT "the city could well deals"

A practical note. If you can only hold one thing, hold the shape: have plus a participle means finished by then, be plus -ing means still going. The modal in front of it is the easy half, because you already know how sure you are before you open your mouth.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best option for each situation.

Somebody asks whether you will have read the report before the meeting. You are fairly sure you will:
A I will probably have read it by then.
B I will probably read it by then, maybe.
C I will probably finished it by then.
You want to say the adjustment is not over yet at a future point. You say:
A In December I will still get used to it.
B In December I will still be getting used to it.
C In December I still getting used to it.
Which sentence is grammatically correct?
A She might well changed her mind by then.
B She might well have changed her mind by then.
C She might well has changed her mind by then.
At a dinner, somebody asks what happens to the economy next year. The strongest answer is:
A Nobody knows. It is impossible to say.
B There is a scenario where it slows and one where it does not. I would bet on the first, unless energy prices fall.
C It will be bad. Everybody says so.
A plan develops in the way you were hoping. In English you say that it:
A panned out.
B envisioned.
C forecast.
