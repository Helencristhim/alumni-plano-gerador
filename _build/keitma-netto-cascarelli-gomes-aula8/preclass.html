Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To follow up -- to come back to somebody about something that is not finished
"I will follow up with the BPO before Thursday."
An action item -- one thing somebody agreed to do, with a name and a date on it
"Three action items, and every one of them has a name."
A recap -- a short written summary of what was said and decided
"The recap is four lines, not four pages."
To copy somebody in -- to add somebody to the message so that they can see it too
"I copied the accounting manager in, because she was mentioned."
Pending -- waiting for somebody else before it can move
"Item five is pending, and it is waiting for the tax team."
To align -- to reach the same understanding as somebody else before acting
"Let us align on the cut-off before we tell the BPO."
A takeaway -- the one thing you want the reader to remember afterwards
"The takeaway is that the cut-off moves, and nothing else."
In writing -- recorded in a message or a document, not only said out loud
"A decision in writing cannot hold two meanings for long."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nine people leave a call believing they agreed the same thing. Six weeks later it turns out that three of them were in different meetings, and nobody noticed because nothing was written down. The fix is eight minutes long and it is not a set of minutes: it is four lines saying what was decided, three actions with a name on each, and one line saying what is still open and who it is waiting for.

Writing that message means reporting what other people said, and English changes the verb when you do. The rule is that the verb steps back one tense. Will becomes would: she said the invoice would arrive on Friday. Is and are become was and were: they said they were reviewing it. Has becomes had: he said the forecast had been updated. Questions lose their inversion and take whether or if: he asked whether the forecast had been updated, never had the forecast been. And an instruction becomes asked me to plus the bare verb: she asked me to send the file by Thursday.

All of that is mechanical, and none of it is where the damage happens. The damage is in the reporting verb, because the verb you choose is a judgement and the reader takes it as one. He said he would look at it and he agreed to deliver it describe the same forty seconds of a call and commit that person to two completely different things. He mentioned, he promised, he confirmed: each one moves the temperature of a working relationship, in writing, permanently. When you are not sure which verb is honest, there is one that never overstates and never accuses, and it is said.

1. What does the text say the fix is, and how long does it take?

2. What happens to a question when you report it?
A It loses the inversion and takes whether or if.
B It keeps the inversion and adds a question mark.

3. Where does the text say the real damage happens?
A In forgetting to step the verb back one tense.
B In the reporting verb, because it is a judgement and the reader takes it as one.
C In copying the wrong people into the message.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech

GRAMMAR

The verb steps back one tense. The reporting verb carries your judgement.

OriginalReportedExample
willwouldShe said the invoice would arrive on Friday.
is / arewas / wereThey said they were reviewing it.
has / havehadHe said the forecast had been updated.
a yes/no questionwhether or if, no inversionHe asked whether the forecast had been updated.
an instructionasked me to + bare verbShe asked me to send the file by Thursday.
time words movetoday → that day · tomorrow → the next dayHe said he would send it the next day.
the trap that costs mostthe reporting verb is a judgementsaid ≠ agreed ≠ promised. If unsure, use said.
the one that travels with ityou copy somebody in on a message, on a dayI copied the manager in on the e-mail, on Monday.

Never: she said the invoice will arrive · he asked had the forecast been updated · she asked me for send the file · I copied him in the email in Monday

Stage 2: Put the Recap in Order

Order

The five lines of a recap e-mail. Put them in the order that a reader can follow.

?"Short recap of this morning's call on the close calendar."
?"Decided: the cut-off moves to six in the evening, from April."
?"Actions: Mark said he would update the calendar by Friday."
?"Pending: the tax sign-off is still open and is waiting for the tax team."
?"Takeaway: nothing else changes this month."
?"If I have written anything different from what you remember, tell me today."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

On the call she said: “The invoice will arrive on Friday.” You write:
A She said the invoice will arrive on Friday.
B She said the invoice would arrive on Friday.
C She said that the invoice arrives on Friday.
He asked: “Has the forecast been updated?” You write:
A He asked had the forecast been updated.
B He asked whether the forecast had been updated.
C He asked if has the forecast been updated.
He only said “I will look at it”. The honest report is:
A He said he would look at it.
B He agreed to deliver it.
C He promised to send it.
You want your recap to invite corrections. The last line is:
A Please confirm you have read this message.
B If I have written anything different from what you remember, tell me today.
C Sorry for any mistake in this summary.
