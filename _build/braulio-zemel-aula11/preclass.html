Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

The Art of Storytelling -- The Anecdote That Earns You the Table

How to offer a story instead of waiting for a question, in ninety seconds and with one turn in it. Key expressions: an anecdote, vivid, a narrative, suspense, a punchline, to digress, to captivate, to elaborate, to set the scene, to wrap up, a twist, deadpan. Structure: used to, be used to and get used to -- the past habit, the thing that is normal now, and the thing that is becoming normal.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

An anecdote -- a short personal story told to make one point
"He has one anecdote about that year and he has told it a hundred times."
Vivid -- producing a clear, sharp picture in somebody else's head
"One vivid detail is worth ten ordinary ones."
A narrative -- the order and flow of a story, as opposed to the facts in it
"The facts were fine. The narrative was the problem."
Suspense -- the feeling of not yet knowing how something ends
"Ten extra seconds of suspense costs nothing and buys the whole table."
A punchline -- the last line, where the story turns and everybody laughs
"He gave away the punchline in the second sentence, and the rest was silence."
To digress -- to go off the subject in the middle of what you were saying
"I am digressing. Where was I? The train."
To captivate -- to hold somebody's complete attention without effort
"She captivated the table for four minutes with a story about a bus."
To elaborate -- to give more detail about something you have just said
"Do not elaborate unless somebody asks you to."
To set the scene -- to say where and when the story happens, in one sentence
"Set the scene in one sentence. San Francisco, 2012, a rented room."
To wrap up -- to bring something to a proper end instead of just stopping
"He never wraps up. The story just runs out of road."
A twist -- the turn in a story that nobody saw coming
"The twist was that the book he had been reading for a year was a dictionary."
Deadpan -- saying something funny with a completely straight face
"He said it deadpan, and it took the table four seconds to catch up."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

San Francisco, 2012. He used to rent a room in a family house instead of a hotel, on purpose, so that he would have to speak English at breakfast. For three weeks he understood almost nothing at that table. He would sit there and nod. And then one morning he realized he had been following the whole conversation for ten minutes without noticing.

That is a story, and it takes forty seconds. What makes it work is not the vocabulary. It is that the scene is set in one sentence, that something twists in the middle, and that it stops at the twist. Say one more sentence about what it taught you and the whole thing collapses. Do not elaborate unless somebody asks.

The three forms of used do most of the work in a story like this and they are not the same. I used to rent a room describes a habit that has stopped. I am used to waking up early describes something that is normal for me now and costs me nothing. I am getting used to having nothing in the diary describes a process that has started and has not finished. Three sentences, and a stranger knows where you are in your life.

One warning. After be used to and get used to, the verb takes -ing. After used to it does not. And would carries repeated actions in a story, never states: he would sit and nod, but he used to be exhausted. Get those right and nobody at the table will notice the grammar at all, which is the entire objective.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Three Forms of Used

GRAMMAR

These three cover the whole of this year for you: what you used to do, what you are used to, and what you are getting used to. The difference is entirely in what comes after the word to.

FormWhat it saysExample
used to + infinitiveA past habit or state that has STOPPED. It always implies and not any more."I used to work six days a week."
be used to + verb-ingAccustomed. It is normal for you now and costs you nothing. Nothing to do with the past."I am used to waking up early."
get used to + verb-ingThe PROCESS of becoming accustomed. Started, not finished."I am getting used to having nothing in the diary."
would + infinitiveA repeated past ACTION inside a story. It cannot carry a state or a feeling."I would leave at ten."
NOT "I would be tired."
The trapsThere is no present form of used to. And the -ing is not optional after be or get used to.NOT "I use to travel a lot these days."
NOT "I am used to wake up early."

A practical note. In a story, used to and would work together: the first one opens the habit and the second one repeats it. I used to rent a room in a family house. Every morning I would sit at that table and understand nothing. Two sentences, and the listener is already in the room with you.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a dinner and it is your turn to say something. Choose the best option.

You want to say that waking up early is normal for you now:
A "I am used to wake up early."
B "I am used to waking up early."
C "I use to wake up early."
You want to say the empty diary is slowly becoming normal:
A "I am getting used to having nothing in the diary."
B "I used to have nothing in the diary."
C "I get used to have nothing in the diary."
You want to describe how tired you were every Sunday night, years ago:
A "I would be exhausted every Sunday night."
B "I used to be exhausted every Sunday night."
C "I am used to being exhausted every Sunday night."
You have just reached the turn in your story. The best move is:
A Say it and stop.
B Explain what the story taught you.
C Go back and add the background you left out.
Somebody interrupts you halfway through. The most natural way back is:
A Start the story again from the beginning.
B "Anyway, where was I? The train."
C Give up on the story and let somebody else talk.

Reflection
