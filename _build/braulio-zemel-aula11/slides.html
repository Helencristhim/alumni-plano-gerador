Chapter 3: The Code

I Used To, and I Am Getting Used To

Three forms, three meanings, one letter of difference

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I used to work six days a week and I do not any more."

"I am used to waking up early. Thirty years does that to you."

"I am getting used to having nothing in the diary."

"On Sundays I would leave the office at ten at night."

Three of these contain the word used and they do not mean the same thing. Look at what comes straight after it in each one.

FormWhat it saysExample
used to + infinitiveA past habit or state that has STOPPED. It always implies and not any more."I used to work six days a week."
be used to + verb-ingAccustomed. It is normal for you now, and it takes no effort. Nothing to do with the past."I am used to waking up early."
get used to + verb-ingThe PROCESS of becoming accustomed. Not there yet, and moving."I am getting used to having nothing in the diary."
would + infinitiveA repeated past ACTION, in a story. It cannot carry a state."I would leave at ten." (not: I would be tired)
The one thing to watchAfter be used to and get used to, the verb takes -ing. After used to, it does not.NOT "I am used to work early."
"I am used to working early."

These three cover the whole of this year for you: what you used to do, what you are used to, and what you are getting used to. Three sentences and somebody knows where you are.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I am used to wake up early."
"I am used to waking up early."
"I use to travel a lot these days."
"I travel a lot these days."
"I would be exhausted every Sunday night."
"I used to be exhausted every Sunday night."

Three rules and they never bend. After be or get used to, the verb takes -ing. There is no present form of used to — for a present habit the plain present is enough. And would carries actions, never states, so a feeling always takes used to. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Chapter 4: San Francisco, 2012

San Francisco, 2012

A rented room, a family, and a month of breakfasts

Dialogue

Stop There. That Is the Story

S
Your wife says you spent a month in San Francisco. Tell me something about it.
B
This was 2012. I used to rent a room in a family house instead of a hotel, on purpose, so that I would have to speak English at breakfast.
S
That is a brave way to do it. Did it work?
B
For three weeks, no. I understood almost nothing at that table. I would sit there and nod.
S
Three weeks is a long time to nod at strangers over coffee.
B
And then one morning I realized I had been following the entire conversation for ten minutes without noticing. I was getting used to it and nobody had told me.
S
Stop there. That is the story. Do not explain it.
B
I was about to say what it taught me about language learning.
Comprehension

About Sylvia

1. How does Sylvia know about the month in San Francisco?
His wife told her.
2. What does she call the decision to rent a room in a family house?
A brave way to do it.
3. What does she say about the three weeks of nodding?
That three weeks is a long time to nod at strangers over coffee.
4. What does she tell him to do at the end, and why?
To stop there, because that is the story -- and not to explain it.
Listening

The Man in the Third Carriage

One anecdote, ninety seconds, one twist. Sound first -- no text.

1. What did she used to see every evening, and for how long?
The same man in the third carriage reading the same enormous book, every day for about a year.
2. Where did she see him again, and what was he doing there?
In a bookshop on the other side of the city, behind the counter.
3. What was the book, and why was he reading it?
A dictionary. He had been learning Portuguese one page a day, because his wife was Brazilian and he had got tired of not understanding her family.
Listening 2

Three Things That Make a Story

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

1. How long should setting the scene take?
One sentence -- where, when and who. Not four.
2. What does he say happens if nothing turns?
You have described a Tuesday, and people are polite about it but they do not remember it.
3. What is the practical exercise he recommends?
Tell the same story three times to three different people in one week -- by the third time you will have cut about a third of the words.

The Habit

The Same Story, Three Times

Before the next class, tell the same anecdote out loud three times, on three different days, to nobody. Time yourself. Do not change the story between the versions -- only cut.

By the third time you will have found the shape without trying, and you will have cut about a third of the words.
