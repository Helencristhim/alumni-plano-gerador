Reading the Room

A skeptical committee, a court case that is still open, a forecast nobody can promise. The best experts do not sound less sure because they know less. They sound exactly as sure as the facts allow. Today you learn to do that.

Chapter 1: First Words

From Emphasis to Precision

Last time you made a case with emphasis: what investors want is stability. But a skeptical room also tests how honest you are about what you do not know. Sometimes the strongest sentence is the careful one.

Think of an expert who sounded too sure and was wrong. What happened to his or her credibility?

Chapter 4: The Code

Tend To, Likely To, To Some Extent

Hedging: saying exactly how sure you are

Grammar Discovery

How Sure Are You?

"Investors tend to prefer stable rules."

"The rule is likely to be challenged in court."

"It could be argued that the tariff is too low."

"To some extent, the delay was predictable."

Now say each sentence without the colored words. Is the new sentence wrong, or just too strong? The blue words go inside the verb phrase. The red words stand at the start and soften the whole sentence.

JobFormExample
What usually happenssubject + tend(s) to + base verbInvestors tend to prefer stable rules.
What will probably happensubject + be (un)likely to + base verbThe rule is likely to be challenged.
The same, with a full sentenceIt is (un)likely that + subject + verbIt is likely that the court will accept it.
An opinion, not a factIt could be argued that + sentenceIt could be argued that the tariff is too low.
Only partly trueto some extent / in most cases / somewhatTo some extent, the delay was predictable.
The trapone hedge per claim; tend to and likely to + base verbNot Maybe it will probably fail or tend to preferring.

In one line: tend to and likely to + base verb; it is likely that and it could be argued that + a sentence; one hedge per claim is enough.

The Opinion on One Page

LEGAL OPINION — EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
New port access rule · prepared by Aguinaldo Ballon for the board of a terminal operator
QuestionCan the new rule be challenged in court?
Short answerYes. The rule is likely to be challenged by at least two operators.
Chance of successGood on the procedure · uncertain on the tariff
Main assumptionNo public consultation was held before the rule
CaveatCourts tend to accept the regulator’s view on tariffs
RecommendationChallenge the procedure first, not the tariff
Disclaimer: this summary is based on the documents available on the date of the opinion. It is not a guarantee of the result of any court case.
1. How sure is the opinion that the rule will be challenged?
Quite sure, but not certain: it says the rule is likely to be challenged.
2. What is the main assumption of the opinion?
That no public consultation was held before the rule.
3. Why does the opinion recommend challenging the procedure first?
Because courts tend to accept the regulator’s view on tariffs, so the tariff argument is weaker.
Listening 1 · The Communication Coach

Read the Room Before You Speak

A coach explains what she teaches lawyers before a big meeting. Sound first — no text.

1. Which cues does she tell people to watch?
The faces, if people lean forward or check their phones, and if the chair is still taking notes.
2. What is usually a mistake in a skeptical room?
To sound too certain.
3. What is an audience less likely to forgive?
A confident answer that turns out to be wrong.
Listening 2 · The Senior Partner

The Opinion With No Caveats

A senior partner remembers a legal opinion that went wrong. Sound first — no text.

1. What did his first opinion say about the tariff rule?
That the rule was illegal, with no caveats.
2. What does he add to his opinions today?
His assumptions and a short disclaimer about the parts of the law that are still open.
3. What can clients not accept, according to him?
An expert who overstates his case and then has to explain why he was wrong.

What I Can Do Now

I use tend to + base verb for what usually happens.
I use be likely to and it is likely that for what will probably happen.
