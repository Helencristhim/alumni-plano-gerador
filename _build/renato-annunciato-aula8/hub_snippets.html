The Development Plan -- Naming the Next Step

The career conversation, in the language of dates and commitments. Key words: development plan, promotion, to shortlist, mentor, secondment, aspiration, readiness, sponsor, successor, to step up, exposure, timeline. Structures: the three futures -- will, going to, and the present continuous for arrangements.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words your company uses about you in rooms you are not in.

Development plan -- a written agreement about what you will learn, and by when
"My development plan has three actions and three dates."
Promotion -- a move to a higher position, with more responsibility
"A promotion is a decision somebody makes, not a reward for time."
To shortlist -- to choose a small group of candidates from a longer list
"The committee will shortlist three managers in November."
Mentor -- a more experienced person who advises you regularly
"My mentor and I are meeting once a month."
Secondment -- a temporary move to another team or country, to learn
"A three-month secondment to the regional team is possible."
Aspiration -- the position or result you want in the future
"My aspiration is a national role within three years."
Readiness -- how prepared you are to take the next step right now
"The committee will confirm his readiness in November."
Sponsor -- a senior person who speaks for you when decisions are made
"My sponsor will speak to the plan in November."
Successor -- the person who would take your job if you moved
"One of my consultants is being prepared as my successor."
To step up -- to take more responsibility than your current job asks for
"He stepped up when the region lost two consultants."
Exposure -- the chance to be seen and known by senior people
"Presenting in October is the first exposure of this kind."
Timeline -- the dates by which each step of a plan will happen
"The plan will be reviewed against this timeline every quarter."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the plan and answer the questions. Notice which future is used, and why.

Renato has a development plan with three actions and three dates. His aspiration is a national commercial role, and the horizon is two to three years. The talent committee meets in November, and it will discuss him there for the first time. Nobody has promised him a promotion; the committee only decides who will be shortlisted.

On readiness, two of the three criteria are already strong. The third is English, which is the declared prerequisite. So the plan starts there. He is going to continue the intensive programme he decided on in July, twice a week, and in October he is presenting to the global commercial team — the date is in the calendar and the invitation has gone out. That is his first real exposure to that audience.

Two other things sit on the plan. He is meeting his mentor on the first Monday of every month, and one of his consultants is being prepared as his successor, because the committee rarely moves a manager nobody can replace. If the committee confirms his readiness, a three-month secondment to the regional team becomes possible.

When he finishes the programme, he will ask his sponsor for a formal review. Everything on the plan is measured against one timeline, and every quarter somebody checks it.

1. Why is it "in October he is presenting to the global team" and not "he will present"?
A Because it is a fixed arrangement: there is a date in the calendar and the invitation has gone out.
B Because "present" cannot be used with "will".
C Because the present continuous is more polite in a written plan.
2. Why "he is going to continue the intensive programme"?
A Because the decision is being made at this moment, while writing.
B Because it is an intention he decided on before now — in July.
C Because "going to" is always used for study.
3. "When he finishes the programme, he will ask his sponsor for a review." Why is there no "will" after "when"?
A Because the sentence is about the past.
B Because "finish" is an irregular verb.
C Because after when, as soon as, before and until, English uses the present, never "will".
4. What does the text say the committee actually decides in November?
A That he gets the promotion.
B Only who will be shortlisted — nobody has promised him a promotion.
C Whether the secondment has already started.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Three Futures

GRAMMAR

One question decides the form: is it in a calendar, was it decided before today, or are you deciding right now?

FormWhen you use itExample
am / is / are + verb-ingA fixed arrangement. There is a date, and usually another person involved.In October I am presenting to the global team.
going to + verbAn intention decided before now, or a prediction with evidence in front of you.I am going to apply next year. Look at the numbers — we are going to miss it.
will + verbA decision made at the moment of speaking, an offer, a promise, or an opinion about the future.I will send you the timeline tonight.
After when, as soon as, before, untilNo will in that half of the sentence: "When I finish the programme, I will apply."
Negativewill not (won't) · am / is / are not going toI am not going to wait for the committee.
QuestionWill you...? · Are you going to...? · Are you presenting...?Are you presenting in October?
Three trapsNever I go to the congress in November — a future arrangement is never the present simple. Never When I will finish. And a prediction you can SEE takes going to, not will.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

Talent Development asks where you want to be. You start with:
A "One day I would like to grow inside the company."
B "My aspiration is a national commercial role, within the next two or three years."
C "It depends on what the company decides for me."
The presentation to the global team is already in the calendar. You say:
A "In October I will present to the global team."
B "In October I am presenting to the global team."
C "In October I present to the global team."
Your manager asks when you will apply. The course ends in June. You say:
A "When I will finish the course, I will apply."
B "When I finish the course, I will apply."
C "When I am finishing the course, I apply."
You are looking at the quarter numbers on the screen right now. You say:
A "We are going to miss the target."
B "We miss the target."
C "We are missing the target next month."
The committee asks who would take your job if you moved. The strongest answer is:
A "Nobody could really replace me in that region."
B "One of my consultants. I am preparing him as my successor from this quarter."
C "That is a question for human resources."
Your manager says the gap is your English. The best response is:
A "I understand everything, the problem is only speaking."
B "I know. I started a programme in August, twice a week, and I am presenting in October."
C "I am going to study English when I have more time."

Explained (Explicando) -- any episode on work and money

Short episodes, around twenty minutes each, that take one idea apart and rebuild it. The narration is clear, the pace is fast but never rushed, and every episode ends by projecting forward — which means you hear the three futures of this lesson used naturally, again and again.

Tip: pick one episode and watch it with English subtitles. Every time the narrator talks about what happens next, write the sentence down. You will collect will, going to and the present continuous without hunting for them.
