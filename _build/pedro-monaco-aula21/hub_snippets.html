The Business Dinner

A dinner with the American team, where nobody wants to talk about the contract. Key expressions: to talk shop, to break the ice, small talk, to hit it off, to open up, to elaborate on, to build on, to go off on a tangent, to ramble, to get a word in edgewise, to run out of things to say, down-to-earth, to pick up the tab, to take a rain check. Structure: emphasis with do, so and such -- I do like it, so bad that, such a long day that.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to the dinner table: how to start a conversation, how to keep it going, and how to end the evening well.

To talk shop -- to talk about work with people you work with, especially in a social situation
"Let's not talk shop at dinner; we've had enough contracts for one day."
To break the ice -- to say or do something that helps people relax when they first meet
"A question about football is a safe way to break the ice in Brazil."
Small talk -- polite conversation about unimportant things, such as the weather or traffic
"He's great with contracts, but small talk makes him nervous."
To hit it off -- to like each other immediately
"The two general counsels seemed to hit it off on the first night."
To open up -- to talk honestly about your feelings or experiences
"After the second coffee, the client started to open up about the merger."
To elaborate on -- to give more details about something, or explain it more fully
"Could you elaborate on that? I'd love to hear more."
To build on -- to use what somebody has said as the starting point for your own idea
"Let me build on what Megan said about the Chicago office."
To go off on a tangent -- to start talking about a different subject in the middle of a conversation
"Sorry, I always go off on a tangent when I'm tired; back to your question."
To ramble -- to talk for a long time in a confused way, without a clear point
"Give a full answer, but don't ramble: three moves and stop."
To get a word in edgewise -- to manage to say something while another person is talking too much
"He talked so much that nobody could get a word in edgewise."
To run out of things to say -- to have nothing more to talk about
"If you run out of things to say, ask a question back."
Down-to-earth -- practical, friendly and easy to talk to
"For a senior partner, she is surprisingly down-to-earth."
To pick up the tab -- to pay the bill for everybody, especially in a restaurant
"At a business dinner, the host is expected to pick up the tab."
To take a rain check -- to say no to an invitation politely, but suggest accepting it another time
"I'll have to take a rain check on drinks; I have an early flight."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

After a long day of meetings, Pedro is at dinner with Megan, a lawyer from the American team. She asks him a simple question: does he like living in São Paulo? A one-word answer would be polite, but it would end the conversation. So Pedro says I do — and the emphasis on do tells Megan that there is more coming.

The next part of his answer adds a result: the traffic is so bad that people plan their whole day around it. Then he balances it with something positive: there is such a variety of food and music that it's hard to get bored. So goes before the adjective bad; such goes before the noun variety.

Later, when Megan asks why he chose law, Pedro uses emphasis again: what I do enjoy is the moment when a messy situation turns into a clear rule. And when a colleague later says the contract never arrived, he corrects him politely: I did send it. It's in your spam folder.

Notice the pattern. After do, does or did, the verb is always in the base form. And so ... that and such ... that do something very useful at a dinner table: they turn one sentence into two.

1. Why does Pedro say I do instead of just yes?
A Because the emphasis prepares a second sentence with more detail.
B Because yes is too informal for a business dinner with lawyers from another country.
C Because Megan asked the question in the past.
2. Which sentence adds a RESULT to the description?
A I do like the city.
B The traffic is so bad that people plan their whole day around it.
C Chicago is much quieter than São Paulo, and Megan seems to prefer quiet cities like that.
3. Why does the text use such in such a variety of food?
A Because such is always more formal than so, and the dinner is a formal occasion.
B Because the sentence is negative.
C Because there is a noun after it.
4. What form of the verb comes after emphatic do, does or did?
A The base form, as in I did send it.
B The past simple, as in I did sent it, because the action is already finished.
C The -ing form.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Emphasis with Do, So and Such

GRAMMAR

In many languages, emphasis lives mostly in the voice. English also uses grammar for it, and two of these forms add a second idea to your answer.

PatternWhen to use itExample
do / does / did + base formEmphasis, often before a contrast, or to correct somebody.I do like the city, but the traffic is terrible.
so + adjective / adverb + thatA stronger adjective, plus its result.The traffic is so bad that people plan their day around it.
such + a / an + adjective + noun + thatThe same, with a singular noun.It was such a long meeting that we skipped lunch.
such + plural / uncountable nounNo a / an before plural or uncountable nouns.They were such good stories that nobody checked the time.
so much / so many + nounQuantity: much (uncountable), many (plural).There were so many questions that dinner lasted four hours.
negative and questionEmphatic do is for affirmative sentences; questions and negatives already use do.Do you like it? I don't mind it. I do like it.

One habit for this week: when somebody asks you a yes/no question, try I do or it is, and then one detail with so ... that or such ... that. Say the do with stress, or the emphasis disappears.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a business dinner. Choose the version a professional would actually use.

1. A guest asks: "Do you like your job?" You want to keep the conversation going:
A Yes.
B I do, although it isn't what most people imagine. Most of my day is about preventing problems.
C Yes, I like, I like very much the job, the job is good and the people are good also.
2. You want to say the meeting was very long and give the result:
A It was so a long meeting that we skipped lunch and had a sandwich at our desks.
B It was such long meeting.
C It was such a long meeting that we skipped lunch.
3. A colleague says you didn't read the report. You correct him politely:
A I did read it; I just didn't have time to comment.
B I did read it yesterday, and I did sent my comments to everybody on the team.
C I read did it.
4. Your guest has been talking for ten minutes, and you want to come in:
A Stop.
B Excuse me, you are rambling and going off on a tangent, and I would like to talk now, please.
C That's a great point. Can I build on it for a second?
5. At the end of the dinner, the guest invites you for one more drink, but you have an early meeting:
A No, thank you.
B I'll have to take a rain check tonight, but I'd love to another time.
C I'll pick up the tab for the drink tomorrow, so we can go to the bar and talk shop all night.
