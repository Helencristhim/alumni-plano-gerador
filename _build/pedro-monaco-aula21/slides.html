Chapter 3: The Code

Do, So, Such

three small words that make an answer bigger

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I do like the city, but the traffic is terrible."

"The traffic is so bad that people plan their whole day around it."

"It was such a long meeting that we skipped lunch."

"She did send the contract. It's in your spam folder."

What do the orange words have in common? Which ones add a second idea to the sentence?

FormWhat it doesExample
do / does / did + base formEMPHASIS, often before a contrast (but, although) or to CORRECT somebody. Say it with stress.I do like the city, but the traffic is terrible.
so + adjective / adverb (+ that ...)Makes the adjective stronger. With that, it adds the RESULT.The traffic is so bad that people plan their day around it.
such + (a / an) + adjective + noun (+ that ...)The same job, but there is a NOUN after it.It was such a long meeting that we skipped lunch.
so much / so many + nounQuantity: much for uncountable nouns, many for plural nouns.There were so many questions that dinner lasted four hours.
such + plural or uncountable nounNo a / an before a plural or uncountable noun.They were such good stories that nobody checked the time.

So goes before an adjective alone. Such goes before a noun. And emphatic do only works if you stress it when you speak.

Meaning

One Answer, Four Ways to Extend It

Each one starts where a one-word answer would stop

"Yes, I do enjoy it, but not for the reason you think."

The emphasis promises a second sentence. Now you have to explain the reason.

"It's such a big city that you never see all of it."

The result clause turns a fact into a small picture.

"The food was so good that we went back the next day."

A one-word answer (good) becomes a story.

"I did read the report, but I didn't have time to comment."

A polite correction: you did one part, and you explain the other.

Chapter 4: The Dinner

Nothing to Do With Contracts

Megan Hale, from the American team, and the first question that isn't about work

Dialogue

Beyond the First Answer

M
We've been talking shop since eight this morning. Can I ask you something that has nothing to do with contracts?
P
Please do. I was hoping somebody would break the ice, because I've read so many indemnity clauses today that I'd happily talk about anything else.
M
Okay, an easy one. Do you like living in São Paulo?
P
I do, although it isn't an easy city. The traffic is so bad that people plan their whole day around it. But there's such a variety of food and music that it's hard to get bored. What about you? Is Chicago like that?
M
Not at all. Chicago is much quieter. Now, here's the harder one: why law? And please don't just tell me it pays well.
P
It doesn't always pay well, believe me! Let me elaborate on that. What I do enjoy is the moment when a messy situation turns into a clear rule. And compliance is such a hidden part of a company that most people only notice it when something goes wrong.
M
You know, you're the first person today who has really opened up. Everybody else just said "fine, thanks" and looked at the menu.
P
I'll take that as a compliment. I do ramble when I like a topic, so feel free to get a word in edgewise. And dinner is on us tonight, so please don't try to pick up the tab.
Comprehension

About Megan

1. How long have Megan and Pedro been talking shop?
Since eight in the morning.
2. Which city does Megan compare with São Paulo, and how is it different?
Chicago. She says it is much quieter.
3. What does Megan ask Pedro not to tell her about law?
That it pays well.
4. According to Megan, what did everybody else do today?
They just said "fine, thanks" and looked at the menu.

2. What does she say the problem is?
Not the language, but the shape of the answer.
