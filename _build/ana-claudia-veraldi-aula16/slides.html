Chapter 4: The Code

How Sure Are You?

must · might · can’t — and must have, for the past

Grammar Discovery

Almost Sure, Not Sure, Almost Sure Not

"The lights are on. They must be home."

"The car is not there. They might be at the beach."

"It is four in the morning. They can't be having a party."

"The grass is wet. It must have rained last night."

None of these sentences is a fact — they are all guesses from a clue. Which one is the most sure? Which one is the least sure? And look at the last one: what changes when the guess is about the PAST?

FormWhat it doesExample
must + verbAlmost sure it IS true (from evidence).They must be home.
might / may / could + verbPossible. You do not know.They might be at the beach.
can’t + verbAlmost sure it is NOT true.They can’t be asleep.
must have + past participleAlmost sure about the PAST.It must have rained.
might have / can’t have + p.p.Possible / impossible in the past.She can’t have seen us.
never mustn’t hereMustn’t means it is forbidden. For a negative guess, use can’t.never: they mustn’t be home

In one line: must = almost sure yes, can’t = almost sure no, might = maybe — add have for the past.

Comprehension

About Julien

1. What does Julien say when Ana thinks the neighbors must love the sea?
That they might just like surfing on holiday, and they should not jump to conclusions.
2. What does Julien first think about the man, and why?
That he can not be very friendly, because he did not say hello.
3. What does Julien say about strangers in France?
That in France people do not say hello to strangers in the street.

What I Can Do Now

I use must when I am almost sure something is true.
I use might, may or could when something is possible.
I use can’t, not mustn’t, when I am almost sure something is not true.
I use must have, might have and can’t have for guesses about the past.
I know the words: a clue, evidence, a hunch, to jump to conclusions, to put two and two together.
