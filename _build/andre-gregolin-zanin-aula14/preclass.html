Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Tentative -- not certain; said carefully, as a first idea
"Her answers were so tentative that nobody knew what she thought."
Credibility -- the quality that makes people believe and trust you
"Every exaggeration costs you a little credibility."
Objective -- based on facts, not on personal feelings
"The exam score is objective. My opinion of the exam is not."
Subjective -- based on personal feelings, tastes or opinions
"Which sport is more beautiful is a subjective question."
To overstate -- to say something is bigger or more certain than it really is
"He overstated everything, so nothing sounded important."
To imply -- to suggest something without saying it directly
"Are you implying that I did not study?"
To infer -- to reach an opinion from the information you have
"From his silence, I inferred that he disagreed."
If anything -- used to say that the truth goes, if anywhere, in the opposite direction
"If anything, the cursinho made me more disciplined, not less."
As far as I can tell -- from what I know or can see, though I may be wrong
"As far as I can tell, nobody noticed the mistake."
To leave room for doubt -- to accept, in what you say, that you might be wrong
"A good doctor always leaves room for doubt."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every claim you make carries a second message: how sure you are. English signals it with two families of words. A hedge makes a claim softer: might, may, arguably, presumably, it seems that, research suggests that. A booster makes it stronger: clearly, certainly, I am convinced that, there is no question that. Neither family is better. The skill is choosing the right one for each claim.

A simple rule works most of the time. Boost what you really believe: I am convinced that medicine is the right choice for me. Hedge what you cannot prove: the evidence seems to suggest that sleep improves memory. A hedge is not a phrase that buys time while you think. It carries real information, so one is enough. Stacking them, as in maybe I think it could possibly be true, does not sound careful. It sounds lost.

A stance adverb at the start of a sentence does a different job. Words like fortunately, interestingly and surprisingly tell the listener your attitude before the content arrives: Surprisingly, the second year was easier than the first.

1. According to the text, what is a hedge for?
A To make a sentence longer and more academic, whatever the claim is about.
B To show that you are not completely sure of a claim.
C To buy time while you think.
2. What should you boost, according to the text?
A What you really believe.
B Every fact, so that you always sound confident in an interview.

3. Which sentence follows the advice in the text?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging and Boosting

GRAMMAR

Boost what you believe. Hedge what you cannot prove.
