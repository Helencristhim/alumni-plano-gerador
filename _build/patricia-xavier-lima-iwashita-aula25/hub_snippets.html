Twelve months of an account in twenty minutes, for an executive who has never met you. Key words: decision-maker, headline, benchmark, footprint, efficiency, cost savings, consolidate, workflow, visibility, headcount. Structure: relative clauses -- who, which, that, whose, where -- to say exactly which person, thing or place you mean, in one sentence.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Decision-maker -- the person who has the final word on a purchase
"Richard is the decision-maker who signs the renewal."
Headline -- the most important point, which you say first
"The headline is that usage has come back on every team."
Benchmark -- a standard that you compare your results with
"Your numbers are above the benchmark for logistics companies."
Footprint -- how widely a product is used across a company
"Our footprint has grown from three teams to six."
Efficiency -- doing more work with the same time or money
"Efficiency is the number that finance cares about most."
Cost savings -- money that a company no longer has to spend
"Most of the cost savings come from the warehouse team."
Consolidate -- to bring several tools or systems into one
"They consolidated two spreadsheets and an old tool into one platform."
Workflow -- the series of steps that people follow to finish a task
"The new workflow saves eleven minutes on every order."
Visibility -- being able to see clearly what is happening
"Finance now has visibility of every open order."
Headcount -- the number of people who work in a team
"They grew the business without adding headcount."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Richard Moore is the new executive sponsor who took over at Northwind in February. He is the decision-maker on the renewal, but he has never met Patrícia, and he is a man whose calendar is full from eight to eight. So Patrícia prepares twenty minutes, not an hour. She opens with the headline, which is simple: usage has come back on every team. Then she gives three numbers. The first is the footprint: the platform is now in six teams, including finance, which never used it before. The second is the workflow that the warehouse team uses: it saves eleven minutes per order, and the warehouse is the place where most of the cost savings come from. The third is the benchmark: Northwind's efficiency is 18% above similar companies. She also explains that the team consolidated two spreadsheets and an old tool into one platform, and that finance now has visibility of every open order — all without adding headcount.

B It is a question about Richard.
C It describes a place.
2. "He is a man whose calendar is full." What does "whose" mean here?
A who is
B his
C where
3. Where do most of the cost savings come from?
A From the finance team.
B From the warehouse, where the team saves eleven minutes per order.
C From new headcount.
4. Which sentence is correct?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses: who / which / that / whose / where

GRAMMAR

How to say which person, which thing or which place you mean -- in the same sentence.

StructureUseExample
whoFor people.Richard is the sponsor who took over in February.
whichFor things. After a comma, always which, never that.The benchmark, which we update every quarter, is clear.
thatFor people or things, only when there are no commas.This is the workflow that saves eleven minutes.
whosePossession: instead of his, her or their.He is a man whose calendar is full.
whereFor places.The warehouse is the place where the team saves time.
, who / , whichCommas = extra information. The sentence works without it.Richard, who replaced the old sponsor, has twenty minutes.
AvoidNever repeat the pronoun at the end: not "the report which I sent it" → "the report which I sent". Never use "that his": not "a man that his calendar is full" → "a man whose calendar is full". Never use which for a person: not "the sponsor which" → "the sponsor who". And never put that after a comma.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen to each sentence, then record yourself saying it. "Who", "which" and "that" are short and weak in the middle of a sentence -- do not stop before them.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Think of one of your real accounts and an executive there who has never met you. Open a business review for that person in five or six sentences: say who you are, give the headline, give three numbers, and ask for the decision that you need. Use at least four relative clauses -- who, which, that, whose or where. One rule: every adjective needs a number next to it. Take your time.
