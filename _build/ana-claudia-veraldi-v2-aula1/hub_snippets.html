Lesson 1 -- Pre-class

The Cost of Choosing

Why the hundredth small decision of the day is harder than the first. Key words: mundane, trivial, to default to, to postpone, to be worn down, willpower, a trade-off, to weigh up. Structure: present habits -- state verbs against action verbs, and the language of frequency.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Mundane -- ordinary and everyday, with nothing interesting about it
"Most of the decisions are mundane: what to eat, which message to answer first."
Trivial -- so small or unimportant that it hardly deserves attention
"Deciding is work, even when the stakes are trivial."
To default to -- to choose something automatically, without stopping to think
"We default to whatever is nearest."
To postpone -- to move something to a later time instead of doing it now
"We postpone, or we say yes because saying no would take an argument."
To be worn down -- made gradually weaker or more tired by something repeated
"Willpower is not the muscle being worn down here. Attention is."
Willpower -- the inner strength to make yourself do something difficult
"What follows is not a lecture about willpower."
A trade-off -- accepting something worse in one place to gain something better in another
"There is a trade-off, of course."
To weigh up -- to consider the good and bad sides carefully before deciding
"Cut the number of options before you start weighing them up."
The stakes -- how much you stand to gain or lose in a situation
"Deciding is work, even when the stakes are trivial."
To run low -- to be close to finishing, with little left
"When the supply runs low we do not stop deciding."
Nearest to hand -- closest and easiest to reach, needing no effort to find
"Shoppers reach for whatever is nearest to hand."
Indifference -- a complete lack of interest in something, one way or the other
"Not out of indifference, but to remove one more question."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the paragraph, then answer the questions below.

Ana knows the pattern by now. In the morning she weighs up two or three options and picks well. By seven in the evening she is still deciding things, but the quality seems to drop. Nine times out of ten she defaults to whatever is nearest, and she usually postpones anything that needs a real argument. She does not believe this is laziness. Most evenings she simply prefers a good enough answer to a perfect one, and she is learning to plan for it instead of fighting it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- State Verbs, Action Verbs and Frequency

GRAMMAR

Two kinds of verb, and the one that never takes -ing.

TypeWhat it describesExample
Action verbSomething you do. It can be in progress, so it takes -ing.
weigh up · compare · postpone · decide · learn		I am weighing up two options right now.
State verbKnowledge, opinion, preference, appearance. Normally no -ing at all.
know · seem · prefer · matter · believe · understand		I know I will not decide well.
never: I am knowing
Both, with a differenceA few verbs change meaning between the two.I think it is true. (opinion)
I am thinking about it. (work in progress)

And where the frequency goes.

PositionRuleExample
Before the main verbSingle-word adverbs: usually, often, rarely, alwaysI usually postpone it.
After to beThe one exception, and the one people get wrongI am usually tired by then.
Start or endLonger expressions of frequencyMost evenings, I default to the nearest option.
I default to it most evenings.

Order

Lesson 1 — The Cost of Choosing

Talk
How to Make Hard Choices -- Ruth Chang, TED (15 min)

A philosopher argues that hard choices feel hard for a reason, and that the reason is not a lack of information. Her answer sits very close to the last line of tonight’s text: some decisions are not about finding the better option, they are about deciding who you are.

Tip: she speaks slowly and structures everything with signposts. Notice how many times she says on the one hand and on the other -- that is the concession language you practised.

Video
The Paradox of Choice -- Barry Schwartz, TED (20 min)

The other half of tonight’s argument, and the evidence behind the advice to cut the number of options before you start weighing them up. More choice does not make people happier; past a certain point it makes them worse off and more likely to regret what they picked.

Tip: watch the first eight minutes with subtitles off. Write down the gist in one sentence, then watch again with subtitles and check how close you were.

Talk
Inside the Mind of a Master Procrastinator -- Tim Urban, TED (14 min)

The funniest fourteen minutes on this list, and the one closest to the verb you practised tonight: to postpone. He is fast, informal and full of the natural speech you will need in a real conversation -- so treat it as listening training, not just entertainment.

Tip: this one is fast. Do not try to catch every word -- that is exactly the habit we are unlearning. Aim for the story and the punchlines.
