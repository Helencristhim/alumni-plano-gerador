I Used to Practice Differently -- A Career That Changed Shape

A practice that went international after eighteen domestic years, and what it cost. Key words: a habit, a routine, a lifestyle, a career, domestic, a turning point, to take over, a partnership, a caseload, to keep up with. Structure: used to for past habits and states that no longer exist.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A habit -- something you do so often that you stop deciding to do it
"Reading the news in English before eight became a habit, not a plan."
A routine -- the fixed order of what you do, and when
"Meetings in the morning, court in the afternoon: that was the routine for eighteen years."
A lifestyle -- the whole way a person lives: hours, money, travel, family
"That lifestyle is not something anybody chooses on purpose."
A career -- the whole line of work a person builds over the years
"He started that part of his career at forty-four."
Domestic -- inside one country only, with nothing crossing a border
"The work was domestic: land, family companies, local disputes."
A turning point -- the moment after which everything went in a different direction
"The turning point was not a decision. It was a client."
To take over -- to start doing something that somebody else was doing
"A younger associate took over the domestic cases."
A partnership -- the agreement that makes several lawyers owners of the same firm
"Three rooms above a bakery became a partnership with eleven people."
A caseload -- how many cases one lawyer is carrying at the same time
"Within two years half of his caseload had a foreign party."
To keep up with -- to stay level with something that keeps changing
"Today the same lawyer has to keep up with three legal systems."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Wilson Prado is a lawyer in Ribeirão Preto, and for eighteen years his work was entirely domestic. He used to know every judge in the district by name. His firm used to be three rooms above a bakery. He did not use to read contracts in English, and he did not use to work with anybody outside the state. Then, in 2011, a sugar mill his firm advised signed its first export contract, and the buyer was Swiss. That was the turning point.

Look at what the two forms are doing. The past simple — signed — reports one event, on one day. Used to does something the past simple cannot: it says this was true for years, and it is not true any more. When you say he used to know every judge, you have already told your listener that he does not do that job now. You did not have to add a second sentence.

The form is easy and the trap is famous. In the affirmative it is used to with a D. In the negative and in the question, did arrives and carries the past by itself, so the D goes away: did not use to, Did you use to. Never did you used to. The verb after it stays bare, exactly as it does after a modal.

Two more things you will use every week. To compare then and now, put it at the end: “The work is more interesting than it used to be.” And do not stretch used to over a single event: in 2011 the mill used to sign its first export contract is wrong, because a first contract happens once. If it happened once, it is the past simple.

1. What does used to tell the listener that the past simple does not?
A That the speaker is being polite about the past.
B That it was true for years and is not true any more.
C That the speaker is not sure when it happened.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "He didn’t used to read contracts in English."
B "He did not use to read contracts in English."
C "He did not used to reading contracts in English."
3. Why can you not say "in 2011 the mill used to sign its first export contract"?
A Because used to never takes a year.
B Because sign is an irregular verb.
C Because a first contract happens once, and used to is for what repeated.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Used To

GRAMMAR

The D disappears the moment did turns up. The verb after it stays bare.

JobFormExample
A habit that endedused to + bare verbI used to work only with domestic clients.
A state that endedused to be / know / haveThe firm used to be three rooms above a bakery.
Negativedid not use to — no DHe did not use to read contracts in English.
QuestionDid + subject + use to — no DDid you use to travel as much as you do now?
Comparing then and now... than it used to beThe work is more interesting than it used to be.
A single eventpast simple, never used toIn 2011 the mill signed its first export contract.
The two trapsnever did you used to, and never used to for something that happened once

Never: did you used to travel · he didn’t used to read · I use to work there · in 2011 he used to sign the contract
