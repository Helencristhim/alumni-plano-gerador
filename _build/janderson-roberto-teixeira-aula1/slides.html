The answer has two halves, and English keeps them apart with two different tenses. What you always do, and what you happen to be doing this week. Get those two right and the first minute stops being frightening.

Chapter 3: The Code

Always, or Right Now?

The two tenses that answer “what do you do?”

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I work at a bank, in investments."

"This week I am covering for a colleague."

"She takes care of the visitors here."

"At the moment we are testing a new system."

Two of these sentences are true every week of the year. Two of them are only true today. Which are which, and what changes in the verb?

FormWhat it saysExample
Present simple: I work / she worksPermanent. True last year, true next year. Your job, your routine, the facts about you."I work at a bank."
"She takes care of the visitors."
Present continuous: I am workingTemporary. True this week, this month, this project — and it will stop."This week I am covering for a colleague."
The third person SIn the present simple, he, she and it take an S. Nothing else does."He deals with clients."
NOT "He deal with clients"
Questions and negativesThe present simple borrows do or does. The continuous just moves am / is / are."Do you deal with clients?"
"Are you covering for someone?"
The words that give it awayEvery day, usually, never → simple. At the moment, this week, right now → continuous."I usually answer straight away."
"At the moment I am reading it."

The question is never “when?” It is always “permanent or temporary?” Answer that first and the verb chooses itself.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I am working at a bank in Sao Paulo."
"I work at a bank in Sao Paulo."
"What you do here?"
"What do you do here?"
"Sorry, I am not understanding you."
"Sorry, I don't understand."

Your job is permanent, so it takes the present simple. A question in the present simple borrows do or does, and the main verb stays in its plain form. And a small group of verbs about the mind — understand, know, want, need, mean — never take the -ing form, because they are not actions. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Chapter 4: The Coffee Break

Four Minutes by the Coffee Machine

Somebody you have never met, and no script

Dialogue

By the Coffee Machine

C
Is the coffee here any good, or should I warn you?
J
I don't drink coffee in the afternoon, so I am the wrong person to ask. Today I am drinking tea.
C
Sensible. I am Claire, by the way. What do you do?
J
Janderson. I work at a bank, in investments. I take care of clients who put money outside Brazil.
C
So you are the person everybody calls when the market moves. Busy week?
J
Very. This week I am covering for a colleague as well, so I am dealing with two desks.
C
Then take the break. I am based on the fourth floor, in legal. We only get busy when somebody else makes a mistake.
J
That sounds restful. How often do you come to this floor?
Comprehension

About Claire

1. Which floor is Claire based on, and in which area?
The fourth floor, in legal.
2. What is the first thing she asks about?
Whether the coffee is any good.
3. When does she say her team gets busy?
Only when somebody else makes a mistake.
4. What does she advise him to do?
To take the break.
Listening

Welcome to the Eighth Floor

A short welcome to visitors. Sound first -- no text.

1. Where is the coffee machine, and how much does it cost?
At the end of the corridor, on the left, and it is free.
2. Why does she say to look at the number on the door?
Because all the meeting rooms have the same name, so only the number is different.
3. Until what time is she at the desk by the lift?
Until six.
Listening 2

The Voice on the Answering Machine

A voicemail greeting. Sound first -- no text.

1. Which two days does he work from home?
Mondays and Fridays.
2. Why is he in meetings most of the afternoon this week?
Because he is covering for a colleague.
3. What three things does he ask you to leave?
Your name, your number, and one line about what you need.

The Habit

Sixty Seconds, Out Loud

Before the next class, say your first minute out loud once a day, to nobody. Two sentences about what you do, one sentence about what you are doing this week, and one question back.

Sixty seconds a day is four hours a year of speaking. Nobody has ever been too busy for that.
