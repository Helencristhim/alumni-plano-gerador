Lesson 13 -- Pre-class

Better, Worse, Almost the Same

Three takes of the same song, and only one goes on the record. Key words: a front-runner, a runner-up, neck and neck, a toss-up, to have the edge over somebody, on par with something, head and shoulders above, polished, raw, consistent, hit-or-miss, to take something up a notch. Structure: comparatives and superlatives with qualifiers -- slightly better, much better, not quite as good as, nowhere near as good as, by far the best.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A front-runner -- the person or option that is most likely to win or to be chosen
"After the blind auditions, she was the clear front-runner."
A runner-up -- the person who finishes in second place in a competition
"He was the runner-up last season, and he came back this year."
Neck and neck -- exactly level in a competition, with no clear winner yet
"Going into the final, the two singers were neck and neck."
A toss-up -- a choice where both options are so close that nobody can say which is better
"Take two or take three? Honestly, it's a toss-up."
To have the edge over somebody -- to be a little better than somebody, with a small advantage
"Her voice is smaller, but she has the edge over him on stage."
On par with something -- at the same level or quality as something else
"The new mix is on par with the one the label approved."
Head and shoulders above -- very clearly better than all the others
"In the final, she was head and shoulders above the rest."
Polished -- finished and professional, with no rough parts
"The second take is more polished, but it feels a little cold."
Raw -- natural and not finished, often with strong emotion
"The first take is raw, and that's exactly why it works."
Consistent -- at the same good level every time
"He isn't the best singer in the group, but he's the most consistent."
Hit-or-miss -- sometimes very good and sometimes bad, so you never know what you will get
"His live shows are hit-or-miss: one night amazing, the next night flat."
To take something up a notch -- to make something a little stronger, bigger or better than before
"In the last chorus, I want you to take it up a notch."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

On Thursday, Dara recorded three takes of her showcase song, and only one can go on the record. Pedro listened to all three the next morning. His first reaction was simple: take one was worse, and takes two and three were better. But “better” does not help a singer. So he wrote down how much better. Take one is nowhere near as polished as the others — a big difference. Take two is much more consistent in the verses. Take three is slightly better than take two overall — a small difference, only one point on his sheet.

Look at the words before the comparison. Slightly, a little and a bit make the difference small. Much, a lot and far make it big. When two things are the same, Pedro says the verse is just as good as in take two; when they are almost the same, it is not quite as clean as take two. And at the top, the bridge in take three is by far the best version Dara has sung. Notice the the: by far the best, never by far best. And never very better or more better.

The words of the judging room complete the picture. Take one is raw; take two is polished but a little cold. Take three is hit-or-miss in the second verse, but in the bridge it is head and shoulders above the other two. For the verses, takes two and three are neck and neck, so the choice is almost a toss-up. In the end, take three has the edge over take two, and it becomes the front-runner. Take two is the runner-up, but one of its lines will save the second verse. Pedro wants the final version on par with the other singers’ tracks, so he asks Dara to take the last chorus up a notch and stay consistent to the end.

1. Pedro says take one is “nowhere near as polished as” the others. What does he mean?
A Take one is a little less polished than the others, but the difference is really very small.
B Take one is much less polished than the others.
C Take one is just as polished as the others.
2. Which sentence about take three is correct?
A The bridge is by far the best part of take three.
B The bridge is by far best part of take three, and everybody in the studio agrees with that.
C The bridge is very better than the verses.
3. Why does Pedro say take three is “slightly better” and not “much better” than take two?
A Because the difference is only one point.
B Because take three is the most raw take, and a raw take always gets a much lower score from the producer.
C Because take two has the highest total.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives with Qualifiers

GRAMMAR

One question: is the difference big, small, almost nothing, or is it the top?

Size of the differenceFormExample
Smallslightly / a little / a bit + comparativeTake three is slightly better than take two.
Bigmuch / a lot / far + comparativeThe vocal is far clearer in mix A.
The samejust as ... asThe verse is just as good as in take two.
Almost the samealmost as / not quite as ... asIt is not quite as clean as take two.
Not even closenowhere near as ... asTake one is nowhere near as polished as the others.
The topby far / easily + the + superlative · one of the + superlative + pluralby far the best bridge · one of the strongest voices
Questionsmuch / a lot also work in questionsIs take three much better, or only a little better?

Never: very better · more better · more strong · so polished as · by far best

Order

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Think of a real choice between two versions you made at work or in your own music: two singers, two takes, two edits. Compare them and say how much better one was, using at least three of these: slightly, much, far, not quite as, nowhere near as, by far the. Then say whether you still agree with your choice today.
