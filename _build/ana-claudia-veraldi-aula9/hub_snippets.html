Understanding Fast American English

Why native speakers sound like one long word, and what to say when you only catch half of it. Key words: to mumble, to catch, the gist, word for word, to trail off, to keep up, filler word, to zone out, slang, to rephrase, to speak up, to drown out. Structure: connected speech -- the reductions gonna, wanna, gotta and kinda, plus linking and the flap T.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To mumble -- to speak quietly and unclearly, so the words run together
"He mumbled the price and I had to ask twice."
To catch -- to hear and understand something that was said
"Sorry, I did not catch the last part."
The gist -- the general meaning of something, without every single word
"I got the gist, but I missed the details."
Word for word -- every single word, exactly as it was said
"Nobody follows a fast speaker word for word."
To trail off -- to stop speaking gradually, so the end of the sentence disappears
"She trailed off and I never heard the ending."
To keep up -- to follow something that is moving too fast for you
"The host talks so fast that I cannot keep up."
Filler word -- a small sound people use while they think, like "um" or "you know"
"Half of that message was filler words."
To zone out -- to stop paying attention without deciding to
"After two minutes of fast English I zone out."
Slang -- very informal words used inside one group or one region
"That is slang; you will not find it in a dictionary."
To rephrase -- to say the same idea again in different words
"Could you rephrase that in a simpler way?"
To speak up -- to talk more loudly so that people can hear you
"Could you speak up a little? The line is bad."
To drown out -- to be so loud that another sound cannot be heard
"The dogs drowned out the rest of the call."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Ana opens a video about restoring old chairs and the American host says: "So I'm gonna show you what I wanna do here, and then we gotta let it dry." On paper that is fourteen words. In her ear it arrives as about five. Nothing is missing: the host simply said going to as gonna, want to as wanna and got to as gotta. That is what a reduction does — two words become one, and the little word in the middle disappears. Then there is linking: the final consonant of one word jumps onto the vowel of the next, so "it is an old chair" comes out as one long sound. And in American English a t between two vowels turns into a fast d, which is why "a lot of" sounds like "a lodda" and "water" sounds like "wader". So Ana does not catch it word for word, and she does not need to: she gets the gist, and when she loses the thread she asks the person to rephrase it. The words were never the problem. The glue between them was.

1. Why does the sentence sound like five words instead of fourteen?
A Because the host is leaving words out to save time.
B Because pairs of words are reduced into one and joined to the next word.
C Because he is using slang that only Americans understand.
2. What happens to a "t" between two vowels in American English?
A It disappears completely.
B It becomes longer and stronger.
C It sounds like a fast "d", so "water" sounds like "wader".
3. According to the text, what is the real problem for the listener?
A The glue between the words, not the words themselves.
B A vocabulary that is too small for the topic.
C The speed of her own reading in English.

GRAMMAR

What happens to written English when it is spoken at full speed.
