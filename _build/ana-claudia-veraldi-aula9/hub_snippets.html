Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Understanding Fast English: Why You Know the Words and Still Miss the Sentence

The words never changed. The space between them did. Key words: to slur, to mumble, to enunciate, to rattle off, to drone on, garbled, a mouthful, under your breath, breakneck, deliberate, to talk a mile a minute, to go in one ear and out the other. Structure: connected speech and reductions -- linking, weak forms, gonna and wanna, and the sounds that vanish.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To slur -- to run sounds together so that separate words are hard to hear
"He slurs the ends of his sentences and I lose the last word every time."
To mumble -- to speak quietly with the mouth almost closed, so nothing is clear
"She was not talking fast. She was mumbling into her collar."
To enunciate -- to pronounce every sound carefully and separately
"Camille enunciates, and that is why she is easier to follow than a native speaker."
To rattle off -- to say something long very fast, without pausing
"He rattled off six dates and I wrote down none of them."
To drone on -- to keep talking in a flat, boring voice for a very long time
"He droned on for forty minutes and nobody asked a single question."
Garbled -- mixed up and impossible to understand, as if the sounds were shuffled
"The message came through garbled and I only got the last four words."
A mouthful -- a word or name that is long and awkward to say
"My surname is a mouthful, so I spell it before anybody asks."
Under your breath -- so quietly that people around you can barely hear you
"He said the price under his breath, as if he was ashamed of it."
Breakneck -- extremely fast, at a speed that is almost dangerous
"She reads the weather at breakneck speed and I catch one city in five."
Deliberate -- slow and careful on purpose, one word at a time
"Her English is slow, but it is deliberate, not uncertain."
To talk a mile a minute -- EXPRESSION -- to speak extremely fast, without stopping for air
"Todd, you are talking a mile a minute. Say that again."
To go in one ear and out the other -- EXPRESSION -- to be heard and then forgotten a second later
"Numbers go in one ear and out the other, so I ask for them in writing."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two people are coming to visit this month, and they could not sound less alike. Todd sends a voice note and the whole thing arrives in one piece: I am gonna be in the city on the ninth, I gotta stay that first night because of the car, and I wanna come out on Friday. Not one word in that message is difficult. What makes it hard is that six or seven words have collapsed into three sounds.

Camille sends the same kind of message and every word arrives on its own. She says I am going to take the bus, and I would like to see the market, and I must tell you one thing. She does not reduce anything, because English is not the language she grew up in, and reductions are the last thing a speaker picks up.

So the problem is not vocabulary at all. Whatcha doin Saturday and What are you going to do on Saturday are the same question, and you already know every word in it. One of them simply had the joints taken out. The more at home a speaker feels, the more of the sentence disappears.

1. Why is Todd harder to follow than Camille?
A Because he uses words that nobody has ever seen before.
B Because several of his words collapse into a single sound.
C Because his message is much longer than hers.
2. "I gotta stay that first night." What are the full words behind gotta?
A I get to stay that first night.
B I am going to stay that first night.
C I have got to stay that first night.
3. According to the text, why does Camille not reduce anything?
A Because English is not the language she grew up in, and reductions come last.
B Because the French language has no reductions of any kind.
C Because she is reading her message from a page.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Four Machines

GRAMMAR

Fast English is the same sentence with the joints taken out. There are only four ways of taking them out.

MachineWhat it doesSlowFast
1. LinkingA final consonant jumps onto the next vowelpick it uppi-ki-tup
2. Weak formsto, of, and, for, can, are lose their vowela cup of coffeea cuppa coffee
3. ReductionsFixed spoken forms, learned as single wordsgoing to · want to · got to · let megonna · wanna · gotta · lemme
4. Vanishing soundsA t between vowels softens, and an h at the start of a small word dropswater · did you · what are youwader · didja · whatcha
Who reduces mostNative speakers at home, with people they know well. Speakers of English as a second language reduce least, anywhere in the world.CamilleTodd

Never: Can you repeat more slow? · I did not listen the last part · He speaks too much fast · and never "I need more vocabulary" when the words were words you already knew.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

Somebody rattles off three dates on the phone and you caught none of them. You say:
A "Yes, perfect, thank you." (and you check later)
B "Could you send me the date in a message? I never catch numbers."
C "Sorry, my English is not good enough for dates."
A friend from Chicago is speaking at breakneck speed and you have caught about half:
A "Sorry, could you slow down a little? I am not catching it."
B "Please, can you repeat more slow?"
C Nothing, and you wait for the next sentence to explain it.
Somebody tells you that you missed the sentence because your vocabulary is too small. You answer:
A "You are right. I am going to learn fifty new words this week."
B "It is not the words. It is the speed."
C "I know. I have always been terrible at listening."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Play thirty seconds of any video where an American is speaking at normal speed. Then record yourself, out loud and without notes: say what the person was talking about, name one reduction you managed to catch (gonna, wanna, gotta, lemme, didja), and finish with the exact sentence you would use to slow that person down. Do not apologize for your English at any point.
