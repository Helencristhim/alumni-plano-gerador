Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Understanding Fast English -- The Sounds That Are Not There

Why fast English sounds like one long word. Key words: to slur, to swallow a sound, a contraction, gonna, to mumble, to rattle something off, word for word, to keep up, crystal clear, to go over your head, to enunciate, a tongue twister. Focus: connected speech -- gonna, wanna, didja, lotta and the weak forms.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To slur -- to run words together so that their edges disappear
"He was tired and started to slur everything."
To swallow a sound -- to leave out a sound that is written but never actually said
"Americans swallow the t in twenty. It comes out as twenny."
A contraction -- the short spoken form of two words: I am becomes I’m
"Almost every contraction is invisible to you until somebody writes it down."
Gonna -- what going to becomes in ordinary speech, in almost every sentence
"I am gonna call the builder this afternoon."
To mumble -- to speak quietly and unclearly, with the mouth barely moving
"He mumbled the price and I had to ask him twice."
To rattle off -- to say something very fast, from memory, without a single pause
"She rattled off six dates and I caught the first one."
Word for word -- trying to catch every single word instead of the meaning
"I listen word for word and that is exactly why I lose everything."
To keep up -- to follow at the same speed and not fall behind
"He was going so fast I could not keep up at all."
Crystal clear -- completely easy to understand, with no effort at all
"The second time she said it, it was crystal clear."
To go over your head -- to be too fast or too complex for you to follow at all
"That whole first minute went completely over my head."
To enunciate -- to pronounce every sound separately and very clearly
"When she enunciates I understand everything. When she relaxes, I lose her."
A tongue twister -- a phrase built to be hard to say, usually for fun
"Say it three times fast. It is a tongue twister."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

For twelve years Ana believed people were mumbling. She could read anything and write anything, and on a call she caught almost nothing, which made her believe her English was somehow fake.

Then somebody showed her a transcript while the recording played. Every word on the page was a word she already knew. She simply had not heard them. Nobody says going to; they say gonna. Nobody says did you; they say didja. Americans swallow the sound in the middle of twenty, and a lot of arrives as a lotta. The sounds are not hidden. They are genuinely not there.

What made it worse was her own method. Listening word for word, she would lose one word, stop to look for it, and lose the next twelve. Now she takes the two words that carry the meaning and lets the small ones go. When somebody rattles something off and she cannot keep up, she says so. The first minute still goes over her head. The rest is now crystal clear.

1. Why did Ana believe her English was fake?
A Because she could read and write well but understood almost nothing on a call.
B Because her vocabulary was too small for real conversations.
C Because she had never studied grammar properly.
2. What did the transcript prove?
A That she needed to learn many more words.
B That every word was one she already knew, and she simply had not heard them.
C That the speakers were pronouncing the words incorrectly.
3. Why does listening word for word make things worse?
A Because you lose one word, stop to look for it, and lose the next twelve.
B Because it is slower than reading a transcript.
C Because native speakers speak faster when they see you concentrating.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Connected Speech

GRAMMAR

You were taught English with spaces between the words. Nobody speaks with spaces.

FormWhat it doesExample
gonna / wanna / gottagoing to, want to, have got to — all three already contain the toI'm gonna call him · I wanna see it
lotta / kinda / sortathe of loses its vowel completelya lotta work · kinda tired
didja / wouldja / couldjathe d and the y fuse into one soundDidja see it?
hafta / gimme / lemmehave to, give me, let meGimme a ring when you can
weak formsto, of, for, and, was and can collapse to one dull soundfish and chips = fish’n chips
the disappearing tbetween vowels in American English it softens or vanisheswater = wader · twenty = twenny
linkinga final consonant jumps onto the next wordan apple = a napple

Never: I am going to gonna call · I wanna to see it · he gonna come · we gotta to leave. Three of those add a second to that is already inside the word, and one drops the verb be, which never goes anywhere. And these are SPOKEN forms: hear them, say them, do not write them in an email.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

Somebody rattles off three dates and you catch none. The best thing to say is:
A "Sorry, could you give me those dates again, a bit slower?"
B "Sorry, my English is very bad, please repeat everything."
C "I did not understand nothing of that."
You caught half, and the half you caught is enough to answer. You:
A Say nothing and hope the rest arrives by email.
B Repeat back what you caught: "So Thursday is off and Friday is on -- right?"
C Ask them to repeat the whole thing from the beginning.
Which of these is the correct spoken form?
A "He gonna come at eight."
B "He is gonna come at eight."
C "He is going to gonna come at eight."
Somebody asks whether you followed the whole call. You followed most of it. You say:
A "Yes, everything, no problem." (and hope)
B "Most of it. The first minute went over my head, the rest was fine."
C "No, I never understand anything on calls."

Reflection
