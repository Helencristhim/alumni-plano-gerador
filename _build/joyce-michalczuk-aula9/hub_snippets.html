Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Q&A Around the World -- Questions From Mumbai, Munich and Paris

How to take questions from a global audience with different accents: check or clarify in one short sentence, answer first, and later report what each person asked. Key words: an accent, to paraphrase, to catch, a follow-up question, to address a question, a range, to take questions, to go off topic, ambiguous, a straight answer. Structure: reporting questions (he asked whether…, she wanted to know what… was).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An accent -- the way people from a certain country or region pronounce a language
"Her German accent is strong, but her questions are very clear."
To paraphrase -- to say what somebody said again, with your own words
"Let me paraphrase your question to make sure I understood."
To catch -- to hear and understand a word or a sentence
"Sorry, I did not catch the last part."
A follow-up question -- a second question about the same topic, after the first answer
"The CFO had a follow-up question about the dates."
To address a question -- to deal with a question and answer it
"Let me address the second question first."
A range -- two numbers that show the lowest and the highest possible value
"I cannot give you an exact number, but the range is two to three million."
To take questions -- to invite and answer questions from the audience
"I am happy to take questions now."
To go off topic -- to start talking about something that is not the main subject
"Sorry, that goes a bit off topic. Can we talk about it after the call?"
Ambiguous -- with more than one possible meaning
"The question was ambiguous, so I asked which year he meant."
A straight answer -- a direct and honest answer, without going around the point
"The board wants a straight answer: yes or no."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

After her presentation, Laura, a tax manager in Santiago, took questions from four countries. The next morning her director asked her: “What did they ask you?” Laura had to report the questions, and she noticed that the sentences changed shape.

The question from Mumbai had been: “What is the new rate?” In her report it became: “Arjun asked me what the new rate was.” The verb moved to the end, like a normal sentence, and it went one step into the past. There is no question mark, and no do or does.

The question from Munich was a yes or no question: “Does the cost go up?” For those, English uses whether or if: “Charlotte wanted to know whether the cost went up.” And the question from Paris was ambiguous, so Laura had asked a short follow-up question in the meeting: “Do you mean this year or next year?”

How did Laura report "What is the new rate?"
A Arjun asked me what the new rate was.
B Arjun asked me what is the new rate?
C Arjun asked me what was the new rate?
Which word does English use to report a yes or no question?
A what
B whether, or if
C does
Why did Laura ask "Do you mean this year or next year?" in the meeting?
A Because the question from Paris was ambiguous.
B Because she wanted more time.
C Because she did not like the question.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reporting a Question

GRAMMAR

The question becomes a normal sentence: no do or does, the verb goes after the subject, and usually one step into the past.

JobFormExample
Wh- questionasked + wh- + subject + verbWhat is the rate? → He asked what the rate was.
Yes or no questionasked + whether / if + subject + verbDoes it go up? → She asked whether it went up.
With canasked + if + subject + couldCan you send it? → He asked if I could send it.
Check in one sentenceSo what you are asking is…So what you are asking is whether the cost goes up.
ClarifyDo you mean… or…?Do you mean this year or next year?
Did not hearI did not catch…Sorry, I did not catch the last part.

Never: he asked me what is the rate · she asked me does the cost go up · he asked if could I send it

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

Nicolas from Paris asks a long question that is not clear. Put the exchange in order.

?"Yes, exactly. We have not decided between the south and Sao Paulo."
?"I am happy to send you a one-page summary after the call."
?"Sorry, do you mean for the new distribution center next year?"
?"OK. The short answer is that location will matter less after 2033."
?"So, with this reform, does the location still matter, or is it the same everywhere now?"
?"During the transition, the state tax still matters, so the location still has an impact."
