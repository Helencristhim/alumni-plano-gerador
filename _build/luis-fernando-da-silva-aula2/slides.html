Chapter 4: The Code

How Long, and How Much So Far

The period that is still open

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I have worked in credit for eight years."

"I have had this portfolio since 2019."

"I joined the company in 2011."

"I worked in customer service before that."

The first two red verbs have two parts. The last two have one. Now look at the blue words: they appear on one side only, and they are the reason.

JobFormExample
A period that started in the past and is still openhave / has + past participleI have worked in credit for eight years.
How long the period isfor + a length of timefor eight years · for two seasons · for a long time
When the period startedsince + a point in timesince 2019 · since March · since the last harvest
One event, finished, with a closed timethe past simpleI joined the company in 2011. I worked there before that.
A result so far, no time expressionhave / has + past participleWe have recovered most of the overdue accounts.
QuestionHow long have you ... ?How long have you worked with this distributor?
The trapnever the present tense with for or sinceNot I work here since 2011. Say I have worked here since 2011.

In one line: if the period is still open, the verb needs two parts.

Note: payment terms in this region follow the harvest. An account that is current in October and overdue in April has not necessarily got worse. It has simply reached the part of the year where nothing has been sold yet.
1. Two lines on this report talk about time in opposite ways. Which are they, and what is the difference?
"Overdue since March" is a period that is still open today. "Region opened 2019" is one finished event with a closed date.
2. Four accounts are between 31 and 90 days overdue and all of them are dealers, not farms. Why is that worth a second look?
Because a dealer sits between the factory and the grower and carries the gap in between, so a dealer in trouble can hide several farms behind it.
3. According to the note, why can an account be current in October and overdue in April without the customer getting worse?
Because payment terms follow the harvest. In April nothing has been sold yet, so the calendar, not the customer, has moved.
Listening 1 · The Credit Manager

One Payday a Year

A credit manager at an American agricultural lender. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does she say a farm is nothing like a mortgage?
Because a mortgage is a person with a salary arriving on the same day every month, and a farm has one payday a year that depends on the weather, on a price nobody controls and on a truck arriving.
2. She says the question she asks is not the obvious one. What is the difference?
She is not asking whether the customer can pay her in March. She is asking whether the customer will still be standing in March.
3. After nineteen years, what does she say is the best predictor, and what is not?
Not the balance sheet. Whether the grower calls before the problem arrives or after. She says the ones who call early have almost never defaulted.
Listening 2 · The Dealer

A Warehouse With a Bank Attached

The owner of a farm supply dealership, on a trade podcast. Sound first — no text.

1. He says he sits in the middle. Between what, exactly, and what does he carry?
Between the factory, which wants its money on the agreed date, and the grower, who wants the product now and the invoice after the harvest seven months later. He carries the difference.
2. What does he say the hard part of the job is, and what is it not?
It is not selling. It is the phone call in a bad year, when a customer he has known for two decades says he cannot pay this season.
3. What has he learned to do about that phone call?
To make it boring: to agree in advance what happens in a bad year, before the bad year, so that nobody has to be brave on the telephone.
