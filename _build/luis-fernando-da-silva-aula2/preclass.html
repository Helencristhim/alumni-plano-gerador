Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A credit analyst -- the person who decides how much a customer can buy before paying, and on what conditions
"As a credit analyst, I see the customer long before the sales team does."
A portfolio -- the whole set of customer accounts that one person looks after
"My portfolio has about two hundred accounts, and I have had this region for six years."
Receivables -- the money a company has invoiced and is still waiting to be paid
"Half of our receivables sit on the books until the harvest is sold."
Agribusiness -- the whole business chain around farming: seeds, inputs, machines, storage and trade
"Agribusiness is not one sector. It is a chain, and everybody in it is waiting for the same harvest."
A distributor -- a company that buys in large quantity from the factory and supplies a whole area
"One distributor can carry more risk than fifty small farms put together."
Compliance -- following the rules and the internal procedures a company has to follow by law
"Compliance is not paperwork. It is the reason I can say no and still keep the customer."
Overdue -- past the date on which it should have been paid
"Two invoices are overdue, and both of them belong to the same dealer."
A credit limit -- the largest amount a customer is allowed to owe the company at any one time
"He has asked me to raise his credit limit twice this season."
Payment terms -- the agreed conditions saying when and how a customer pays
"Our payment terms follow the harvest, not the calendar month."
To chase a payment -- to keep contacting a customer, politely and repeatedly, until the money arrives
"I have been chasing that payment since March, and he always answers the phone."
To default -- to fail to pay a debt at all, not simply to pay it late
"In nineteen years, the customers who called me early have almost never defaulted."
The harvest -- the moment when the crop is collected and, in this business, the moment money exists
"A farm has one payday a year, and it is called the harvest."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A credit analyst opens the same report every Monday. He has worked in credit for eight years and he has had this region since 2019. Both of those periods started in the past and are still open this morning, so the verb comes in two parts: have plus the past participle. The words next to them are the clue. For measures how long the period is; since says the point where it started.

Then the same person talks about the events that are closed. He joined the company in 2011 and he worked in customer service before that. Those happened once, on a date that is finished, so the past simple does the job. English is strict here: as soon as a closed time expression appears, the two-part verb is wrong. Not I have joined the company in 2011, but I joined the company in 2011.

The report itself is written in the same two tenses. Most of the portfolio is current, and most of the receivables are money the company has invoiced and is still waiting for. Four accounts have been overdue since March, and all four are the same kind of customer: a distributor that supplies a whole area and lives on the gap between two dates. Two of them have asked for a higher credit limit this season, which the analyst can only approve inside compliance. Nobody in the region has defaulted in three years, and nobody has had to chase a payment for long, because the payment terms follow the harvest. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: never say I work here since 2011. The present tense cannot carry since or for.

1. "I have worked in credit for eight years." Why not "I work in credit for eight years"?
A Because "work" cannot be used in the present tense.
B Because the period started in the past and is still open, and English will not let the present tense carry "for" or "since".
C Because eight years is too long a period for the present tense.
2. What is the difference between "for" and "since" in this text?
A "For" gives the length of the period; "since" gives the point where it started.
B "For" is formal and "since" is informal.
C "For" is used with years and "since" with months.
3. Why is it "I joined the company in 2011" and not "I have joined the company in 2011"?
A Because "join" has no past participle.
B Because the company still exists today.
C Because "in 2011" is a closed time, and a closed time takes the past simple.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect with For and Since

GRAMMAR

One question decides everything: is the period still open?

JobFormExample
A period that started in the past and is still openhave / has + past participleI have worked in credit for eight years.
How long the period isfor + a length of timefor eight years · for two seasons · for a long time
When the period startedsince + a point in timesince 2019 · since March · since the last harvest
One event, finished, with a closed timethe past simpleI joined the company in 2011.
A result so far, no time expressionhave / has + past participleWe have recovered most of the overdue accounts.
Negative and questionhave not + participle · How long have you ... ?Nobody has defaulted in three years. How long have you worked with them?
The trapnever the present tense with for or sinceNot I work here since 2011. Say I have worked here since 2011.

Never: I work here since 2011 · I am working in credit for eight years · I have joined the company in 2011 · how long time do you work with this distributor? · the invoice is overdue since March.

Quiz

A "They pay every month, like everybody else."
