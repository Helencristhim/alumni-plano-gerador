Chapter 3: The Code

The Past Tense That Is Not About the Past

English steps backwards in time to step back from a claim

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I would rather we did not settle this tonight."

"It is essential that every figure be checked before it is quoted."

"Suppose we were to accept his premise for a moment."

"If I were you, I would concede the smaller point first."

Four sentences about now or about the future, and every verb in them has stepped into the past. What is the past form actually doing here?

FormWhat it doesExample
would rather + pastA preference about somebody else's behaviour. The past form makes it a wish rather than an instruction, which is why it does not sound like an order."I would rather you did not quote that figure."
It is essential / vital
/ imperative that + base form		The present subjunctive: the bare verb, with no -s and no to. Formal, and unmistakably firm."It is essential that he be present."
"I insist that she have the floor."
Suppose / Imagine
+ past or were to		Opens a hypothesis without committing you to it — the safest way to test an argument you may not accept."Suppose we were to accept that premise."
If I were you
· as if / as though		Were for every person, not was. This is the last surviving trace of the old subjunctive, and it survives precisely in the polite uses."If I were you, I would concede that."
"He talks as though he had read it."
It is time / I wish
· would sooner		Same mechanism: the past form signals that the thing is not true, or not yet true, and gently makes that everybody's problem rather than an accusation."It is high time somebody said so."
"I wish he would qualify that."

The past form is distance, not time. English moves a verb backwards to move the speaker back — away from the claim, away from the instruction, away from the confrontation. It is the most useful thing this language does at a difficult table.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I would rather you do not quote that figure."
"I would rather you did not quote that figure."
"It is essential that he is present at the meeting."
"It is essential that he be present at the meeting."
"If I was you, I would concede the smaller point."
"If I were you, I would concede the smaller point."

Three different structures, one habit underneath: English wants the unreal form and Portuguese offers the plain present. After would rather + another subject, use the past. After essential / vital / insist / suggest that, use the bare verb. And in the polite hypothesis it is were, in every person, however informal the room.

Chapter 4: The Diplomat at Dinner

Thirty Years of Disagreeing

A retired diplomat, and the two sentences she used most

Dialogue

Concede the Small, Hold the Large

N
Thirty years of negotiating, and I would say two sentences did most of the work. The first was that is a fair point.
H
And you meant it, or you said it to buy time?
N
Both, usually. If you never concede a point, the other side digs in and nothing moves for two days.
H
That is the part I find hardest. Conceding feels like giving ground on the whole argument, not on one piece of it.
N
It is the opposite. Concede the small point loudly and people stop expecting you to concede the large one. What was the second sentence, do you think?
H
I would guess something that stops the conversation without ending it. Something like I would rather we came back to this tomorrow.
N
Close. It was I take issue with one word in that. Naming the word turns a quarrel into a problem, and problems can be solved.
H
Whereas a quarrel just has to be won. Forgive me if I am speaking out of turn, but that sounds exhausting for thirty years.
Comprehension

About Nadia

1. What does Nadia say happens if you never concede a point?
The other side digs in and nothing moves for two days.
2. Why does she say conceding the small point is worth doing loudly?
Because then people stop expecting you to concede the large one.
3. What was her second sentence?
"I take issue with one word in that."
4. Why does she say naming the word matters?
Because it turns a quarrel into a problem, and problems can be solved.
Listening 1

The Rule of the Second Sentence

A short talk to new negotiators. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the first rule she gives?
Never answer the strongest version of the argument you were given — answer the strongest version that exists.
2. How long does she say you should wait before responding?
Long enough for the other person to add the sentence they were holding back.
3. What does she say the third rule costs you?
Nothing, because conceding a small point is free and buys the room.
Listening 2

Two Ministers, One Word

A radio account of a negotiation. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the sticking point in the negotiation?
A single word in the second paragraph, not the money.
2. What did the two sides do for eleven hours?
They talked past each other about entirely different questions.
3. How was it finally resolved?
One side conceded the word and the other conceded the deadline.
