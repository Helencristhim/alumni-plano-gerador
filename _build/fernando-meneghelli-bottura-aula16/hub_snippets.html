The Numbers Room -- Results for People Who Do Not Read Numbers

Explaining a year of results to a chief executive who is not from finance: one sentence for the year, what happened once and what will stay, and who checked the number. Key words: a headwind, a tailwind, year on year, a one-off, a run rate, cash flow, to break even, a provision, a forecast, to restate, plain English, to boil down. Structure: the impersonal passive and the causative have something done.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. You know most of these already. The goal is that they arrive on time, when somebody who is not from finance asks how your year went.

A headwind -- something outside your control that pushes results down
“The exchange rate was the biggest headwind of the year.”
A tailwind -- something outside your control that helps results
“Part of the growth was a tailwind from the election year, and I said so.”
Year on year -- compared with the same period last year
“Revenue is up eight percent year on year.”
A one-off -- a cost or a gain that will not happen again
“The office move was a one-off, so it will not be in next year’s numbers.”
A run rate -- what a number would be over a year if the current pace continued
“The savings have a run rate of two million a year from March.”
Cash flow -- the money that actually comes in and goes out
“The profit was fine; the cash flow was the problem.”
To break even -- to earn exactly what you spend, with no profit and no loss
“The new office broke even in its second year.”
A provision -- money set aside for a cost you expect but have not paid
“We made a provision for the client who was clearly not going to pay.”
A forecast -- what you expect the numbers to be, and what that rests on
“The forecast was reviewed by our two largest clients.”
To restate -- to publish a corrected version of numbers already reported
“We never had to restate a single quarter in nine years.”
Plain English -- simple language with no technical jargon
“Say it in plain English first, and then give the technical term.”
To boil down -- to reduce something to its most important part
“The whole year boils down to one client and one exchange rate.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The chief executive came up through the creative side, and she asked me to explain the year as I would to her board. So I gave it in one sentence: revenue was up eight percent year on year, and the margin was down one point.

Then I separated the two halves of that point. Half was a one-off, the office move. The other half was a headwind from the exchange rate, and that one would still be there. It is expected that the market will recover in the second half, and I said that too, but I said I would not build the plan on it.

What I would build it on was the volume our two largest clients had confirmed. We had the forecast reviewed by both of them before it went to the board. That sentence did more than the whole spreadsheet: it told her who had checked the number.

1. How does he open his answer?
A With the full spreadsheet, line by line, so that nothing important is missed.
B With the exchange rate.
C With one sentence about revenue and margin.
2. Which part of the margin will still be there next year?
A The cost of the office move, which will be repeated every year from now on.
B The exchange-rate headwind.
C Neither of them.
3. What does he say about the market recovering?
A That it is expected, but he would not build the plan on it.
B That it will certainly recover in the first half.
C That he does not mention it at all.
4. Who reviewed the forecast?
A He reviewed it himself, alone, the night before the board meeting.
B The two largest clients.
C The board.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Who Believes It, Who Checked It

GRAMMAR

Two structures for talking about numbers. The impersonal passive reports a general view without naming who holds it. The causative have + object + past participle says that you arranged the work and somebody else did it.

FormMeaningExample
It is expected / said / believed that...A general view, nobody namedIt is expected that the market will recover in the second half.
X is believed / thought to be...The same idea, starting with the topicThe decline is believed to be temporary.
have + object + past participleYou arranged it; somebody else did itWe had the forecast reviewed by our two largest clients.
NegativeUse the auxiliary of the tenseIt is not expected that prices will fall. We did not have the numbers restated.
QuestionInvert the auxiliaryIs it expected that the market will recover? Did you have the forecast reviewed?
The trapI had the accounts audited (somebody audited them for me) is not I had audited the accounts (past perfect: I audited them myself, earlier).

In an interview, use the impersonal passive for the market and the causative for your own work. A board trusts the passive that names the check.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The person asking is not from finance. Choose the answer that works for them.

A chief executive from the creative side asks how your year went. You start with:
A The EBITDA bridge, line by line, from the opening number to the closing number.
B An apology for the margin.
C One sentence on revenue and margin, and why.
The margin fell, and part of the fall was the cost of moving offices. You say:
A That part was a one-off, and it will not be in next year's numbers.
B Nothing, because a detail like that only needs to appear in the appendix.
C That the market caused it.
A director asks why she should believe next year's forecast. The strongest answer is:
A "It is expected that the market will recover, so the forecast is safe."
B "Forecasts are always uncertain."
C "We had it reviewed by our two largest clients."
Part of your growth came from an election year that will not repeat. You:
A Name the tailwind before the board finds it.
B Present the whole growth as the result of your own decisions during the year.
C Leave it out.
You need a technical term the board may not know. You:
A Use it and move on; they will ask if they need to.
B Say it in plain English first, then give the term.
C Avoid the topic completely so that nobody in the room feels lost.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is correcting this one, and there is no score.

Choose one real year from your career. Record ninety seconds explaining it to a board where nobody is from finance. Give the year in one sentence, separate what happened once from what will stay, and say who checked one of the numbers, using "we had ... reviewed" or "I had ... audited". Then listen once and count the technical terms you used without explaining them.
