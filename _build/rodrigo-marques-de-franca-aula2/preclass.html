Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Weekly Read -- How a Long Article Builds an Argument

The kind of piece you already read every week, taken apart: the claim, the caveat, and the long sentences that carry them. Key words: a case in point, to bear out, a caveat, sweeping, to hinge on, entrenched, a proxy, to gain traction, to lag behind, at odds with, a downside, to overstate. Structure: relative clauses, defining and non-defining.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A case in point -- an example that proves exactly what has just been said
"A case in point is software, where the week is already built around tasks."
To bear out -- to confirm that an earlier claim was in fact true
"The numbers bear out the basic claim, but only in one industry."
A caveat -- a warning that limits how far a claim can be taken
"I agree with the piece, with one caveat about the sample."
Sweeping -- covering far too much at once, with no room for exceptions
"That is a sweeping statement, and the exceptions are the interesting part."
To hinge on -- to depend completely on one single thing
"The whole case hinges on how you measure output."
Entrenched -- fixed so deeply in place that it is very hard to change
"The habit of counting hours is entrenched for a reason."
A proxy -- something you measure because you cannot measure what you actually want
"Hours worked is a proxy for effort, and a poor one."
To gain traction -- to start being accepted, or to start actually working
"The idea has gained traction fastest where it was needed least."
To lag behind -- to move more slowly than the others and fall further back
"Everywhere the work is tied to presence, the idea has lagged behind."
At odds with -- in conflict with something else, so that the two do not fit together
"The reform is at odds with the way we decide who is working hard."
A downside -- the disadvantage that comes with something otherwise good
"The downside of a proxy is that people start managing the proxy."
To overstate -- to describe something as bigger or more certain than it really is
"It would be easy to overstate the objection, so let me be careful."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every few years an argument appears that claims the five-day week is over. The trials that get the headlines are the ones in which output held steady while hours fell, and the numbers do bear out the basic claim. Iceland, which ran the largest trial, published everything in full, and Japan, which followed two years later, found something similar.

The caveat is the sample. A firm whose work is easy to count can lose a day and make it up by cutting meetings; a hospital cannot. So the sweeping version of the argument — the one that says the shorter week simply works — starts to come apart. The idea has gained traction fastest in the industries that needed it least, and has lagged behind everywhere the output is the hour itself. Software is a case in point.

It would be easy to overstate the objection. The honest position is that the case hinges on measurement, and that our habit of counting hours is entrenched because hours are the one thing everybody agrees on. The downside of any proxy is that it eventually gets managed instead of the thing it stands for — which is why a serious version of this reform is at odds with the way most organizations currently decide who is working hard.

1. "The trials that get the headlines are the ones in which output held steady." Why are there no commas around that get the headlines?
A Because the clause is short.
B Because the clause identifies which trials. Delete it and you no longer know which trials are meant.
C Because commas are never used after the word trials.
2. "Iceland, which ran the largest trial, published everything in full." Why which and not that?
A Because the clause is inside commas, and a clause inside commas never takes that.
B Because Iceland is a country and countries always take which.
C Because the verb ran is in the past.
3. "A firm whose work is easy to count can lose a day." What does whose tell you here?
A That the firm belongs to somebody.
B That the writer does not know which firm is meant.
C That the work belongs to the firm. Whose marks possession, and it works for things as well as people.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

One test decides everything: delete the clause. If you still know which one, it takes commas.

JobFormExample
Identifies which oneno commas · that or whichThe trials that measured output were the easy ones.
Only adds informationcommas · which, never thatIceland, which ran the largest trial, published it.
Peoplewho (or that, when identifying)The staff who stayed were cheaper to keep.
Possessionwhose — people and thingsA firm whose output is easy to count.
After a prepositionin which · at which · for whomThe point at which the argument comes apart.
You may drop itonly as object, only without commasThe article (that) I read this morning.
Never repeat the objectone pronoun onlyThe article that I read (not: read it).

Never: Iceland, that ran the trial · The staff which stayed · The article that I read it · The trials, that measured output, were... · The firm which its output is easy to count

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to limit a claim without rejecting it. You say:
A "I agree, with one caveat about the sample."
B "That is completely wrong."
C "I have no opinion on that."
You add extra information about a country everybody can already identify. You say:
A "Iceland that ran the largest trial published everything."
B "Iceland, which ran the largest trial, published everything."
C "Iceland, that ran the largest trial, published everything."
Somebody says the reform works everywhere. You want to say that is too general. You say:
A "You did not read the article properly."
B "That is a sweeping claim, and the exceptions are the interesting part."
C "It has lagged behind, so it is entrenched."
You want to name what the whole argument depends on. You say:
A "The case is a proxy for measurement."
B "The whole case hinges on how you measure output."
C "The whole case bears out the downside."
