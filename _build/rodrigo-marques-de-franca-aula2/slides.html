Chapter 4: The Sentence That Holds It

The Clause That Identifies, and the One That Adds

Two jobs, and the commas decide which

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"The trials that get the headlines are the ones with the best numbers."

"Iceland, which ran the largest trial, published the numbers in full."

"A team whose week is built around tasks can absorb the change."

"Our support desk, which answers a phone all week, absorbed the whole cost."

Now delete each red clause and read the sentence again. Two of them survive the deletion. Two of them stop making sense — and the commas told you in advance which was which.

Identifies which oneno commas · that or whichThe trials that measured output were the easy ones.

The trapnever that after a commaNot Iceland, that ran the trial,...

In one line: no commas = you need it to know which one. Commas = you could delete it and still know which one.

Take It Apart

Where Is the Main Clause?

Say the sentence without the extra clauses, then click to compare

1. "The trials that get the headlines are the ones in which output held steady while hours fell, and they are real."
Main clause: "The trials are real." Everything else identifies which trials, and under what conditions.
2. "Output, in an office, is usually a proxy for something nobody can count directly."
Main clause: "Output is a proxy." The rest tells you where, and a proxy for what.
3. "A team whose week is organized around discrete tasks can lose a day and make it up by cutting meetings."
Main clause: "A team can lose a day and make it up." The whose clause identifies which kind of team.
4. "Any serious version of this reform, therefore, is at odds with the way most organizations currently decide who is working hard."
Main clause: "Any version of this reform is at odds with the way organizations decide." The rest is qualification.

Listening 1 · An Economist

Real and Narrow at the Same Time

An economist on a podcast, on what the trials actually show. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say people hear only half of?
That the evidence is real and narrow at the same time. People hear only one of the two.
2. What is his objection to the firms in the trials?
A firm that volunteers for a study like this already thinks it has slack in the week, so it is not a random sample of the economy.
3. Which number does he say is the interesting one, and why does nobody use it?
Turnover: the people stayed, and staff who stay are cheaper than staff you replace twice a year. Nobody uses it because turnover is slow and headlines are not.
Listening 2 · A Manager

What Nobody Warned Me About

The person who actually ran the pilot inside a company. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say has to happen first, and what went wrong when they did it the other way round?
Meetings have to be killed before the day is cut. Doing it the other way round for three weeks compressed the same chaos into less time and made everybody miserable.
2. Which team barely noticed the change, and which one absorbed the whole cost?
The teams whose work is project-based barely noticed. The support desk, which has to answer a phone whether or not it is Friday, absorbed the whole cost.
3. What is her honest summary at the end?
That it worked, that the reporting on it is far too tidy, and that anybody who calls it simply a scheduling decision has never run one.
