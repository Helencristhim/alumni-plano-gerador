Lesson 2 -- The Weekly Read

Podcast
The Indicator from Planet Money (NPR) -- "Test driving a four-day work week"

Nine minutes on the British trial the article in this lesson is built on. It is the same argument you took apart in class, at natural American speed, and the transcript sits on the same page so you can read and listen together.

Tip: listen first, then read the transcript and mark every relative clause. Ask yourself which ones would keep their meaning if you deleted them.

Talk
Juliet Schor -- "The case for a 4-day work week" (TED, 12 min)

The strongest version of the argument, made by the economist who ran the trials. Watch it as the case for the prosecution: she states the claim cleanly, and the caveats you found in class are exactly what she does not say.

Tip: write down the one sentence in which she states her claim. Then write the caveat you would add if you were replying to her.

Watch on TED ↗
Video
DW News -- "Economists oppose trend towards a 4-day week"

The other side, and the useful one for you. Watch it straight after the TED talk and notice how the objection is built: not by denying the numbers, but by asking what the numbers were measuring. That move is the whole lesson.

Tip: pause when somebody raises an objection and say it back in your own words, starting with "the point is that". Then decide which of the two arguments you actually believe.
