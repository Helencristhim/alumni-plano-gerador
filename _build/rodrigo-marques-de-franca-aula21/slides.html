Chapter 3: The Code

Put the Weight First

When the order of the words changes, the listener pays attention

Grammar Discovery

Strange Order. Strong Message.

Not only did I learn the law, I also learned the business.

Rarely have I felt as useful as when a deal closes.

Only after I moved to São Paulo did I really see it.

Little did I know that those first years were the most rewarding.

Where do the red words start? And after them: is it I did or did I — the order of a statement or of a question?

Start withThenExample
Not onlyaux + subject + verb ..., (but) alsoNot only did I learn the law, I also learned the business.
Never / Rarely / Seldomaux + subject + verbNever have I been so scared.
Only later / Only after + clause / Only when + clauseaux + subject + verbOnly later did I understand it.
Littledid + subject + know / realizeLittle did I know how hard it would be.
Emphatic dodo / does / did + base verb, in a normal statementI do enjoy it. I did try.
The trapsno inversion · did + past form · inversion in a chat with friendsNot never I have seen, not only later did I realized.

In one line: a negative or limiting word at the start pulls the auxiliary in front of the subject.

Chapter 4: Real Voices

On the Record

One interview, two recordings, one email

Dialogue

Counsel Across Borders

M
Rodrigo, welcome to the show. I've asked sixty lawyers this question, and only three said yes. Did you always want to be a lawyer?
R
Honestly, no. I started in Salvador, almost by accident, and in my first week I was thrown in at the deep end: a client meeting, alone.
M
That sounds familiar. On my first day as a reporter, my editor sent me to a murder trial on my own. So how did you learn the ropes?
R
I had a mentor, an older partner. Not only did he make me write every contract twice, he made me explain every clause out loud.
M
And when did all that start to pay off?
R
Only after I moved to São Paulo did I really see it. Two years later, I landed my first international client. That was my breakthrough.
M
You tried litigation for a while, and then you pivoted back to corporate work. Why?
R
I do enjoy a good argument, believe me. But rarely have I felt as useful as when a deal closes. That's where I thrive.
M
Last question, and it's for our listeners in law school. How do they make a name for themselves?
R
Lay the groundwork early, and find a niche you actually enjoy. Little did I know, in Salvador, that those boring first years would be the most rewarding.
Comprehension

About Megan

1. How many lawyers has Megan asked her first question, and how many said yes?
Sixty lawyers. Only three said yes.
2. What happened to Megan on her first day as a reporter?
Her editor sent her to a murder trial on her own.
3. Who is Megan's last question for?
For the listeners of the show who are still in law school.
Listening 1 · The Producer

A Scene, Not a Resume

A podcast producer on what makes a good guest. Sound first — no text.

1. Which guests struggle, according to her?
The ones who give their resume: dates, job titles and the names of firms.
2. What do listeners remember?
A scene — one moment — not a list.
3. What homework does she give her guests?
To choose three moments from their career and practice telling each one in under a minute.
Listening 2 · The Retired Partner

Learn the Client First

A retired law firm partner on what he would do differently. Sound first — no text.

1. What did he believe in his first year as a lawyer?
That the best lawyer was the one who knew the most cases.
2. Why did he lose a client he had represented for six years?
The client never knew what he was thinking. He had never asked the client what worried him.
3. What was his answer to the young associate?
Learn the law, but learn the client first.

What I Can Do Now

I tell one moment of my career instead of a list of dates.
I start a sentence with "not only", "never" or "rarely" and put the auxiliary before the subject.
