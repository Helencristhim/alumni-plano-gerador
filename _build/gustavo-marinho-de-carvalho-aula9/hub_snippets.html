One sentence instead of three, and the pause in the middle disappears. Key words: a counterpart, a point of contact, to be in charge of, a stakeholder, to report to, a workload, to cover for, to be based in. Structure: relative clauses.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A counterpart -- the person who has the same job as you, on the other side
"Talk to my counterpart there. She does exactly what I do."
A point of contact -- the person you deal with about something, on a given side
"Who is my point of contact once you are on holiday?"
To be in charge of -- to have control of something and be responsible for it
"She is in charge of the contracts, and nothing moves without her."
A stakeholder -- anybody affected by a decision, whose view has to count
"There are four stakeholders on that decision and you have met one."
To report to -- to have somebody as your manager
"He reports to the person who signed the contract, which helps."
A workload -- the amount of work a person has to do
"Her workload doubled in March and nobody adjusted anything."
To cover for -- to do somebody's work while they are away
"Rafael is covering for her until the eighteenth."
To be based in -- to have your main office in a place
"The team that handles Europe is based in Manchester."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two short sentences with the same person in both are one sentence waiting to happen. The client is based in London. He handles our account. Join them and you get the client who handles our account is based in London. That is a relative clause, and it is the cheapest fluency there is at this level: the pause between your two short sentences was doing no work, and it made you sound less sure than you were.

The joining word depends on what you are describing. A person takes who or that; a thing takes which or that; a place takes where; and possession takes whose — the supplier whose contract ends in March. When the relative pronoun is the object rather than the subject, you can drop it altogether: the report I sent last week is complete and natural.

Then there is the comma, which is not decoration. The client who handles our account is based in London has no commas, and the clause is telling you WHICH client. James, who I spoke to yesterday, confirmed the date has commas, and the clause is extra: you already knew which James. One rule follows from this and it is absolute — that never appears between commas. And one warning for a speaker of Portuguese: the relative pronoun already IS the subject, so the client who he handles our account has one subject too many.

1. Why does the text call relative clauses the cheapest fluency at this level?
A Because they are the shortest sentences.
B Because the pause between two short sentences was doing no work and made you sound less sure.
C Because they are used only in writing.
2. What do the commas in “James, who I spoke to yesterday, confirmed the date” tell you?
A That the information is extra, because you already knew which James.
B That James is the most important person in the sentence.
C That the sentence is formal.
3. What is wrong with “the client who he handles our account”?
A Who cannot be used for people.
B There is one subject too many: who is already the subject.
C It needs a comma before who.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

No commas means the clause tells you WHICH one. Commas mean you already knew, and this is extra.

JobFormExample
Identifying a personwho / that, no commasThe client who handles our account is based in London.
Identifying a thingwhich / that, no commasThe report that I sent has been approved.
Extra information about somebody already identifiedwho / which, WITH commasJames, who I spoke to yesterday, confirmed the date.
A placewherethe office where the contracts team sits
Possessionwhosethe supplier whose contract ends in March
You may drop it when it is the object(that) I sentthe report I sent last week
The trapno double subject, and never that with commasNot who he handles. Not James, that I spoke to.

Never: The client who he handles our account · James, that I spoke to · The report who I sent · the supplier where contract ends
