Chapter 3: The Missing S

One Letter, Two Reputations

The buyer hears it even when nobody mentions it

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Do?

"I run the company, and we specialize in data."

"Dataside specializes in data, and it has almost three hundred people."

"The buyer wants a faster close, and she does not move on price."

"Our stakeholders want the same numbers, and they do not wait."

Two of these verbs carry an extra letter. Look at what comes before the verb in the red lines, and at what comes before it in the blue ones.

JobFormExample
I / you / we / theybare verbWe specialize in data.
he / she / itverb + -sShe wants a faster close.
the company, the buyer, the marketcounts as it → verb + -sDataside has three hundred people.
two or more people or thingsbare verbOur stakeholders want the same numbers.
negative, he / she / itdoes not + bare verbHe does not understand the model.
question, he / she / itdoes + subject + bare verbDoes the buyer need more time?
the trapthe -s moves to does, never to bothNot she doesn't wants. Say she doesn't want.

In one line: if the subject can be replaced by he, she or it, the verb takes the -s. Your company is an it.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Same Questions, At Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one plan on paper

Dialogue

Two Minutes at a Conference

D
Sorry to interrupt — Diane Whitfield, Harbor Ridge Partners. Somebody said I should find the founder of the Brazilian data company.
C
That is me. Caio Amante. I am the founder and CEO of Dataside.
D
Great. So, quickly, before they close the room — what does Dataside actually do?
C
We specialize in data and artificial intelligence. Our people work inside the client company, so it is consulting, not software.
D
How big are you? Headcount, and where does the revenue come from?
C
Almost three hundred people. Most of our revenue comes from long contracts, and no client is more than eight percent of it.
D
That is a healthy number. Most founders I meet cannot answer that one in a sentence. What are you looking for right now — capital, a partner, or an exit?
C
Right now, expansion into the United States. If the right partner comes with it, I am open to that conversation. And you — what does Harbor Ridge look for in a company like ours?
Comprehension

About Diane

1. Who does Diane work for, and how does she introduce herself?
She introduces herself as Diane Whitfield, from Harbor Ridge Partners, and she says somebody told her to find the founder of the Brazilian data company.
2. What two things does she want to know when she asks how big the company is?
The headcount, and where the revenue comes from.
3. What does she say about most founders she meets, and what does she ask immediately after?
She says most of them cannot answer that question in a sentence, and then she asks whether he is looking for capital, a partner, or an exit.
Listening 1 · A Voice Note

What an Investor Is Actually Listening For

An American investor recording a note to her own team after a day of founder meetings. Sound first — no text.

1. How many founders did she meet, and what does she say about the language?
Eleven founders, and she says the ones who lost her were not the ones with the worst English.
2. What three things does she say she is listening for?
How big the company is, where the money comes from, and what the founder actually wants.
3. What does she say she does when a founder goes quiet?
She writes one word in the margin, and she says that word is almost never about English.
Listening 2 · An Advisor

What Really Kills a Cross-Border Deal

An M&A advisor on the deals that fall apart for reasons nobody writes in the report. Sound first — no text.

1. What reason does he say goes into the report, and what reason does he say is the real one?
The report says valuation. He says the real reason is usually that the buyer stopped believing the founder could run the company in their language.
2. What does he say a buyer does when a founder cannot follow the conversation?
The buyer starts talking to the CFO instead, and from that moment the founder is a risk on a slide.
3. What is his advice at the end?
Learn to say the three sentences about your own company before you learn anything else, and never let a meeting continue on something you did not understand.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Founders Who Sold in a Second Language

A short radio piece on founders who negotiated their own exit in English. Sound first — no text.

1. What did the study find about founders who spoke through an interpreter?
They closed at a lower multiple than founders who spoke for themselves, even when the businesses were the same size.
2. What does the piece say the difference was not?
It was not grammar, and it was not accent.
3. What one habit do the successful founders share, according to the piece?
They stop the meeting the moment they lose the thread, and they ask for the sentence again.
