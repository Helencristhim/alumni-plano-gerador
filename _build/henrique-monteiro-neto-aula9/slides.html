Chapter 3: The Code

Saying Exactly How Sure You Are

Between overclaiming and saying nothing at all

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Arguably, the model was never designed for an election like this one."

"On balance, I would expect a narrow win."

"The result is, to some extent, a function of turnout."

"Presumably they saw the same numbers we did."

Take each of these out and the sentence still works. So what is each of them adding — and what would you lose?

FormWhat it doesExample
Arguably · Conceivably
· Plausibly		Marks a claim as defensible rather than proven. Arguably invites the argument; conceivably concedes it is a long shot."Arguably, the model was never built for this."
On balance · By and large
· Broadly speaking		Says you have weighed competing evidence and come down on one side. Far stronger than I think, and far more honest than a flat claim."On balance, I would expect a narrow win."
To some extent · In part
· Up to a point		Concedes partial truth. Up to a point is the most useful and the most English: it agrees and warns you at the same time."That is true up to a point."
Presumably · Ostensibly
· Allegedly		Three different sources of doubt: presumably = I infer it; ostensibly = that is the stated reason and I doubt it; allegedly = somebody claims it."Ostensibly it was a scheduling problem."
Verb hedges:
tend to · appear to
would suggest		Hedging inside the verb rather than at the front. The data would suggest is the most quoted sentence shape in any serious report."The figures would suggest a small swing."

Hedging is not weakness and it is not padding. It is precision about a second thing: not what you claim, but how confident you are in the claim. A speaker who never hedges cannot be trusted with a difficult fact — and a speaker who hedges everything has said nothing at all.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"It is arguably that the model failed."
"Arguably, the model failed."
"In my opinion I think that maybe it could possibly be wrong."
"I suspect it is wrong."
"Presumably that they saw the same numbers."
"Presumably they saw the same numbers."

Three failures, and the middle one is the dangerous one at this level. Arguably and presumably are ADVERBS: they modify the sentence directly and take no that. And stacking four hedges into one sentence does not make you careful — it makes you unreadable. One hedge, chosen deliberately, beats four hedges chosen anxiously.

Chapter 4: Three Points, Two Points

The Pollster Who Is Wrong in Public

Her job is to be wrong by a known amount, and to say so beforehand

Dialogue

Three Points, Two Points

R
The sentence I most dislike reading about my own work is the polls were wrong. Sometimes they were. Usually they were wrong by about the amount we said they would be.
H
Which is a defence nobody accepts, presumably, because it sounds like an excuse prepared in advance.
R
It is prepared in advance. That is the entire point of a margin of error. If I tell you three points ahead and give you a two-point margin, I have told you it may be a tie.
H
And the headline says three points ahead.
R
The headline says three points ahead. Arguably that is our failure as much as theirs, because we publish a number and a range and we know which one gets quoted.
H
What would you change, if you could change one thing about how it is reported?
R
I would ban the decimal point. Forty-seven point two is false precision, and the decimal does more damage than any wrong forecast I have ever published.
H
On balance I suspect you are right, though I would want to know whether anybody reads the range even when it is printed.
Comprehension

About Rachel

1. What sentence does Rachel most dislike reading?
"The polls were wrong."
2. What does she say a margin of error actually tells you?
That a three-point lead with a two-point margin may in fact be a tie.
3. Whose failure does she say the headline is?
Arguably her own profession's as much as the newspaper's, because they know which number gets quoted.
4. What one thing would she change?
She would ban the decimal point, because it is false precision.
Listening 1

What a Poll Can Say

A statistician, explaining slowly. Sound first — no text.

1. How large a sample does she say is usually enough, and why?
About a thousand, because it gives a margin of roughly three points.
2. What does she say the margin of error does NOT include?
Everything except sampling — question wording, who refuses to answer, and who turns out.
3. What is her rule of thumb a week before a vote?
Double the published margin.
Listening 2

When Everybody Agrees

A polling analyst on herding. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say should make you suspicious?
When every poll agrees to the decimal point.
2. Why does he say herding happens?
Because being wrong alone costs a pollster far more than being wrong with everybody else.
3. What does he say about the outlier?
That it is usually the one being honest, and it is the one nobody publishes.
