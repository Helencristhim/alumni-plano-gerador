How Sure Are You? — Forecasts, Polls and the Language of Doubt

A three-point lead, a two-point margin, and the small words that tell your listener how much to trust you. Key expressions: a margin of error, an outlier, a sample, statistically significant, to skew, an aggregate, a swing, turnout, neck and neck, a bellwether, a rule of thumb, to hedge, a track record, to call an election, herding, a snapshot, to be off by, false precision. Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

A margin of error -- the range within which the real figure probably sits
"A three-point lead with a two-point margin of error is not a lead."
An outlier -- a single result far away from all the others
"One poll was an outlier and it got four times the coverage."
A sample -- the group actually questioned, standing in for everybody else
"The sample was a thousand people, which is more than enough if it is honest."
Statistically significant -- large enough that chance alone probably does not explain it
"The shift is real but it is not statistically significant, and both halves matter."
To skew -- to pull results in one direction, usually without anybody intending it
"Calling only landlines skews the sample by about a decade of age."
An aggregate -- an average built from many separate results
"The aggregate moved two points and no single poll did."
A swing -- a movement of support from one side to the other
"A four-point swing in eight weeks is unusual and worth explaining."
Turnout -- the proportion of people who actually vote
"Everything in that forecast depends on turnout, and turnout is the hardest thing to model."
Neck and neck -- so close that neither side is meaningfully ahead
"They have been neck and neck since March."
A bellwether -- a place or a group whose result usually predicts the wider one
"That district was a bellwether for forty years and then simply stopped being one."
A rule of thumb -- a rough practical method that is usually good enough
"The rule of thumb is to double the margin a week before the vote."
To hedge -- to avoid committing fully, in order to be less wrong
"He hedged so carefully that his forecast could not be checked."
A track record -- the history of how well somebody has predicted in the past
"Ask for the track record before you take the confidence seriously."
To call an election -- to declare the result publicly before all the votes are counted
"Two networks called it at eleven and one waited until three."
Herding -- pollsters quietly adjusting their results towards everybody else's
"When every poll agrees to the decimal, suspect herding rather than certainty."
A snapshot -- a picture of one moment, which is not the same as a forecast
"A poll is a snapshot, and it is read as a prophecy."
To be off by -- to be wrong by a stated amount
"The final average was off by three points, in the same direction as last time."
False precision -- numbers stated more exactly than the method can support
"Forty-seven point two per cent is false precision and everybody quotes it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Arguably the single most misread sentence in public life is a poll result. A three-point lead with a two-point margin of error is not a lead; it is a range that includes a tie, and the range is printed underneath the number that gets quoted. A poll is a snapshot, taken on a particular week, of the people who agreed to answer the telephone.

On balance, the mathematics is more reliable than its reputation. A properly randomised sample of about a thousand gives a margin of roughly three points, whatever the size of the country — a fact almost nobody believes on first hearing. What the margin does not cover is everything that is not sampling: the wording of the question, the people who refuse, and above all turnout, which to some extent decides the whole thing and is the hardest quantity to model.

Two habits are worth acquiring. The first is to look at the aggregate rather than at any single poll, since an outlier attracts coverage in inverse proportion to its reliability. The second is more counter-intuitive. When every poll in the final week agrees to the decimal, the right reaction is suspicion rather than confidence: herding is not fraud, and it is not usually conscious, but being wrong alone costs a pollster far more than being wrong in company.

Presumably none of this will change the headlines. The rule of thumb a week before a vote is to double the published margin, and if the two sides are still neck and neck after that, the honest answer is that nobody knows. Ostensibly the decimal point exists to add accuracy. It is false precision, it is quoted everywhere, and the profession's own track record would suggest that it has done more damage than any forecast that was simply off by three points.

1. "Arguably the single most misread sentence…" What is arguably doing?
A Saying the claim is proven.
B Marking the claim as defensible rather than established.
C Introducing a question.
2. Why is a three-point lead with a two-point margin not a lead?
A Because the poll was too small.
B Because the range includes the possibility of a tie.
C Because the poll was an outlier.
3. What does the text say the margin of error does NOT cover?
A Everything that is not sampling — question wording, refusals and turnout.
B The number of people questioned.
C The randomisation of the sample.
4. Why should agreement between every poll make you suspicious?
A Because it proves the pollsters are dishonest.
B Because herding is likelier than certainty when being wrong alone is so costly.
C Because polls are always wrong.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging — Saying How Sure You Are

GRAMMAR

Hedging adds a second layer of information to a claim: not what you assert, but how much weight you are putting behind it. Used well it makes you more credible, not less — and used badly it makes you inaudible.

FormWhat it doesExample
Arguably · Conceivably
Plausibly · Not unreasonably		Marks a claim as defensible rather than proven. Arguably invites the argument you expect to have; conceivably admits it is a long shot."Arguably, the model was never built for this."
On balance · By and large
Broadly speaking · All things considered		Says you have weighed competing evidence and landed somewhere. Much stronger than I think and much more honest than a flat assertion."On balance, I would expect a narrow win."
To some extent · In part
Up to a point · In some respects		Concedes partial truth. Up to a point agrees and warns at the same time, and is one of the most useful phrases in an English argument."That is true up to a point."
Presumably · Ostensibly
· Allegedly · Apparently		Different SOURCES of doubt, not different amounts. Presumably: I am inferring. Ostensibly: that is the stated reason and I doubt it. Allegedly: somebody claims it and I am not vouching for them."Ostensibly it was a scheduling problem."
Verb hedges:
tend to · appear to
would suggest · may well		Hedging inside the verb instead of at the front of the sentence. The data would suggest is the standard shape of a careful conclusion in English."The figures would suggest a small swing."

Two things to avoid. First, arguably, presumably and ostensibly are ADVERBS — they take no that. Second, and far more important at your level: do not stack them. In my opinion I think that maybe it could possibly be wrong is four hedges and no claim. One hedge, chosen on purpose, is precision. Four hedges is anxiety.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are being asked to predict something in public. Choose the version that is both honest and useful.

You want to mark a claim as defensible rather than proven:
A "It is arguably that the model failed."
B "Arguably, the model failed."
C "Arguably that the model failed."
You have weighed both sides and want to commit, carefully:
A "On balance, I would expect a narrow win."
B "In balance, I would expect a narrow win."
C "On the balance, I expect maybe a narrow win possibly."
You are inferring something rather than knowing it:
A "Presumably that they saw the same numbers."
B "Presumably they saw the same numbers."
C "It is presumably that they saw the same numbers."
Somebody says all the polls agree, so the result is certain:
A "Yes, that settles it."
B "When every poll agrees to the decimal, I would suspect herding rather than certainty."
C "The aggregate is an outlier, so it is neck and neck."
You are asked for a prediction and want to be useful rather than safe:
A "It is impossible to say."
B "On balance, a narrow win — and turnout among the under-thirties is what would change my mind."
C "In my opinion I think that maybe it could possibly go either way."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Make one prediction out loud about something you follow — politics, an economy, an institution. In about ninety seconds, do four things: state the prediction, say how confident you are using ONE deliberate hedge, name the single piece of evidence that would change your mind, and say what your own track record on this kind of question has been. The last part is the hardest and the most valuable.
