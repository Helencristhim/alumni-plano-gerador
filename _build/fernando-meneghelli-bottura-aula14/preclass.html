Lesson 14 -- Pre-class

The Panel -- Four People, One Answer

Answering the person who asked and the room at the same time, protecting a point without refusing anybody, and giving the same answer when a second member asks it. Key words: a panel, the chair, a non-executive director, to field a question, to bring somebody in, to hand back, an overlap, to acknowledge somebody, a silent one, to read the room, a cross-question, to wrap up. Structure: discourse markers for a panel.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These twelve are for disagreeing with somebody more senior than you, in a language that is not yours — firmly, and never defensively.

A panel -- the group of people interviewing you at the same time
“The panel was four people, and only one of them was from finance.”
The chair -- the person who runs the meeting and decides the order
“The chair speaks least and decides most, so answer the room, not only her.”
A non-executive director -- a board member who does not work in the company day to day
“The non-executive director asked the only question about risk.”
To field a question -- to take a question and deal with it, calmly
“I fielded the question about the margin twice, from two different people.”
To bring somebody in -- to invite another person into the conversation
“I brought the operations director in by naming the part that was hers.”
To hand back -- to finish and return the turn to the panel
“End the answer and hand back clearly, or somebody will interrupt you.”
An overlap -- two people covering the same ground without knowing it
“There is usually an overlap between the chief executive and the chair.”
To acknowledge somebody -- to show you heard who asked, before you answer
“Acknowledge the person who asked, then give the answer to the room.”
A silent one -- the panel member who says almost nothing and scores you anyway
“The silent one on the left wrote for the whole hour.”
To read the room -- to notice what the group is reacting to while you talk
“Reading the room is knowing when the third example is one too many.”
A cross-question -- a question that follows from what another member just asked
“Her cross-question was harder than the original one.”
To wrap up -- to close your answer in one sentence
“Wrap up before they have to do it for you.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A panel is not one interview with four people in the room. It is four interviews at once, and only one of them is about finance. The chair speaks least and decides most, the operations director is checking whether I claim ground that is his, and the silent one writes everything down.

So the first line of the answer goes to the person who asked, and the rest goes to the room. To your point about the cash cycle, the number was sixty days. If somebody is brought in before I have finished, I ask, in one sentence: may I finish the cash part first. Nobody has ever refused that.

The other rule is the one that decides a panel. When two of them ask about the same decision, I say out loud that it came up earlier and I give the same answer, in the same words. Adding something new the second time is how four people end up comparing two versions of me. And I wrap up before they have to do it: that is the short answer, and I am happy to go deeper if it is useful.

2. Where does the first line of an answer go?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Four Sentences That Steer a Room

GRAMMAR

Four short structures decide who an answer belongs to: the person who asked, the room, the colleague it really belongs to, and the panel when you hand back.

FormMeaningExample
To your point about...Answers one person by name of the topicTo your point about the cash cycle, the number was sixty days.
Coming back to what you asked earlier...Closes a loop you left openComing back to what you asked earlier, we ended the year at thirty-two.
May I finish...?Protects your point without refusing anybodyMay I finish the cash part first? It is one sentence.
That is probably one for...Passes a question without dropping it
The trapAfter May I comes the base verb, with no to: May I finish, never May I to finish.
Why it matters in an interview"I used to find that difficult, and I am used to it now" answers half the hostile questions in any process. It shows change without claiming to have been perfect — which is precisely what a panel is listening for.

The three together make a complete arc in one breath: what you used to do, what you are used to now, and how long it took to get used to it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The question has an accusation inside it. Choose the answer that is not defensive.

The chair asks a question in a panel of four. You look at:
A Only the chair, because she asked.
B The chair for the first line, then the whole room.
C The most senior person, wherever the question came from.
Somebody is brought in while you are mid-answer. You:
A Stop and let them speak.
B Ask to finish your point, saying how long it will take.
C Speak faster so you finish before them.
A second panel member asks about a decision you already explained. You:
A Give the same answer, in the same words, and say it came up earlier.
B Give a different example to keep it interesting.
C Say that you covered it already.
A question is really about operations, not finance. You:
A Answer it anyway, to look confident.
B Pass it to the right person, and give the finance side of it.
C Say it is not your area and stop.
You have finished your answer. The best way to end is:
A Wait in silence for the next question.
B One sentence that closes it and hands the turn back.
C Add a third example, in case it helps.
