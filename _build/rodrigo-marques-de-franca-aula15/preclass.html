Lesson 15 -- Pre-class

The Client Who Is Wrong -- Giving Bad News

How to tell somebody what they do not want to hear, and keep them on your side. Key words: to break the news, to sugarcoat, to be upfront, to vent, to defuse, to weigh up, a setback, a long shot, to bear the brunt of, a silver lining, damage control, to manage expectations. Structure: cleft sentences for focus and reframing -- What we can do is... / It's... that..., not...

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To break the news -- to tell somebody bad news that they do not know yet
"I had to break the news to the client before the press found out."
To sugarcoat -- to make bad news sound better than it really is
"I'm not going to sugarcoat it: the claim is weak."
To be upfront -- to be honest and direct about something from the very start
"Let me be upfront with you: this will take at least a year."
To vent -- to express strong feelings, especially anger, in order to release them
"Let her vent for a minute before you explain the clause."
To defuse -- to make a tense or dangerous situation calmer
"A simple question defused the whole meeting."
To weigh up -- to consider the good and bad points of something carefully
"Let's weigh up both options before you decide."
A setback -- something that delays progress or makes a situation worse
"The expert's report is a setback, but it is not the end of the case."
A long shot -- something that has only a small chance of success
"The claim for lost profits is a long shot."
To bear the brunt of -- to suffer the worst part of something bad
"Our sales team bore the brunt of the delay."
A silver lining -- something good that comes from a bad situation
"The silver lining is that the case should settle quickly."
Damage control -- actions taken to limit the harm after something has gone wrong
"The rest of the week was damage control."
To manage expectations -- to make sure somebody does not expect more than is realistic
"Our first job is to manage the client's expectations."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Claire Benson was sure she would recover two million dollars. Her supplier had delivered three months late, and her team had borne the brunt of it. Rodrigo read the contract on Monday morning and called her that afternoon. He decided to be upfront: the claim for lost profits was a long shot, and he was not going to sugarcoat it.

Claire was angry, and he let her vent. Then he explained the problem. What the contract does is cap the supplier's liability at the price of the order. It's the lost profits that the clause excludes, not the delay itself.

After that, he changed the focus. What we can do is claim the direct costs, he said, and those are well documented. It was a setback, but there was a silver lining: that part of the case was strong and should settle quickly. By the end of the call, Claire had stopped asking who was to blame and was weighing up her options.

1. "What the contract does is cap the supplier's liability." Why does Rodrigo say it this way?
A To show that he is not sure what the contract says and needs to read it again before the call.
B To put the spotlight on what the contract does, at the end of the sentence.
C To ask Claire a question.
2. "It's the lost profits that the clause excludes, not the delay." What is Rodrigo correcting?
A The idea that the clause excludes everything about the delay.
B The amount of money that Claire's team spent on overtime during the weekends in March.
C The date of the delivery.
3. How had the conversation changed by the end of the call?
A Claire had decided to sue for the full amount.
B Claire was no longer asking who was to blame; she was considering her options.
C Claire had ended the call.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences

GRAMMAR

The facts stay the same. A cleft sentence moves the important part to the end, where the listener pays most attention.

JobFormExample
Spotlight on the solutionWhat + subject + verb + is + focusWhat we can do is claim the direct costs.
Spotlight on an actionWhat + subject + does / did + is + verbWhat the clause does is cap the liability.
Correct a misunderstandingIt is / was + focus + that + restIt's the profits that it excludes, not the delay.
Name the real problemWhat worries me is...What worries me is the timing.
QuestionWhat is it that...? · Is it X that...?Is it the cost that worries you?
NegativeIt isn't X that..., it's YIt isn't the delay that hurts us, it's the cap.
Limit, honestlyAll we can do is + verbAll we can do now is wait.

Never: It's the cap what worries me · What we can do it is · The thing what concerns me

Stage 2: Give the News in the Right Order

Order

Warning, fact, reason, feeling, next step, decision. Put the call in that order.

?"Claire, I'll be upfront with you: it's not the news we wanted."
?"The claim for lost profits is a long shot."
?"What the contract does is cap their liability at the price of the order."
?"I can see why you're frustrated, and you have every right to be."
?"What we can do is claim the direct costs, which are well documented."
?"Let's weigh up both options, and you can decide by Friday."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A client is shouting about a decision that went against him. You say first:
A "Calm down, please. Shouting will not change the decision, and we have a lot to discuss today."
B "I can see why you're upset. Take a minute."
C "It was not my fault."
The client thinks the judge rejected the whole claim. In fact, only the expert report was rejected. You say:
A "It's the expert report that the judge rejected, not the claim."
B "It's the expert report what the judge rejected, so the rest of the claim is still in front of the court."
C "The judge rejected everything."
A friend's flight has been cancelled and you are helping. You want to move the conversation forward. You say:
A "There is nothing we can do about it now, so it is probably better to just wait and see what happens."
B "What we can do is book the morning flight right now."
C "It is a long shot."
At the first meeting, a new client expects to win everything. You want to be honest from the start. You say:
A "I'll be upfront with you: some of this claim is a long shot."
B "I'll sugarcoat it for you: everything will be fine, and we will certainly win the whole claim in a few months."
C "We will see."
