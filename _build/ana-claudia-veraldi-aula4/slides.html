Two things before Thursday. Listen for what he WANTS, not for every word.

And Then Bram Replies

Same project, very different style. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00

No. He says clearly that he is not against it — he just wants the list agreed before anybody says yes.

Chapter 4: The Code

Asking Again, Without Sounding Rude

The same request, four levels of directness

Discovery

Same Request, Four Distances

"Sorry, what was that?"

"Sorry, I did not catch that."

"Could you repeat that, please?"

"Sorry, would you mind saying that again?"

All four ask for exactly the same thing. What changes is how much distance the sentence puts between you and the other person — and how much distance that person expects.

SentenceDistanceWho it fits
Sorry, what was that?CloseAmericans, Australians, colleagues you know
Sorry, I did not catch that.NeutralWorks everywhere. Your default.
Could you repeat that, please?PoliteFirst meetings, clients, larger groups
Would you mind saying that again?CarefulNordic colleagues, formal settings, seniority
Could you give me the short version?NeutralWhen you caught the words but missed the point
Just to check, are you saying ...?NeutralBefore you agree to anything that matters
What? on its own—Nobody you do not know well. It is not neutral.

In one line: the more distance the culture expects, the longer the sentence gets.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"What?"
"Sorry, I did not catch that."
"Repeat, please."
"Could you repeat that, please?"
"Sorry, I did not listen you."
"Sorry, I did not hear you."
Nodding and saying nothing when you did not understand.
"Sorry, I lost you at the part about phase one."

The first three are language. The fourth one is not, and it is the expensive one: every meeting where you nodded instead of asking is a meeting where a decision was made without you in it.

A
Sorry Priya, I did not catch that last word. Could you say it again?
P
Sequencing. The order. Which item comes first, second, third.
A
Got it, thank you. And just to check, are you saying we should agree the order today, not only the list?
P

P

A
Now I follow you. Then my three are the ones I sent on Tuesday, and I am happy to start.
Comprehension

About Priya

1. What word did Priya have to explain, and how did she explain it?
Sequencing. She rephrased it in simple words: the order — which item comes first, second, third.
2. What is Priya's point about the list?
That agreeing the list is not enough — they have to agree the order today, or they only think they are on the same page.
3. How many times does Ana ask for help, and does the conversation suffer?
Three times: to repeat a word, to confirm a decision, and to get the short version. The conversation moves faster because of it, not slower.

Something like: Hi Mike, sorry, your note came through muffled and I could not make out most of it. Could you send me the short version before three?
2. You need Lars to confirm the new date before the call. What do you ask?
Just to check, are you saying the deadline is now the fifteenth, not the eighth? — confirming before you plan around it.
3. Would you use the same sentence with Mike in Austin and with Lars in Oslo? Why not?
No. With Mike, Sorry, what was that? is fine and friendly. With Lars, the same words land as impatient — Would you mind saying that again? fits the distance he expects.

Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Lars Trails Off

Keywords:

Would you mind ...? to rephrase on the same page
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Two Minutes, No Pretending

Scenario: I am going to brief you on a project, badly. I will talk fast, use a word that does not exist, and talk over you once. Your only job is to leave this conversation knowing exactly what you have to do — and to never once pretend you understood.

No keywords, no notes, two minutes.
