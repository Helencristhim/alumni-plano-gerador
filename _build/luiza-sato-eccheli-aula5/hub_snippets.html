Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Housing -- the rooms or apartments where people live, especially the ones a university offers
"The university offers housing for exchange students."
A roommate -- a person who shares a room or an apartment with you
"My roommate was from Germany, and we cooked together on Sundays."
To settle in -- to start to feel comfortable in a new place or situation
"It took me three weeks to settle in."
Homesick -- sad because you are far from your home and your family
"I felt homesick every Sunday night."
Culture shock -- the confusion people feel when they live in a very different culture for the first time
"The culture shock was not the language; it was dinner at six."
A stipend -- a fixed amount of money paid regularly to help a student with living costs
"The stipend covers rent, but not much more."
To budget -- to plan how much money you will spend on each thing
"If you do not budget, London eats your money in a week."
A course load -- the amount of work from all the classes a student is taking
"Law students usually have a heavy course load."
An elective -- a course that a student chooses, not one that is required
"I took an elective on human rights and loved it."
Office hours -- the times when a professor is available to talk to students
"Go to office hours in the first week, not the last."
To fall behind -- to fail to do work as fast as you should, so you have more to do later
"I missed two classes and fell behind with the reading."
To make the most of -- to use a situation or a chance in the best possible way
"Make the most of every weekend, because the semester ends fast."
To look back on -- to think about something that happened in the past
"When I look back on that year, I remember the people, not the exams."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Almost every conversation about an exchange year uses sentences that are not real. Students imagine a year that has not started, and people who came back describe a year that could have been different. English shows that a sentence is not real by moving its verbs one step back in time.

To imagine the present or the future, use if + past and would: “If I were in London now, I would go to office hours in the first week.” With be, English uses were for every person in careful speech, and “If I were you” is the classic way to give advice.

To talk about a different past, the regret, use if + had + participle and would have + participle: “If he had budgeted, he would not have spent his stipend in a month.” The most common mistake is to put would in the if-clause, as in “if I would have known”. English never does that.

Real life often mixes the two. A choice in the past can change the present: “If I had chosen a lighter course load, I would be less tired now.” And a fact that is true now can explain a past result: “If she were more organized, she would not have fallen behind.” Once you control these forms, you can make the most of other people’s mistakes before you make your own.

1. According to the text, how does English show that a sentence is not real?
A By using the future tense.
B By moving the verbs one step back in time.
C By adding the word maybe.
2. Which sentence does the text say is always wrong?
A If I would have known, I would have applied.
B If I were you, I would apply.
C If he had budgeted, he would have saved.
3. What does “If I had chosen a lighter course load, I would be less tired now” show?
A A regret with a past result.
B A plan for the future.
C A past choice with a present result.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Second, Third and Mixed Conditionals

GRAMMAR

Past for an imagined present, had + participle for an imagined past, and never would after if.

JobFormExample
Imagined presentif + past, would + verbIf I were there, I would go.
Imagined pastif + had + participle, would have + participleIf he had budgeted, he would have saved.
Past cause, present resultif + had + participle, would + verbIf I had chosen fewer classes, I would be less tired.
Present fact, past resultif + past, would have + participleIf she were organized, she would not have fallen behind.
AdviceIf I were you, I would + verbIf I were you, I would apply now.
Less certainmight / couldWe might have gotten a room.

Never: if I would have known · if I am in London now, I would go · he would not spend it last month · I would have been less tired now
