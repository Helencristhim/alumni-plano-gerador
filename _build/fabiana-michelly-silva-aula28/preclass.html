Lesson 28 -- Pre-class

Resilience in Communication -- The Polite No

Handling pushback from an angry VP of Sales and a CFO who interrupts, without losing the relationship. Key words: heated, vent, rattled, take it personally, back down, non-negotiable, stand your ground, keep a cool head, hear someone out, validate, reframe, bounce back. Structure: would rather + past and it's time + past -- firm, polite requests and refusals (I'd rather we didn't..., It's time we agreed...).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Heated -- full of anger or strong emotion (a discussion or a call)
"The call with Greg got heated very quickly."
Vent -- to express anger or frustration so that you feel better
"Let him vent for a minute before you answer."
Rattled -- nervous and upset, so you lose your calm
"The interruptions left her a little rattled."
Take it personally -- to feel attacked when a comment is really about work
"He is angry with the customer, so don't take it personally."
Back down -- to stop insisting and accept what the other person wants
"Greg will not back down easily."
Non-negotiable -- impossible to change or discuss
"The maintenance window is non-negotiable."
Stand your ground -- to keep your position when other people try to make you change it
"Stay polite, but stand your ground on the date."
Keep a cool head -- to stay calm and think clearly in a difficult situation
"She kept a cool head when the CFO raised his voice."
Hear someone out -- to listen to someone until they have finished, without interrupting
"Hear him out first, then give your answer."
Validate -- to show that you understand why someone feels the way they do
"Validate the pressure he is under before you say no."
Reframe -- to present a situation in a different way so people see it differently
"She reframed the budget as insurance, not a cost."
Bounce back -- to recover quickly after a difficult moment
"After a heated call, bounce back before the next meeting."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Greg Walsh, the VP of Sales, calls Fabiana. He promised the Chicago order for the fourteenth, and the call gets heated. Fabiana feels a little rattled, so she asks Marcus for help. His advice: hear him out, validate the pressure he is under, and stand your ground on the date. "And don't take it personally," he says.

Fabiana prepares two sentences. "I'd rather we didn't promise the fourteenth." "It's time we agreed on a date we can keep." The verbs look like the past, but the meaning is now. After would rather + another person, and after it's time, English uses the past form to make a firm request sound polite. For her own preference, she uses the base verb: "I'd rather wait until Friday."

The same week, the CFO interrupts her twice and calls the budget a luxury. She keeps a cool head, does not apologize, and reframes it: not a cost, but insurance. Greg does not back down at first, but the twenty-first is non-negotiable, and by the end of the week both conversations end with a plan.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Would Rather and It's Time

GRAMMAR

The verb is in the past form, but the meaning is now. The past form adds distance, so a firm request sounds polite.

StructureWhat it saysExample
would rather + someone + pastwhat you prefer another person to doI'd rather you sent it in writing.
Negative: would rather + someone + didn'ta polite noI'd rather we didn't promise the fourteenth.
would rather + base verbyour own preferenceI'd rather wait until Friday.
it's (high) time + someone + pastsomething should already be happeningIt's time we agreed on a date.
Questionasking about someone's preferenceWould you rather we waited until Friday?
Rememberno to after rather; past form after the subjectI'd rather wait · I'd rather you waited

Never: I'd rather you send · I'd rather we don't · It's time we agree · I'd rather to wait
