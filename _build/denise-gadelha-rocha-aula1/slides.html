Chapter 3: The Code

We Have Grown, or We Grew?

The tense that reports a period

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"We have grown eleven percent year on year."

"In March we closed the largest account in the region."

"Since January the loss ratio has come down four points."

"Last quarter we lost two brokers in Argentina."

Two sentences use have. Two do not. What is different about the time in each one?

FormWhen you use itExample
have / has + past participleThe period is still open, or the result matters right nowWe have grown eleven percent this year.
past simpleThe time is finished and named, or both sides know which time you meanIn March we closed the account.
Markers that pull havethis quarter · so far · since January · over the last six months · already · still · to date
Markers that block itlast quarter · in March · in 2024 · yesterday · two weeks ago · when I joined
Ongoing since a dateStarted in the past, still true today: have been + -ingI have been leading the region since 2024.
Two trapsNever We have closed it in March — a named finished time takes the past simple. And never Since January we grew — since always takes the present perfect.

The Structure

Ninety Seconds, Four Moves

The opening is the only part of a presentation you can fully control. Build it once, use it every quarter.

1 · The frame

"I will walk you through the quarter in three parts: the numbers, the risk, and the outlook."

2 · The headline, first

"The headline figure is this: we have grown eleven percent year on year."

3 · The reason

"Two things drove it: the corporate portfolio, and better underwriting in the south."

4 · The takeaway, and the ask

"The takeaway is that the growth is real, but our exposure has grown with it. I need a decision on that today."

Frame → headline → reason → takeaway. Never fifteen slides of background first.

Chapter 4: Milan on the Line

Meet Stefano

Dialogue

The Quarterly Review

S
Denise, good evening. We have twenty minutes before the board call. Give me the region.
D
Good evening, Stefano. I will walk you through it in three parts: the numbers, the risk, and the outlook.
S
Start with the number. That is the only part the board will remember.
D
The headline figure is eleven percent growth year on year. The top line is forty million in premium, and the corporate portfolio drove almost all of it.
S
Good. And the bottom line? Growth that costs us money is not growth.
D
The loss ratio has come down four points since January, so the bottom line holds. But I want to flag one thing before you ask me.
S
Go on.
D
Our exposure to climate risk in the south has grown with the portfolio. Better underwriting is holding it, but it will not hold for another two quarters.
S
Thank you for saying that now and not in October. Put it on one slide and I will take it to the board myself.
Comprehension

About Stefano

1. What does he want to hear first, and why?
The number — because it is the only part the board will remember.
2. Why is he not satisfied with the growth figure alone?
Because growth that costs the company money is not growth — he wants the bottom line too.
3. How does he react to the bad news about exposure?
He thanks her for raising it in July instead of October, and he offers to take it to the board himself.
Listening

What a Global Head Actually Wants

He sits through eleven of these calls a quarter. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he want in the first thirty seconds?
The headline — not the context, not the methodology, and not an apology for the connection.
2. What happens if you open with fifteen slides of background?
He stops listening and starts reading his email.
3. What do the directors he trusts most have in common?
Not the best English — they know which number matters.
4. What does he say about native speakers on the call?
There are none. He is Italian and half the room speaks Spanish — waiting for perfect English means waiting forever.
Listening 2

The First Ninety Seconds

A director who made the same jump. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What was the hardest part of moving into a director role for her?
Not the strategy — the first ninety seconds of every presentation.
2. What happened to her introductions when she was nervous?
The more nervous she was, the longer they became.
3. What is the three-sentence rule she still uses?
One sentence for the headline, one for the reason, one for what she wants from the room.
4. What does she say about bad news?
Flag it yourself, early. Nobody has ever been fired for saying it early — people get into trouble when the number appears on slide fourteen.

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "Actually we are reviewing the underwriting limits."
"Currently we are reviewing the underwriting limits." (actually = in fact, not now)
2. "I will assist to the board meeting on Thursday."
"I will attend the board meeting on Thursday." (assist = help someone)
3. "I am leading the region since the promotion."
"I have been leading the region since the promotion."
4. "We have closed the largest account in March."
"We closed the largest account in March." (a named finished month takes the past simple)
