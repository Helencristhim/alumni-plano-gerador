Chapter 3: The Code

How English Says No Politely

Soft openings, would and might — and when to stand your ground

Grammar Discovery

What Makes a No Easier to Hear?

"You are wrong." → "I see your point, but I’m not sure the numbers work."

"That is too risky." → "That might be too risky for our working capital."

"Take a smaller stake." → "I would suggest taking a smaller stake."

"No." → "With respect, I have to disagree on the payment terms."

The blue sentences and the red ones say the same thing. What did English add to the red ones? Look for three tools: an opening that accepts something first, a modal verb that makes the claim less absolute, and a phrase that is firm but polite.

ToolFormExample
Soft openingI see your point, but ... / I take your point, but ...I see your point, but the terms worry me.
Tentative disagreementI’m not sure (that) ... / I’m not convinced (that) ...I’m not sure that our cash can carry it.
Would and mightwould + suggest / say / prefer; might + beI would suggest a consortium. That might be difficult.
Partial agreementUp to a point, ... / To some extent, ...Up to a point, you’re right.
Your view, as a viewIt seems to me that ... / As I see it, ...As I see it, the risk is all on our side.
Firm but politeWith respect, I have to disagree (on ...)With respect, I have to disagree on safety.

In one line: accept something first, soften the claim with would, might or not sure — and never open with you are wrong.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Question, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one charter

Dialogue

The Bid My Uncle Wanted

S
Tell me about a serious conflict with somebody senior to you, and tell me how it ended.
F
In 2021 my uncle, who founded the company, wanted to bid for a large public hospital. I thought the payment terms were too risky for us, and we clashed about it for a week.
S
How did you tell him that you disagreed? He was the founder.
F
Not very well, at first. Our first conversation got heated. The second time I heard him out, and then I said: I see your point, but I am not sure our cash can carry a six-month delay.
S
And where did the two of you find common ground?
F
We both wanted the hospital. The sticking point was the risk, so I suggested that we bid as part of a consortium, with a bigger partner and a smaller stake.
S
Did he accept that?
F
He met me halfway. We won a third of the contract, and when the payments were late, the bigger partner carried the delay. On public contracts in general, we have agreed to disagree.
Comprehension

About Susan

1. What two things does Susan ask for in her first question?
A serious conflict with somebody senior, and how it ended.
2. Why does she ask how he told his uncle that he disagreed?
Because his uncle was the founder — somebody senior, where the words matter more.
3. After he describes the conflict, what does she ask about?
Where they found common ground, and whether his uncle accepted his proposal.
Listening 1 · An Interviewer

The Two Ways to Fail This Question

An admissions interviewer on what she listens for in the conflict question. Sound first — no text.

1. Which two kinds of candidates fall into the trap?
The ones who say they have never had a conflict, and the ones who describe a conflict they won and call the other person an idiot.
2. Why does she want to hear the words the candidate actually used?
Because the words show whether he can disagree without making an enemy.
3. What does she say about the ending?
She wants it even if it was not happy. Sometimes the best answer is: we never agreed, but we found a way to work together.
Listening 2 · A Project Director

The Architect and the Glass Facade

A project director on a long disagreement with an architect. Sound first — no text.

1. What did they disagree about, and why?
A glass facade on the west side. She wanted it, and he knew the budget could not carry it.
2. What did he change in the third meeting?
He asked her to take him through the design from the beginning and did not interrupt her once, for forty minutes.
3. How did it end?
A supplier twenty percent cheaper and a simpler frame. Neither got what they wanted at the start; both got something better.
Real Document

The Rules for Your Study Group

LEARNING TEAM CHARTER — Executive MBA
To be read and signed by every member of your learning team in week one

1. Disagree with the idea, never with the person.

2. Hear every member out before you answer. Nobody is interrupted in the first two minutes.

3. Criticism must be constructive: every “no” comes with a suggestion.

4. If two members are at odds for more than one week, ask the program office to help.

5. When the team cannot agree, record both views in the report. It is acceptable to agree to disagree.

Learning teams are assigned by the school and mix at least four countries and four industries.
1. What does rule 3 say every “no” must come with?
A suggestion — the criticism has to be constructive.
2. When should a team ask the program office for help?
When two members have been at odds for more than one week.
3. What happens when the team cannot agree?
Both views are recorded in the report; it is acceptable to agree to disagree.
