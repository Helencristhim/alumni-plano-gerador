How the Work Changed

Everything used to be on paper, and a credit decision took eleven days. English has two verbs for telling that story, and neither of them is the past simple.

Today’s Goal

A Story, Not a List of Dates

1. The Words

to evolve, a shift, to expand, to replace, to automate, a decade, paperwork...

2. Two Forms

used to, for what was true then and is not now · would, for what you did again and again

3. Your Story

how you started, what changed, and what you would keep

In Portuguese one tense does all of this. In English there are three, and the difference between them is exactly the difference between a report and a story.

One Question

What Would You Keep?

Name one thing about the way this work used to be done that you would bring back tomorrow if you could.

And one thing that disappeared and that you are glad is gone.

Chapter 3: The Code

Used To, and Would

The two forms Portuguese does not have

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"We used to do everything on paper."

"I would drive out to a farm every Tuesday."

"There used to be a filing cabinet where that desk is."

"In 2013 I moved into credit."

Three of these describe the old normal and one describes a single day. Now the harder question: the blue one could be swapped for the red form. Could the third sentence go the other way?

JobFormExample
A habit that stoppedused to + base verbWe used to do everything on paper.
A state that is no longer trueused to + be / have / knowThere used to be a filing cabinet there.
A repeated action, in a storywould + base verbI would drive out to a farm every Tuesday.
The limit of wouldwould does NOT work with statesNot I would be an analyst. Say I used to be an analyst.
One finished event, with a datethe past simpleIn 2013 I moved into credit.
Negative and questiondid not use to · did you use to ... ?I did not use to handle such large accounts.
The trapthe -d only survives in the positiveI used to drive · but: did you use to drive?

In one line: used to covers habits and states. Would covers habits only, and it needs a story around it.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Somebody Who Was Here Before You

One conversation, two recordings, one page

Dialogue

Two People Who Remember the Same Thing

R
Thirty years I have been doing this, and I still cannot get used to how fast a file moves now. How long have you been in credit?
L
Eight years in credit, longer in the company. It was not always like this, though. When I started, half of this office was filing cabinets.
R
Ours too. We used to keep every signed contract in a room with a lock on it, and once a year somebody had to go in and check nothing had gone missing.
L
And the driving. Back in the day I would spend two days a week on the road, going out to farms. Now I see more customers in a morning than I used to see in a month.
R
Do you miss it? I ask everyone that, and the answer tells me more about them than their job title does.
L
I do not miss the paperwork. But when you sat in somebody’s kitchen for two hours, you learned things the system will never tell you.
R
That is exactly it. We automated the wrong half. The forms needed automating. The kitchen table did not.
L
Although I would not go back. The transition was uncomfortable, but a decision that used to take eleven days takes me forty minutes now.
R
Nobody wants the folder back. What people miss is the two-hour drive home, when you had time to think about what you had just seen.
L
Now you have said something I will think about all week. When you look back on thirty years, what changed that nobody talks about?
Comprehension

About Ruth

1. How long has Ruth been in this business, and what does she say she still cannot get used to?
Thirty years. She still cannot get used to how fast a file moves now.
2. What does she say her company used to do with signed contracts?
They kept every signed contract in a room with a lock on it, and once a year somebody had to check that nothing had gone missing.
3. She says one question tells her more about a person than their job title. What is the question, and what is her own conclusion about automation?
The question is whether they miss the old way. Her conclusion is that they automated the wrong half: the forms needed automating, the kitchen table did not.
Listening 1 · Since 1988

Two Hours for One Signature

Somebody who started in this sector in 1988. Sound first — no text.

1. What did a normal Tuesday look like for him, and how often did he do it?
He would drive out to a farm with a folder, sit at somebody's kitchen table for two hours, and drive back with a signature. He did that four times a week.
2. What does he say he does not miss, and what does he miss?
He does not miss the paper at all, but he does miss the kitchen table.
3. What did those two hours teach him that a system cannot, and what one thing would he keep?
He would see whether the machinery was maintained and notice that the son had left. He would keep the drive home, when he had two hours to think about what he had just seen.
Listening 2 · Three Decades

The Smaller Half of the Story

A sector analyst on what actually changed in thirty years. Sound first — no text.

1. What do people say the big change was, and what does she argue instead?
People talk about technology. She argues that is the smaller half of the story, and that the bigger change is who carries the risk.
2. How was risk spread thirty years ago, and what happened to it?
The manufacturer sold to a distributor, the distributor to the farm, and each carried their own piece. The chain compressed, companies expanded, some of the middle disappeared, and the risk now sits on one or two balance sheets.
3. Where did credit teams used to sit in the process, and where do they sit now?
They used to be an administrative function at the end. Now they sit close to the beginning, because deciding who gets product is deciding what the company is exposed to.
