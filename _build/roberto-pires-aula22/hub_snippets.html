Stage 1.1: Words for the Deal

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. These eight words are on every receipt and in every market.

Refund -- the money the store gives back when you return something
"Can I get a refund, please?"
To exchange -- to give an item back and take a different one instead
"I would like to exchange this shirt for a larger one."
Faulty -- broken, or not working the way it should
"The zipper is faulty: it does not close."
Tag -- the small label on a new item, with the price on it
"Please keep the tag on the shirt."
Bargain -- something you buy for much less than the normal price
"Forty-five euros for two bags was a real bargain."
On sale -- sold at a lower price than usual, for a short time
"Every coat is on sale this week."
Return policy -- the rules of a store about bringing items back
"Excuse me, what is your return policy?"
To haggle -- to talk about the price until the two of you agree
"At the market, people haggle every day."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the situation and answer the questions.

At the market, the tag on the leather bag says sixty euros. Roberto smiles and asks: "How about forty?" The seller says fifty. Roberto tries again: "What about forty-five, and I take two?" The seller thinks for a second and says: "That works for me." A real bargain. Later, at the clothes store, Roberto puts a shirt on the counter. "I'd like to exchange this, please. It is the wrong size." The woman looks at the receipt and asks: "A refund, or an exchange?" Roberto chooses the exchange. Everything in the store is on sale this week, and the return policy gives him thirty days. Nothing is faulty here: he simply needs a larger size.

1. What was Roberto's second offer at the market?
A Sixty euros for one bag.
B Forty-five euros, if he took two bags.
C Fifty euros, and nothing more.
2. Why did Roberto go to the counter with the shirt?
A Because the shirt was faulty.
B Because he wanted his money back.
C Because it was the wrong size and he wanted another one.
3. What kind of word comes after "How about" and "What about"?
A The word "to" and then a verb.
B A noun, or a verb ending in -ing.
C Always a question word.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- How About & What About

Grammar

Use How about or What about to make an offer or a suggestion. They mean the same thing, and you can use them one after the other in the same conversation. After about you put a noun ("How about forty euros?") or a verb with -ing ("How about taking two?"). Never put "to" there: "How about to take two" does not exist in English. To accept, say That works for me. To say no politely, say I'm afraid I can't do that.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best sentence for each situation.

The tag says sixty euros and you want to pay less. What do you say?
A "How about to pay forty euros?"
B "How about forty euros for the bag?"
C "What about of forty euros?"
The seller says no to your price. You want to offer an action instead. What do you say?
A "What about I take two?"
B "What about taking two for the same price?"
C "What about to take two?"
You bought a bag yesterday and the zipper does not close. What do you say at the counter?
A "This bag is faulty. Can I get a refund, please?"
B "This bag is on sale. Give me the money."
C "This bag is a bargain. I want the tag."
The shop assistant offers you store credit, but you want your money back. What do you say?
A "No. I am afraid of you."
B "I'm afraid I can't do that. What about a refund?"
C "That works for me, thank you."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record your answer. There is no right or wrong answer here — speak in full sentences.
