Chapter 3: Bigger, Faster, Half

A Number Compared

Is an argument, not just information

Grammar Discovery

Four Sentences, Four Numbers

"Brazil adopted instant payments faster than almost any country."

"A cross-border payment can cost several times more than a domestic one."

"Small companies are the fastest-growing segment in the market."

"Two corridors carry almost half of the volume."

Every sentence has a number or an amount in it. What is each red phrase doing to that number, and which one talks about only one part of the whole?

What you want to doFormExample
Compare two thingsshort adjective + -er than · more + long adjective + thanfaster than · more predictable than

The trapnever two comparatives togetherNot more faster, not the most fast. Say faster, the fastest.
The other trapsuperlative + in, not of, for a placeNot the biggest of the world. Say the biggest in the world.

In one line: one number, one comparison, one pause — that is how a number becomes an argument.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

You Have the First Word

One panel, three recordings, and the card you bring on stage

Dialogue

Four Minutes in Miami

S
Fabio, you have the first word. Why should anybody in this room be thinking about Brazil?
F
Let me set the scene with one number. Brazil adopted instant payments faster than almost any country in the world. Today most Brazilian adults pay each other in seconds.
S
That is a great sound bite. But cross-border is a different story, isn't it?
F
It is, and I'm glad you brought it up. Inside the country, a payment takes seconds. Across the border, the same company can wait two days and pay several times more in fees. That gap is the opportunity.
S
Let me push you a little. Some would argue that Brazil's regulation makes it too complex for global partners. How do you respond?
F
That's an important point, and I'd push back a little on it. The regulation is detailed, yes, but it is more predictable than most people think. For a partner, predictable is worth more than simple.
S
So what is your talking point for the partners in this room?
F
Start with one corridor. Brazil trades more with China than with any other country, and the United States is second. Those two corridors are where a partner learns the fastest.
S
We have thirty seconds left. A closing remark?
F
Brazil is not the easiest market in this room. It may be the one that grows the fastest, once the border stops being slower than the country.
Comprehension

About Sofia

1. What does Sofia ask Fabio first, and what does she say about his answer?
She asks why anybody in the room should think about Brazil. She calls his answer a great sound bite, but says cross-border is a different story.
2. What hard question does Sofia ask, and how does she introduce it?
She says some would argue that Brazil's regulation makes it too complex for global partners. She introduces it with "Let me push you a little."
3. How much time does Sofia give him at the end, and for what?
Thirty seconds, for a closing remark.
Listening 1 · The Pause

Say the Number, Then Stop

A presentation coach on how numbers land. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the most common mistake people make with a number?
They say the number and rush to the next sentence, as if they were embarrassed by it.
2. How long does the audience need, and what should you do in that time?
About one second. Stop, count to one in your head, and then say what the number means.
3. What does she say about comparisons and about precision?
One comparison per sentence, not two. And round your numbers: nobody remembers forty-seven point three percent, everybody remembers almost half.
Listening 2 · From the Audience

One Minute to Decide

A European partnerships director on what she listens for. Sound first — no text.

1. How quickly does she decide about a speaker, and what are the three things she listens for?
In about a minute. The size of the opportunity in one number, the problem stated honestly, and what it means for her.
2. What makes her stop believing a speaker, and what makes her take notes?
She stops believing when a speaker says the market has no challenges. She takes notes when a speaker says the regulation is complex but predictable.
3. What did the best Brazilian speaker she heard last year say, and what did she do?
Start with one corridor, we will give you the data, and in six months you will know. She called him the following week.
Listening 3 · From the Chair

The Hard Question Is a Gift

A moderator after a hundred panels. Sound first — no text.

1. What should an opening statement give the moderator, and what happens if you give five?
One talking point he can come back to. If you give five, he picks the one you care about least.
2. When somebody says your market is too risky, what is the room waiting for?
Not for you to win the argument, but to see if you stay calm. Acknowledge it, answer with one fact, and go back to your talking point.
3. What does he say about the closing remark?
Keep it under twenty seconds, because the last sentence is the one they remember.
