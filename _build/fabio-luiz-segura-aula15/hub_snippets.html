Vocabulary

A panel moderator -- the person who introduces the speakers, asks the questions and controls the time
"The panel moderator gave each of us four minutes, and she meant four."
A panelist -- one of the speakers sitting on the stage in a panel discussion
"Four panelists, four countries, and I was the only one from Latin America."
An opening statement -- the first short speech each speaker gives before the questions start
"My opening statement had one number, one problem and one invitation."
A talking point -- one main idea you prepare in advance and keep coming back to
"Whatever they asked, I came back to the same talking point: start with one corridor."
A sound bite -- a short, memorable sentence that people repeat after they hear it
"Money moves in seconds, paperwork moves in days. That was the sound bite of the panel."
The Q&A -- the part of a session when the audience asks the speakers questions
"The opening statement was easy. The Q&A was where the real conversation started."
A closing remark -- the final sentence or two a speaker says before the session ends
"Keep your closing remark under twenty seconds. It is the sentence they remember."
To bring up -- to introduce a subject into a conversation or a presentation
"I'm glad you brought up regulation. It is the question everybody in the room is thinking."
To set the scene -- to give the background the audience needs before your main point
"Let me set the scene with one number, and then I will tell you why it matters to you."
To land a message -- to say something so that the audience understands it and remembers it
"I said the number, paused for one second, and the message landed."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

On a panel, nobody remembers ten numbers. People remember one, and they remember it because it was compared to something. Brazil has more than two hundred million people is information. Brazil adopted instant payments faster than almost any country in the world is an argument.

The grammar of that argument is the grammar of comparison. To compare two things, a short adjective takes -er than (faster than, cheaper than) and a long one takes more ... than (more predictable than). To say how big the difference is, you add a number: twice as fast as, or several times more expensive than. For the extreme, you use the -est or the most, followed by in for a place: one of the largest systems in the world, never of the world.

Proportions do the same work with a part of the whole: almost half of the volume, a third of our clients, one in eight payments. And the honest words around a number — almost, about, more than, roughly — are not weakness. They are how professionals round, and rounded numbers are the ones an audience can repeat.

The last rule is not grammar. Say the number, then stop for one second. The audience needs that second to understand what the number means. Rush past it, and the most important sentence of your opening statement becomes background noise.

1. According to the text, what turns a number into an argument?
A Saying many numbers, one after the other.
B Comparing it to something.
C Saying it very precisely, with decimals.
2. Which sentence follows the rule for the extreme?
A One of the largest systems in the world.
B One of the most large systems of the world.
C One of the more larger systems in the world.
3. What does the text say about words like almost, about and roughly?
A They make the speaker sound unsure.
B They should never be used with data.
C They are how professionals round, and rounded numbers are easier to repeat.

GRAMMAR

One number, one comparison, one pause.

Stage 2: Put the Opening Statement in Order

Order

You have four minutes on a panel about emerging markets. Put your opening statement in a logical order.

?"Thank you. Let me set the scene with one number."
?"Brazil adopted instant payments faster than almost any country in the world."
?"Across the border, the same payment can take two days and cost several times more."
?"The regulation is detailed, but it is more predictable than most people think."
?"So my suggestion to partners is simple: start with one corridor."
?"Brazil is not the easiest market in this room, but it may be the fastest-growing."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The moderator asks why the audience should care about Brazil. You open with:
A Brazil is a very big country with a lot of potential and many opportunities.
B Let me set the scene with one number. Brazil adopted instant payments faster than almost any country in the world.
C Brazil is more bigger than many countries of the world.
You want to compare domestic and cross-border payments. You say:
A Domestic payments are two times more fast than cross-border ones.
B Domestic payments are the most fast of the world.
C Domestic payments are much faster than cross-border ones: seconds against two days.
A panelist says Brazil is too complex for global partners. You say:
A That's an important point. The regulation is detailed, but it is more predictable than most people think.
B That is wrong. Brazil is not complex.
C Yes, you are right, it is too complex.
The moderator gives you thirty seconds for a closing remark. You say:
A Let me explain the ten main points of our strategy again.
B Brazil is not the easiest market in this room, but it may be the fastest-growing.
C Thank you, that is all, no more comments.

Talk
Chris Anderson — “TED's secret to great public speaking” (TED, 8 min)

The head of TED explains the one thing every good talk does: it builds a single idea in the listener's mind, step by step. It is the same principle as one talking point on a panel, from the person who has watched more talks than anybody.

Tip: write his main idea as one sentence. Then check your own opening statement: does it build one idea, or five?
