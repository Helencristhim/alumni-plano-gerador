Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

Must, Have To, Should -- Rules, Advice and What You Do Not Have To Do

Explaining rules, giving advice and asking for things politely. Key words: strict, to warn, a shortcut, on purpose, to skip, to bother, a stakeholder, a guideline. Structure: modal verbs of obligation and advice (have to, must, must not, do not have to, should, can), and the trap where "must not" and "do not have to" mean opposite things.

On purpose -- not by accident, because you decided to
"She wore new shoes on purpose, because they looked good."
To skip -- to not do something you normally do
"I shouldn't skip breakfast before the gym."
To bother -- to make the effort to do something
"Don't bother calling, just come over."
A stakeholder -- a person with an interest in the result of a project
"The main stakeholder checks the logo personally."

"The guidelines say we must not change the logo colour."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The flat in Lisbon comes with a short list of rules. Guests have to leave the keys in the box downstairs, and they must not take them to the airport, because somebody did that in March and it cost everybody an afternoon. You can have friends over, but you must not have parties: the neighbours are strict about noise. The rubbish goes out on Tuesday nights, so you do not have to do it every day. And you do not have to clean before you leave, because the host does that.

At work this week the list is shorter but heavier. We must not change the logo colour, not even a little, and the main stakeholder checks it personally. On the other hand, we do not have to use their font in social posts, which everybody assumes we have to. My advice is that we should send the layouts on Thursday instead of Friday. If they see the work early, they can ask for small changes instead of big ones. And when I write to the client, I never say you must send me the file. I say could you send me the file today?, because in English the first one sounds like an order.

1. What is the difference between "you must not have parties" and "you do not have to clean"?
A The first is forbidden. The second is optional, and nobody is asking for it.
B They mean the same thing, one is just more formal.
C The first is advice. The second is a rule.
2. Why does the text say "we should send the layouts on Thursday" and not "we must send"?
A Because Thursday is a rule written in the guidelines.
B Because it is a recommendation, not an obligation.
C Because "must" cannot be used with the word layouts.
3. Why does she write "could you send me the file today?" to a client?
A Because "you must send me the file" is grammatically wrong.
B Because clients do not understand the word must.
C Because "you must" pointed at another person sounds like an order in English.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Rule, Advice or Free Choice

GRAMMAR

Three levels of pressure, and one pair that traps everybody.

FormMeaningExample
have toObligation from outside: a rule, a law, somebody else's decision.I have to be there at six.
She has to confirm the seats.
mustObligation you feel yourself, or a written rule in a notice.I must stop working at midnight.
must not (mustn't)Forbidden. Do not do it.You must not change the logo.
do not have to (don't have to)Optional. You can if you want. This is NOT the same as must not.You don't have to bring food.
should / shouldn'tAdvice and recommendation. The softest, and the one you will use most.You should bring water.
You shouldn't skip breakfast.
can / can'tPermission and ability.You can use the kitchen.
I can't hear you.
The formEvery modal is followed by the base verb: no to and no -s. Say "she should send", never "she shoulds to send". Questions invert: Do I have to...? Should I...? Can I...?

One warning about tone: you must pointed at another person sounds like an order in English. With a client or a colleague, ask instead: Could you...? That single change is the difference between sounding firm and sounding rude.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are explaining rules and giving advice. Choose the best thing to say.

Bringing food is optional, because there is a restaurant. You say:
A "You don't have to bring food, there's a place at the top."
B "You mustn't bring food, there's a place at the top."
C "You don't must to bring food."
Changing the logo colour is completely forbidden. You say:
A "We don't have to change the logo colour."
B "We mustn't change the logo colour."
C "We shouldn't to change the logo colour."
You need a file from the client by today, and you want to sound professional. You write:
A "You must send me the file today."
B "Could you send me the file today, please?"
C "You have to send the file, it is a rule."
You want to recommend Thursday, without making it an obligation. You say:
A "I think we should send the layouts on Thursday."
B "We must send the layouts on Thursday."
C "We shoulds send the layouts on Thursday."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is correcting this one.

A new colleague joins your team today and asks how things work. Speak for about ninety seconds. Explain two rules nobody can break, two things that are only advice, and one thing everybody assumes is obligatory but is not. Use have to, must not, do not have to and should at least once each. Watch your tone: with people, "could you" is friendlier than "you must". If a sentence breaks in the middle, say "let me start that again" out loud and keep going.
