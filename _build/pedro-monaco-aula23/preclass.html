Lesson 23 -- Pre-class

The Deal That Fell Through

A deal falls through two days before signing, and Pedro joins the post-mortem call with the US office. Key expressions: a post-mortem, to drop the ball, to fall through, to second-guess, a wake-up call, to learn the hard way, to dwell on, to move on, water under the bridge, to take on board, to fall short of, food for thought, to bear the brunt of, to make amends. Structure: wish, if only, would rather and it's time -- I wish we had, if only they would, I'd rather you didn't, it's time we changed.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to the conversation after something goes wrong: the mistake, the regret, and moving on.

A post-mortem -- a meeting after a project or deal to understand what went wrong and what to change
"We'll hold a short post-mortem on Monday, and nobody is on trial."
To drop the ball -- to make a mistake by not doing something you were responsible for
"Somebody dropped the ball on the background check, and we found out too late."
To fall through -- (of a plan or deal) to fail to happen
"The Houston deal fell through two days before signing."
To second-guess -- to keep questioning a decision after it has been made
"Stop second-guessing the timeline; we chose it together."
A wake-up call -- an event that suddenly shows you there is a serious problem
"Losing the client was a wake-up call for the whole legal team."
To learn the hard way -- to learn something by making a painful mistake
"I learned the hard way that a verbal promise is not a contract."
To dwell on -- to keep thinking or talking about something unpleasant for too long
"Let's not dwell on the mistake; let's fix the process."
To move on -- to stop thinking about something in the past and start something new
"We lost the deal, but it's time we moved on to the next one."
Water under the bridge -- something in the past that is no longer important or worth arguing about
"The argument with their lawyers is water under the bridge now."
To take on board -- to understand advice or criticism and start acting on it
"I've taken your comments on board, and I've rewritten the checklist."
To fall short of -- to fail to reach a level or standard that was expected
"The final offer fell short of what the board had approved."
Food for thought -- an idea that makes you think seriously about something
"Her comment about the checklist gave me real food for thought."
To bear the brunt of -- to suffer the worst part of something bad
"The sales team bore the brunt of the client's anger."
To make amends -- to do something good to show you are sorry for a mistake
"We made amends with a discount on the next two contracts."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

On Friday, the deal with a distributor in Houston fell through, two days before signing. On Monday, Laura Brooks, from the US office, calls Pedro for a short post-mortem. Laura starts with a regret: "If only I hadn't promised the board a closing date in June." Pedro takes his part too: "I wish we had started due diligence earlier." But he does not want the call to become a list of complaints. "I'd rather we didn't blame anybody," he says. "The sales team worked on this client for six months, and I wish someone would tell them it wasn't their fault." Then he proposes a change: "It's time we checked every intermediary before the term sheet, not after." Laura agrees, and the call ends with a plan instead of a problem.

1. Laura says If only I hadn't promised the board a closing date. What is she talking about?
A A regret about something she did in the past.
B Something she is planning to promise the board before the next closing date in June.
C A promise that she is still able to change before the meeting.
2. Why does Pedro say I wish someone WOULD tell them, with would?
A Because he wants another person to do something that is not happening.
B Because the sentence is about a regret from six months ago, when the sales team started.
C Because would is more polite in a call with somebody from the US office.
3. Which sentence is a polite preference about what OTHER people do?
A I'd rather we didn't blame anybody.
B I wish we had started due diligence earlier, before the term sheet was signed.
C It's time we checked every intermediary before the term sheet, not after.
4. What tense comes after it's time we?
A The past simple, as in it's time we checked.
B The present simple, because the change is going to happen now, in the future.
C The past perfect, as in it's time we had checked every intermediary.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Wish, If Only, Would Rather and It's Time

GRAMMAR

To talk about something that is not real, English takes one step back in time: the past for now, the past perfect for the past.

PatternWhen to use itExample
wish + past simpleA situation now that you would like to be different.I wish we had more time.
wish + past perfectA regret about the past.I wish I had read the side letter.
wish + would + base formYou want another person to change. Often annoyed.I wish they would answer our emails.
if only + the same tensesThe same as wish, but stronger.If only we had known about the lawsuit.
would rather + subject + past simpleA polite preference about another person's action.I'd rather you didn't send it yet.
it's time + subject + past simpleSomething should already be happening.It's time we updated the checklist.

One habit for this week: every time you think about something you would change, say it in English with I wish. Then add the change: it's time we... And never say I wish I would have known; the regret is I wish I had known.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are in a post-mortem meeting. Choose the version a professional would actually use.

1. Your manager asks what went wrong with the deal. You want to take responsibility as a team:
A I wish we had started due diligence earlier.
B I wish we would start the due diligence earlier, because that was the problem with this deal.
C I want that we started due diligence earlier than we did on this deal.
2. A colleague keeps talking about a mistake from last year. You want to stop it politely:
A Let's not dwell on it. It's water under the bridge now.
B Let's dwell on it a little more, because it's water under the bridge for everybody now.
C Please stop it, it's not important, and nobody here wants to hear about it again.
3. Your colleague wants to call the client today, but you think it is too early:
A I'd rather you didn't call them today.
B I'd rather you don't call them today, because I think it's still a bit too early for that.
C I'd rather not that you call them today, because it is too early for a call.
4. The same problem has happened three times. You want to propose a change:
A It's time we changed the process.
B It's time we change the process, because the same problem has happened three times this year.
C It's the time that we will change the process after this third problem.
5. Your boss gave you feedback last week, and you have already acted on it:
A I've taken your comments on board.
B I've taken your comments on the board, and I have already shown them to everybody.
C I've taken your comments with me, and I will think about them next week.
