Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read it in context. These are the words for a retrospective: looking back at what was built, and forward to what comes next.

Portfolio -- a collection of work that shows a person's skills and development
"Her portfolio includes an essay, two supplements and a formal analysis."
Retrospective -- an exhibition or review that looks back over a body of work
"Today is a small retrospective of twelve lessons."
Synthesis -- the combination of separate ideas into a connected whole
"The final essay is a synthesis of everything she has practiced."
Culmination -- the highest or final point of a process
"The mock interview is the culmination of the program."
Benchmark -- a standard used to measure progress or quality
"Her Cambridge Reading score is the benchmark for today."
Milestone -- an important stage or event in a process
"Finishing the personal statement was a milestone."
Cumulative -- increasing by adding one part to another over time
"The effect of twelve lessons is cumulative."
Consolidate -- to make knowledge or skills stronger and more lasting
"The review helps consolidate what she learned."
Nuance -- a very small but important difference in meaning or expression
"C2 lives in nuance, not in rare words."
Versatility -- the ability to adapt to many different tasks or styles
"Her versatility lets her move from essay to interview."
Calibrate -- to adjust something carefully so that it is accurate
"She learned to calibrate her claims to her evidence."
Rehang -- to rearrange how the works in a museum are displayed
"The museum rehang placed Tarsila beside Picasso."
Narrative arc -- the shape of a story from beginning to turning point to resolution
"The personal statement has a clear narrative arc."
Hindsight -- understanding an event only after it has happened
"In hindsight, the museum guard was the first reader of her essay."
Unflagging -- never becoming tired or weaker
"Her unflagging curiosity carried her through twelve lessons."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Twelve lessons ago, this program began with a claim: that the difference between fluent English and precise English is not a matter of rare vocabulary but of choices. Every lesson since has added one choice to the list, and it is worth seeing them together.

Some of those choices make prose denser. His refusal to decorate the wall turns an action into a subject that can be evaluated. A deceptively simple composition uses a single adverb to hold a contrast. It is the light that calls Matthew points the reader at what matters. Others give a sentence rhetorical shape. Only later did I understand lifts a reflective moment through inversion, and trained in Paris, admired at home and ignored abroad, she became makes the reader wait for the point.

A third group governs the relationship between writer and reader. While the museum has cared for the sculptures, their removal remains contested concedes before it disagrees. The objection is not unreasonable acknowledges an opposing view with restraint. Conduct research on rather than make research about shows that the writer has listened to how academic English actually sounds.

Two lessons pulled in the opposite direction, and they matter just as much. In speech, markers such as the short answer is and that said guide a listener who cannot reread. In revision, decided replaces made a decision, and empty openings disappear. Density, in other words, is a tool, not a virtue.

The final skill is not in any single lesson. It is the judgment to choose, for each sentence, the one move it needs and no more. A paragraph that displays every technique is a catalog. A paragraph that uses exactly the right one is a voice.

A Precise English uses more rare vocabulary.
B It is a matter of choices, not of rare words.

2. Why does the writer say two lessons pulled in the opposite direction?
A Because they taught informal English only.
