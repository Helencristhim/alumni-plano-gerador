The Page You Take Into the Room

CLOSING PLAN
ninety seconds · six moves · no new facts
Prepared byAna Ladeia Tavares
1. Open withwhat nobody disputes
2. The throughlinenobody decided to do this
3. Concedethe delay in raising it — say it first
4. The weightnot once in two years did anybody...
5. The askall she is asking for is one line
6. Wrap upone sentence, then stop talking
Six lines, not six paragraphs. A plan you can read at a glance is a plan you can still use when you have lost your place. Do not labour any of them, and do not add a seventh: the room stops listening at ninety seconds whether you have finished or not.
1. Why does the plan put the concession at number three rather than at the end?
Because saying the weak point first, before the other side does, buys credibility for everything that comes after it.
2. Why six lines rather than six paragraphs?
Because a plan you can read at a glance is one you can still use after you have lost your place, which is exactly when you need it.
3. Say lines four and five out loud as full sentences, using the structures the plan is pointing at.
Not once in two years did anybody tell her what had happened. All she is asking for is one line on the copyright page.
Listening 1 · The Advocacy Coach

Ninety Seconds, No New Facts

An advocacy coach on what the last speech is for. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say a closing argument is not, and what is the difficulty that creates?
It is not the place for new facts. The difficulty is that everything you can say has already been said, so the only thing left to choose is the order and the weight.
2. What does she tell people to do with their weakest point?
Concede it in the first minute, before the other side reaches it. She says a point conceded early costs almost nothing, and the same point taken from you costs the room's trust.
3. What does she say about people who are nervous?
That poise is a skill and not a temperament. She says the two things that make somebody look poised are a plan short enough to read at a glance and the willingness to stop talking early.
Listening 2 · The Tenth Simulation

The Difference Between the First and the Tenth

A law student on what changed between his first mock mediation and his tenth. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say did NOT change between the first simulation and the tenth?
His vocabulary and his nerves. He says he knew almost the same words on the first day, and that he was just as nervous in the tenth.
2. What does he say did change, and how did he notice it?
He stopped abandoning sentences halfway through. He noticed because somebody was counting, and the number went from eleven in the first session to two in the tenth.
3. What does he say to people who think they need more vocabulary?
That almost nobody in his group needed more words. What they needed was to finish the sentence they had already started, and that is a habit rather than a level.
