Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Where the World Reaches You

Where your picture of the world actually comes from, and how to say what you know apart from what you are guessing. Key expressions: to keep abreast of, well-informed, a vested interest, a blind spot, to be hard-pressed to, to take with a grain of salt, an outlet, coverage, to break a story, spin, a talking point, to weigh in on, hearsay, to follow closely, at face value, a sweeping statement, to be swayed by, in hindsight. Structure: modal perfects for speculation about the past — must have, may well have, can't have, should have, would have.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

To keep abreast of -- to stay properly informed about something as it develops
"I try to keep abreast of the European elections, not only ours."
Well-informed -- knowing what is actually going on, not only the headlines
"He is the most well-informed person at that table, and the quietest."
A vested interest -- a personal reason to want one particular outcome
"Every side in that debate has a vested interest, so read all of them."
A blind spot -- something you consistently fail to notice, without knowing it
"Africa is a blind spot in almost everything I read."
To be hard-pressed to -- to find something so difficult that you probably will not manage it
"You would be hard-pressed to name a decade with more upheaval than that one."
To take with a grain of salt -- to treat a claim with healthy doubt instead of believing it outright
"A poll a year before an election is something I take with a grain of salt."
An outlet -- a newspaper, channel or site that publishes the news
"I follow four outlets, and two of them disagree with me on purpose."
Coverage -- the amount and the kind of attention the press gives a story
"The coverage lasted three days and then the story simply disappeared."
To break a story -- to be the first to publish something important
"A small regional paper broke the story and the national ones followed."
Spin -- the favorable version somebody puts on the plain facts
"Strip the spin away and the number underneath is still bad."
A talking point -- a subject people keep coming back to in conversation
"That one speech became the talking point of the entire week."
To weigh in on -- to add your opinion to a discussion that is already running
"I rarely weigh in on something I have read about only once."
Hearsay -- information you cannot check because it came to you second-hand
"Most of what circulates on those groups is hearsay with a photograph attached."
To follow closely -- to pay continuous and careful attention to something
"I have followed American politics closely since the nineties."
At face value -- accepting something exactly as it appears, without questioning it
"Nobody should take a government statement at face value."
A sweeping statement -- a claim so general that it stops being accurate
"Saying that all politicians are the same is a sweeping statement."
To be swayed by -- to have your opinion moved by an argument or by a person
"I was swayed by that piece, and I did not expect to be."
In hindsight -- judging now, with knowledge that nobody had at the time
"In hindsight, the warning signs were all there in March."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He is well-informed in the way that takes thirty years and no shortcuts. Four outlets every morning, two of which he chose precisely because they argue against him, and a habit of reading the ones he distrusts first. He would tell you it is not virtue. It is that he has been caught out too often by hearsay that arrived looking like a fact.

Last month a small regional paper broke a story about three public contracts. By Thursday the national coverage was everywhere; by Friday a minister had gone. He read all of it and took almost none of it at face value. The documents must have been sitting there since March, he said, so somebody can't have been reading them. And the officials involved may well have known for months.

What interests him is not the scandal but the spin. Every side has a vested interest, and the same three numbers can be arranged into two opposite stories. He is hard-pressed to remember a single week when that was not true. So he follows the story closely for a fortnight, and only then does he weigh in on it — usually at a dinner, usually when somebody makes a sweeping statement he cannot let stand.

He is honest about the rest. Africa is a blind spot, and he knows it. Once or twice a year he is swayed by an argument he sat down intending to demolish. And there are decisions that, in hindsight, he should have seen coming and did not. He takes his own certainty with a grain of salt too. It is the only way to keep abreast of a world that keeps rearranging itself.

1. Why does the text say the documents "must have been sitting there since March"?
A Because the speaker read them in March and knows it.
B Because he is drawing a confident conclusion from evidence, not stating knowledge.
C Because it is an obligation that somebody failed to meet.
2. "Somebody can't have been reading them." What does can't have do here?
A It expresses the negative of a confident conclusion.
B It expresses a prohibition in the past.
C It says the action was physically impossible.
3. "They may well have known for months." How sure is the speaker?
A Certain — he has proof.
B Not at all — he is inventing it.
C He thinks it is likely, and he is careful not to claim proof.
4. Why does the text say he takes his own certainty "with a grain of salt"?
A Because he doubts his own conclusions on purpose, the way he doubts everyone else's.
B Because he has stopped reading the news.
C Because he never has an opinion about anything.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Perfects — the Scale of Certainty About the Past

GRAMMAR

Five ways to talk about something that already happened when you were not there to see it. The auxiliary is what tells your listener how sure you are, so choosing it is not decoration.

FormWhat it doesExample
must have
+ past participle		A confident conclusion drawn from evidence. You did not see it; you are inferring it, and you are prepared to say so out loud."The leak must have come from inside."
may / might / could haveA real possibility and nothing stronger. May well have pushes it towards likely without ever claiming proof."They may well have known for months."
can't / couldn't haveThe negative of must have. This is the one Portuguese speakers get wrong, because mustn't have feels symmetrical — and does not exist in this meaning."She can't have expected that."
should / ought to haveRetrospective judgement. The right thing was not done, and hindsight makes that visible."We should have seen it coming."
would have
+ past participle		The unreal past: what was going to be true under a condition that never came about."A week earlier, it would have changed the vote."

One structure underneath all five: modal + have + past participle. The verb after have never changes form, however far back the event is. And in speech the have reduces to 've — must've come, should've seen — which is why it disappears from the ear long before it disappears from the grammar.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a dinner with people who follow the news as closely as you do. Choose the sharpest thing to say.

Somebody says the minister knew everything from the start. You have no proof, but you think it is likely:
A "He must knew from the start."
B "He may well have known from the start — though nobody has shown it yet."
C "He mustn't have known from the start."
You are sure the story came from inside the ministry, though you did not see it happen:
A "The leak must have come from inside the ministry."
B "The leak should have come from inside the ministry."
C "The leak would come from inside the ministry."
You want to reject the idea that she anticipated the reaction:
A "She mustn't have expected that reaction."
B "She can't have expected that reaction."
C "She did not must expect that reaction."
Somebody says all coverage of that region is worthless. You disagree with the shape of the claim:
A "That is a sweeping statement. Two outlets got it right, and it is worth asking why."
B "Yes, all of it is worthless."
C "I do not weigh in on that, it is only hearsay."
You want to say that a story arrived first in a small paper:
A "A regional paper made the coverage of the story."
B "A regional paper broke the story, and the national outlets followed."
C "A regional paper took the story at face value."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Think of one story you have followed closely in the last month. In about ninety seconds, say three things: what is established beyond argument, what is spin and whose interest it serves, and what you are still only guessing. Mark the third part explicitly, with must have, may well have or can't have — the point is to be heard being careful, not to be right.
