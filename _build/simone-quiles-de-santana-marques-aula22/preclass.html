Lesson 22 -- Pre-class

Contract Negotiation -- The Redline Round

Fight a redline round in English without ever saying no: sort the boilerplate from the changes that matter, name the sticking point, and answer with a softened, hypothetical offer. New vocabulary: redline, boilerplate, sticking point, cap, carve-out, side letter, walk away, good faith, fallback, subject to contract. Grammar: "would" for hypothetical offers -- we would be prepared to, would you be willing to, we would need X before we could Y.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Redline -- a change that one side marks in the text of a draft contract
"Forty redlines came back, and thirty-five of them are standard."
Boilerplate -- the standard clauses that appear in almost every contract of that kind
"Do not spend a minute on the boilerplate; it wins you nothing."
Sticking point -- the one issue that stops the two sides from reaching an agreement
"The real sticking point is the liability cap, and everyone knows it."
Cap -- a maximum limit the parties agree on, usually on liability
"They want the cap at one times the annual fee; we cannot live with that."
Carve-out -- an exception taken out of a general rule in a contract
"We asked for a carve-out for fraud, so the cap does not cover it."
Side letter -- a short separate agreement that sits next to the main contract
"Our fallback is a side letter that covers the same ground."
Walk away -- to leave a negotiation without signing anything
"Nobody is going to walk away over one carve-out."
Good faith -- an honest intention to deal fairly and reach an agreement
"If the tone turns hard, stay in good faith and keep the door open."
Fallback -- the second position you can accept if the first one is refused
"Our fallback is a longer notice period, but we do not show it yet."
Subject to contract -- words meaning that nothing is binding until the contract is signed
"Mark every email subject to contract while the round is open."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The draft came back with forty redlines. Thirty-five of them are boilerplate, so Simone accepts them without arguing: a round that fights standard clauses dies of old age. Five changes are left, and one of them is the real sticking point -- the liability cap. The other side wants it at one times the annual fee, and her client cannot live with that. She does not say no. She says: "That would be difficult for my client. We would be prepared to move on the cap if you accepted a carve-out for fraud." Nothing has been conceded, and the other side can now see the shape of the trade. If they refuse, her fallback is a side letter that covers the same ground outside the main document, and she is not showing it today. "Would you be willing to look at a longer notice period instead?" she asks. Every email goes out marked subject to contract, and whatever the tone becomes, she stays in good faith: nobody should have to walk away over one exception.

1. Why does Simone accept thirty-five of the redlines without arguing?
A They are boilerplate, and fighting standard clauses wins nothing.
B Her client has already signed them.
C The other side refused to discuss them.
2. What does "we would be prepared to move" tell the other side?
A That the cap has already been agreed.
B That nothing is conceded yet, but there is a trade available.
C That the negotiation is over.
3. Why does she mark every email "subject to contract"?
A To show that the round is closed.
B To ask the other side for a side letter.
C So that nothing she writes binds her client before signature.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- "Would" for Hypothetical Offers

GRAMMAR

In a negotiation round the strongest lawyers almost never say no. A flat refusal ends the exchange and tells the other side nothing about what you could accept. The word would keeps the offer hypothetical: nothing is conceded, the door stays open, and the other side sees the shape of the trade. Two traps. After would the verb is always the base form ("would to move" → "would move"). And in the trade half of the sentence, after if, the verb takes the past form ("if you accepted", not "if you will accept").

FormUseExample
We would be prepared to + base verboffer a move without conceding it"We would be prepared to move on the cap."
Would you be willing to + base verbinvite a trade, never order one"Would you be willing to look at sixty days?"
That would be difficultrefuse without the word no"That would be difficult for my client."
We would need X before we could Yname your price for saying yes"We would need exclusivity before we could sign."
... if you accepted / agreedkeep the trade hypothetical"... if you accepted a carve-out for fraud."

Key point: "We would be prepared to move on the cap if you accepted a carve-out" holds exactly the same line as "No, we cannot accept that cap" -- and hands the other side a map at the same time.

Stage 2: Put the Round in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the steps of a negotiation round in the correct order.

?You make a softened offer instead of a refusal: we would be prepared to move if you accepted a carve-out.
?The draft comes back from the other side and you read every redline in the document.
?If everything is refused, you take out your fallback, stay in good faith and never walk away in the same meeting.
?You separate the boilerplate from the five changes that really matter to your client.
?You name the sticking point out loud, because a round that never names it goes in circles.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best sentence for each situation.

They ask for a cap your client cannot accept, and you want to keep the door open. Which is best?
A "No. We cannot accept that cap."
B "We would be prepared to move on the cap if you accepted a carve-out for fraud."
C "We would be prepared to moving on the cap."
You want to invite the other side to trade something else. Which is best?
A "Would you be willing to look at a longer notice period instead?"
B "Look at a longer notice period instead."
C "Would you be willing that you look at a longer notice period?"
They deleted the exclusivity clause and you need it back. Which is best?
A "We would to need exclusivity back before signing."
B "We would need exclusivity back before we could sign."
C "Put exclusivity back or we finish here."
