Chapter 3 -- The Softening Code

Saying No Without the Word No

would be prepared to · would you be willing to

Discovery

Listen and Notice

Look at the orange words. Has anything actually been agreed? Is the door open or closed?

"We would be prepared to move on the cap if you accepted a carve-out."

"Would you be willing to look at a longer notice period instead?"

"That would be difficult for my client, but let me take it back to them."

"We would need a carve-out for fraud before we could accept that cap."

FormUseExample
We would be prepared to + base verboffer a move without conceding it yet"We would be prepared to move on the cap."
Would you be willing to + base verbinvite the other side to trade, never order"Would you be willing to look at a longer notice period?"
That would be difficultrefuse without the word no"That would be difficult for my client."
We would need X before we could Yname your price for saying yes"We would need a carve-out before we could accept it."
... if you accepted / agreedthe trade stays hypothetical, so nothing is promised"... if you accepted a carve-out for fraud."

Key idea: would makes the offer hypothetical. Nothing has been conceded, the door stays open, and the other side can see the shape of the trade. Two traps: after would the verb is always the base form ("would to move" → "would move"), and in the trade half of the sentence the verb goes to the past form ("if you accepted", not "if you will accept").

The Structure

One Refusal, Three Answers

Same position on the cap. Only the door moves.

Answer 1 · Closed

"No. We cannot accept that cap."

Clear, and it ends the exchange. The other side learns nothing about what you could accept.

Answer 2 · Half open

"That would be difficult for my client."

A refusal without the word no. Safe on a first call, but it still gives no map.

Answer 3 · Open, with a map

"We would be prepared to move on the cap if you accepted a carve-out for fraud."

Nothing conceded, and the other side now knows exactly what the trade looks like.

Answer 3 is not weaker than Answer 1. It holds the same line and hands over the map.

Dialogue

The Call on the Cap

D

"Thank you for the mark-up. Most of our redlines are boilerplate, so let us go straight to the one that matters: the liability cap."

S

"Agreed, that is the sticking point. One times the fee would be difficult for my client. We would be prepared to move if you accepted a carve-out for fraud."

D

"A carve-out for fraud is a large ask. My client is close to walking away from this clause entirely."

S

"Nobody is going to walk away over one exception. Would you be willing to look at a longer notice period instead, if the cap stayed where it is?"

D

"That is more interesting. I would need to take sixty days back to my client, but I can see the trade."

S

"Please do, and I will confirm in writing today, marked subject to contract. We are still in good faith, and I am not putting anything else on the table yet."

Dialogue Review

About the Call

1. Why did the other lawyer want to go straight to the cap?
Because most of his own redlines were boilerplate, and the cap was the only change that mattered.
2. How did he react to the request for a carve-out for fraud?
He called it a large ask and said his client was close to walking away from that clause entirely.
3. What did he say he would do about the longer notice period?
He would need to take sixty days back to his client, but he could already see the trade.

0:000:00
1. What does she do with the thirty-five boilerplate changes?
She accepts them without a word, because arguing about a standard clause is how a round dies of old age.
2. What is her rule for every answer tomorrow?
She never opens her mouth with the word no: every answer starts with "would".
