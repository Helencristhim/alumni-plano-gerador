Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A treaty -- a formal written agreement between two or more states
"The treaty was negotiated for six years before anyone signed it."
A signatory -- a state or a person that has signed an agreement
"Every signatory received a copy of the final text."
To ratify -- to make an agreement officially valid, usually after a national parliament approves it
"The treaty must be ratified by the national congress."
Binding -- describes an obligation that must be followed by law
"A signature alone does not make the treaty binding."
A provision -- one specific rule or condition inside a legal document
"Article 12 contains the provision on the protection of refugees."
To enter into force -- to start having legal effect from a certain date
"The agreement entered into force thirty days after the last ratification."
A party -- a state or organization that has formally accepted an agreement and is bound by it
"Only a party to the treaty can bring a complaint under it."
A reservation -- a formal statement that a state will not be bound by a particular part of a treaty
"Some states ratified the convention with a reservation on article 9."
To comply with -- to act according to a rule, a law or an order
"States are expected to comply with the treaties they have ratified."
A breach -- an action that breaks a law, a rule or an agreement
"Closing the border was a breach of the agreement."
To enforce -- to make people or states obey a law or a rule
"There is no international police force to enforce the decision."
Sovereignty -- the power of a state to govern itself without control from outside
"Some politicians say the court is a threat to national sovereignty."
To withdraw from -- to officially stop being part of an agreement or an organization
"A state can withdraw from the treaty, but only after giving notice."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Legal English has a favorite structure, and you see it in every article of every treaty: the passive voice. Instead of saying who acts, the text puts the action first. “The treaty was signed in Geneva” tells you what happened and when. Who held the pen is not important, so it is not mentioned.

The passive is built with be + past participle, and the verb be carries the time. “The convention has been ratified by forty states” describes a result that is still true. “The case is being heard this week” describes something in progress. When the person who acts matters, it comes after by, never after from: “It was approved by the congress.”

Modal verbs work the same way. A reservation can be made at the moment of ratification, and the text must be approved before it becomes binding. The most common mistake is to forget be after the modal, as in “a reservation can make”, which means something completely different.

Finally, legal and academic texts often report a general belief without saying who holds it: “It is often said that international law has no teeth.” Once you recognize the passive, a long legal sentence becomes much easier to read, because you stop looking for a subject that was never there.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Passive Voice in Legal English

GRAMMAR

Be + past participle puts the action first; add by only when the actor matters.

JobFormExample
A finished actionwas / were + past participleThe treaty was signed in 2019.
A result still truehas / have been + past participleIt has been ratified by forty states.
Something in progressis / are being + past participleThe case is being heard this week.
Possibility or obligationmodal + be + past participleIt must be approved first.
Naming the actorpassive + by + agentIt was ratified by the congress.
A general beliefit is said / believed thatIt is said that treaties are weak.

Never: the treaty was ratify · signed from forty states · a reservation can make · the rules are applied since 2020
