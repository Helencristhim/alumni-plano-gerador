Chapter 1: The Advice

The Best Advice You Ever Ignored

One piece of advice somebody gave you that turned out to be right, and that you did not follow at the time. What was it, and why did you ignore it?

Where You Start

Three Strengths, One Sentence

A suggestion

"You should ask for a receipt." -- an opinion, easy to refuse

A recommendation

"You ought to ask for a receipt." -- slightly more formal, slightly heavier

A warning

"You had better ask for a receipt." -- and there will be a consequence

Giving advice in a second language is a balance problem. Too soft and nobody hears it; too strong and you sound like somebody's father. Your job all day is telling people what to do with their money, so the difference between these three is not decoration — it is the difference between a suggestion and a warning.

Chapter 3: The Code

Should, Ought To, Had Better

Three strengths of the same advice, and a consequence hiding in the third

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"You should ask for a receipt."

"You ought to think twice before signing that."

"You had better call him before six."

"You should not decide this today."

All four give advice about the same kind of situation. One of them contains a threat that nobody says out loud. Which one, and what makes it feel heavier?

FormWhat it saysExample
should + plain verbThe everyday one. An opinion, easy to accept and easy to refuse."You should ask."
"You should not sign it today."
ought to + plain verbAlmost the same, slightly more formal, and it points at what is right rather than what you think."You ought to think twice."
had better + plain verbA warning. There is an unspoken or else behind it."You had better call him before six."
"You had better not be late."
No to, no s, no doAll three are modal-type verbs: the verb after them is plain, and questions and negatives never borrow do.NOT "he should to ask"
NOT "he shoulds ask"
"Should I call him?"
Softening itIn a first conversation, wrap the advice. It is the same advice with the edge taken off."I would ask for a receipt."
"If I were you, I would wait."

In practice: should for almost everything, ought to when you want to sound a little more formal, and had better only when there really is a consequence. Used casually, had better sounds like a threat.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"You should to ask for a receipt."
"You should ask for a receipt."
"He shoulds think twice before signing."
"He should think twice before signing."
"Do you should call him today?"
"Should I call him today?"

After should, ought to and had better the verb stays in its plain form, so there is no second to and no S in the third person. And these verbs make their own questions and negatives, so they never borrow do: it is should I, never do I should. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Listening

Three Rules for Giving Advice

A short radio segment. Sound first -- no text.

1. What is the first rule?
Ask what they have already decided, because most people have decided and want permission.
2. Why does the speaker say you should not say "you have to"?
Because nobody has to do anything, and the phrase makes people defend the other option.
3. What is the third rule?
Say what you would do, not what they should do -- it is the same advice and it is much easier to hear.
Listening 2

I Need a Second Opinion

A voice message asking for advice. Sound first -- no text.

1. What are the two options she is deciding between?
Signing on Friday, or asking for two more weeks.
2. What is she afraid of if she asks for more time?
That they will think she is not serious and go to somebody else.
3. What does she ask him to do?
To tell her what he would do, not what she should do.
