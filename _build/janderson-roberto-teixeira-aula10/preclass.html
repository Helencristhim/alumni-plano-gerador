Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

What Would You Do? -- Giving Advice at Three Different Strengths

How to tell somebody what to do without sounding like an order, and the three strengths English uses for it. Key expressions: to recommend, to be tempted, to regret, common sense, to warn, a risk, to keep an eye on, to think twice, in the long run, to play it safe. Structure: should, ought to and had better.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These ten stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

To recommend -- to say that something is good and that somebody should try it
"I would recommend waiting until Monday."
To be tempted -- to want to do something even when it may not be wise
"I am tempted to say yes, and I know I should not."
To regret -- to feel sorry about something you did or did not do
"Nobody regrets asking one extra question."
Common sense -- the ability to make sensible practical decisions
"Half of this job is common sense and the other half is patience."
To warn -- to tell somebody about a possible danger or problem
"I warned him twice and he did it anyway."
A risk -- the chance that something bad will happen
"There is always a risk, and pretending there is not is the real one."
To keep an eye on -- to watch something carefully over a period of time
"We should keep an eye on it for a couple of weeks."
To think twice -- to consider carefully before deciding
"You ought to think twice before signing that."
In the long run -- over a long period of time, in the end
"In the long run it usually works out."
To play it safe -- to choose the option with the least risk
"When in doubt, play it safe and ask."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

People almost never want advice. They want permission. Somebody describes two options, asks what you would do, and if you listen carefully you can usually hear which one they have already chosen. The useful question is not what should I do. It is what are you tempted to do, and the answer to that arrives in about two seconds.

That does not mean advice is useless. It means the strength matters. You should ask for a receipt is an opinion and it costs nothing to refuse. You ought to think twice before signing is heavier and a little more formal. You had better call him before six is a warning, and there is a consequence sitting behind it that nobody says out loud.

In Janderson's job this is not decoration. He spends his day telling people what to do with money that is not his. He has to recommend without promising, name a risk without frightening anybody, and say honestly what he would keep an eye on. Half of that is common sense and the other half is choosing the right verb.

And there is one sentence that does more work than all the modal verbs together: if I were you, I would. It is the same advice with the edge taken off. Nobody regrets hearing it, nobody feels judged, and in the long run people follow it more often than they follow an instruction. When in doubt, play it safe and use that one.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Should, Ought To and Had Better

GRAMMAR

Three ways to say the same thing at three different weights. Choosing the wrong weight is what makes advice in a second language sound either invisible or aggressive.

FormWhat it saysExample
should + plain verbThe everyday one. An opinion, easy to accept and easy to refuse."You should ask."
"You should not sign it today."
ought to + plain verbSlightly more formal. Points at what is right rather than at what you think."You ought to think twice."
had better + plain verbA warning, with an unspoken consequence behind it."You had better call him before six."
"You had better not be late."
No to, no S, no doThe verb after them stays plain, and they make their own questions and negatives.NOT "should to ask" · NOT "he shoulds"
"Should I call him?"
The softenersThe same advice, with the edge taken off. Safest in a first conversation."I would wait."
"If I were you, I would wait."
Asking instead of tellingOften the strongest move is a question, not a recommendation."What are you tempted to do?"
"What would you regret more?"

One rule for a client conversation: had better almost never belongs there. It sounds like a threat in a language you do not fully control. I would recommend and if I were you do the same job and leave the decision where it belongs.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Somebody has asked you what they should do. Choose the best thing to say.

You want to give an everyday opinion:
A You should to ask for a receipt.
B You should ask for a receipt.
C You shoulds ask for a receipt.
You want to ask for advice yourself:
A Do I should call him today?
B Should I call him today?
C I should call him today?
You want to warn somebody that there is a real consequence:
A You had better call him before six.
B You should better call him before six.
C You had better to call him before six.
You are talking to a client and you want to be clear but not heavy:
A You had better sell it.
B I would recommend waiting, and I would keep an eye on it.
C You have to wait, it is obvious.
You want to hear what they really want before you answer:
A What are you tempted to do?
B What is your final decision?
C Why did you not decide already?
