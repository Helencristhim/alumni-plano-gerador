Stage 2: Grammar Tip -- approve / approval / approved, and be + afraid

GRAMMAR

One family for the decision, and one rule about adjectives that stops the most repeated mistake of your consultancy session.

FormWhat it doesExample
approve (verb)the action you takeI approve the request today.
approval (noun)the official yes as a thingThe position needs the approval of the CFO.
approved (past participle)describes the request after the decisionThe request was approved on Friday.
be + afraid / sure / sorry / readyadjectives always go with be, never with haveI'm afraid we can't. I'm sure about the date.
Watch out"I have afraid", "I have sure" and "I am approval". Afraid goes with be; approval is a thing, not an action.

Quick test: if the word describes you, use I am. If it is a thing you give or need, use approval. If it is what you do, use approve.

The words you have just met, one by one. Listen to each expression and read it in context: you already do all of this at work. Here it gets its English name.

decline -- to say no to a request, in a formal and polite way

clarification -- extra information that makes something clearer

at this stage -- at this point of the process, with the possibility of change later

subject to approval -- depending on somebody saying yes
"The start date is subject to approval by the CFO."
hold off on -- to wait before doing something

Stage 5: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Every answer to a request has three parts, and they always come in the same order: the decision, the reason and the next step. What changes between a good answer and a bad one is not the decision. It is whether the other two parts are there.

Look at the yes first. "The replacement is approved, subject to approval by the CFO." The manager reads a positive decision and, in the same line, learns that one sign-off is still missing. Nobody is surprised two weeks later.

Now the not yet. "Could you provide more details about the workload and the monthly cost?" This is not a refusal; it is a clarification. The request was incomplete, and a requirement is missing. The manager has something to do, and the conversation continues.

And the no. "I'm afraid we can't approve a new position at this stage." Two expressions carry the weight. I'm afraid announces bad news politely, and it is always I am, never I have: afraid is an adjective, like sure, sorry and ready, and adjectives go with the verb be. At this stage says today, not forever.

One more family to keep straight, because it appears in every one of these messages. Approve is the action: I approve the request. Approval is the thing: the request needs my approval. Approved describes the request after the decision: the position was approved on Friday. Three forms, one decision, and one sentence is enough to show which one you control.
