Chapter 3: The Code

Offering and Exploring

First conditional, second conditional, and the distance between them

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"If you drop the notice period, we can meet halfway on the fee."

"If we accepted that clause, we would need a cap on the indemnity."

"Unless the carve-out stays in, we will have to escalate it."

"If I were you, I would hold firm on the term and give ground on the fee."

Two of these put something real on the table. Two of them explore an idea without offering anything at all. Which are which — and what is different about the verbs?

FormWhat it doesExample
first conditionalIf + present, then will, can or may. A real, live offer: if this happens, that follows. Say it and you have committed."If you drop the notice period, we can meet halfway."
second conditionalIf + past, then would, could or might. Not unreal — DISTANT. It puts an idea on the table without putting your name on it."If we accepted that clause, we would need a cap."
never would in the if-clauseWould lives in the main half only. If we would accept is the single commonest conditional error in professional English, and it is audible immediately."If we accepted..."
never "if we would accept..."
unless · provided that · as long asUnless = if not, and it never takes a negative after it. Provided that and as long as attach a condition to an offer you are otherwise making."Unless the carve-out stays in, we will escalate."
"...provided that notice is given in writing."
if I wereIn the second conditional, were is used for every person — especially in if I were you and if it were up to me, which are how advice and reluctance get said politely."If I were you, I would hold firm."
"If it were up to me, we would sign today."

Portuguese distinguishes these two with the subjunctive and the future, so the IDEA transfers cleanly. What does not transfer is the shape: the past tense inside the if-clause feels wrong to a Portuguese ear, and would keeps trying to move into the wrong half of the sentence.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If you will drop the notice period, we can meet halfway."
"If you drop the notice period, we can meet halfway."
"If we would accept that clause, we would need a cap."
"If we accepted that clause, we would need a cap."
"If I was you, I would escalate it today."
"If I were you, I would escalate it today."

The first puts will inside the if-clause, where the present tense already carries the future. The second is the big one: would belongs to the main half only, and the if-half takes the past. The third is was where the second conditional wants were — and if I were you is the phrase you will use most often of all.

Chapter 4: Negotiating the Clause

Ninety Days, or Thirty

Karen has the same clause open on her screen

Dialogue

What If We Moved on Notice?

K
We are close on everything except termination. Ninety days of notice is a lot for a first contract.
P
It is. If we accepted thirty, we would want a carve-out for anything already in progress.
K
That is reasonable in principle. What would that carve-out look like in practice?
P
Work already commissioned gets finished and paid. If the work has not started, the notice period applies and nothing else does.
K
I could live with that. The sticking point on our side is really the fee, not the notice.
P
Then let us be direct. If you hold firm on the fee, we give ground on notice. If you move on the fee, we can meet halfway on both.
K
And if neither of us moves at all today?
P
Then I escalate it and you do the same, which helps nobody. If it were up to me, we would close this on the call.
Comprehension

About Karen

1. What does Karen say is the only open point at the start?
Termination — ninety days of notice, which she says is a lot for a first contract.
2. How does she react to the idea of a carve-out?
She says it is reasonable in principle, and asks what it would look like in practice.
3. What does she say the real sticking point is on her side?
The fee, not the notice period.
4. What does she ask at the end?
What happens if neither side moves at all that day.
Listening 1

Putting It on the Table Without Offering It

A negotiator on the most useful tense in English. Sound first — no text.

1. What one piece of grammar would she teach, and to whom?
The second conditional, to everybody who negotiates in a second language.
2. Why can you not find out what something is worth by offering it?
Because the moment you offer it, you have given it away.
3. What is the difference between people who have only the first conditional and people who have both?
The first group negotiates in offers and runs out of things to offer; the second can explore for an hour and commit in the last five minutes.
Listening 2

The Person Who Can Leave

A commercial director on why a fallback changes the whole conversation. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say the strongest position is?
Not being right — having somewhere else to go.
2. What changes when you have a genuine fallback?
You stop arguing and start explaining, and you can say what you will do instead without it sounding like a threat.
3. What is the mistake he warns about?
Bluffing: if you say you will walk away and you cannot, it gets found out in one meeting and nothing you say afterwards has weight.
