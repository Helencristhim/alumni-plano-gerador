Negotiating the Clause

A termination clause negotiated live, in which nobody offers anything for the first four lines. Key expressions: a concession, a sticking point, a deal-breaker, to meet halfway, leverage, to walk away, a fallback, to hold firm, to give ground, bad faith, a carve-out, to be non-negotiable, to split the difference, to escalate. Structure: first and second conditionals — offering against exploring, unless and provided that, and if I were you.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to a negotiating table, and you will hear most of them in the first ten minutes of any real one.

A concession -- something you give up in order to reach agreement
"We made one concession and asked for two things in return."
A sticking point -- the single issue that is stopping agreement
"The sticking point was never the price."
A deal-breaker -- something you will not accept under any conditions
"An uncapped indemnity is a deal-breaker for us."
To meet halfway -- to move towards each other until both sides can accept it
"If you drop the notice period, we can meet halfway on the fee."
Leverage -- the advantage that lets you push for more
"Our only leverage is that they need this signed before the quarter ends."
To walk away -- to end the negotiation with no agreement at all
"We would walk away before we accepted that clause."
A fallback -- the option you take if the one you want fails
"Our fallback is a twelve-month term with a review at six."
To hold firm -- to refuse to move on a point
"We can hold firm on the cap if we give ground on the term."
To give ground -- to concede a little, without conceding the point
"Neither side wanted to give ground in the first hour."
Bad faith -- dishonest intention in the way somebody negotiates
"Changing the draft the night before is close to bad faith."
A carve-out -- an exception written into an otherwise general rule
"We asked for a carve-out for claims we cannot control."
To be non-negotiable -- impossible to change, whatever is offered
"The governing law is non-negotiable on their side."
To split the difference -- to agree on the midpoint between two positions
"They offered to split the difference and we took it."
To escalate -- to move a decision up to somebody more senior
"If we cannot agree today, I will have to escalate it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two conditionals you need at a negotiating table and they do completely different jobs. The first one is an offer: if + present, then will, can or may. If you drop the notice period, we can meet halfway on the fee. Say that and you have committed to something. If they take it, you are bound by it.

The second one is an exploration: if + past, then would, could or might. If we accepted thirty days, we would want a carve-out for work already commissioned. Nothing has been accepted. Nothing has been offered. But the idea is now on the table and you can find out what it is worth — and take it back afterwards without having lost anything. That is why experienced negotiators live in this tense for the first hour.

One rule governs the form and it is absolute: would never appears in the if-half. If we accepted..., never if we would accept.... And in the second conditional, English uses were for every person — if I were you, if it were up to me — which is how advice and reluctance are made polite.

Finally the connectors. Unless means if not, so it never takes a negative after it: unless the carve-out stays in, not unless it does not stay in. Provided that and as long as attach a condition to an offer you are otherwise making, which is a useful way to say yes and keep something back at the same time.

1. What does the first conditional do at a negotiating table?
A It explores an idea without committing to it.
B It makes a real offer: if this happens, that follows.
C It refuses a proposal politely.
2. Why do experienced negotiators use the second conditional for the first hour?
A Because it is more formal.
B Because it puts an idea on the table without offering it, so it can be taken back.
C Because it is the only polite way to disagree.
3. Which is correct?
A "If we would accept that clause, we would need a cap."
B "If we accepted that clause, we would need a cap."
C "If we will accept that clause, we would need a cap."
4. Which sentence uses "unless" correctly?
A "Unless you do not send the carve-out, we will escalate."
B "Unless you send the carve-out, we will escalate."
C "Unless if you send the carve-out, we will escalate."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- First and Second Conditionals

GRAMMAR

Portuguese makes this same distinction with the subjunctive, so the IDEA transfers cleanly. What does not transfer is the shape: a past tense inside the if-clause feels wrong to a Portuguese ear, and would keeps trying to move into the half of the sentence where it does not belong.

FormWhat it doesExample
first conditionalIf + present, then will, can or may. A real, live offer: if this happens, that follows. Say it and you have committed."If you drop the notice period, we can meet halfway."
second conditionalIf + past, then would, could or might. Not unreal — DISTANT. It puts an idea on the table without putting your name on it."If we accepted that clause, we would need a cap."
never would in the if-clauseWould lives in the main half only. If we would accept is the single commonest conditional error in professional English, and it is audible immediately."If we accepted..."
never "if we would accept..."
unless · provided that · as long asUnless = if not, and it never takes a negative after it. Provided that and as long as attach a condition to an offer you are otherwise making."Unless the carve-out stays in, we will escalate."
"...provided that notice is given in writing."
if I wereIn the second conditional, were is used for every person — especially in if I were you and if it were up to me, which are how advice and reluctance get said politely."If I were you, I would hold firm."
"If it were up to me, we would sign today."

One habit for this week: before your next negotiation, write three questions that begin if we accepted... or if you moved on.... Ask at least one of them. You will find out something you would not have found out by making an offer.

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to how a clause gets negotiated from first position to signature, then put the steps in order.

?Both sides open with the position they would like, and neither expects to get it.
?The sticking point is named out loud, so that the rest can stop being argued about.
?Ideas are explored in the second conditional, without anybody offering anything yet.
?One side gives ground on the term in exchange for holding firm on the fee.
?What is left is either agreed on the call or escalated to somebody more senior.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are negotiating a clause. Choose the version that does what you actually want it to do.

You want to find out what thirty days would be worth to them, without accepting it:
A "We accept thirty days if you give us a carve-out."
B "If we accepted thirty days, we would want a carve-out for work already commissioned."
C "If we would accept thirty days, we want a carve-out."
You want to make a real offer that they can take today:
A "If you drop the notice period, we can meet halfway on the fee."
B "If you dropped the notice period, we might possibly consider something."
C "If you will drop the notice period, we can meet halfway."
You want to give advice to a colleague:
A "If I was you, I would escalate it."
B "If I were you, I would escalate it."
C "If I would be you, I would escalate it."
You want to attach a condition to something you are agreeing to:
A "We can agree the fee today, provided that notice is given in writing."
B "We can agree the fee today, unless notice is not given in writing."
C "We agree the fee today if you will give notice in writing."
They ask whether you would ever walk away, and you have no real alternative:
A "Yes, we would walk away tomorrow."
B "That is a deal-breaker as it stands, and I would rather find the version that is not."
C "Maybe, I do not know, possibly."
