Lesson 18 -- Pre-class

The American Executive Conversation -- The Question That Comes Back

The three minutes before the meeting, and the two words that keep them going. Key words: small talk, icebreaker, rapport, common ground, register, approachable, to reciprocate, to touch base. System: echo questions and short follow-up questions -- borrow his auxiliary, keep the tense, keep it positive, then open the door again in three words and send the question back.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Small talk -- light conversation about neutral subjects that people use before the real business starts
"The deal was decided in the meeting, but it was made possible in three minutes of small talk."
Icebreaker -- a first question or comment that makes two strangers comfortable enough to keep talking
"The flight is a safe icebreaker at any conference in the world."
Rapport -- the easy, friendly connection that makes two people want to keep talking to each other
"We had no agreement on the budget, but we had rapport, and that is why the call happened."
Common ground -- an interest or experience that two people share and can build a conversation on
"Two minutes in, we found common ground: we had both survived the same migration."
Register -- the level of formality you choose for a situation, from very casual to very formal
"The register goes up the moment the small talk ends and the numbers begin."
Approachable -- easy to talk to, and never making the other person feel small
"Forty years of experience and still approachable: that is the combination people remember."
To reciprocate -- to give back the same kind of thing you received, such as returning a question
"He asked about my career for four minutes and I never reciprocated."
To touch base -- to make short contact with someone, so that the relationship stays alive
"Let me touch base with you after the summit, once you are back in Boston."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Lucia arrives twenty minutes early at the summit in San Francisco, which is exactly when the small talk happens. A man next to her says he flew in from Boston the night before, and instead of answering "Yes", she uses the two words that keep an American conversation alive: "Oh, did you?" That is an echo question. It borrows the auxiliary of his sentence, keeps the same tense and stays positive, and it is not a real question at all: it says I heard you, and I want the rest. He tells her he has been coming to this summit for six years, and she echoes again, this time with his auxiliary: "Have you?" Then she opens the door with three words: "How so?" One minute later they have found common ground -- they both moved from infrastructure into safety work -- and the rapport is built. When he finally asks about her career, she answers and then she reciprocates: "Forty years, most of them at IBM. How about you?" At minute three she changes the register and makes the turn: "So, you mentioned the safety track." The small talk is over, the business has started, and nobody felt interrupted. She leaves after offering to touch base the following week, and he remembers her as the most approachable person in the room.

1. Why does Lucia say "Oh, did you?" instead of "Yes"?
A Because an echo question shows that she heard him and asks him to continue, while "Yes" ends the turn.
B Because she did not understand what he said and needs him to repeat it.
C Because "Yes" is grammatically wrong as an answer to a statement in English.
2. Where does the word "did" in "Oh, did you?" come from?
A From the future, because she is asking about his next trip.
B From his sentence: it was in the past simple, which has no auxiliary, so the echo borrows "did".
C From nowhere in particular: any auxiliary works in an echo question.
3. What does Lucia do after she answers his question about her career?
A She waits in silence for him to choose the next subject.
B She gives him her full career history, year by year.
C She reciprocates and sends the same question straight back to him.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Echo Questions and Short Follow-Ups

GRAMMAR

The whole mechanism in five lines. The echo borrows the auxiliary of his sentence.

What he saysWhere the echo comes fromYou say
"I moved back last year."Past simple carries no auxiliary, so the echo borrows did.Did you?
"I run the safety program."Present simple carries no auxiliary either: do or does.Do you?
"We have been testing it."The auxiliary is already in his sentence: reuse it.Have you?
"It was harder than we expected."The verb to be is its own auxiliary, and modals echo themselves.Was it? / Will you?
Short follow-upsThree or four words that open the door again, without taking the floor.How so? Such as? What kind? Since when?
Three rulesThe echo keeps the same tense, it stays positive even after a negative sentence, and the subject becomes a pronoun: "The migration was late." gives you "Was it?" The voice goes up at the end, and then you stop and let him continue.
