You Learned to Mark Your Own Edge. Now Take Theirs

Last time you put the edge on your own claim: roughly, arguably, to be fair, broadly speaking. Every one of those moves protected what you said. Tonight the sentence belongs to the other person, and the only thing that matters is what you do in the two seconds after it lands.

Chapter 4: The Follow-Up Code

Echo · Follow Up · Send It Back

Five moves that keep the other person talking

The Conversation Map

Five Moves That Keep the Other Person Talking

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I just moved back from Tokyo." → "Oh, did you?"

"We have been testing it since January." → "Have you?"

"The migration was harder than we expected." → "Was it?"

"I run the AI safety program." → "Do you? What does that involve?"

The short answers are only two words long. Where does each one come from?

What he saysWhere the echo comes fromYou say
"I moved back last year."Past simple has no auxiliary, so the echo borrows didDid you?
"I run the safety program."Present simple has no auxiliary either: do or doesDo you?
"We have been testing it."The auxiliary is already there: reuse itHave you?
"It was harder than we expected."The verb to be is its own auxiliaryWas it?
"I will be in Boston in May."Modals echo themselvesWill you?
Three rules with no exceptions: the echo stays in the same tense, it stays positive even after a negative sentence, and the subject becomes a pronoun ("The migration was late." → "Was it?").

An echo question is not a real question. It says: I heard you, and I want the rest. The voice goes up at the end, and then you stop and let him continue.

Focus

One Sentence, Four Doors

He says: "Honestly, the migration was harder than we expected." Four follow-ups, four different conversations. Say each one, then tap to check

"How so?" ___He explains the reason. You get the thinking behind the problem.
"Such as?" ___He gives you a concrete example. You get the detail, not the theory.
"Was it?" ___He simply keeps going, wherever he wanted to go. You take nothing over.
"How did you handle it?" ___He tells you what he did about it. You move him from the problem to the decision.

Three of the four are shorter than five words. In a hallway, the shortest question wins.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I just moved back from Tokyo." → "Yes." — the conversation stops right there
"I just moved back from Tokyo." → "Oh, did you? How long were you there?"
"We finished the migration in March." → "Do you?" — wrong auxiliary, wrong tense
"We finished the migration in March." → "Did you? How did that go?"
"How long have you been in technology?" → "Forty years." — and nothing comes back
"How long have you been in technology?" → "Forty years, most of them at IBM. How about you?"

In Portuguese a polite silence is fine. In American English the silence after his sentence reads as no interest — never as politeness, and never as a language problem. Two words are enough to prevent it.

Three Minutes in a Hallway

G
First time at this summit? I flew in from Boston last night and I am still on the wrong clock.
L
Oh, did you? I came in on Sunday, so I have had two days to recover.
G
Smart. I have been coming to this one for six years, mostly for the safety track.
L
Have you? Six years is a long time in this field. What keeps you coming back?
G
Honestly, the hallway conversations. The talks go online two weeks later anyway.
L
That is fair. I spent most of my career on the infrastructure side and moved into safety work two years ago. How about you?
G
Same story, different order. I started in policy and came to the engineering side late.
L
Did you? I would call that the useful order. So, you mentioned the safety track. What are you presenting this year?
G
Nothing this year, I am only here to listen. But send me a note next week and we can touch base before the Boston meeting.
Did You Catch It?

About the Conversation

1. Where did Greg fly in from, and when did he arrive?
From Boston, the night before, and he says he is still on the wrong clock.
2. How long has Greg been coming to this summit, and what does he really come for?
Six years, officially for the safety track, but really for the hallway conversations.
3. Where did Greg start his career, and what did he do afterwards?
He started in policy and came to the engineering side late.
Listening 1

What She Watches For in the First Minute

An executive coach explains the one habit that changes a networking event. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she say she counts when she watches two people talk?
How many questions each person asks -- and the number is almost never equal.
2. What does she say people remember about a conversation a week later?
Not what the other person said, but how they felt -- and they felt good when they were asked about themselves.
3. What does she tell people to do when they run out of things to say?
Ask about the last thing the other person said, in three words, instead of changing the subject.
Listening 2

He Answered Everything and Asked Nothing

A hiring manager records a note after a final-round interview. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he say about the quality of the candidate's answers?
They were strong -- clear, well structured, with real numbers in them.
2. What was missing for the whole hour, according to him?
The candidate never asked him anything back, so the hour felt like a test and not a conversation.
3. What does he ask the recruiter to tell the next candidates?
To bring two or three real questions and to ask them early, not only at the end.
