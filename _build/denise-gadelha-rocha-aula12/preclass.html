Lesson 12 -- Pre-class

Wish You Were Prepared -- Expressing Regret, Distance and Desired Reality in Executive English

How to put a past failure on the record without putting a person on trial. Key words: raise a concern, misgivings, voice, unspoken, grievance, benefit of the doubt, let something slide, gloss over, bring something forward, standing item, course-correct, friction. Structure: wish and if only.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words for what a room already knows and has not said.

To raise a concern -- to put a worry on the table formally, so that it is on the record
"I would rather raise a concern in June than explain it in October."
Misgivings -- private doubts about a decision, usually before anything goes wrong
"Two people had misgivings about that number and neither of them said so."
To voice -- to say out loud what people have only been thinking
"Nobody voiced it, and eleven weeks later it cost us four points."
Unspoken -- understood by everyone in the room and said by nobody
"The doubt was unspoken, which is not the same as absent."
Grievance -- a complaint someone carries because they feel they were treated unfairly
"This is not a grievance. Nobody is being asked to explain themselves."
Benefit of the doubt -- assuming good intentions when you cannot yet prove them
"They gave the numbers the benefit of the doubt, and the instinct was decent."
To let something slide -- to see a problem and decide, deliberately, not to act on it
"We let it slide for eleven weeks because no meeting owned it."
To gloss over -- to mention something so briefly that nobody has to deal with it
"The summary glossed over the reserving gap in half a sentence."
To bring something forward -- to move a date earlier than it was planned
"If only we had brought the review forward by one month."
Standing item -- a topic that is on every agenda by default, without being requested
"Reserving is now a standing item, with an owner here and one in Milan."
To course-correct -- to change direction early, while the correction is still small
"I would rather course-correct monthly than apologise twice a year."
Friction -- the everyday resistance between people or teams that slows work down
"Ten minutes of friction every month is cheaper than a surprise in October."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Watch what happens to the verb after every "wish".

The quarter closed four points below plan, and the reserving gap that explains it was already visible in the June data. Nobody hid it. Two colleagues had misgivings and did not voice them; they gave the numbers the benefit of the doubt, because the alternative was to challenge a market they do not cover. The doubt was unspoken, which is not the same as absent.

"I wish we had known in June," Denise wrote in the retrospective. She chose the sentence carefully. It carries the date, it carries the failure, and it carries no defendant. What she did not write was "you told us too late", which states the same fact and arrives with somebody to blame. "If only we had brought the review forward by one month," she added, "the correction would have cost a quarter of what it now costs."

There is a third sentence she deliberately left out. "I wish they would tell us earlier when a decision changes" is not a regret about June at all: it is a judgement on how people behave now, and in a group whose head office sits in another country, that sentence travels a long way without her tone attached. "I wish the reserving page arrived before the call" would have been safer, because it describes a process and not a person.

The note ends with what she wants, not with what went wrong. Reserving becomes a standing item, with a named owner in Brazil and one in Milan, and anyone with a misgiving brings it to that call unfinished. Ten minutes of friction every month, so that the directorate can course-correct while the correction is still small. In the meeting on Tuesday she will open with a fourth kind of wish, and this one is not a regret at all: "I wish to raise a concern before we sign this off."

1. "I wish we had known in June." Why the past perfect, and not "I wish we knew"?
A Because it is a regret about a finished moment in the past. "I wish we knew" would be about a reality she does not have now.
B Because the sentence is written and not spoken.
C Because "know" has no simple past form after wish.
2. Why does the text say "I wish we had known in June" carries no defendant?
A Because it does not mention the reserving gap.
B Because it states the date and the failure without naming a person who failed, unlike "you told us too late".
C Because it is softer, and softer sentences are always safer.
3. Why did she leave out "I wish they would tell us earlier"?
A Because wish + would is a judgement on how people behave now, and it travels without her tone attached.
B Because wish + would is grammatically incorrect in written English.
C Because she does not want anything to change.
4. "I wish to raise a concern before we sign this off." How is this wish different from the others?
A It is the strongest regret of the four.
B It is the same structure, with the verb in the infinitive for emphasis.
C Nothing about it is unreal. Wish + to + infinitive is a formal way of saying "I would like to" — it is a request, not a regret.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Wish and If Only

GRAMMAR

The verb after "wish" always steps back one tense. Which tense you step back to is what changes the meaning — and, at work, who ends up on trial.

PatternWhat it meansExample
wish + past simpleA present reality you do not have"I wish the page arrived before the call."
wish + past perfectRegret about the past — the safest one at work, because it has a date and no defendant"I wish we had known in June."
wish + would + infinitiveIrritation with somebody else's behaviour. Never used about yourself"I wish they would tell us earlier."
wish + couldAn ability you do not have"I wish I could be in both calls."
if onlyThe same three patterns, more emphatic, and it can stand on its own: "If only we had brought the review forward."
wish + to + infinitiveThe odd one out: formal and completely REAL. "I wish to raise a concern" means "I would like to raise a concern". It belongs in meetings, minutes and e-mail.
The two trapsNever "I wish I would have known" — after wish, the past is had known. Never "I wish I know" — the verb steps back: knew.
Register noteI wish I were in that meeting: "were" for every person survives in careful written English, and it is the register a board note is written in.
