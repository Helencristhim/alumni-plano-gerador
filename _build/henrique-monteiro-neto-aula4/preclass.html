Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

What Really Happened — Putting the Emphasis Where You Want It

Two versions of one blackout, and the sentence shapes that decide which version the room believes. Key expressions: an account, to come to light, a discrepancy, to corroborate, firsthand, to piece together, a loose end, to downplay, to be at the heart of, to hinge on, beyond dispute, to take somebody at their word, to square with, a red herring, to shed light on, in the event, to all intents and purposes, a chain of events. Structure: cleft sentences for emphasis — what … is, it was … that, the reason why … is that, all I am saying is.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

An account -- one person's version of what happened, told in order
"His account and hers agree on everything except the time."
To come to light -- to become known after being unnoticed or hidden
"The maintenance log only came to light in the second year."
A discrepancy -- a difference between two versions that ought to match
"There is a nine-minute discrepancy nobody has explained."
To corroborate -- to support a claim with independent evidence
"Two engineers corroborated the timeline, and neither knew the other."
Firsthand -- experienced directly, not heard from somebody else
"She is the only witness with firsthand knowledge of the room."
To piece together -- to build a whole picture out of fragments that arrive separately
"It took two years to piece together nine hours."
A loose end -- a detail that remains unexplained after everything else fits
"The report closed with three loose ends and admitted it."
To downplay -- to make something sound less serious than it actually is
"The first statement downplayed the failure and aged badly."
To be at the heart of -- to be the central thing on which everything else depends
"A single decision was at the heart of all nine hours."
To hinge on -- to depend entirely on one particular thing
"The whole case hinges on what the log says about 02:14."
Beyond dispute -- accepted by everyone, and no longer arguable
"That the alarm sounded is beyond dispute."
To take somebody at their word -- to believe somebody without asking for evidence
"For eleven months the inquiry took the operator at his word."
To square with -- to be consistent with something else
"His statement does not square with the recording."
A red herring -- something that pulls attention away from the real question
"The weather was a red herring and cost them a year."
To shed light on -- to make something clearer that was not understood before
"One page of that log shed light on the entire sequence."
In the event -- as things actually turned out, whatever was expected
"In the event, the backup never started at all."
To all intents and purposes -- in every way that actually matters, if not formally
"To all intents and purposes the city had no power for nine hours."
A chain of events -- the sequence in which one thing caused the next
"The chain of events began with a decision taken in March."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

What is beyond dispute is the timeline. An alarm at 02:11, a disconnection at 02:14, four hundred thousand people without power by 02:20, and nine hours before the last of them had it back. Everything else in this case is an account given by somebody, and accounts can be checked against each other.

What the first report did was blame the weather. That version held for eleven months, partly because the inquiry took the operator at his word and partly because there was no obvious reason not to. What it never explained was the nine-minute discrepancy between the alarm and the disconnection, and it downplayed that in a single line on page forty.

It was a maintenance log, not a whistleblower, that changed everything. It came to light in the second year and shed light on the whole chain of events: an inspection postponed twice and then removed from the calendar. Two engineers corroborated it, neither knowing the other had. The storm, in the event, was a red herring. What was at the heart of those nine hours was a decision taken the previous March.

The reason why one question survived two years is that the system which recorded it was replaced in January, so to all intents and purposes the record is gone. The case still hinges on a name nobody can produce, and the final report lists it among three loose ends. All the inquiry is saying is that the statement does not square with the log, and that the only witness with firsthand knowledge was ignored for a year.

1. "It was a maintenance log, not a whistleblower, that changed everything." What is this structure doing?
A Isolating one element as the decisive one, and ruling out the alternative.
B Saying that two things changed everything.
C Describing when the change happened.
2. Why is it "the reason why … is that" and not "is because"?
A Because because is informal.
B Because the reason why already carries the causal meaning, so because repeats it.
C Because the sentence is in the passive.
3. "What the first report did was blame the weather." What is the effect of saying it this way?
A It puts the spotlight on the action itself, and prepares the reader for a but.
B It makes the sentence a question.
C It shows the report is still being written.
4. What does the text mean by saying the storm was a red herring?
A That the storm never happened.
B That it pulled attention away from the real cause.
C That it was the main cause after all.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences — Moving the Weight

GRAMMAR

A cleft sentence splits one plain statement into two parts so that you can decide which half the listener hears first. Nothing is added and nothing is removed — only the weight moves.

FormWhat it doesExample
What … is / was
(wh-cleft)		Spotlight on whatever follows is. Inside the what clause the word order stays normal: what strikes me, never what me strikes."What strikes me is the silence."
"What he did was ignore the alarm."
It was X that / who
(it-cleft)		Isolates one element as decisive, and usually implies the alternative it is not. Always that — even with a time or a place, where Portuguese would push you towards when or where."It was the log, not the weather, that decided it."
"It was in March that it was postponed."
The reason why …
is that		Announces an explanation. Is because says the same thing twice and is the single most common slip at this level."The reason why it took two years is that nobody asked."
All / The only thing
The thing is		Narrows your claim before the other person can widen it. In a disagreement this is defensive armour, and it is very hard to argue against."All I am saying is that it does not square."
"The only thing we know is the timeline."
What happened was
What we need is		Opens a narrative or a proposal from the top. In speech it also buys you a beat: you commit to the shape of the sentence before you have finished building it."What happened was that the backup never started."

There is a practical reason to drill these beyond elegance. A cleft opening lets you begin a difficult sentence while you are still deciding how it ends — which is exactly the moment where a fluent speaker sounds fluent and everybody else hesitates.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are explaining a complicated case to somebody who has not followed it. Choose the version that lands.

You want the spotlight on what surprised you:
A "What me surprises is the silence in the first hour."
B "What surprises me is the silence in the first hour."
C "What is surprising me is the silence in the first hour."
You want to say the log, and not the storm, was decisive:
A "It was the log that decided the case, not the storm."
B "It was the log what decided the case, not the storm."
C "It had the log that decided the case, not the storm."
You want to give the explanation for a two-year delay:
A "The reason why it took two years is because nobody asked."
B "The reason why it took two years is that nobody asked."
C "The reason of it took two years is nobody asked."
Somebody accuses you of claiming there was a cover-up. You want to narrow your claim:
A "All I am saying is that the statement does not square with the log."
B "I am saying only the statement does not square the log."
C "What I say is all that the statement does not square with the log."
You want to say that something happened in March, with the emphasis on the date:
A "It was in March when the inspection was postponed."
B "It was in March that the inspection was postponed."
C "It had in March that the inspection was postponed."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Take a story you have followed in the last month. In about ninety seconds, tell it twice. First put the weight on the cause you find most convincing; then tell the same facts again with the weight somewhere else — on a person, on a date, on a decision. Change nothing that is true. The test is whether somebody listening would come away with two different impressions of the same event.
