Options and Consent -- The Shared Decision

Two treatments, one patient, and a decision that is not yours to make. Key words: an option, a trade-off, informed consent, to weigh, a concern, a course of treatment, reversible, to hold off, to check in, to go ahead with, realistic, to talk it through. Structure: second conditional and modals of possibility.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An option -- a choice that is available, among others
"There are two reasonable options, and doing nothing is a third."
A trade-off -- something you give up in order to gain something else
"The trade-off is speed against the risk of side effects."
Informed consent -- agreement given after the risks and the alternatives were explained
"Informed consent is the conversation, not the signature."
To weigh -- to compare two things carefully in order to decide
"Let us weigh the two options side by side."
A concern -- something that worries the patient, said or unsaid
"Before we go on, is there a concern you have not mentioned?"
A course of treatment -- the full series of doses or sessions, from start to finish
"A course of treatment runs for twelve weeks."
Reversible -- able to be undone, if it turns out to be the wrong choice
"The cream is reversible. If it does not suit you, we stop."
To hold off -- to wait before starting something, on purpose
"We could hold off for a month and see what it does."
To check in -- to ask how somebody is doing, partway through
"I will check in with you after three weeks, not at the end."
To go ahead with -- to decide to do it, after considering it
"If you want to go ahead with the tablets, we can start on Monday."
Realistic -- matching what can actually happen, not what we hope
"Let me be realistic with you about the timeline."
To talk it through -- to discuss every side of something before deciding
"Take the week. Talk it through at home and call me."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A hypothetical needs its own grammar, and English marks it. If you describe an option nobody has chosen using the grammar of something already decided, you have decided for the patient -- and in a consent conversation that is a clinical problem, not a stylistic one.

Compare the two. If you start the cream on Monday, I will see you in six weeks -- that is a plan, and it is going ahead. If you chose the cream, it would take about twelve weeks -- nothing has been chosen. The verb after if looks past, and nothing here happened in the past: the past form is simply the marker of hypothesis. And the rule with no exceptions: never would after if.

Alongside it sit the modals of possibility. The tablets might work faster; they could affect the liver. Both leave the outcome open, which is exactly what an honest option does. And the sentence that hands the decision back is what would you like to do? -- not what do you want.

The rest is the shape of the conversation. Name the trade-off. Say what is reversible and what is not. Offer to hold off, because waiting is a real third option and a patient who is not offered it cannot choose it. Give your own view -- in your position I would probably... -- and then say plainly that it is your reasoning, not a decision. Ask whether there is a concern that has not been mentioned. Tell them to talk it through at home. That is what informed consent means: the conversation, not the signature.

1. "If you chose the cream, it would take twelve weeks." Has she chosen the cream?
A Yes -- the past form says the decision is made.
B No -- the past form after if marks a hypothesis, not a past event.
C It is impossible to tell from the grammar.
2. Why does the text call using the first conditional for an undecided option a clinical problem?
A Because it is grammatically incorrect in medical English.
B Because it makes the sentence longer than necessary.
C Because it presents an open choice as already settled, which decides for the patient.
3. Why does the text say waiting has to be offered out loud?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Second Conditional and Modals of Possibility

GRAMMAR

will means it is going ahead. would means nobody has decided. The past form after if is not past -- it is the marker of a hypothesis.

A plan that is going aheadif + present, willIf you start Monday, I will see you in six weeks.

Never: if you would choose the cream · if you choose the tablets, we would need a blood test · if you chose the cream, it will take twelve weeks · what do you want to do? · it is your decision (said alone, as the whole answer)
