Options and Consent

Chapter 3: The Code

Decided, or Still Open

The grammar of a choice nobody has made yet

Grammar Discovery

Which Two Have Not Been Decided?

"If you start the cream on Monday, I will see you in six weeks."

"If you chose the cream, it would take longer to work."

"If you went for the tablets, we would need a blood test first."

"The tablets might work faster, but they could affect your liver."

Look at the red verbs after if. Two are present, two look past — but nothing here happened in the past. And look at the blue words: will in one, would in the others. What is different about how settled each sentence is?

JobFormExample
A plan that is going aheadif + present, willIf you start Monday, I will see you in six weeks.
A choice nobody has madeif + past, would + base verbIf you chose the cream, it would take longer.
The past is NOT pastit marks the hypothesischose here means if you were to choose.
Never would after ifthe condition stays pastNot if you would choose the cream.
A real possibilitymight / could + base verbThey might work faster. They could affect your liver.
Handing the decision backWhat would you like to do?What would you like to do? Not what do you want.
Giving your view without decidingIn your position, I would...In your position, I would probably start with the cream.

In one line: will means it is going ahead; would means nobody has decided yet — and in a consent conversation that difference is clinical, not decorative.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Decision, at Full Speed

One consultation, two recordings, one consent form

Dialogue

Two Options, One Decision

D
There are two reasonable options here, and waiting is a third. Let me put them side by side and then you tell me what you think.
A
Please. And be realistic with me about how long each one takes.
D
If you chose the cream, it would take about twelve weeks, and it is reversible — if it does not suit you, we stop.
A
And the tablets?
D
They might work faster, maybe six weeks. But we would need a blood test first, and they could affect your liver. That is the trade-off: speed against monitoring.
A
What would you do, if it were you?
D
In your position I would probably start with the cream, because it is reversible. But that is my reasoning, not a decision. Is there a concern you have not mentioned?
A
My sister had a bad reaction to something similar. I would like to talk it through at home before I decide.
Comprehension

About Mrs. Alvarez

1. What does she ask for at the very start, before any detail?
That he be realistic with her about how long each option takes.
2. What is the trade-off between the two options, in his words?
Speed against monitoring: the tablets might work in about six weeks, but they need a blood test first and could affect the liver.
3. What is her real concern, and what made it come out?
Her sister had a bad reaction to something similar. It only came out because he asked whether there was a concern she had not mentioned.
Listening 1 · An Examiner

What the Station Actually Scores

An examiner on what separates a pass from a fail in a communication station. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say candidates almost always do to the second option?
They present it in less detail and with less warmth than the first, which tells the patient which one to pick without either of them noticing.
2. What does he say about the phrase it is your decision?
That said on its own it is abandonment, not autonomy. The patient asked for a view, and refusing to give one is not neutrality.
3. Which single question does he say is missed most often, and why does it matter?
Whether there is a concern the patient has not mentioned. He says the real reason a treatment is refused almost never comes out unless somebody asks for it directly.
Listening 2 · A Patient

The Appointment Where I Actually Chose

A patient describing the consultation that felt different. Sound first — no text.

1. What had happened in the previous consultations that made this one different?
She had been given a plan and asked to agree to it. Nobody had put two options in front of her before.
2. What exactly did the doctor say that she remembers?
In your position I would probably start with the cream, because it is reversible -- and then that it was his reasoning, not a decision.
3. What does she say about being told to take the week?
That it was the first time a decision had not had to be made in the room, and that she chose the tablets in the end -- but chose them.
Real Document

The Form at the End

TREATMENT DECISION RECORD
dermatology · to be completed with the patient
Options discussed. Topical treatment; oral treatment; watchful waiting.
Expected benefit. Topical: slower, around twelve weeks. Oral: possibly six weeks.
Risks explained. Oral treatment may affect liver function; blood test required before starting and during the course.
Reversibility. Topical treatment can be stopped at any time with no further action.
Patient concerns raised. Family history of adverse reaction to a similar agent.
Decision. Deferred at patient’s request — to be confirmed by telephone within seven days.
Teaching document written for this lesson in the format American clinics use. It records that a conversation took place and what was in it; it is not a consent form for real use.
1. The form lists three options. Which one did the doctor offer without being asked, and why does that matter?
Watchful waiting. It matters because doing nothing for now is a real clinical option, and a patient who is not offered it cannot choose it.
2. What does the line "decision: deferred at patient’s request" record?
That she was not required to decide in the room, and that the decision will be confirmed by telephone within seven days.
3. Why does the form have a line for concerns the patient raised?
Because informed consent is the conversation, not the signature -- and what the patient was worried about is part of the record of that conversation.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can present two options with the same care, and offer waiting as a third.
I use would for what has not been decided, and will only for what is going ahead.
I never say if you would choose, and I never mix the two conditionals in one sentence.
I can give my own view without deciding for the patient.
I always ask whether there is a concern that has not been mentioned.
