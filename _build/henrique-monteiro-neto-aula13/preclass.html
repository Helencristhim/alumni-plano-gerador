Lesson 13 -- Pre-class

Who Said What — Reporting an Argument Accurately

Three positions in a debate, reported without taking a side. Key expressions: the gist, to paraphrase, to take out of context, to misrepresent, a caricature, to do justice to, the thrust of an argument, a nuance, to conflate, a distinction, in a nutshell, to read between the lines, to put words in somebody's mouth, cherry-picking, to give the benefit of the doubt, to boil down to, to have an axe to grind, to hold water. Structure: reporting verbs with nuance and their fixed patterns — concede that, deny doing, accuse of doing, dismiss as.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

The gist -- the general meaning, without the detail
"Give me the gist in two sentences and I will decide whether I want the rest."
To paraphrase -- to say the same thing in different words
"If I paraphrase you badly, stop me — that is the whole point of doing it out loud."
To take out of context -- to repeat words in a way that changes what they meant
"Any sentence taken out of context can be made to sound indefensible."
To misrepresent -- to describe somebody's position inaccurately
"He misrepresented her twice and apologised for neither."
A caricature -- an exaggerated version of a position, easy to laugh at and easy to defeat
"What he attacked was a caricature, and the room enjoyed it."
To do justice to -- to represent something as well as it deserves
"Two minutes does not do justice to an argument that took her a decade."
The thrust of an argument -- the main direction and force of it, apart from the details
"I disagree with the detail and I accept the thrust of it."
A nuance -- a small difference of meaning that changes the whole
"The nuance is in the second clause, and it is the only part that matters."
To conflate -- to treat two different things as if they were one
"The article conflates cause and correlation for eight hundred words."
A distinction -- a difference between two things that are easily confused
"That is a real distinction and it is doing all the work in his argument."
In a nutshell -- stated in the shortest possible form
"In a nutshell: the evidence is good and the conclusion goes too far."
To read between the lines -- to understand what is implied but not stated
"Read between the lines and the statement is a resignation."
To put words in somebody's mouth -- to claim somebody said something they did not say
"I am not putting words in your mouth — tell me if I have."
Cherry-picking -- choosing only the evidence that supports you
"Both sides are cherry-picking from the same report, which is almost funny."
To give the benefit of the doubt -- to assume the more generous interpretation when you cannot be sure
"I would give her the benefit of the doubt on that sentence."
To boil down to -- to reduce to the one essential point
"It boils down to whether you trust the sample, and nothing else."
To have an axe to grind -- to have a private motive behind an apparently neutral position
"He has an axe to grind and he is still right about the numbers."
To hold water -- to be sound enough to survive examination
"The first half holds water. The second half does not."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is no neutral way to report what somebody said. She said, she conceded and she claimed can describe the same three seconds of speech, and each hands the listener a different verdict before they have thought about it. The verb is chosen first and examined afterwards, if at all.

Start with what the summary is for. If you want the gist, you will lose the detail, and that is an honest trade. What is not honest is cherry-picking the half that suits you, or building a caricature that gets a laugh. A caricature always misrepresents, and it is the most enjoyable form of dishonesty because the room rewards it immediately.

The technical failures are subtler. To conflate two things is to lose a distinction the other person was relying on; to quote a sentence out of context is to change its meaning while repeating it exactly. Both can be done in good faith by somebody in a hurry, which is why the professional habit is to say the summary out loud: let me paraphrase you, and stop me if I get it wrong. Nobody objects to being asked, and about one time in five they stop you.

The test that survives all of this is one sentence long. Would you use the same reporting verb about somebody you agreed with? If the answer is no, you have taken a side and called it reporting. It is perfectly reasonable to conclude that an argument does not hold water, that it boils down to one unexamined assumption, or that the person has an axe to grind. What is not reasonable is to smuggle that conclusion into a verb and present the result as a summary.

1. Why does the text say there is no neutral way to report speech?
A Because most people lie about what was said.
B Because the verb chosen hands the listener a verdict before they think.
C Because summaries always leave out detail.
2. What does the text say is dishonest, as opposed to merely lossy?
A Giving the gist rather than the detail.
B Cherry-picking and building a caricature.
C Paraphrasing out loud.
3. What does it mean to conflate two things?
A To treat them as one, losing a distinction the other person needed.
B To compare them carefully.
C To quote them out of context.
4. What is the test the text proposes?
A Whether the summary is short enough.
B Whether you would use the same verb about somebody you agreed with.
C Whether the other person objects.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reporting Verbs — the Verb Is the Verdict

GRAMMAR

English has dozens of reporting verbs and almost none of them is neutral. Each carries a judgement about the speaker's position, and each takes a FIXED pattern that has to be learned as a unit.

FormWhat it doesExample
concede · acknowledge
admit · grant + THAT		Granting a point. Acknowledge is the most neutral, concede implies pressure, admit implies fault. Choosing between them is already an editorial decision."She conceded that the figures were disputed."
contend · maintain
insist · argue + THAT		Asserting against disagreement. Maintain and insist hint that the person is holding a line against evidence; contend is the fairest of the four."He contends that the data were misread."
dismiss · reject
+ noun + AS		Rejecting without engaging. The pattern is fixed: dismiss SOMETHING AS SOMETHING. Never dismiss that and never dismiss as with no object."She dismissed the objection as irrelevant."
accuse sb OF + -ing
deny + -ing
admit TO + -ing		The three patterns that trip people at C1, because none of them takes an infinitive and Portuguese suggests one for all three."He accused her of misrepresenting him."
"She denied knowing."
"He admitted to reading only the summary."
allege · claim
point out · note		Allege and claim report without endorsing — both are audibly sceptical. Point out is the reverse: it asserts that the person was RIGHT, in three innocent-looking words."He claims he was not there."
"She points out that the date is wrong."

Learn the patterns as fixed units, because they do not follow from meaning: concede that, deny + -ing, accuse somebody of + -ing, dismiss something as, admit to + -ing, congratulate somebody on + -ing. And keep the professional test nearby: would you choose the same verb for somebody on your own side?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are reporting what somebody said, in public, while they are listening. Choose the version that works.

You want to say she granted a point under pressure:
A "She conceded to that the figures were disputed."
B "She conceded that the figures were disputed."
C "She conceded of the figures being disputed."
You want to report that he said he did not know:
A "He denied to have known about the meeting."
B "He denied knowing about the meeting."
C "He denied that to know about the meeting."
You want to report an accusation:
A "She accused him of misrepresenting her position."
B "She accused him to misrepresent her position."
C "She accused him for misrepresenting her position."
You want to report that he rejected an objection without engaging with it:
A "He dismissed the objection like irrelevant."
B "He dismissed that the objection was irrelevant."
C "He dismissed the objection as irrelevant."
Somebody says your summary was unfair. The best response is:
A "That is exactly what you said."
B "Then let me paraphrase you again, and stop me if I get it wrong."
C "You are putting words in my mouth."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Choose a debate you have followed where you disagree with one side. In about ninety seconds, report BOTH positions as fairly as you can — choosing each reporting verb deliberately and saying, once, why you chose it. Then apply the test out loud: would you have used the same verb if the positions were reversed? Answer honestly. The exercise is the answer, not the summary.
