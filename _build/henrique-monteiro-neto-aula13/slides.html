Chapter 3: The Code

The Verb Is Already a Verdict

Same sentence, three different judgements

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"She conceded that the figures were disputed."

"He contends that the data were misread from the start."

"She dismissed the objection as irrelevant."

"He accused her of misrepresenting his position."

All four could be written with said. What does each verb add that said does not — and is any of them neutral?

FormWhat it doesExample
concede · acknowledge
· admit + that		Grants a point, usually reluctantly. Admit implies fault; acknowledge is neutral; concede implies pressure. Choosing between them is already an editorial act."She conceded that the figures were disputed."
contend · maintain
insist · argue + that		Asserts under disagreement. Maintain and insist imply that the person is holding a line against evidence; contend is the fairest of the group."He maintains that nothing was hidden."
dismiss / reject
+ noun + AS		Rejects without engaging. The pattern is dismiss something AS something — never dismiss that."She dismissed the objection as irrelevant."
accuse of · deny + -ing
· admit to		The patterns that trip everybody. accuse somebody OF doing, deny DOING, admit TO doing — and none of these takes an infinitive."He accused her of misrepresenting him."
"She denied knowing about it."
allege · claim
point out · note		Allege and claim report without endorsing, and both are audibly sceptical. Point out is the opposite: it quietly says the person was RIGHT."He claims he was not there." (I doubt it)
"She points out that the date is wrong." (and it is)

There is no neutral reporting verb. Said comes closest, and even said is a choice when everybody around you is writing claimed. The professional habit worth acquiring is simply this: notice which verb you reached for, and ask whether you would use the same one for somebody you agreed with.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"She conceded to that the figures were disputed."
"She conceded that the figures were disputed."
"He denied to have known about the meeting."
"He denied knowing about the meeting."
"She accused him to misrepresent her position."
"She accused him of misrepresenting her position."

The patterns are fixed and they do not follow logic, so they have to be learned as units: concede that, deny + -ing, accuse somebody of + -ing, dismiss something as, admit to + -ing. The commonest error at C1 is reaching for an infinitive after any of them — and none of these five accepts one.

Chapter 4: Three Positions, One Summary

Three Positions, One Summary

A moderator who has to restate what three people said, fairly, while they listen

Dialogue

The Moderator and the Verb

N
The part of the job nobody believes is difficult is the summary. Three people have spoken for forty minutes and I have ninety seconds to say what each of them thinks, while all three are sitting there.
H
And any verb you choose is a judgement. If you say one of them conceded, you have told the audience she was under pressure.
N
Exactly that. Conceded, acknowledged and agreed report the same three seconds of audio and hand the listener three different scenes.
H
So what do you use when you genuinely do not want to take a side?
N
Said. And said is not neutral either, it is just the least loaded thing available. What I try hardest to avoid is a caricature, because a caricature always gets a laugh and it always misrepresents.
H
Do you ever check the summary with them afterwards?
N
During, if I can. I say let me paraphrase you and stop me if I get it wrong. Nobody ever objects to being asked, and about one time in five they stop me.
H
Which is a considerably higher error rate than any of us would admit to in ordinary conversation.
Comprehension

About Nora

1. What does Nora say is the difficult part of her job?
The summary — ninety seconds to say what three people think, while they are sitting there.
2. What does she say about conceded, acknowledged and agreed?
That they report the same three seconds of audio and hand the listener three different scenes.
3. What does she use when she does not want to take a side?
"Said" — which she says is not neutral either, only the least loaded thing available.
4. How often does somebody stop her when she paraphrases them?
About one time in five.
Listening 1

Ninety Seconds, Three Positions

A moderator summing up a panel. Sound first — no text.

1. What does the first speaker contend?
That the scheme worked and the cost is being exaggerated by its critics.
2. What did the second speaker concede?
That waiting times fell, before objecting to the design.
3. How does the moderator describe the third position?
She says he claims the data are unreliable — and she says which word she chose deliberately.
Listening 2

The Verb That Decides

An editor on the words a newsroom argues about. Sound first — no text.

1. Which verb does she say causes the most argument in the newsroom?
"Claimed" — because it tells the reader not to believe the person.
2. What test does she apply?
Whether she would use the same verb for somebody she agreed with.
3. What does she say about "point out"?
That it quietly asserts the person was right, and that it slips past everybody.
