Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

Handling Objections -- Disagreeing Without Breaking Anything

Why the first objection is almost never the real one, and how one word changes the temperature of a claim. Key words: an objection, pushback, a concession, leverage, a counterpart, scope, liability, a timeline, to back something up, to work around, to meet halfway, to hold your ground. Structure: modal verbs of certainty and diplomatic softening.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An objection -- a reason the other side gives for not agreeing yet
"The first objection is almost never the real one."
Pushback -- resistance from the other side, said out loud rather than hidden
"There was real pushback on the timeline, and I was glad to hear it."
A concession -- something you give up so that the other side can say yes
"I would rather make one concession on the date than touch the price."
Leverage -- something you have that the other side wants, which gives you room to negotiate
"The date is the only leverage we have left, so I am not giving it away first."
A counterpart -- the person on the other side who has the same job as you
"My counterpart there cannot defend the number to his own board."
Scope -- exactly what is included in the work, and what is not
"We could move the date, but the scope would have to change with it."
Liability -- legal responsibility if something goes wrong and somebody loses money
"You may want to look at the liability clause before we talk about price."
A timeline -- the plan of when each part of the work happens, from start to finish
"There was real pushback on the timeline, not on the product."
To back something up -- to support what you said with a number, a document or an example
"Can you back that up with a number? Then I will take it to my side."
To work around -- to find a way past an obstacle instead of removing it
"We cannot change their approval process, but we can work around it."
To meet halfway -- to agree on something between the two positions, so both sides move
"I would suggest we meet halfway on the timeline and hold the price."
To hold your ground -- to keep your position when the other side pushes, without being rude
"The ones who hold their ground on everything win the meeting and lose the account."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The note after the call is three lines long. The objection was the price, but the price is almost never the real one. The counterpart on the other side cannot defend the number to a board that was not in the meeting, which is a different problem with a different answer. There was also real pushback on the timeline, and that one is worth listening to.

What happens next depends almost entirely on the words. This will fix it and this might fix it are the same claim at two different temperatures, and only the second one survives being wrong. You must consider the audit risk and you may want to look at the audit risk before Thursday ask for exactly the same thing, and only the second one leaves the other person room to say no without losing face. Experienced negotiators use the weakest form that still says the thing, which sounds like weakness and is the opposite of it.

Then comes the arithmetic. We could move the date, but the scope would have to change with it. I would suggest one concession on the delivery date and none on the price, because the date is the only leverage left. Anything the other side claims, ask them to back it up with a number. And where the obstacle cannot be removed — an approval process, a liability clause the legal team will not touch — do not fight it: work around it. The people who hold their ground on everything win the meeting and lose the account; the ones who meet halfway on everything have nothing left by the second round.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Verbs and Temperature

GRAMMAR

The modal does not change the argument. It changes how sure you are, and how much room you leave the other person.

TemperatureWordExample
A promise you can be held towillThis will save your team two weeks.
Possible, and you are not suremight / may / couldThis might reduce your exposure.
Advice, with room to refuseyou may want to / you might want toYou may want to look at that clause.
Your recommendation, ownedI would suggest / I would ratherI would suggest we meet halfway on the date.
An offer with a conditionwe could ..., but ...We could move the date, but the scope would change.
Very strong — use with caremustIn English must sounds like an order to a client.
The form, alwaysmodal + plain verb, no to, no snot she must to sign, not it mights work

Never: She must to sign it · It mights work · You must consider our proposal (to a client) · This will solve all your problems (when you have not seen the data)

Stage 2: Put the Answer in Order

Order

A client has just told you the price is too high. Put your answer in a logical order.

?"So the number is higher than what you had budgeted."
?"It sounds like the issue may not be the number itself. Is that fair?"
?"You may want to look at the liability clause before we talk about price."
?"I would suggest we meet halfway on the timeline and hold the price."
?"We could start with one company, so you would have a real number in six weeks."
?"Can you back that up with a number? Then I will take it to my side."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want a client to look at a clause, and you are selling to them. You say:
A "You must consider the liability clause."
B "You may want to look at the liability clause before Thursday."
C "You must to look at the liability clause."
You have not seen their data yet, and you are asked whether the system will solve their problem. You say:
A "This will solve all your compliance problems."
B "This might solve most of it. I would need to see the volume before I promise anything."
C "I cannot say anything about that."
A client says the price is too high. The first move is:
A Explain immediately why the price is justified.
B "So the number is higher than what you had budgeted." Repeat it in their words, then name what may be underneath it.
C Offer a discount straight away.
Their approval process is slow and it is not going to change. You say:
A "We cannot change that, but we could work around it — let me show you how."
B "You must change your approval process."
C "Then there is nothing we can do."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Second round of a negotiation. The other side has pushed back on the price, the timeline and the scope, all three at once. In three minutes, say which one you will hold, which one you will give ground on, and which one you will work around — and say all three in a way that keeps the relationship for the next two years. Use at least four different modal verbs, and do not use must once.

Lesson 6 — Handling Objections

Talk
William Ury -- "The walk from no to yes" (TED, 18 min)

The man who wrote the book on negotiation, arguing that the way out of a deadlock is almost never a better argument. His move is the same one you practised today: step out of the two positions and name what is actually underneath them. Slow, deliberate American English, with a story running through the whole talk.

Tip: watch with English subtitles. Write down the one question he says changes a negotiation, and try it on the next objection you hear at work.
