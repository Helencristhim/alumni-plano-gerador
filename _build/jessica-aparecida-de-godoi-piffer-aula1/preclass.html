The Ten Minutes Outside the Room -- Small Talk That Does Not Stop

The presentations are over and nobody leaves. Key expressions: small talk, to strike up a conversation, an icebreaker, to have something in common, to hit it off, an acquaintance, to catch up, to follow up, small world, to bring somebody up to speed. Structure: the present perfect for experience, against the past simple.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Small talk -- light conversation with people you do not know well, before or after the real business
"The presentations were fine. It was the small talk afterwards that I found hard."
To strike up a conversation -- to start talking to somebody you have never met
"I struck up a conversation with a German student in the break."
An icebreaker -- the first thing you say to open a conversation with a stranger
"The coffee machine is a terrible icebreaker, and it works every single time."
To have something in common -- to share an interest or an experience with another person
"We had one thing in common: we had both studied Spanish in Argentina."
To hit it off -- to like somebody immediately, from the first minutes
"We hit it off in the coffee line and talked for the rest of the afternoon."
An acquaintance -- somebody you know a little, but who is not a close friend
"After three days he was not a friend, but he was a useful acquaintance."
To catch up -- to talk about everything that has happened since you last met
"We have not spoken since March, so we spent the first ten minutes catching up."
To follow up -- to contact somebody again after a first conversation
"I said I would send him the article, and I never followed up."
Small world -- what you say when you find an unexpected connection with somebody
"Your sister studied in Malta? Small world."
To bring somebody up to speed -- to give somebody the information they missed
"He arrived late, so I brought him up to speed in two sentences."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A conference room empties slowly. The presentations were good, the numbers held, nobody asked a question anybody could not answer. And then the schedule says lunch and networking, ninety minutes of it, and half the room quietly reaches for a phone. The part of the day that decides who remembers whom is the part nobody rehearses.

What makes that part hard is rarely vocabulary. It is a choice English forces on you every time you talk about yourself. I have worked with teams in three countries and I worked with a German team in 2020 are both true, and they are not the same sentence. The first one is a life so far, with no date, and English marks it with have plus the participle. The second one is a closed moment, and it has a date sitting in it, so the verb goes into the past simple. In Portuguese one form covers both, which is why the choice feels invisible until somebody points at it.

The test is short. Look for a finished time word: in 2020, last August, yesterday, three years ago. If one is there, the past simple wins, and no amount of have will rescue the sentence. If there is none, and the point is the experience rather than the day it happened, use the perfect. The words ever, never, already and before pull the perfect towards them, which is why Have you ever been to Chicago? is the natural question at a coffee table and Did you ever go to Chicago? sounds like the start of an investigation.

There is a second habit worth naming, and it has nothing to do with grammar. A conversation dies when an answer contains exactly what was asked and nothing more. Small talk stays alive on the extra detail: not two years, but two years here, and before that a German team and a Dutch one. That extra clause is the door. Somebody walks through it, finds that you have something in common, and forty minutes later you are no longer a name on a badge.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Somebody who knows nothing about you has just sat down. Say who you are, where you have worked, one place you have been and one place you have never been, and one thing you do that has nothing to do with your job. Finish with a question back. Not one word about tax.
