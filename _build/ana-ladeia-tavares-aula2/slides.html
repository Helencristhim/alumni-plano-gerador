Chapter 4: The Code

The Past That Did Not Happen

One sentence, two halves

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"If somebody had read clause seven, none of this would have happened."

"She could have proved it in a minute if she had kept the email."

"They would not have been liable if they had disclosed the second print run."

"Might things have turned out differently if anyone had picked up the phone?"

The blue half always sits next to if, and it is always the same shape. The red half changes one word, and that word is what makes the sentence certain, possible or merely imaginable.

JobFormExample
The condition that did not happenif + had + past participleIf somebody had read clause seven...
The result that did not happenwould have + past participle...none of this would have happened.
A result that was possible, not certaincould have / might have + past participleShe could have proved it in a minute.
Ordereither half can come first — comma only when if opensShe could have proved it if she had kept the email.
Spoken formcontractions everywhereIf she’d known, she wouldn’t have signed.
Questionwould / could / might + subject + have + participleWould the outcome have been different?
The two trapsnever would after if; never a past simple after hadNot if I would have known, not if I had knew. Say if I had known.

In one line: had plus participle on the if side, would have plus participle on the other. The if side never takes would.

The Note She Writes Before the Session

What was never donenobody read 7.2 · nobody disclosed the reprint
Our client does not want the book withdrawn and has never asked for that. She is asserting two things: a fee for the two editions that were not licensed, and a credit on every edition from now on. A compromise on the figure is acceptable; a compromise on the credit is not.
1. According to the note, what exactly did clause 7.2 allow, and which two editions broke it?
It allowed the hardback edition only. The paperback in March and the e-book in September went beyond it.
2. The note lists two things that were never done. Say each one back as a full sentence about what would have changed.
If somebody had read clause 7.2, the paperback would not have carried the photograph. If they had disclosed the reprint, the client would have found out in March instead of two years later.
3. Which point is open to a compromise and which one is not, and why would a lawyer write that down before walking in?
The figure is open; the credit is not. Writing it down first stops you from giving away the thing that matters while you are busy arguing about money.
Listening 1 · The Rights Manager

Nobody Reads Clause Seven

A rights manager at a publishing house, on the mistake that took two years. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say the licence covered, and what does she say everybody assumed instead?
It covered the hardback and nothing else. Everybody assumed that a cover licence covered the book, in every form it would ever take.
2. What does she say about the moment the paperback was approved?
Nine people saw the file and not one of them opened the licence, because by then the cover had stopped being a decision and had become a fact.
3. What does she say she would do differently now, and what does she say about blaming the designer?
She would put the limits of every licence on the front page of the file, in one line. She says blaming the designer would have been easy and would have changed nothing, because the designer was never the person who signed it.
Listening 2 · The Mediator

The Most Useful Ten Minutes of Any Session

A mediator on why he makes both sides say what would have gone differently. Sound first — no text.

1. What question does he ask both sides, and at what point in the session does he ask it?
He asks what they would have done differently, and he asks it after both opening statements, before anybody talks about money.
2. Why does he say the question works, even though nothing about the past can be changed?
Because it is the only question in the room that nobody can lose. Saying what you would have done differently is not the same as saying you were wrong, so people answer it honestly.
3. What does he say happens when one side refuses to answer it?
He says that side almost always ends up paying more, because a room that hears no regret has nothing to build a compromise on.
