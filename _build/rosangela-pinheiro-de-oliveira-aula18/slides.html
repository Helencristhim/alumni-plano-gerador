Chapter 3: If I Were You

Four Ways to Give Advice

From "it was nice" to advice somebody can use

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I'd recommend going in May, not in August."

"It's worth taking the boat, even for one day."

"If I were you, I'd stay two nights."

"In August it's too crowded, and the beach isn't big enough."

All four sentences give advice. Look at the green words: what shape does the verb take after recommend and after worth? What shape after I'd? And where does too sit, and where does enough sit?

FrameShapeExample
I'd recommend+ verb-ing (or + a noun)I'd recommend going in May. · I'd recommend the boat.
It's worth+ verb-ingIt's worth taking the boat. · It's not worth waiting two hours.
If I were you, I'd+ the plain verb (no "to")If I were you, I'd stay two nights. (never "I'd to stay")
too / enoughtoo + adjective · adjective + enoughIt's too crowded. · It isn't big enough.

After recommend and after worth, the verb wears -ing — the same shape as after "enjoy" and after "used for". After I'd, the verb is plain. And too goes in front of the adjective, while enough comes after it.

The Anatomy

Four Cards, One Recommendation

The Measuring Words

Too Much, or Not Enough

Two little words carry every honest review. Too means more than you want. Enough means the right amount — and not enough means less than you need.

too + adjective — more than you want

"In August it's too crowded."
"That street is too touristy for me."

adjective + enough — the right amount

"One day isn't long enough for Florence."
"In May the sea is warm enough to swim."

too goes in FRONT of the adjective. enough goes AFTER it. Say them out loud three times, and the order stops being a decision.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I recommend to go in May."
"I'd recommend going in May." — after "recommend", the verb wears "-ing".
"One day isn't enough long for Florence."
"One day isn't long enough for Florence." — "enough" comes AFTER the adjective.
"If I was you, I'd to take the boat."
"If I were you, I'd take the boat." — always "were", and a plain verb after "I'd".

Three little checks: after "recommend", is it -ing? Is enough after the adjective? And is it if I were you? Answer those, and these cannot beat you.

Expression of the Day

Honestly, I'd Skip It

Honestly? I'd skip it — it's a bit overrated.

= here is what I really think, and I am telling you as a friend

The word "honestly" tells the other person: this is not the guidebook talking, this is me. It is the most valuable sentence a traveler can hear, and it costs you four words.
And "a bit" makes it kind: "a bit overrated" is honest without being rude. Nobody is offended, and everybody remembers your advice.

Listening

Thirty Years on the Same Road

A driver who has heard every question about this coast, and knows which answer people actually need. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What is the question every passenger asks Bruno, and what does he say a good answer needs?
They all ask what they should see. He says a good answer is not a list of ten places: it is one opinion and one reason. He would rather tell you one thing that is worth your morning than ten things you will forget.
2. Which place does he call overrated, and what does he tell people to do instead?
The famous beach everybody photographs: in August it is too crowded and the sand is not big enough for the people on it. He tells them to skip it, take the early boat, and see the coast from the water instead.
3. What does Bruno say about tourists who only repeat what the guidebook says?
Their trip is fine, but it is somebody else's trip. The passengers who come back years later are the ones who took one honest recommendation from a real person, and he says nobody ever remembers a guidebook the way they remember a stranger at breakfast.
Listening 2

The First Time Somebody Asked Me

Diane is seventy, and for years every place she visited was "nice". Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. For years, what was the only thing Diane could say about the places she visited?
That they were "nice". Every city, every church, every dinner was nice, because it was the only word she had. She says she saw more than she could ever say, and that made her sound like a person with no opinions.
2. What happened at the hotel in Seville, and what did she answer?
A young couple asked her where they should eat. She almost said "everything is nice", but instead she said the restaurants on the square were pricey and a bit touristy, and that if she were them, she would walk two streets and eat with the local families.
3. What did the couple do the next morning, and what does Diane say that changed for her?
They came to her table and said it was the best dinner of their trip. She says that was the day she stopped being a tourist somebody helped, and became a traveler somebody asks -- and that six honest words did it, not perfect grammar.

Comprehension

About What Daniel Asked

1. Why does Daniel interrupt the coffee, and what is his problem?
He and Emma are going to the coast the next day and they have no idea what to do. He interrupts because he thinks she knows Italy.
2. What does Daniel's guidebook say about the famous beach, and how does he react to the opposite advice?
The book says it is the number one thing on the coast. When he hears it is overrated and too crowded, he is surprised and says nobody tells you that.
3. What does Daniel ask next, and what is Emma doing while they talk?
He asks what she would do with the afternoon instead. Emma is writing everything down.
4. What does Daniel say the conversation is better than, and what does that tell you about what he wanted?
Better than the guidebook. He did not want more information — he wanted one real person's opinion, with a reason. Nobody at that table asked for perfect English.

The Artifact

The Review She Wrote

GUEST REVIEW · HOTEL SAN NICCOLÒ, FLORENCE
Rosângela P. · Brazil · stayed 3 nights in May
★★★★☆ 4 out of 5
“Charming, and the roof is the reason to book”
A small, charming hotel two streets from the river. The rooms are simple and the breakfast room is tiny, but the view from the roof at seven in the morning is breathtaking — it was the highlight of our three days.
Two honest notes. The restaurant next door is pricey and rather touristy, so I'd recommend walking five minutes to the small square instead. And one night is not long enough here: if I were you, I'd stay at least two.
Would you recommend this hotel?Yes — it's worth booking early
1. She gives the hotel four stars, not five. Which two things stop it from being a five?
The rooms are simple and the breakfast room is tiny. Notice that every opinion in the review comes with a reason — that is what makes people trust it.
2. Find "long enough" in the review. Why not "enough long"? And what does the sentence advise?
"Enough" always comes after the adjective: long enough, big enough, warm enough. The sentence advises staying at least two nights, because one night is less time than you need there.
3. Now write your own review out loud: a hotel or a restaurant you really know. One good thing, one honest note, one recommendation.
For example: "A charming little place near the park. The staff are kind and the coffee is excellent. The tables outside are a bit noisy, so I'd recommend sitting inside. If I were you, I'd book on a Sunday — Saturday is too busy."

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can describe a place with the right word: charming, lively, touristy, overrated, breathtaking.
I can recommend something: "I'd recommend going early" — with "-ing" after "recommend".
I can say something is worth it or not: "It's worth taking the boat", "It's not worth waiting."
I can give personal advice with "If I were you, I'd..." and judge with "too" and "enough".
When somebody asks what I think, I give an opinion and a reason — and I do not apologize for my English.
