Lesson 18 -- Pre-class

What Do You Think? -- Recommending a Place

A breakfast room in Florence, a young couple with a guidebook, and the question you never expected to hear in English: "You know Italy -- what do you recommend?" Key words: a must-see, charming, breathtaking, lively, a highlight, overrated, touristy, pricey, to skip, disappointing. The grammar: turning an opinion into advice -- "I'd recommend going early", "It's worth taking the boat", "If I were you, I'd stay two nights", "It's too crowded", "It isn't big enough". Expression: Honestly? I'd skip it -- it's a bit overrated.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to every word and read the example. These are the words you need when somebody asks what a place is really like. Think of one real place for each word before you move on.

A must-see -- a place you really should not miss
"The cathedral is a must-see. Go early, before the line."
Charming -- pretty and warm in a small, old-fashioned way
"It's a charming little town, with three streets and one church."
Breathtaking -- so beautiful that you stop and stare
"The view from the top is breathtaking."
Lively -- full of people, music and energy, in a good way
"The square is very lively at night, with music until eleven."
A highlight -- the best part of a trip or a day
"The boat to the islands was the highlight of the week."
Overrated -- people say it is wonderful, but it is not that good
"Honestly, that famous beach is a little overrated."
Touristy -- full of tourists, and not very real anymore
"The main street is too touristy. Walk two blocks and it changes."
Pricey -- a little more expensive than it should be
"The restaurants on the square are pricey, but the food is good."
To skip -- to decide not to do something, and go on to the next thing
"I'd skip the museum and spend the afternoon on the water."
Disappointing -- worse than you expected
"The famous restaurant was disappointing: slow, and not very good."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions below.

It is a quiet May morning in Florence, and Rosângela is alone at a breakfast table with her second coffee. At the next table a young couple from Canada have a guidebook open between the plates: the coast tomorrow, one day, and forty things in the book. Then the young man turns around and says the sentence nobody has ever said to her in English: "You know Italy, don't you? What do you recommend?"

She puts the cup down and answers. "I'd recommend starting early — the road gets busy after ten. The little town on the hill is charming." He points at the famous beach, the number one thing in the book. "Honestly? I'd skip it. It's a bit overrated, and in summer it's too crowded — the sand isn't big enough for everybody. It's worth taking the boat instead: the view from the water is breathtaking. And if I were you, I'd stay two nights. One day isn't long enough."

Ten minutes, maybe twelve, and the young woman writes every word in her own notebook. Four years ago, in Paris, Rosângela was the one at that table with the book, waiting for her son to order for her. Nobody this morning was listening for her tenses. Advice is not grammar: it is one opinion and one reason, said out loud by somebody who was actually there.

"I'd recommend starting early." Why "starting" and not "to start"?
A Because the sentence is about the morning.
B Because after "recommend" the verb always wears "-ing" — never "recommend to start".
C Because "start" cannot be used with "to".
"In summer it's too crowded — the sand isn't big enough." What is the difference between "too" and "enough" here?
A "Too crowded" = more people than you want. "Not big enough" = less space than you need.
B They mean the same thing, and you can use either one.
C "Too" is for people and "enough" is for places.
"It's worth taking the boat." What does this sentence tell the other person?
A That the boat is free.
B That the boat is a good use of their time and money — and after "worth" the verb takes "-ing".
C That the boat is the only way to get there.
"If I were you, I'd stay two nights." Why "were", and why "stay" with no "to"?
A Because the sentence is in the past.
B Because "was" is only for questions.
C Because this frame is always "if I were you", for every person — and after "I'd" the verb is plain, never "I'd to stay".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Turning an Opinion Into Advice

GRAMMAR

An opinion becomes useful the moment you put it inside a frame. English has four of them, and they cover almost every recommendation you will ever give.

FrameShapeExample
I'd recommend+ verb-ing (or + a noun)I'd recommend going in May. · I'd recommend the boat.
It's worth+ verb-ingIt's worth taking the boat. · It's not worth waiting two hours.
If I were you, I'd+ the plain verb (no "to")If I were you, I'd stay two nights.
too + adjectivemore than you wantIn August it's too crowded. · That street is too touristy.
adjective + enoughthe right amount (or, with "not", less than you need)The sea is warm enough to swim. · One day isn't long enough.

The three rules to remember: (1) After recommend and after worth, the verb wears -ing — the same shape as after "enjoy" and after "used for". (2) In this frame it is always if I were you, for every person, and the verb after I'd is plain: "I'd stay", never "I'd to stay". (3) too goes in FRONT of the adjective, enough goes AFTER it: "too crowded", "big enough".

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Pick the best answer for each moment at the table.

A traveler asks when to visit the coast. You want to recommend May. You say:
A "I recommend to go in May."
B "I'd recommend going in May. In August it's too crowded."
C "May is nice."
They have one afternoon and ask what is worth doing. You say:
A "It's worth taking the boat. The view is breathtaking."
B "It's worth to take the boat."
C "The boat is worth."
They plan one single day in Florence, and you think that is not enough. You say:
A "One day isn't enough long."
B "If I were you, I'd stay two nights. One day isn't long enough."
C "If I was you, I'd to stay two nights."
The famous beach is on every postcard, but you did not like it. You want to be honest and kind. You say:
A "That beach is terrible. Don't go."
B "Honestly? It's a bit overrated. I'd skip it and take the boat instead."
C "I don't know. It was nice."
The restaurants on the main square cost a lot and are full of tourists. You say:
A "They're pricey and a bit touristy. I'd walk two streets and eat there."
B "They are very touristy people."
C "Sorry, my English is not good enough to explain."
You have given all your advice, and you want the conversation to continue. You say:
A "Okay, good luck." (and you look at your coffee)
B "And you? What did you like best so far?"
C "That's all I know, sorry."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt. There is no right or wrong version. Speak for 60 seconds, with no script.

Somebody is coming to your city for one single day and asks what they should do. Give two recommendations and one honest warning, and a reason for each one. Use "I'd recommend + -ing" at least once, "it's worth + -ing" or "if I were you, I'd..." at least once, and one "too" or "enough". Say what a place is really like: charming, lively, touristy, pricey, breathtaking, overrated. For seventeen lessons you were the one asking for help. This time, you are the one with the answer.
