Turning Point (Block Checkpoint)

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A turning point -- a moment when a situation starts to change in an important way
"Six months in Seoul was a turning point for my English."
A wake-up call -- something that suddenly shows you that you need to change
"Freezing in that meeting was a real wake-up call."
Out of your comfort zone -- in a situation that feels new, difficult or a little scary
"Presenting in English takes me out of my comfort zone."
A setback -- a problem that stops your progress for a while
"Losing that project was a setback, not the end."
To bounce back -- to become successful or confident again after a problem
"She bounced back after a terrible first audition."
To look back on something -- to think about something that happened in the past
"When I look back on that year, I see how much I changed."
To make up your mind -- to decide something after thinking about it
"I finally made up my mind to apply."
A stepping stone -- a stage that helps you get to something bigger
"This course is a stepping stone to Berklee Online."
To pay off -- to bring a good result after a lot of effort
"All those hours of practice finally paid off."
To come a long way -- to make a lot of progress since an earlier time
"You have come a long way since your first lesson."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In 2018, Pedro was working in TV in Brazil when he made up his mind to study color grading in Seoul. It was a turning point. He used to avoid conversations in English, but in Seoul he had no choice: he did not speak Korean, so every class was taught in English. It took him out of his comfort zone every single day.

In the first week, he said hardly anything. Then his teacher told him that he understood much more than he said. That was a wake-up call. There were a few setbacks after that, but he bounced back from most of them, and the six months paid off.

When he looks back on that trip today, he sees it as a stepping stone. He has used English at work almost every day since then, and he has come a long way. Now he is preparing an application for an online music program. If he got in, he would study songwriting first. Notice how each tool has a job: the past continuous sets the scene, used to shows a habit that stopped, the passive shows a process, and reported speech moves one step back in time: is becomes was.

1. “His teacher told him that he understood much more than he said.” What were the exact words of the teacher?
A “You understand much more than you say.”
B “You understood much more than you said, and I am sure that everybody in the class noticed it.”
C “You will understand more than you say.”
2. “He used to avoid conversations in English.” What does this mean?
A He still avoids them today, and he is a little nervous about the application video.
B He is used to conversations in English.
C He avoided them in the past, but he does not avoid them now.
3. Why does the text say “every class was taught in English” and not who taught?
A Because the teacher was not important to Pedro and he did not like the course.
B Because the process matters here, not the person.
C Because nobody taught the classes in Seoul, and the students had to learn everything alone from videos.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Toolkit So Far (Review)

GRAMMAR

No new rule today. First decide the job, then choose the tool.

The jobThe toolExample
Something true from the past until nowpresent perfect + for / sinceI have used English every day since 2019.
A story: the background and the eventpast continuous + past simpleI was working in TV when I decided to go.
A habit that stoppedused to + verbI used to avoid meetings in English.
An imagined situationif + past, would + verbIf I got in, I would study songwriting.
What somebody saidreported speech: one step back in timeShe told me that my English was good.
The process, not the personbe + past participleEvery class was taught in English.
How much better or worseslightly / much / far + comparativeMy English is much better now.
How many, how mucha few / a little / very few / hardly anyI had very few friends at first.

Never: I am working here since 2016 · she said me · was teach · more better · very few time

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

In the interview, they ask how long you have worked in TV. You started in 2016. You say:
A “I am working in TV since 2016, mostly on music shows like The Voice.”
B “I work in TV for 2016.”
C “I have worked in TV since 2016.”
A colleague asks what your teacher in Seoul said about your English. You say:
A “She told me that my English was better than I thought.”
B “She said me that my English was good, and that I had to talk more in class.”
C “She told that my English is good.”
You want to say that your English today is a lot better than in 2018. You say:
A “My English is more better than in 2018, especially when I talk about my work.”
B “My English is much better than it was in 2018.”
C “My English is very better now.”
The interviewer asks about a problem that stopped you for a while. You say:
A “I had a setback in the first month, but I bounced back.”
B “There was a wake-up call, but I paid it off.”
C “I had a stepping stone in the first month, so I stopped the course for a while.”

Reflection
