Lesson 3 · Defending Your Point

How Sure You Sound

In an argument, the difference between it is and it might be is not politeness. It is how much you are willing to defend.

Warm-Up

Will Anybody Still Write Code By Hand in 2035?

You are in the middle of a development course, so you are the person in this room who has to bet on the answer.

Take a side and hold it for a minute. Then one more sentence: which part of your answer are you sure about, and which part are you guessing?

Today’s Goal

Three Levels of Certainty

1. The Words

reliable, a shortcut, bias, overrated, hands-on, in practice...

2. One Structure

might, must, cannot, is likely to — the dial between sure and guessing

3. Your Two Minutes

one claim you will defend, one you only suspect — and the words that separate them

Sounding too certain about something you are guessing costs you the whole argument the moment somebody pushes. English has a dial for this, and today you get the dial.

Chapter 3: The Code

The Dial Between Sure and Guessing

One opinion, four settings

Grammar Discovery

Order These Four Sentences

"The tool might replace part of the job, but nobody knows which part."

"Junior developers are likely to use it every single day."

"That must be why the course starts with the fundamentals."

"It cannot be true that nobody reads the code any more."

Everything outside the red words is a plain statement. The red word is the only thing that decides how much you are promising, and how hard somebody can push back.

How certainFormExample
Possible, no more than thatmay / might / could + base formIt might replace part of the job.
Probablewill probably + base · is likely to + baseThey are likely to use it every day.
A conclusion from evidencemust + base formThat must be why the course starts there.
A conclusion that it is NOT socannot / can’t + base formIt cannot be true that nobody reads it.
Opening an opinionI would argue that... · It seems to me that... · As far as I can see...I would argue that the fundamentals matter more now.
Softening a claimtends to + base · in most cases · up to a pointA tool like that tends to help experienced people more.
The three trapscan ≠ might · cannot ≠ must not · one opinion opener, not twoNot it can happen tomorrow: say it might happen. Not he must not be a developer: say he cannot be. Not in my opinion I think: pick one.

In one line: the modal is the promise. Choose it on purpose, because that is what the other person will hold you to.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Somebody Who Does This For a Living

One conversation, two recordings, one real posting

Dialogue

A Coffee With a Working Developer

E
So you are halfway through the course. Tell me honestly, do you think learning the fundamentals is still worth it?
V
I would argue that it matters more now, not less. The tools are fast, but they are not reliable enough to trust without reading what they wrote.
E
You have a point, up to a point. But be careful, because that argument is starting to sound overrated in job interviews. Everybody says it.
V
Then let me say it differently. In practice, the people who get stuck on my course are the ones who rely on the tool before they can read the output.
E
That is better, because it is something I can check. And it might be true. It tends to be the pattern I see with new hires as well.
V
So would you say a shortcut is always a bad idea?
E
No, and I would not trust anybody who said that. A shortcut is fine when you could do the long way and chose not to. It cannot be fine when you could not.
V
That is the clearest version of it I have heard. And you, how do you keep up with all of it without going crazy?
Comprehension

About Erin

1. What warning does Erin give about the argument that fundamentals matter?
That everybody says it, so it is starting to sound overrated in job interviews. She agrees with it up to a point, but not with the way it is usually said.
2. Why does Erin prefer the second version of the argument to the first?
Because the second one is something she can check. She says it might be true and that it tends to be the pattern she sees with new hires.
3. What is Erin’s rule about shortcuts, and what does she say about people who reject them completely?
A shortcut is fine when you could do the long way and chose not to, and it cannot be fine when you could not. She says she would not trust anybody who said shortcuts are always bad.
Listening 1 · The Engineer

What Actually Changed

A senior engineer on a technology podcast. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say the tools actually replaced, and what did they not replace?
They replaced the slow, repetitive half of the work, the part he calls typing. They did not replace deciding what should be built or noticing when the answer is wrong.
2. What does he say happens to people who cannot read the output, and how sure does he sound about it?
He says they are likely to get stuck at exactly the moment the tool is wrong, and he is careful to say likely rather than certain, because he says he has only seen it on his own teams.
3. What is the one prediction he refuses to make, and why?
He refuses to say which jobs will disappear. He says anybody who gives you a list is selling you something, because nobody predicted the last change either.
Listening 2 · The Hiring Manager

What I Ask in the First Ten Minutes

A hiring manager describing how she runs a first interview. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say she is listening for in the first ten minutes?
Not what the candidate knows, but how they talk about what they do not know. She says the second thing predicts the first year better.
2. What answer does she say ends an interview badly, and what does she prefer instead?
Total certainty about everything ends it badly. She prefers a candidate who says what they would check first and how they would find out.
3. What does she say about candidates who say "I do not know"?
She says it is not a problem on its own. The problem is stopping there, and she is waiting for the sentence that comes after it.

Scenario: An interviewer asks the open question: where do you think your field is going, and what does that mean for somebody starting now? Take a position, separate clearly what you would defend from what you only suspect, back one part with something concrete, and finish with the question you would ask them back.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can say the same opinion at three different levels of certainty, on purpose.
I use might and could for what is possible, is likely to for what is probable, and must and cannot for what I am concluding.
I never say maybe it can happen tomorrow, and I never say he must not be, when I mean he cannot be.
I can concede one part of an argument and hold the rest, instead of agreeing with everything or nothing.
I know the words: reliable, a shortcut, bias, to rely on something, overrated, to replace, hands-on, to keep up with something, in practice, to have a point.
