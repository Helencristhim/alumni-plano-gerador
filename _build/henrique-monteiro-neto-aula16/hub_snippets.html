I Regret to Say, I Regret Saying — When the Form Changes the Meaning

A correspondent’s memoir of the one night she got wrong, and the verbs that mean one thing with -ing and another with to. Key expressions: to look back on, a vivid recollection, to come to terms with, to dwell on, wistful, to make amends, to have second thoughts, to set the record straight, a turning point, to linger, bittersweet, to ring true, to own up to, rose-tinted, to haunt, a lapse, self-serving, to take stock. Structure: verb patterns that change meaning — remember, forget, regret, stop, go on, try and mean + -ing or to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

To look back on -- to think about a period of your past from the present
"I look back on those years with more affection than I felt at the time."
A vivid recollection -- a memory so clear it feels as if it happened yesterday
"I have a vivid recollection of the room and none at all of the date."
To come to terms with -- to slowly accept something painful that cannot be changed
"It took her a decade to come to terms with what she had written."
To dwell on -- to keep thinking about something unpleasant for too long
"There is no point dwelling on a mistake you have already corrected."
Wistful -- gently sad when thinking about something you have lost
"The last chapter is wistful rather than bitter, which surprised me."
To make amends -- to do something to repair the harm you have caused
"The book is partly an attempt to make amends, and it says so."
To have second thoughts -- to start doubting a decision you had already made
"She had second thoughts about the chapter the night before it went to print."
To set the record straight -- to correct a false account that has been widely believed
"The second edition exists to set the record straight on one date."
A turning point -- the moment after which things are never quite the same
"That phone call was the turning point, although nobody knew it then."
To linger -- to stay longer than expected, in a place or in the mind
"The smell of the smoke lingered for days, and the guilt for years."
Bittersweet -- happy and sad at the same time
"Going back thirty years later was bittersweet, and she does not pretend otherwise."
To ring true -- to sound honest and believable
"The small details ring true. It is the big claims I would check."
To own up to -- to admit openly that you did something wrong
"He owns up to the small errors and is silent about the large one."
Rose-tinted -- seeing the past as better than it really was
"Every memoir of that decade is a little rose-tinted, including mine."
To haunt -- to keep returning to your mind and troubling you
"The question she failed to ask haunts the whole book."
A lapse -- a brief failure of judgment or attention
"It was a lapse, not a pattern, and the book treats it as one."
Self-serving -- told in a way that mainly protects the teller
"A self-serving memoir is easy to spot: nobody in it is ever wiser than the author."
To take stock -- to stop and think carefully about where you stand
"At sixty she took stock, and the book is what came out of it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When I look back on my years abroad, the night of the tanks is the one I remember telling most often. I have a vivid recollection of the hotel roof, the noise and the cold. I also remember thinking that I would never see anything like it again. What I did not remember to do, the following morning, was check who my only source really was.

I had tried to reach three people overnight and failed, so I tried quoting a man I had met once, to see whether his account held up. It did not. I forgot to mention that his brother was in the government, and I will never forget reading that sentence in print. My editor did not stop to argue. She simply read it back to me, and I stopped defending it.

I regret to say that the mistake lingered for years. I went on to cover the country for a decade, and I went on telling the story in a slightly rose-tinted version. Writing the book meant rereading every notebook, and it was the notebooks that set the record straight. I do not regret writing it. It was, in the end, my way to own up to a lapse and to make amends.

1. "I will never forget reading that sentence in print." What does the writer mean?
A She must remember to read the sentence again before the next edition.
B She never read the sentence at all.
C She read it, and the memory has stayed with her.
2. "My editor did not stop to argue." What did the editor NOT do?
A She did not pause in order to have an argument.
B She did not stop arguing once she had started.
C She did not argue for very long before hanging up the phone on the writer.
3. "I tried quoting a man I had met once." Why -ing here and not to?
A Because quoting him was physically very difficult to do on that particular morning.
B Because it was an experiment, to see whether it would work.
C Because it happened in the past.
4. "Writing the book meant rereading every notebook." What does meant express?
A That she intended to reread the notebooks and in the end decided against it.
B That the notebooks had a secret meaning.
C That rereading the notebooks was a necessary part of writing it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Verb Patterns That Change Meaning

GRAMMAR

Most verbs take either -ing or to, and the choice is just a fact about the verb. These seven are different: they take both, and the meaning changes. The wrong choice is still a correct English sentence — it simply says something else, which is why nobody stops you.

VerbThe two meaningsExample
remember / forget+ -ing = a memory of something already done.
+ to = a task that comes next.		"I remember meeting him." / "Don’t forget to call."
regret+ -ing = sorry about something you did.
+ to say / inform / tell = formal, sorry about news you are about to give.		"I regret saying it." / "We regret to inform you…"
stop+ -ing = the activity ends.
+ to = pause in order to do something else.		"He stopped smoking." / "He stopped to smoke."
go on+ -ing = continue the same activity.
+ to = move on to something new.		"She went on talking." / "She went on to win."
try+ -ing = an experiment.
+ to = an effort; it is hard.		"Try restarting it." / "I tried to open it."
mean+ -ing = involve, have as a result.
+ to = intend.		"It means waiting." / "I meant to tell you."

Rule of thumb: -ing looks back (the action is already happening or done); to looks forward (the action comes next). Watch regret: with ordinary verbs it takes -ing, and regret to belongs almost only with say, inform, tell, announce. And to regret something you did NOT do, use regret not + -ing: "I regret not checking sooner."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are talking about the past — your own and other people’s. Choose the version that says what you mean.

A colleague asks about your first night shift, twenty years ago. You recall it clearly:
A "I remember working that night."
B "I remember to work that night, and I still think about it."
C "I remembered to have worked that night."
You are chairing a meeting and must announce, formally, that a speaker has cancelled:
A "I regret saying that Dr. Lima cannot join us today, unfortunately, as planned."
B "I regret to say that Dr. Lima cannot join us today."
C "I regret to have said that Dr. Lima cannot join us."
You quit coffee last year. You say:
A "I stopped drinking coffee last year."
B "I stopped to drink coffee last year, and I sleep much better now."
C "I stopped for drinking coffee last year."
A friend's laptop will not connect. You suggest an experiment:
A "Try to restart the router, because I know it is very hard for you to do."
B "Try restart the router."
C "Try restarting the router."
You wanted to send a message yesterday and did not. You say:
A "I meant sending you a message yesterday, but the day got away from me."
B "I meant to send you a message yesterday."
C "I meant send you a message yesterday."
