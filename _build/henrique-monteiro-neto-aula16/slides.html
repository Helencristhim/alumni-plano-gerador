Chapter 3: The Code

Same Verb, Two Meanings

Remember, forget, regret, stop, go on, try, mean

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I remember standing on the roof that night."

"Please remember to check the date before you file."

"She stopped to look at the tanks."

"She stopped looking at the tanks."

Same verb in each pair. In which sentence did the action happen BEFORE the verb, and in which does it come AFTER?

VerbThe two meaningsExample
remember / forget+ -ing: a memory of something that already happened.
+ to: a task you must not miss.		"I remember standing there." / "Remember to check the date."
regret+ -ing: you are sorry about something you did.
+ to say / inform / tell: formal — you are sorry about what you are about to say.		"I regret writing it." / "I regret to say that it is late."
stop+ -ing: the activity ends.
+ to: you pause one activity IN ORDER TO do another.		"She stopped arguing." / "She stopped to argue."
go on+ -ing: continue the same thing.
+ to: move on to something new, later.		"He went on writing about it." / "He went on to cover the war."
try+ -ing: an experiment — do it and see if it helps.
+ to: an effort — it is difficult.		"Try calling the embassy." / "I tried to reach them all night."
mean+ -ing: involve, have as a result.
+ to: intend.		"It means rewriting the chapter." / "I meant to answer."

A useful rule of thumb: -ing looks back at something already happening or done; to looks forward to something that comes next. Try is the exception that fits less neatly — there, -ing is an experiment and to is an effort.

Dialogue

The Author and the Notebook

C
Here is the embarrassing part. I remember filing that story the same night. I would have sworn to it. The notebook says I filed it two days later.
H
So the memory was not just wrong, it was self-serving. Two days makes you slower, and less heroic.
C
Exactly. Which is why I stopped trusting the version I remember and went back to the paper.
H
Do you regret writing the book at all? Some people would have left that night alone.
C
No. What I regret is not checking my own notes sooner. I went on to rewrite three chapters once I did.
H
Then why keep the mistake in? You could simply have stopped to correct the dates and moved on.
C
Because the mistake is the most honest thing in the book. It shows how a memory improves a story, given enough time.
H
So the book owns up to the error and keeps it on display. That rings truer than a clean version would.
Comprehension

About Clara

1. What did Clara remember wrongly?
That she filed the story the same night; the notebook says two days later.
2. Does she regret writing the book?
No. She regrets not checking her own notes sooner.
3. What did she do once she checked them?
She went on to rewrite three chapters.
4. Why does she keep the mistake in the book?
Because it is honest: it shows how a memory improves a story over time.
Listening 1

Where the Two Disagree

The author, at a book event. Sound first — no text.

1. What happened to her diary?
She tried to keep one and gave up after a month.
2. What does she find when she goes back to the notebooks?
The sentences she remembers writing are not there; something flatter is.
3. What is her rule when memory and notebook disagree?
The page wins, and she says so in the text.
Listening 2

How a Historian Reads a Memoir

A historian on a radio panel. Sound first — no text.

1. Why should memoirs be read gratefully?
They record what nobody else could see, like the mood in a room.
2. Why should they be read suspiciously?
The author has the strongest interest in how the story ends.
3. Which mistakes do authors usually own up to?
The small, forgivable ones; the large ones go into the passive, or disappear.
