The Questions You Ask Back

A final round is fifty minutes of answers and five minutes of questions. Only one of those is graded twice.

Chapter 1: Warm-Up

Same Question, Three Ways

He has just said: “Do you have any questions for me?”

“No, I think you covered everything.”

“Who does this role report to?”

“Could you tell me who this role reports to six months from now?”

The first one says you were auditioning. The second one is a fair question that lands like an interrogation. The third one gets the same answer and leaves him talking.

The Big Idea

Four Blocks, Four Different Questions

BlockThe question that person is really answering
Technical depthDoes she still know how this works, or did she stop learning in 2015?
The failed programWhat does she do when she is wrong, and does she get defensive when I push?
The peer blockDo I want this person in the room on a bad Monday? One no here ends the process.
The deciderIs she choosing us, or is she just trying to be chosen?

Only the last block is answered by the questions you ask. That is why the five minutes at the end are not the polite part.

Chapter 3: The Question You Ask Back

Put a Frame in Front of It

Same information, completely different room

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"What does the role involve?" → "Could you tell me what the role involves?"

"When will the decision be made?" → "Do you know when the decision will be made?"

"Is this a new headcount?" → "I was wondering whether this is a new headcount."

"How did you measure that?" → "I would like to know how you measured that."

In every pair on the right, three things happened at once. The subject moved back in front of the verb. A little word disappeared. And one of them needed a brand new word that the direct question never had. Which is which?

What changesThe ruleExample
The framePut one of these in front: Could you tell me, Do you know, Would you mind telling me, I was wondering, I would like to know."Could you tell me ..."
The word orderAfter the frame, the subject goes back in front of the verb. Nothing is inverted any more."what the role involves" — never what does the role involve
Do, does, didThey disappear. They only exist to build a direct question, and the tense moves onto the main verb."How did you measure that?" → "how you measured that"
Yes-or-no questionsThere is no question word to carry them, so they take whether (or if). Use whether in writing and always before or not."Is this a new headcount?" → "whether this is a new headcount"
The full stopThe end of the sentence follows the frame, not the question inside it. A question frame takes a question mark: "Do you know when the decision will be made?" A statement frame takes a period: "I was wondering whether this is a new headcount."

The frame is what makes a hard question safe to ask. Everything behind it is just a normal sentence.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Could you tell me what does the role involve?"
"Could you tell me what the role involves?"
"I would like to know if is this a new headcount."
"I would like to know whether this is a new headcount."
"Do you know when will the decision be made?"
"Do you know when the decision will be made?"

Behind the frame, nothing is inverted. Does and did go home, will waits behind its subject, and a yes-or-no question needs whether before it can travel.

Yes or No Needs a Carrier

Turn each one into a question you could ask the person who decides

"Is this a new headcount?" → "I was wondering ___whether this is a new headcount or a backfill."
"Is there another round after this?" → "Could you tell me ___whether there is another round after this one?"
"Did the last person in this role leave the company?" → "I would like to know ___whether the last person in this role left the company."
"Where does compensation get discussed?" → "Do you know ___where compensation gets discussed?" (question frame, so the sentence ends with a question mark)
"What would be a red flag for you in this first year?" → "I would like to know ___what would be a red flag for you in this first year." (statement frame, so the sentence ends with a period)
Chapter 4: The Interview

The Last Ten Minutes

Marcus, VP of Safety, is the one who decides

Dialogue

The Final Block

M
We are close to time, so let me ask the one I always save for last. In your first ninety days here, what would you actually do?
L
Listen, mostly. I would sit through three review cycles before I changed anything, because I have been wrong about a system in my first month before and it cost us a quarter.
M
That is a fair answer. All right, we have about eight minutes. Do you have any questions for me?
L
I have four. Could you tell me who this role reports to six months from now? The reporting line on the posting and the one Dana described are not the same.
M
Good catch, and nobody has asked me that yet. It reports to me today, and to the VP of Research once we fill that seat in the spring.
L
Thank you. I was wondering whether this is a new headcount or a backfill, and how you would know, at the end of the first year, that the hire had worked.
M
New headcount. And the honest answer to the second one is that safety stops being the last stop before launch and starts being part of the design. I do not have a number for that yet, which is arguably the hardest thing about the job.
L
That is useful. Last one, and I will keep it short. Do you know when the team expects to decide, and whether there is another round after this one?
M
Two weeks, and no, this was the last one. Dana will send you the timeline tonight. Take anything about the offer itself to her, not to me.
Comprehension

About Marcus

1. Who does Marcus say the role reports to today, and who will it report to later?
It reports to him today, and to the VP of Research once that seat is filled in the spring.
2. What does Marcus say he does not have yet?
A number for measuring whether safety has stopped being the last stop before launch and become part of the design.
3. What does Marcus say about the timeline, and who should she talk to about the offer?
Two weeks, and this was the last round. Dana will send the timeline that night, and anything about the offer goes to Dana rather than to him.
Listening

The Night Before

Dana, the recruiter, leaves a voice note about how Thursday works. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does Dana say about the peer block?
That it is not a formality and counts as much as the block with Marcus: if that person says no, the process does not move forward.
2. How many questions does Dana say to bring, and what should they not be about?
Four, not one, and they should not be spent on things that could have been found on the company website.
3. What is the one subject Dana says not to open on Thursday, and where should it go instead?
Compensation. It should go to Dana on Friday, because that is what she is there for.
Listening 2

The Last Five Minutes

An interview coach on the questions candidates never ask. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does the coach say the interviewer hears when a candidate has no questions?
That the candidate was auditioning rather than deciding, and that nobody senior is only auditioning.
2. What two things does he say change inside the sentence once the frame is there?
The word order goes back to normal, subject before verb, and the little do or does at the front simply disappears.
3. Why does he say compensation should go to the recruiter?
Because in an American process that is her job and not his, and asking him makes the whole hour look like it was about the number.

The Answer That Does Not Match

Keywords:

Free Practice

The Whole Final Block

Scenario: Twelve o'clock, the last block, and the VP is the one who decides. Answer his hard question first. Then he asks whether you have questions, and the next eight minutes belong to you: four of them, each with a frame, and at least one that could still make you say no.

No chips, no notes. The room is yours!
