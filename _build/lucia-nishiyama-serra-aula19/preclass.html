Lesson 19 -- Pre-class

Milestone -- The Simulated US Interview: The Questions You Ask Back

A full final round for an AI safety role, and the five minutes at the end that decide as much as the fifty before them. Key words: hiring loop, onsite, shortlist, to probe, red flag, dealbreaker, reporting line, compensation. Structure: indirect questions -- the frame in front, the subject back before the verb, the do that disappears, and the whether that carries a yes-or-no question.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. These are the words an American recruiter uses about you when you are not in the room.

Hiring loop -- the full series of interviews a company runs before it decides
"The hiring loop took five weeks from the first call."
Onsite -- the final and longest round, several interviews on the same day
"The onsite runs for three hours, with one break."
Shortlist -- the small group of candidates still in the running after the early rounds
"You are one of three people left on the shortlist."
To probe -- to keep asking about one point to see how deep the answer really goes
"Expect her to probe the failed program hard."
Red flag -- a small sign in an answer that makes the interviewer worry about something bigger
"Blaming the whole team was the only red flag in the hour."
Dealbreaker -- the one condition that would make you say no, however good the rest is
"Moving to another country was the only dealbreaker for me."
Reporting line -- who a person answers to, and who answers to that person
"The reporting line on the posting is out of date."
Compensation -- the whole package a company pays: salary, bonus, equity and benefits together
"I would rather discuss compensation with the recruiter."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

A recruiter sends the agenda the night before the last block of the hiring loop. Three candidates are still on the shortlist, the onsite runs for three hours, and one of the interviewers is there to probe a program that failed six years ago. The email ends with a warning: the reporting line on the job posting is out of date, so ask the VP about it rather than the recruiter. What almost nobody prepares for is the last ten minutes, when he asks whether the candidate has questions. That block is graded like any other, and the answer that costs the most is the polite one: no, I think you covered everything. What he hears is somebody who came to be chosen rather than to choose, and at a senior level that is a red flag of its own. So the questions matter, and the shape of them matters as much. A direct question in that moment lands hard. Who does this role report to sounds like an interrogation. Put a frame in front of it and the same question becomes a conversation: could you tell me who this role reports to. Notice what happens behind the frame. The subject goes back in front of the verb, so it is what the role involves and never what does the role involve. The little do, does or did simply disappears, because it only existed to build a direct question, and the tense moves onto the main verb: how did you measure that becomes how you measured that. A question with no question word cannot travel on its own, so it takes whether: is this a new headcount becomes I was wondering whether this is a new headcount. And the end of the sentence follows the frame rather than the question inside it, which is why could you tell me ends with a question mark and I was wondering ends with a period. One last piece of advice from every American recruiter. Ask about the first year, about what makes the job hard, about anything that would be a dealbreaker for you. But leave compensation to the recruiter, because asking the person who decides makes the whole hour look like it was about the number.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions

GRAMMAR

One frame in front, and four things change behind it.

What changesThe ruleExample
The framePut one of these in front of the question: Could you tell me, Do you know, Would you mind telling me, I was wondering, I would like to know."Could you tell me ..."
"I was wondering ..."
The word orderBehind the frame, the subject goes back in front of the verb. Nothing is inverted any more."what the role involves"
never what does the role involve
Do, does, didThey disappear. They only exist to build a direct question, and the tense they carried moves onto the main verb."How did you measure that?"
→ "how you measured that"
Yes-or-no questionsThere is no question word to carry them, so they take whether (or if). Use whether in writing and always before or not."Is this a new headcount?"
→ "whether this is a new headcount"
The full stopThe end of the sentence follows the frame, not the question inside it. A question frame takes a question mark: "Do you know when the decision will be made?" A statement frame takes a period: "I was wondering whether this is a new headcount." And note the fixed form: it is would you mind telling me, never would you mind to tell me.
