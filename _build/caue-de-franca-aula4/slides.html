Chapter 4: The Code

Stop Watching or Stop to Watch?

Four verbs, two meanings each

Grammar Discovery

Same Verb, Different Meaning

"I stopped watching dubbed movies years ago."

"In the middle of the episode, she stopped to write down a new word."

"Try switching the subtitles to English for a week."

"I remember watching this cartoon as a kid. Remember to choose English tonight!"

Look at the blue verbs and the red words after them. In sentence 1, does he still watch dubbed movies? In sentence 2, did she stop the episode forever, or for a moment, to do something? In sentence 4, the same verb appears twice: one is a memory, one is a task.

JobFormExample
Stop + -ingyou quit an activityI stopped watching dubbed movies.
Stop + toyou pause one activity to do anotherShe stopped to write down a word.
Remember / forget + -inga memory of something in the pastI remember watching it as a kid.
Remember / forget + toa task: something you need to doRemember to choose English.
Try + -ing / try + to-ing: an experiment · to: an effort, often difficultTry switching the subtitles. I tried to follow the jokes.
The trapstop + to does not mean quitNot I stopped to watch dubbed movies. Say I stopped watching.

In one line: -ing looks at the activity itself or a memory; to looks forward, to a purpose or a task.

Listening 1 · A Language Teacher

Three Steps, One Month

A British teacher explains how she moves her students from subtitles to no subtitles. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she ask her students to do in the first week?
Keep the original audio and switch the subtitles to English.
2. Why does she ask them to rewatch something they know?
Because they know the plot, so they can focus on the language.
3. What does she tell students who feel tired in the first days?
That it is normal, and it gets easier after a week or two.
Listening 2 · A Professional Subtitler

Behind the Captions

An Australian subtitler explains what closed captions are and who uses them. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the difference between subtitles and closed captions, according to him?
Captions also describe sounds, like music or a door that slams.
2. Who were closed captions first made for?
For people who are deaf or hard of hearing.
3. Why do many other people use them today?
Because of background noise, fast dialogue and different accents.
Listening 3 · A Mom and Her Cartoons

Same Cartoon, New Language

An American mother talks about how her kids are learning Spanish with cartoons. Sound first — no text.

1. Which cartoons does she put in Spanish?
The ones her kids already know and love in English.
2. Why does that work, in her opinion?
They already know the story, so they pay attention to the words.
3. What surprised her last month?
Her son used a Spanish expression from a cartoon in a real conversation.
