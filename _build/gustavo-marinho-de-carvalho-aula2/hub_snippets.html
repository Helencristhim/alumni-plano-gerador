Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A headline -- the title of a news story, printed in large letters at the top
"Most people never read the article. They read the headline and stop there."
Coverage -- the amount of attention the news gives to a subject
"The story got three days of coverage and then it disappeared."
To report -- to tell people about something that has happened
"Two newspapers reported the same numbers and reached opposite conclusions."
A source -- the person or document that information comes from
"She never publishes anything without a second source."
An outcome -- the result at the end of a process or an event
"Nobody knows the outcome yet, and the vote was six weeks ago."
To take place -- to happen, at a particular time or in a particular place
"The meeting took place on Tuesday morning, in their London office."
Widespread -- happening in many places, or among many people
"The problem is not in one city. It is widespread."
To point out -- to say something so that other people notice it
"She pointed out that the date had already been moved twice."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways of talking about the same piece of news, and English keeps them apart with the verb. The first way puts the event in a finished moment. The vote took place on Tuesday. I read about it on Friday morning. She pointed out the problem in the call yesterday. Tuesday, Friday morning and yesterday are all over, and the sentence says so.

The second way says nothing about when. It says that something is different now. Prices have gone up since January. They have already announced the new plan. I have read three articles about it this week. Nobody is telling you the day, and that is the point: what matters is the result you are living in, not the moment it started. This week is not finished either, so it belongs here too.

And then there is the mistake that a Brazilian speaker makes almost every time, because Portuguese lets you do it. I have read it yesterday does not exist in English. The moment you name a finished time — yesterday, last Tuesday, in 2022 — you have to use the first shape: I read it yesterday. Nothing else in the sentence changes. It is not a question of politeness or of level: the two shapes simply cannot share a finished time.

1. “Prices have gone up since January.” What does this shape tell you?
A That it happened in January and then stopped.
B That something is different now, and the sentence does not name the day.
C That it will probably happen next January.
2. Why does the text say “this week” belongs with the second shape?
A Because a week is longer than a day.
B Because news is always weekly.
C Because this week is not finished yet.
3. According to the text, what makes “I have read it yesterday” impossible?
A The sentence names a finished time, and that shape cannot take one.
B The verb read is irregular.
C It is correct, but too formal for a call.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Simple vs Present Perfect

GRAMMAR

The question is not when it happened. The question is whether the sentence names the moment.

JobFormExample
A finished moment, and you say whenpast simpleThe vote took place last Tuesday.
A finished moment, and everybody knows whenpast simpleI read it on Friday morning.

News that is new to the listenerhave / has + past participleThey have announced a new plan.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The client asks whether you saw the story. It was published on Monday. You answer:
A Yes, I have read it on Monday.
B Yes, I read it on Monday, and I have been following it since then.
C Yes, I am reading it on Monday.
You want to say that the situation is different now, without naming a day. You say:
A A lot has changed since the vote.
B A lot changed since the vote.
C A lot is changing since the vote.
The client brings up a political story from your country and asks what you think. The safest strong move is:
A Give your verdict immediately, so that she knows where you stand.
B Say that you never talk about the news and change the subject.
C Say what happened, say what has changed since then, and ask what the reaction was there.
You have only read the headline and nothing else. The honest and useful answer is:
A I have only seen the headline, so tell me what the coverage has been like there.
B Nothing, because you should never admit that you have not read it.
C I read all about it and I agree with the newspaper.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Pick one story you read this week, from anywhere in the world. Say what happened and when, say what has changed since then, say what you still do not know, and finish with one question you would ask a client in England about it.
