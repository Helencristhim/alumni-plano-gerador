Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

The Passive Voice -- How a System Is Described from the Inside

Every official document in English leaves the people out, and it does that on purpose. Key words: an application, a requirement, to evaluate, to select, criteria, to undergo, to submit, to get something done, it is widely believed that, to be considered a. Structure: the passive voice in the present, past and present perfect.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An application -- the whole package you send when you formally ask to be accepted somewhere
"My application was submitted three days before the deadline."
A requirement -- something you have to provide or do before you can even be considered
"A recent photograph is a requirement, not a suggestion."
To evaluate -- to look at something carefully in order to judge how good it is
"Every paper is evaluated by two examiners who never meet."
To select -- to choose a small number out of a much larger group
"Four hundred candidates are selected out of eighty thousand."
Criteria -- the list of things a decision is officially based on
"The criteria are published in advance, and they do not change."
To undergo -- to go through something long or difficult that is done to you
"Every candidate undergoes the same two days of examinations."
To submit -- to formally send a document to the people who will decide
"Applications must be submitted before the twentieth of November."
To get something done -- an informal way of saying something was arranged or completed for you
"I need to get my documents translated before Friday."
It is widely believed that -- reports what most people think, without saying who those people are
"It is widely believed that the second day is harder than the first."
To be considered a -- to be treated by everybody as belonging to a certain category
"It is considered a fair system by almost everybody who has been through it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences can carry exactly the same fact and still make different points. Eighty thousand people take the exam is about the people. The exam is taken by eighty thousand people is about the exam. Nothing has been added or removed — what changed is which one sits at the front, and English marks that change with two pieces and only two: a form of be, and the past participle.

Once you have those two pieces, every tense follows the same pattern. Present: the exam is taken. Past: the form was submitted. Still counting: the results have already been published. With a modal: applications must be submitted — and notice that be does not disappear after the modal, which is the single most common mistake in this structure. In speech there is an informal version built with get: I got accepted, he got eliminated.

The interesting question is not how to build it. It is when to use it, and the answer is simple: use it when the doer does not matter, is obvious, or is unknown. That is why every notice, every regulation and every status page is written this way — the office is telling you that no individual decided anything. When the doer does matter, bring it back with by: candidates are selected by a committee of three. And it is always by. The Portuguese por becomes by here, never for.

1. What two pieces does every passive sentence need?
A A modal verb and an object.
B A form of be and the past participle.
C The word by and a plural noun.
2. The text calls one thing the single most common mistake in this structure. Which?
A Dropping be after a modal, as in must submitted.
B Using the present instead of the past.
C Putting the doer at the front.
3. When does the text say you should use the passive?
A When you want to sound more polite.
B When the doer does not matter, is obvious, or is unknown.
