Lesson 14 -- Pre-class

The Memo That Changed the Deal

One two-page memo, one claim nobody disclosed, and a deal that survived on new terms. Key expressions: an executive summary, a caveat, to mitigate, exposure, a stakeholder, to brief, an oversight, contingent on, the bottom line, escrow, a warranty, a valuation, to err on the side of caution, a closing date. Structure: nominalization and formal written register -- our review of, the seller's failure to, following, prior to, due to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to a memo that recommends a deal, warns about it and protects it.

An executive summary -- the short section at the top of a document that gives the main points
"The board reads the executive summary and, if we are lucky, the first page after it."
A caveat -- a warning that something is only true under certain conditions
"The figure comes with one caveat: it is based on the documents we were shown."
To mitigate -- to make a risk or its effects less serious
"The escrow does not remove the risk, but it does mitigate it."
Exposure -- the amount of risk or loss you could face
"Our exposure on the tax claim is roughly four million dollars."
A stakeholder -- a person or group affected by a decision and interested in its result
"Every stakeholder wants the deal closed, and none of them wants the claim."
To brief -- to give somebody the information they need before they act
"I need to brief the CFO before the board meets at nine."
An oversight -- a mistake made because something was not noticed
"The seller calls it an oversight, and we would like to believe that."
Contingent on -- depending on something else happening first
"Board approval is contingent on the completion of the audit."
The bottom line -- the most important fact, or the final result of everything
"The bottom line is that the deal still works, but not at this price."
Escrow -- money held by a third party until the conditions of a deal are met
"Four million stays in escrow until the claim is finally resolved."
A warranty -- a promise in a contract that certain facts are true
"If one warranty is wrong, the buyer starts asking about all the others."
A valuation -- an estimate of how much a company or asset is worth
"The valuation assumed that the subsidiary had no hidden liabilities."
To err on the side of caution -- to choose the safer option when you are not sure
"When the numbers are uncertain, we err on the side of caution."
A closing date -- the day on which a deal is formally completed
"The closing date moved from March to May, and nobody was happy about it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Spoken English is built out of verbs: we went through the accounts, we found a claim, we think they should drop the price. Written English for a board is built out of nouns. The same content becomes: Following our review of the accounts, we recommend a reduction in the price. Nothing new was added. The actions simply turned into things.

That change is called nominalization, and it does three jobs. It puts the important event at the front of the sentence, where the reader looks first. It lets you pack a whole clause into two words: the seller's failure to disclose the claim instead of the fact that the seller did not tell us about the claim. And it sounds impersonal, which is exactly what a recommendation to a board should sound like.

Adjectives can do it too. They are likely to resist becomes the likelihood of resistance; the figure may not be accurate becomes the accuracy of the figure. And the small words change with them: following replaces after, prior to replaces before, due to replaces because. All three need a noun after them, never a full clause.

The danger is the same density that makes it work. A paragraph made only of nouns hides who did what, and out loud it is almost impossible to follow. The professional skill is to switch: nouns on the page, verbs in the meeting.

1. Why does a memo to a board use nouns like review and reduction instead of verbs?
A Because verbs are informal and not allowed in legal writing.
B Because the nouns put the main event at the front and pack a clause into two words.
C Because nouns are easier to translate into other languages.
2. Which version is correct in formal written English?
A Following we reviewed the accounts, we recommend a reduction.
B Following our review of the accounts, we recommend a reduction.
C Following the accounts were reviewed, we recommend a reduction.
3. What is the noun from the adjective likely?
A likelihood
B likeliness of
C likeness
4. According to the text, what is the danger of nominalization?
A It makes the memo too short for a board.
B A paragraph made only of nouns hides who did what and is hard to follow out loud.
C It makes the writer sound too personal.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Nominalization and Formal Register

GRAMMAR

Your first language nominalizes too, so the idea is not new. What is new is the preposition after the noun, and the habit of switching back to verbs the moment you start speaking.

PatternWhen it happensExample
verb → nounThe action becomes a THING: review, approval, failure, reduction, disclosure, acquisition. Once it is a noun it can sit at the front of the sentence, where the reader looks first."we reviewed" → "our review of the accounts"
adjective → nounA quality becomes a thing you can measure or discuss: likely → likelihood, available → availability, willing → willingness, accurate → accuracy."they are likely to resist" → "the likelihood of resistance"
the X of Y · Y's XThe doer and the object come back as of or as a possessive. This is how a nominal sentence keeps the facts without a single verb doing the work."the seller's failure to disclose the claim"
preposition instead of conjunctionfollowing replaces after, prior to replaces before, due to and in light of replace because. A preposition needs a noun after it, never a full clause."Following our review, we recommend..." not "following we reviewed"
a reduction IN, an increase INThe nouns of change take in, not of: a reduction in the price, an increase in the risk, a delay in the closing. This is the preposition Portuguese gets wrong."a reduction in the purchase price"

One habit for this week: take the first paragraph of any memo or contract summary you wrote in Portuguese and write its first sentence in English twice -- once with nouns for the page, once with verbs for the meeting. If both versions say the same thing, you have it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are writing for a board, or presenting to one. Choose the version a professional would actually use.

1. You are writing the first line of a memo to the board:
A We looked at the accounts and we think the price should go down.
B Following our review of the accounts, we recommend a reduction in the price.
C Following we looked at the accounts, we recommend to reduce the price.
2. You want to say formally that the seller did not disclose the claim:
A The seller's failure to disclose the claim is a concern.
B The seller's fail of disclosing the claim is a concern.
C The seller failure disclosed the claim is a concern.
3. You need the formal preposition for before in a memo:
A Before to the closing, the escrow must be agreed.
B Prior to the closing, the escrow must be agreed.
C Prior the closing, the escrow must be agreed.
4. You are presenting the same memo out loud in a meeting:
A Following the completion of our review, our recommendation is a reduction.
B We went through the accounts, found a claim nobody mentioned, and we think the price has to come down.
C The review completion resulted in a reduction recommendation.
5. You want the noun of change with the right preposition:
A We propose a reduction of the price.
B We propose a reduction in the price.
C We propose a reduction on the price.
