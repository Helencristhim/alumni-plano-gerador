Chapter 3: The Code

When the Action Becomes a Thing

Nominalization and formal written register

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Following our review of the accounts, we recommend a reduction in the price."

"The seller's failure to disclose the claim raises a serious question."

"Board approval is contingent on the completion of the audit."

"The likelihood of resistance from the seller is high."

Every orange word has a verb or an adjective hiding inside it. Find each one. Then look at what comes right after the orange words: which small word keeps appearing, and what does it connect?

FormWhat it doesExample
verb → nounThe action becomes a THING: review, approval, failure, reduction, disclosure, acquisition. Once it is a noun it can sit at the front of the sentence, where the reader looks first."we reviewed" → "our review of the accounts"
adjective → nounA quality becomes a thing you can measure or discuss: likely → likelihood, available → availability, willing → willingness, accurate → accuracy."they are likely to resist" → "the likelihood of resistance"
the X of Y · Y's XThe doer and the object come back as of or as a possessive. This is how a nominal sentence keeps the facts without a single verb doing the work."the seller's failure to disclose the claim"
preposition instead of conjunctionfollowing replaces after, prior to replaces before, due to and in light of replace because. A preposition needs a noun after it, never a full clause."Following our review, we recommend..." not "following we reviewed"
a reduction IN, an increase INThe nouns of change take in, not of: a reduction in the price, an increase in the risk, a delay in the closing. This is the preposition Portuguese gets wrong."a reduction in the purchase price"

Portuguese does this too, so the nouns are not the hard part. The hard part is the preposition after them, and the discipline of switching back to verbs the moment you stand up to speak.

Register

Same Content, Two Registers

The page first, then the meeting

"Following our review of the accounts, we recommend a reduction in the price."

On the page. The recommendation is in the first line, and nobody is the subject.

"We went through the accounts, and we think the price has to come down."

In the meeting. Same content, with people doing things.

"The seller's failure to disclose the claim raises a serious question."

On the page. Formal, impersonal, and nobody is accused of anything.

"They did not tell us about the claim, and that worries me."

In the meeting. Direct, personal, and a lot easier to follow.

The page rewards density, because the reader can go back. A room does not, because a listener cannot. Write it with nouns, then say it with verbs.

Listening 1

Nouns on the Page, Verbs in the Room

A senior partner on how she reads a junior lawyer's memo. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she look for first in a memo?
The recommendation, in the first paragraph.
2. Why does she say nouns work on the page?
They pack a whole event into two words and put it at the front of the sentence, where the reader can find it.
3. What does she tell lawyers to do when they present the memo out loud?
Turn the nouns back into verbs.
Listening 2

The Memo I Still Remember

A chief financial officer on the two pages that saved his company four million dollars. Sound first — no text.

1. How long was the memo he remembers best, and what was in its first line?
Two pages; the first line said they recommended going ahead, subject to three conditions.
2. How did the seller react to the escrow, and what did they finally accept?
They threatened to walk away twice, and in the end accepted three million in escrow instead of four.
3. What happened to the tax claim eighteen months later?
It was settled for almost exactly the amount held in escrow.
