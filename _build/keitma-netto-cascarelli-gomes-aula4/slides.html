Chapter 4: Finished or Still True

Two Tenses, One Question

Is the period over, or is it still running?

Grammar Discovery

Why Do These Verbs Change Shape?

"In January the team took over two more countries."

"Year to date, not one report has been late."

"They rolled out the template in March, and they bought nothing."

"Since then the team has streamlined eleven other steps."

Look at the time expression in each sentence, not at the verb. In the orange ones, is that period over? And in the blue ones?

ShapeWhen you need itExample
past simple
took, rolled out, was		the action is finished and the period is finishedWe rolled out the template in March.
present perfect
have / has + past participle		the period is still runningYear to date, not one report has been late.
present perfectit happened, the result is still true, and when does not matterTwo people have been promoted. Nobody has left.
since · forsince + a point in time · for + a length of timesince March · for nine months
ever · never · already · yetthese live with the present perfectHave you ever presented in English? · It has not arrived yet.
the first trapa finished time word forces the past simple, however recent it feelsNot I have signed it yesterday. Say I signed it yesterday.
the second trapago always takes the past simpleTwelve months ago the team had a problem.

In one line: ask whether the period is over. If it is, use the past simple. If it is still running, use the present perfect.

Chapter 5: Real Speed

A Year, Said Out Loud

Two voice notes and one scorecard

Listening 1 · A Voice Note

Do Not Give Them a List of Months

A finance director recording advice before a year-end review. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say happens by minute four of a list of months?
Everybody has stopped listening.
2. What are the two sentences he wants instead, and which tense is each one in?
The first is what changed, in the past — we rolled out the new template in March. The second is what is still true today, not in the past — since then, not one report has been late.
3. What does he say about the second sentence?
It is the one they will repeat to their own boss.
Listening 2 · A Manager

The Answer She Should Have Given

A service center manager, thinking again about a question she was asked last week.

1. What answer did she give last week, and why does she call it wrong?
She said three months, because that is what the calendar says — March to June. She says that is not really the answer.
2. What does she say took three months, and what has taken a year?
Catching up on the backlog took three months. It has taken a year for the team to stop asking permission before improving something.
3. What three things does she list as having changed since then?
Two people have been promoted, nobody has left, and eleven small steps have disappeared from the process.

2. What is the backlog today, and what was it in January?
It is zero days today, and it was eleven days in January.
3. Which line of the note is in the past simple, and which one is in the present perfect — and why?
“The template was rolled out in March” is past simple, because March is finished. “Since then no report has been late” is present perfect, because that period is still running.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can summarise a year in two sentences instead of a list of months.
I ask myself whether the period is over, and that tells me which tense I need.
I use the past simple with yesterday, last month, in March and three months ago.
I use since with a point in time and for with a length of time.
I know the words: year to date, to roll out, backlog, to streamline, headcount, initiative, to take over, to catch up.
