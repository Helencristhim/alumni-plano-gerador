Chapter 4: The Code

Comparing Two Numbers Out Loud

Where the brazilian speaker loses a meeting, and it is never the vocabulary

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"On site we have six times as many people as we have on the payroll."

"My tenure is far longer than the median."

"Construction has nowhere near as many participants as finance."

"Latin America is the second smallest region in the cohort."

Two of them end with as, one ends with than, and one ends with nothing at all. Look at the red part in each: the word at the front decides which one comes at the end, and mixing them is the single most common mistake with numbers.

JobFormExample
A multipleX times as many / as much assix times as many people as on the payroll
More or less-er / more ... thanMy tenure is longer than the median.
Making it biggerfar / much / considerably + comparativefar longer than the median
Making it smallerslightly / marginally + comparativeslightly older than the median
Equal, or not equalas many as / not as many asnowhere near as many as finance
Rankingthe second / third + superlativethe second smallest region
The trapa multiple never takes more or thanNot twice more than. Say twice as many as.

In one line: a multiple takes as ... as; a difference takes than; and the double of does not exist in English.

Real Document

The Class Profile

Construction and infrastructure4%

The figures above are medians rather than averages, because two participants with unusually long careers would otherwise skew the picture. Around a fifth of the cohort owns or co-owns the business they run, and that group accounts for most of the capstone projects that reach implementation.
1. Why does the page use medians rather than averages, in its own words?
Because two participants with unusually long careers would otherwise skew the picture.
2. Compare construction with financial services in this cohort, in one sentence.
Financial services accounts for twenty-four per cent and construction for four, so financial services has six times as many participants as construction.
3. What does the last sentence say about the group that owns the business it runs?
It is around a fifth of the cohort, and it accounts for most of the capstone projects that reach implementation.
Listening 1 · Admissions

How the Room Gets Built

An admissions director on what the class profile is really for. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say the class profile is for, and who is it written for?
It is a recruiting document, written for candidates who are deciding whether they belong. It is not a report on the school.
2. Why does she say they publish the median and not the average?
Because two or three outliers with very long careers pull the average up and make everybody else look junior. The median does not move.
3. What does she say about candidates from sectors that are underrepresented?
That they think the small number is a warning, and it is the opposite. The committee is building a room, and a sector at four per cent is one they are short of.
Listening 2 · A Participant

What I Got Wrong About the Room

Somebody in the middle of the program, on the first week. Sound first — no text.

1. What did he assume about the other participants before he arrived?
That everybody would be from finance or consulting, that their English would be better than his, and that he would be the only one who had never worked outside his own country.
2. What did he find out in the first week?
That roughly a third of the room was not American, that several of them were harder to understand than he was, and that nobody cared.
3. What does he say made him stand out, and what did not?
Running a company he owns, with twenty-two years in it, made him stand out. Trying to sound like the finance people did not, and he says he wasted a month on it.
