Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

Who Is Actually in the Room -- Reading the Class Profile

Every program publishes a page of numbers about its cohort. Read it properly and it tells you where you fit. Key words: a median, a range, to account for, underrepresented, a sector, tenure, to skew, an outlier, a benchmark, roughly, to be on a par with, to stand out. Structure: comparatives and superlatives with data.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A median -- the middle value, with half the cases above it and half below
"The median age in the cohort is forty-one, which puts me just above the middle."
A range -- the distance between the lowest and the highest case in a set
"The age range is twenty-nine to fifty-eight, which is wider than I expected."
To account for -- to make up a given share of a total
"Engineering accounts for about a fifth of the cohort."
Underrepresented -- present in smaller numbers than you would expect
"Latin America is underrepresented in every cohort I have looked at."
A sector -- a part of the economy grouped by the kind of work the companies in it do
"Construction is the smallest sector in the room, and that is an advantage."
Tenure -- the length of time somebody has spent in a job or in one company
"My tenure is twenty-two years in the same firm, which is unusual in that room."
To skew -- to be unbalanced towards one side, so the picture is not symmetrical
"Two very senior participants skew the average, but not the median."
An outlier -- a case that sits far away from all the others, and that the average hides
"There is always one outlier: somebody with forty years in the same job."
A benchmark -- a figure you measure yourself against, because everybody uses the same one
"Ten years of management is the benchmark, and I am twelve years past it."
Roughly -- approximately, said with less precision than you could give, on purpose
"Roughly a third of the cohort comes from outside the United States."
To be on a par with -- to be at the same level as somebody or something else
"My tenure is on a par with the most experienced people in the room."
To stand out -- to be noticeably different from everything around it
"You do not stand out by being the same as everybody, only louder."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A company has twenty-five people on the payroll and up to a hundred and fifty on site. There are two ways to say that in English and only one of them works. A multiple takes as ... as: on site we have six times as many people as we have on the payroll. It never takes more and it never takes than. Twice more than and the double of are the two commonest mistakes a Portuguese speaker makes with numbers, and in a meeting they do not sound wrong, they sound like a different figure.

A difference, on the other hand, takes than. My tenure is longer than the median. How much longer is a separate decision, and English makes it with one word in front: far longer, much longer and considerably longer make the gap bigger, while slightly older and marginally higher make it small on purpose. That single word is doing the work that a whole sentence would otherwise do.

Then there is equality, which uses the same as ... as frame as the multiple: construction has as many participants as energy, or, if it does not, nowhere near as many as finance. And finally the ranking, which needs no second term at all: Latin America is the second smallest region in the cohort. The superlative already contains the comparison, which is why nothing follows it except the group it belongs to. Get those four right and you can read any page of data out loud without stopping.

1. Why is "six times more people than on the payroll" wrong?
A Because a multiple takes "as ... as", never "more" and never "than".
B Because "six times" is too large a number to use with "than".
C Because "people" is plural.
2. What is the difference between "far longer than" and "slightly longer than"?
A One is formal and the other is informal.
B The word in front decides how big the gap is, without adding a sentence.
C One is used with time and the other with quantity.
3. According to the text, why does "the second smallest region" need nothing after it?
A Because the region is too small to compare.
B Because superlatives are always used alone in English.
C Because the superlative already contains the comparison; only the group it belongs to follows.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparing Numbers

GRAMMAR

A multiple takes as ... as. A difference takes than. They never swap.

JobFormExample
A multipleX times as many / as much assix times as many people as on the payroll
More or less-er / more ... thanMy tenure is longer than the median.
Making the gap biggerfar / much / considerably + comparativefar longer than the median
Making the gap smallerslightly / marginally + comparativeslightly older than the median
Equal, or not equalas many as / not as many asnowhere near as many as finance
Rankingthe second / third + superlative + groupthe second smallest region in the cohort
Proportionto account for + a shareConstruction accounts for roughly four per cent.

Never: six times more than · the double of construction · not as many than finance · more longer than the median · the most second small region.

Stage 2: Put the Comparison in Order

Order

Somebody has asked where you fit on the class profile. Put your answer in a logical order.

?"I am forty-six, so I am slightly older than the median."
?"The range is twenty-nine to fifty-eight, so I am well inside it."
?"My tenure is far longer than the median, which probably makes me an outlier."
?"Construction accounts for roughly four per cent of the room."
?"So I am not one more of the same, which is the point."
?"How many people in the cohort actually own the business they run?"

Talk
TED -- Hans Rosling, "The best stats you've ever seen" (20 min)

A doctor and statistician narrating numbers out loud, live, for twenty minutes, and making every comparison in the vocabulary you worked on today. If you want to hear what fluent data description sounds like in English, this is the recording people still send each other twenty years later.

Tip: write down five of his comparisons exactly as he says them, then rewrite each one with a number from your own company.
