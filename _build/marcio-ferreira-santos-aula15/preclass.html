Lesson 15 -- Pre-class

Comparing Options Under Pressure

Choosing between two pricing options and defending the choice in one minute. Key words: upside, downside, to outweigh, to outperform, negligible, head-to-head, cannibalization, fallback. Structure: comparatives, superlatives and degree modifiers -- the size of the difference.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Upside -- the possible gain if things go well
"The upside of Option A is volume."
Downside -- the possible loss or problem that comes with a choice
"The downside of Option B is reach."
To outweigh -- to be bigger or more important than something else
"The margin cost outweighs the volume gain."
To outperform -- to do better than something else
"The loyalty price outperformed the broad cut in the test."
Negligible -- so small that it does not matter
"The difference in cost is negligible."
Head-to-head -- compared directly, one against the other
"We tested the two prices head-to-head in two regions."
Cannibalization -- when a new offer takes sales from your own products
"Cannibalization was higher than we expected."
Fallback -- a second plan you use if the first one fails
"Option A is our fallback, not our first choice."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

On Monday the pricing committee had ten minutes and two options. Option A was a broad price cut on two hundred basic items. Its upside was clear: it brought twice as much volume as Option B. But it was also far more expensive, almost two points of margin. Option B was a loyalty price on forty top items, only in the app. It was significantly cheaper, and it paid back more than twice as fast. Its downside was reach: only a third of the customers use the app, and cannibalization was slightly higher. Marcio chose Option B. In his view, the volume gain did not outweigh the margin cost, and the difference between the two was not negligible. As a fallback, he proposed a head-to-head test of A against B in one region. After ninety days, B outperformed A on margin. Doing nothing was never on the table, because it was by far the most expensive option in six months.

1. Why does the text say "twice as much volume as Option B" and not "two times more volume that Option B"?
A The pattern is twice + as + adjective or noun + as. The comparison uses as, not that.
B Because "volume" is a plural noun.
C Because "two times more" is only used in the past tense, and this part of the story happened on Monday.
2. "Cannibalization was slightly higher" means:
A Cannibalization was much higher, so it was the main reason to reject Option B.
B The difference in cannibalization was small.
C There was no cannibalization at all.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A "Option B is more cheaper than Option A."
B "Option B is very cheaper than Option A, so the committee chose it."
C "Option B is significantly cheaper than Option A."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives, Superlatives and Degree Modifiers

GRAMMAR

In a pricing committee, "cheaper" is not an answer. "How much cheaper" is the answer.

StructureRuleExample
ComparativeShort adjective + -er than. Long adjective: more ... than. Never both together.cheaper than · more expensive than
Small differenceslightly / a little + comparativeslightly higher
Big differencesignificantly / much / far / a lot + comparative. Never very before a comparative.much cheaper · far smaller
Exact sizetwice / three times + as ... astwice as fast as Option A
Superlativethe -est / the most when you compare three or more options. By far makes it stronger: by far the safest, by far the most expensive. Negative: not as ... as -- "Option A is not as profitable as Option B."
