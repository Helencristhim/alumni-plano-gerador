Chapter 4: The Code

Talking About the Past That Did Not Happen

Third conditional and the past modals that go with it

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"If we had seen the red flag, we would have stopped in April."

"We should have cut our losses eight months earlier."

"Somebody could have escalated it, and nobody did."

"The margin must have been wrong from the start."

Every red part has the same two words hiding inside it: have plus a past participle. What changes is the word in front of it, and that word is the difference between an explanation, a regret, a missed possibility and a conclusion. Look at the blue condition too: it is not a plain past.

JobFormExample
A past that did not happenIf + had + participle, would have + participleIf we had seen it, we would have stopped.
Regret, or criticismshould have + participleWe should have cut our losses earlier.
A possibility nobody tookcould have + participleSomebody could have escalated it.
A conclusion about the pastmust have / cannot have + participleThe margin must have been wrong.
A near missmight have / would nearly have + participleIt might have gone the other way.
In speechhave contracts and sounds like ofWe should've stopped. (never should of in writing)
The trapthe condition takes two steps back, not oneNot if I knew, I would not take it.

In one line: everything that did not happen carries have plus the participle, and the word in front decides whether you are explaining, regretting or concluding.

Every line above describes what happened, and every one of them was known inside the company within three months. The last line is the only one that requires a sentence about a past that did not happen, and it is the line that gets left blank, on this sheet and on most others.
1. Compare the two margins on this sheet, and say what the gap between them means.
Eleven per cent at signature and three point eight at closing, so the project lost around two thirds of its margin between the two dates.
2. How many months passed between the first red flag and the escalation, and what does the note say about that?
Seven months, from month two to month nine. The note says every line was known inside the company within three months.
3. Why does the note single out the last line?
Because it is the only line that requires a sentence about a past that did not happen, and it is the line that gets left blank.
Listening 1 · A Professor

The Question I Ask Every Case

A professor opening a case discussion, in the register you will sit in. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say she is not interested in, and what does she ask instead?
She is not interested in who was to blame. She asks at what point the decision had already been taken, even though nobody had said so yet.
2. What does she say about the phrase they should have known?
That it is almost always the weakest thing said in the room, because the information usually was there and the question is why nobody acted on it.
3. What does she want students to be able to do by the end of the hour?
To name the moment when cutting their losses was still cheap, and to say what would have had to be true for somebody to do it.
Listening 2 · A Project Director

We Knew in Month Two

A project director on the eleven months, told without drama. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the first red flag, and how does he describe the way they treated it?
A supplier asking for payment in advance in month two. They treated it as a problem with that one supplier rather than as information about the whole project.
2. What does he say about the eight months and the sunk cost?
That by month eight the money was already gone and could not come back, and that they kept deciding as though it could, which cost them another four months.
3. What does he say they do differently now?
They hold a post-mortem on every project, and the last question on the sheet is the one they used to leave blank.
