Chapter 3: The Code

Will, Going To, Likely To

How sure are you?

Grammar Discovery

How Sure Are You?

"I think demand for heavy machinery will grow next year."

"Look at the orders: the first quarter is going to be strong."

"Interest rates are likely to fall slowly."

"This time next year, we will be producing at full capacity."

All four sentences are about the future, but each one says something different. Sentence 1 is an opinion (I think). Sentence 2 has evidence that we can see now (look at the orders). Sentence 3 is a probability, more formal and more careful. Sentence 4 describes an action in progress at a moment in the future.

JobFormExample
Opinion, predictionwill (+ probably / definitely) + verbDemand will probably grow.
Prediction from evidence nowam / is / are going to + verbLook at the orders: it is going to be a strong quarter.
Probability, formalbe likely / unlikely to + verbRates are likely to fall.
What experts predictbe expected to + verbInvestment is expected to continue.
In progress at a future timewill be + verb-ingThis time next year, we will be producing in three shifts.
The trapafter likely and expected, use to + verbNot “Demand is likely grow.” Say Demand is likely to grow.

In one line: opinion = will; evidence = going to; probability = likely to / expected to; in progress then = will be doing.

Listening 1 · An American Analyst

What’s Likely, Not What’s Certain

An American market analyst gives his view for heavy industry in the Americas. Sound first — no text.

1. What honest answer does he give his clients every January?
That he does not know exactly, but he can tell them what is likely.
2. Where is the big opportunity, in his view?
In infrastructure: ports, railways and energy will need a lot of steel and castings.
3. What is his advice?
Plan for steady growth, not for the best case, and be ready to move fast if the market recovers sooner.
Listening 2 · A Finance Director in India

The Only Forecast I’m Sure About

The finance director of a car parts company in Mumbai explains how she plans for the exchange rate. Sound first — no text.

1. Why is the exchange rate so important for her company?
Because half of their sales are in dollars.
2. What does her team prepare instead of one forecast?
Three scenarios: a good one, a bad one and a middle one.
3. What is the only forecast she is sure about?
That this time next year they will still be talking about the exchange rate.
Listening 3 · An Australian Consultant

Trends, Not Headlines

An Australian mining consultant explains why he looks at ten years, not at one quarter. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he look at, and what does he ignore?
He looks at trends and ignores headlines, because headlines change every week.
2. Why will the world need more metal?
Because electric cars, wind farms and new power lines use huge amounts of copper, aluminum and steel.
3. What question does he ask his clients?
Are they thinking about next quarter, or about the next ten years?
