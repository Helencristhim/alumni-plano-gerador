Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

The Three Columns -- Verbs That Choose What Comes Next

In Portuguese the verb after a verb is almost always an infinitive. In English the first verb chooses, and with four verbs it chooses the meaning as well. Key words: to commit to something, to intend, to contemplate, to resume, to hesitate, to persist, to dedicate yourself to, to end up doing, cannot help doing, to be worth doing. Structure: verbs followed by a gerund or an infinitive.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In Portuguese, when one verb follows another, the second one is almost always an infinitive. English does not work that way. The verb in front chooses, and there is no rule underneath the choice — you learn it in groups. Some verbs always take the -ing form: enjoy, avoid, consider, keep, practise, suggest, finish, postpone, give up. I gave up playing competitively. Others always take to: want, need, decide, plan, manage, hope, expect, agree, refuse, intend. I intend to take the exam in November.

Then there is a small group that takes both, and this is the part worth real attention, because both versions are correct English and they mean different things. I stopped playing basketball means I quit. I stopped to think means I paused, in order to think — I was doing something else entirely. The same split runs through try, remember and forget: I tried studying at night is an experiment; I tried to study at night is an attempt that may have failed. No spellchecker will ever catch this, because nothing is wrong.

Two more patterns and you have the whole picture. First, some verbs take an object and then to: my teacher told me to review it, my father wants me to rest. Second, and this is the one that catches almost everybody: after a preposition English always uses -ing, and the word to is sometimes a preposition rather than part of an infinitive. That is why it is I look forward to seeing you and I am used to waking up early, never to see or to wake. Three fixed patterns work the same way and are worth memorising whole: end up doing, cannot help doing, be worth doing.

1. What is the difference between I stopped playing and I stopped to think?
A There is no difference. Both are correct and mean the same.
B The first means I quit. The second means I paused in order to think.
C The first is informal and the second is formal.
2. Why is “I look forward to see you” wrong?
A Because look forward is always followed by a noun.
B Because that to is a preposition, and after a preposition English uses -ing.
C Because see is an irregular verb.
3. Why does the text say no spellchecker will catch the stop and try mistakes?
A Because they are too rare to be in the dictionary.
B Because both versions are correct English, so nothing is wrong — only the meaning is different.
C Because spellcheckers ignore verbs.
