Working the Floor -- Ninety Seconds With a Stranger

Nine hundred booths and a visitor who stops for ninety seconds. Key words: a booth, an exhibitor, a lead, a sample, a distributor, a catalog, a badge, to follow up. Structure: question forms and short answers -- at a fair, the one who asks controls the conversation.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A booth -- the small space a company has at a fair, to show its products
"Our booth is in hall three, next to the coffee."
An exhibitor -- a company that shows its products at a fair, in its own space
"Nine hundred exhibitors, and four days to see them."
A lead -- a person who might become a customer, but has not bought anything yet
"We came home with sixty leads and four real ones."
A sample -- a small quantity of a product you give away, so people can try it
"Do you have a sample I can take with me?"
A distributor -- a company that buys from you and sells to the shops in its own market
"We work through one distributor in each country."
A catalog -- the book, printed or digital, with every product you sell and its details
"The catalog is on the table. Take one."
A badge -- the card with your name and company on it, that hangs around your neck
"Read the badge before you open your mouth."
To follow up -- to contact somebody again, after the first short meeting
"A fair is worth nothing if nobody follows up on Monday."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A visitor stops at the booth and says she has two minutes. Most exhibitors would start presenting. Roberto does not. He asks: "Where are you based, and what do you buy?" She is a distributor in Toronto, selling on to four hundred stores.

Then the question that matters: "Who do you buy this category from today?" Two suppliers in Europe, and their prices rose twice last year. She asks one back: "Did you exhibit here last year?" No, they did not; this is their first year.

He offers a sample and the catalog, and asks the last one: "Who handles distribution decisions on your side?" Note the shape of that question — there is no do in it, because who is already the subject. She answers "I do", up to a volume, and above that her director decides. Then she gives him her badge and one instruction: follow up on Monday. She is now a lead, and ninety seconds produced more than a brochure ever could.

1. "Where are you based?" Why is are before you?
A Because the sentence is in the past.
B Because in a question the helper jumps in front of the subject, and with be the verb itself is the helper.
C Because where always comes with are.
2. "Who handles distribution on your side?" Why is there no do in this question?
A Because who is the subject of the sentence, so there is nothing to jump in front of.
B Because questions with who never take a helper, in any case.
C Because handles already ends in -s.
3. "Did you exhibit here last year?" She answers: "No, we did not." Why not "No, we did not exhibited"?
A Because exhibit is irregular.
B Because short answers are only used with be.
C Because a short answer repeats only the helper. The verb does not come back at all.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Question Forms and Short Answers

GRAMMAR

Get the shape wrong and people answer a question you did not ask. Get it right and ninety seconds is enough.

UseFormExample
With be(Wh-) + am / is / are + subjectWhere are you based?
With any other verb, present(Wh-) + do / does + subject + naked verbDo you sell to Europe? Does he handle Canada?
In the past(Wh-) + did + subject + naked verbDid you exhibit here last year?
Question word as subjectWho / What + verb, no helperWho handles distribution? What happened last year?
Short answerrepeat only the helperYes, we do. / No, we did not. / Yes, I am.
The trapa rising voice is not a questionNot You sell to Europe? Not Where you are based?

Never: Where you are based? · Who does handles distribution? · Does your company sells to Europe? · You exhibited here last year? · No, we did not exhibited.

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

A visitor stops at your booth and says she has two minutes. Put your side in a logical order.

?"Before I take your two minutes: where are you based?"
?"And who do you buy this category from today?"
?"We make packaged foods, private label mostly."
?"Do you need a sample, or is the catalog more useful today?"
?"Who approves a first order on your side?"
?"I will scan your badge and follow up on Monday."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

A visitor stops at your booth. You want to know where the company is. You ask:
A "Where you are based?"
B "Where are you based?"
C "Where do you are based?"
You want to know which person in that company decides about distribution. You ask:
A "Who does handles distribution?"
B "Who handles distribution?"
C "Who is handle distribution?"
You want to know whether they were at the fair last year. You ask:
A "Did you come to this fair last year?"
B "You came to this fair last year?"
C "Did you came to this fair last year?"
Somebody asks: "Do you work with a distributor?" The natural short answer is:
A "Yes, we work with a distributor we work."
B "Yes, we are."
C "Yes, we do. One in each country."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Somebody sits next to you at the fair dinner and asks what you do. In one minute: two sentences about your company, then four questions, until you know what that person sells, where, to whom, and whether you should meet again.

Eleven minutes on the skill this lesson trains: asking instead of performing. Her rules are made for exactly the situation you are in at a fair — a stranger, very little time, and a conversation that dies if you only talk about yourself.
