The Why Us Supplement -- Specificity, Collocation and a Mid-Program Review

An essay on why most Why Us supplements could be about any school, the language colleges use to describe themselves, and precision at the level of the phrase. Key words: interdisciplinary, seminar-based, archival, curatorial, conservation, mentorship, rigor, fascination, preoccupation, devotion, cultivate, foster, immerse, distinctive, trajectory. Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read it in context. These are the words colleges use about themselves, and the words that let you describe how you fit.

Interdisciplinary -- combining the methods or knowledge of two or more academic fields
"The course is interdisciplinary, joining art history and chemistry."
Seminar-based -- taught mainly through small group discussion rather than large lectures
"The first-year program is seminar-based."
Archival -- relating to collections of historical documents and records
"Students conduct archival research in the college library."
Curatorial -- relating to the work of selecting, organizing and presenting objects in a museum
"Seniors can take on curatorial projects in the campus museum."
Conservation -- the work of protecting and repairing works of art and objects
"She wants to study conservation science."
Mentorship -- guidance given over time by a more experienced person
"The program pairs every student with a faculty mentor, and mentorship continues for four years."
Rigor -- the quality of being thorough, careful and demanding
"Applicants often mention the rigor of the program without explaining it."
Fascination -- a very strong interest that attracts and holds your attention
"Her fascination with medieval manuscripts began with one page."
Preoccupation -- something that fills your thoughts almost constantly
"Color became a preoccupation that followed her into every museum."
Devotion -- deep, lasting commitment and love for something
"Devotion is a strong word for an essay; use it only with evidence."
Cultivate -- to develop a skill, interest or relationship through deliberate effort
"She has cultivated an eye for detail through years of drawing."
Foster -- to encourage something to develop over time
"The college fosters close collaboration between students and faculty."
Immerse -- to involve yourself deeply and completely in an activity or place
"The summer program immerses students in the museum's collection."
Distinctive -- clearly different from others in a way that is easy to recognize
"What is distinctive about the program is its object-based teaching."
Trajectory -- the path of development that something follows over time
"The supplement should connect your past interests to your future trajectory."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Advanced learners of English rarely make grammatical mistakes in their application essays. What gives them away is something subtler: combinations of words that are perfectly correct and yet no native writer would choose. A student who writes that she wants to make research is understood immediately, but the reader hears a translation. Academic English conducts research, pursues a question and fosters collaboration. These habitual pairings are called collocations, and at the highest levels of proficiency they matter more than vocabulary size.

Collocations are not logical, which is exactly why they are difficult. There is no rule that explains why collaboration is close rather than near, or why a fascination is with something rather than about it. They are learned by noticing, and the most reliable way to notice them is to read the kind of text you intend to write. A course catalog, a faculty page and a museum's description of its programs are full of the combinations an applicant needs.

Near-synonyms present a related challenge. Interest, fascination, preoccupation and devotion all describe a strong attraction to a subject, but they differ in intensity and in what they imply. Interest is safe and modest. Fascination suggests that the subject holds the writer's attention. Preoccupation can sound almost obsessive, and devotion claims a lifelong commitment that a seventeen-year-old applicant may find difficult to prove. Choosing among them is a question of evidence as much as of style: the stronger the word, the more the essay must show to support it.

None of this means an applicant should imitate the voice of an institution. A supplement that reproduces a brochure's phrases without connecting them to anything the writer has done sounds borrowed, however natural its collocations. The goal is to use the language precisely enough that a reader forgets it was ever learned, and notices only what the writer has to say.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Collocation and Near-Synonyms

GRAMMAR

At C2, precision lives in combinations: the verb a noun expects, the adjective that sounds native, and the near-synonym whose intensity the evidence can support.

FormWhat it doesExample
verb + nounthe verb academic English expectsconduct research, pursue a question, foster collaboration
adjective + nounthe adjective a native writer choosesclose collaboration, rigorous training, distinctive approach
noun + prepositionthe preposition the noun demandsa fascination with, research on, a commitment to
near-synonyms by intensitymatch the word to the evidenceinterest → fascination → preoccupation → devotion
The C2 trapborrowing a brochure's phrases without connecting them to anything you have done. Natural collocations cannot rescue a sentence that says nothing about you.

When unsure, search the phrase in quotation marks on university websites and see which version appears in real academic writing.
