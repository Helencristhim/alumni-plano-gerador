Chapter 3: For and Since

How Long Have You Been Here?

Two words that measure time — and one trap for every Brazilian

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I've studied English for ten months."

"We've been in Lisbon since Monday."

"My husband and I have traveled together for forty years."

"I haven't needed a translator since March."

All four sentences started in the past and are still true today. Look at the green words: what kind of time comes after for, and what kind of time comes after since?

WordWhat follows itExample
forhow long — a period of timefor ten months · for two weeks · for forty years
sincewhen it started — a point in timesince Monday · since 2019 · since last September
have / has + past participleit started before, and it is still true nowI have lived here · she has worked here
How long have you...?the question that asks for bothHow long have you been in Portugal? — For two weeks. / Since Monday.

One test decides it every time: can you count it on a clock (for), or can you point to it on a calendar (since)?

The Anatomy

Three Cards, One Answer

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I am here since Monday."
"I've been here since Monday." — with "since" or "for", English uses have/has, never the plain present.
"I've studied English since ten months."
"I've studied English for ten months." — ten months is a period, so it takes "for".
"How long are you in Europe?"
"How long have you been in Europe?" — the question about duration always starts with "How long have".

One check catches all three: if the sentence has for or since, look for have or has. If it isn't there, the sentence isn't finished yet.

Expression of the Day

I've Come a Long Way

I've come a long way since my first trip.

= look at where I started, and look at me now

English speakers use this sentence about themselves without embarrassment. It is not bragging. It is a fact with a starting point, and the starting point is what makes it true.
Notice what carries it: since my first trip. Without the starting point, it is an opinion. With it, it is a measurement.

Listening

Twenty-Two Years Behind the Same Desk

A night manager who says he can tell, in ten seconds, which guests are traveling on their own. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. How long has Andrew worked at that desk, and what does he say he can tell in ten seconds?
He has worked at the same front desk since 2003, so for twenty-two years. He says he can tell in ten seconds which guests are traveling on their own, because of who looks at him when he speaks.
2. What does he say about guests who arrive with somebody who speaks for them?
They look at the other person instead of at him, and he ends up giving the information to the wrong pair of ears. He says the trip belongs to the person who is listening, and that is not always the person who paid for the room.
3. What does Andrew say about the guests who come back years later, and what does he notice about their English?
They come back with the same small mistakes they had the first time, and it makes no difference at all. What changed is not their grammar: it is that they now ask him the question themselves, and stay for the answer.
Listening 2

Teresa Came Home

The woman who rehearsed her whole journey in her kitchen has been back for two weeks. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. How long has Teresa been home, and what does she say about the sentences she rehearsed?
She has been home for two weeks. She says she used almost none of the sentences she rehearsed, and that this is not a problem: the rehearsal was not a script, it was practice at opening her mouth.
2. What went wrong on her second day, and how did she solve it?
Her hotel had no record of her booking. She had never practiced that sentence, so she said it badly, in the wrong order, and the woman at the desk understood her anyway and found the reservation under her first name.
3. What does Teresa say she is doing now, and what advice does she give at the end?
She has already booked the next trip, for the spring, and she still speaks English out loud in her kitchen for twenty minutes a day. Her advice is to keep it up after the lessons stop, because the lessons end and the traveler does not.

S
Different how? Better hotels?
R

Comprehension

About What Stephen Said

1. Where is Stephen from, and what does he say about arriving at airports?
He is from Chicago. He always arrives two hours early, and he jokes that his wife says he was born in a departure lounge.
2. What two questions does Stephen ask, and which one is about duration?
He asks how long she has been in Portugal, and then he asks in what way this trip was different. The first one is the duration question: "How long have you been in Portugal?"
3. What did Stephen assume before she told him about the ten months?
He assumed she had spoken English all her life. Nothing in the conversation had suggested to him that she was a beginner.
4. What does Stephen say about his own sixty-one years in Chicago, and why does it matter here?
He has lived there for sixty-one years and his Spanish is still four words. Time alone teaches nobody a language: what teaches it is opening your mouth, which is exactly what ten months of practice bought her.

The Artifact

The Email She Sent Herself

RE: Reservation — 12 to 15 May
From: Casa do Miradouro, Lisbon  ·  To: Rosângela P. de Oliveira
Dear Rosângela,
Thank you for your message. Yes, early check-in is possible: the room will be ready at eleven. The airport shuttle stops at the corner of our street every thirty minutes, and it takes about twenty-five minutes. And yes, we can keep your bags on the last day.
We look forward to seeing you.
Ana — Casa do Miradouro
— Original message —
Dear Casa do Miradouro,
My name is Rosângela and I have a reservation for three nights, from 12 to 15 May. Our flight lands at eight in the morning, so I'd like to ask three things. Could you tell me if early check-in is possible? Do you know where the airport shuttle stops? And would it be possible to leave our bags at the hotel on the last day?
We've been coming to Portugal since 2019, and this is our first time in your neighborhood.
Thank you, and see you in May.
Rosângela
1. She asked three things. Did the hotel answer all three, and what does that tell you about the email?
Yes: early check-in at eleven, the shuttle at the corner every thirty minutes, and the bags on the last day. Three clear questions got three clear answers — a long, polite email with vague questions usually gets one line back.
2. Find "for three nights" and "since 2019". Why "for" in one and "since" in the other?
Three nights is a period of time, so it takes "for". 2019 is a point on the calendar — when it started — so it takes "since". And "we've been coming" carries the have, because it started then and is still true.
3. Now write your own out loud: an email to the next hotel. Say who you are, how long you are staying, and ask two questions.
For example: "My name is Rosângela and I have a reservation for four nights, from 3 to 7 June. Could you tell me if there is a lift? And do you know if the museum is far on foot? We've been coming to Europe since 2019, and this is our first time in Porto."
