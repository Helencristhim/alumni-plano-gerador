Rosângela Is Ready -- Twenty Lessons, and the Road Behind

The last lesson of the program. Every traveler asks the same two questions -- how long have you been here, and how long have you studied English -- and tonight you get the answer. Key words: to come a long way, progress, a milestone, to rely on, to manage, proud of, to look back, step by step, to keep it up, a goal. The grammar: for and since with the present perfect -- "I've studied English for ten months", "We've been here since Monday", "How long have you been in Portugal?". Expression: I've come a long way since my first trip.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to every word and read the example. These are the words you need to talk about a long road: the one behind you, and the one ahead. Think of one real moment of your own for each word before you move on.

To come a long way -- to make a lot of progress since you started
"You've come a long way. Last year you couldn't order a coffee."
Progress -- the change you can see when you compare today with the first day
"My progress is slow, but it never stopped."
A milestone -- a moment that marks an important point in a long journey
"Checking in alone was a milestone for me."
To rely on -- to need somebody else to do something for you
"For years I relied on my son at every counter."
To manage -- to succeed in doing something difficult, by yourself
"The shuttle was gone, but I managed to find a taxi."
Proud of -- feeling good about something you did yourself
"I'm proud of that phone call. I made it in English, alone."
To look back -- to think about a time that has already passed
"When I look back, the hardest part was the first phone call."
Step by step -- a little at a time, one small piece after another
"Nobody learns a language in a month. It's step by step."
To keep it up -- to continue doing something you are already doing well
"Twenty minutes a day. Keep it up and the trip will be easy."
A goal -- something you want to reach, and work for
"My goal for next year is three weeks in Portugal, on my own."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions below.

Gate 22, two hours before the flight home. Rosângela and her husband have been sitting there since two o'clock, because he likes to arrive early and she has stopped arguing about it. A man asks if the seat is free, sits down, and says he is Stephen, from Chicago. Then he asks the question every traveler asks: "How long have you been in Portugal?"

"For twelve days," she says. "Lisbon, then the north, then two nights in Porto. We've been coming to Europe since 2019, but this one was different." He asks how. And she tells him: her husband does not speak English, for years she relied on her son for every counter and every phone call, and this time she did all the talking herself. "I've only studied English for ten months."

Stephen puts his coffee down. He says he assumed she had spoken English all her life, and then he admits that he has lived in Chicago for sixty-one years and his Spanish is still four words. She laughs. Step by step, she tells him. She has come a long way, and she is proud of it. Next year, three weeks. That is the goal.

"We've been sitting here since two o'clock." Why "since" and not "for"?
A Because two o'clock is in the afternoon.
B Because two o'clock is the moment it started — a point in time. "Since" always answers "when did it start?"
C Because the sentence is about the past.
"For twelve days." Why "for" here, and not "since"?
A Because twelve days is a period of time — how long, not when. Periods take "for".
B Because the trip is finished.
C Because "since" is only used with years.
"How long have you been in Portugal?" Why not "How long are you in Portugal?"
A Because the question is polite and the plain present is rude.
B Because she is going home tomorrow.
C Because it started in the past and is still true now. English measures that with "have/has", never with the plain present.
"He has lived in Chicago for sixty-one years and his Spanish is still four words." What is the point of that sentence in the text?
A That Spanish is a difficult language.
B That time alone teaches nobody a language. Ten months of speaking is worth more than sixty-one years of not trying.
C That people from Chicago do not like languages.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- For and Since

GRAMMAR

Two small words carry every answer about duration. They are not interchangeable, and the test that separates them takes one second.

WordWhat follows itExample
forhow long — a period of timeI've studied English for ten months. · We've been here for two weeks.
sincewhen it started — a point in timeWe've been here since Monday. · I've traveled to Europe since 2019.
have / has + past participleit started before and is still true nowI have lived here. · He has worked at the desk since 2003.
How long have you...?the question that asks for the durationHow long have you been in Portugal? — For twelve days. / Since Monday.
the negativeworks the same wayI haven't needed a translator since March.

The three rules to remember: (1) for + a period you could count on a clock: for ten months, for two weeks, for forty years. (2) since + a point you could find on a calendar: since Monday, since 2019, since my first trip. (3) If the sentence has "for" or "since", look for have or has — "I am here since Monday" is the one mistake every Brazilian makes, and it disappears the moment you look for the "have".

Quiz

A "I am here since Monday."

A "I've studied English for ten months."

Your first trip to Europe was in 2019, and you have gone back every year. You say:
A "We've been coming to Europe since 2019."

C "We are coming to Europe since 2019."
Somebody says your English is very good and asks if you have always spoken it. You want to be honest and not apologize. You say:
A "No, sorry, my English is very bad."

Somebody asks who usually deals with the hotels and the counters on your trips. You say:
