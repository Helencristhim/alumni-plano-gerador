The Ten Days on One Page

TRIP ITINERARY
V. Petrilli · 2 travellers · Miami · 10 nights
Day 1 — ArrivalBag did not arrive
Day 2 — CityBag delivered, 11:40
Day 3 — Move to the coastRoom not ready at 2
Days 4 to 5 — Nothing plannedOn purpose
Day 6 — Boat tripThree times, three sources
Days 7 to 9 — CoastNo hiccups
Day 10 — Check-outOne line on the bill
Four marked days out of ten. Six days where nothing happened at all, which is what an itinerary actually looks like.
1. Which four days are marked, and what happened on each one?
Day 1, the bag did not arrive. Day 3, the room was not ready at two. Day 6, three sources gave three times for the boat. Day 10, there was one line on the bill.
2. How many days had nothing wrong with them, and how does that change the picture?
Six of the ten. The four hiccups are a small part of the trip, even though they are the part everybody worries about beforehand.
3. Take one marked day and say the sentence you would use on it.
Any of these works: my bag has not arrived yet, here is the tag. Could you store the bags and tell me a realistic time? The woman on the phone told me it left at nine. There is a line here I do not recognise, could you tell me what it is for?

Grammar Discovery

Four Sentences, Four Lessons

"My bag has not arrived yet, and here is the tag."

"We have been travelling since five, so could you store the bags?"

"If the room is not ready by four, I will need something else."

"The woman on the phone told me it left at nine."

Every one of these came out of the text you have just read, and every one of them is a lesson you have already had. Name what each sentence is doing before you open the table.

In one line: nine structures, and every real problem on a trip is two of them put together.

Listening 1 · A Trip Guide

The First Thirty Seconds After

Fifteen years of ten-day trips, and the one thing that separates two kinds of traveller. Sound first — no text.

1. How many things does he say go wrong on a ten-day trip, and what are they?
About four. A bag arrives late, a room is not ready at two, somebody gives the wrong time, and there is one line on a bill that nobody explained.
2. What is the difference between the two kinds of traveller he describes?
Some wait near the desk hoping somebody notices them, and by the time they speak the person who could have fixed it has gone home. The others walk up, say what happened, say what they need, and go back to their holiday.
3. What does he say about your English at the end?
It does not have to be good for the second version. It has to be quick.
Listening 2 · The Last Morning

Check-Out at Eleven

Three things guests never ask for, and one sentence that changes the last day. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say about the bags after check-out?
Check out is at eleven, but the bags can stay. They store them for free until the evening, and almost nobody asks, so people carry a suitcase around the city for no reason.
2. When does she say to ask for the final bill, and why then?
The night before, not on the morning itself, because that is when you have time to read it and ask about the line you do not recognise.
3. What is the one sentence she would write on the door, and what does the hotel do with it?
Tell them the night before what time your flight is. Then they book the shuttle, hold a late room for you to change in, and have the bags at the door when you come back.

Role-Play 1 of 3 — Guided

Four Desks, Thirty Seconds Each

Scenario: Four desks, one after the other, with no pause. The bag that did not arrive, the room that is not ready, the boat with three different times, and the line on the bill. Thirty seconds at each one.

Keyword chips:

Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

The Last Morning

Scenario: It is the night before you fly home, and the person at the desk offers you nothing. Ask for the three things yourself: the bill tonight, the bags stored tomorrow, and what happens between check-out and your flight.

Keywords:

could you a backup plan if the flight is late smoothly
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Ten Days in Sixty Seconds

Scenario: You are back, and somebody asks how the trip was. Tell the whole thing: where you went, what went wrong, what you did about it, and what you would do differently. Problems included.

No keywords, no notes, sixty seconds. It only counts if it never stops at it was good.
