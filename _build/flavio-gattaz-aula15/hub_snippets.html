Lesson 15 -- Pre-class

The Failure Question -- Tell Me About a Time You Failed

The panel already knows you have failed. What it scores is who owns the mistake and what is different today because of it. Key words: a setback, to own up to something, to fall short, to misjudge something, to shift the blame, a wake-up call, a root cause, to dwell on something, to bounce back, self-awareness, to course-correct, to learn something the hard way. Structure: expressing regret -- wish and if only with the past perfect, regret + -ing, and regret to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A setback -- a problem that delays your progress or makes things worse for a while
"Losing our best engineer was a setback, but the site was running again in a month."
To own up to something -- to admit openly that you did something wrong, before anybody forces you to
"I owned up to the mistake in the Monday meeting, in front of all three engineers."
To fall short -- to fail to reach the standard or the target that was expected
"The second site fell short of the schedule by eleven weeks."
To misjudge something -- to form the wrong opinion about a person or a situation
"I misjudged what he wanted. He was a great engineer who never wanted to be a manager."
To shift the blame -- to make somebody else responsible for a mistake that was yours
"It would have been easy to shift the blame to the supplier, and it would have been wrong."
A wake-up call -- an event that suddenly shows you a serious problem you had not seen
"His resignation letter was a wake-up call about how we promote people."
A root cause -- the real, original reason a problem happened, not the first thing you can see
"The delay was the symptom. The root cause was a promotion with no training behind it."
To dwell on something -- to keep thinking or talking about something bad for longer than is useful
"Say the regret once. If you dwell on it, the panel stops hearing the lesson."
To bounce back -- to recover quickly after something has gone wrong
"The site bounced back in six weeks, but the engineer did not come back."
Self-awareness -- knowing clearly how you behave, and how other people see you
"The failure question is really a test of self-awareness, not of the failure."
To course-correct -- to change direction as soon as you see you are going the wrong way
"We course-corrected in month three and split the two sites again."
To learn something the hard way -- to understand something only after a painful mistake
"I learned the hard way that a title is not the same thing as the tools to do the job."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every admissions panel asks for a failure, and most candidates bring a small, safe one. That is the first mistake. A failure too small to hurt anybody tells the interviewer that nobody ever trusted you with anything that could go wrong. The second mistake is the grammar of regret, and here English is stricter than Portuguese. When you talk about a past you cannot change, the verb after wish moves one step further back: "I did not ask him" becomes I wish I had asked him. Not "I wish I asked him", and never "I wish I would have asked him".

If only works the same way, but it is stronger and more emotional: If only I had waited three months. In an interview, use it once, if at all. Regret is different again. The action you are sorry about takes -ing: I regret promoting him so fast. The negative puts not before the -ing: I regret not training him before the move. Regret to has another job altogether: it is the formal way of giving bad news now, and you have probably read it at the top of a rejection letter — We regret to inform you.

The most important rule is not grammatical. Say the regret once, and then pivot to the present: what you do differently now, and how somebody could see it on your sites today. A candidate who dwells on the regret sounds like a man still inside the failure. A candidate who says it once and moves on sounds like a man who has already fixed it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Expressing Regret

GRAMMAR

After wish and if only, go one tense back. After regret, use -ing. In an interview, say it once.

ToolFormExample
Wish (past)wish + past perfectI wish I had asked him first.
If only (past)if only + past perfect; stronger, more emotionalIf only I had waited three months.
Wish (now)wish + past simple for a present you want to be differentI wish we had a third engineer today.
Regret + -ingthe action you are sorry aboutI regret promoting him so fast.
Regret not + -ingthe thing you did not do; not goes before the -ingI regret not training him before the move.
Regret toformal bad news, now: inform, say, tellWe regret to inform you that...

Never: I wish I asked him · I wish I would have asked him · if only I would have waited · I regret to promote him · I regret to not train him.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The panel asks for a time you failed. Which failure is the best choice?
A "Once I sent a report to the client with two typos in it."
B "I promoted my best engineer too fast, he resigned, and a site was eleven weeks late."
C "Honestly, my biggest failure is that I work too hard."
You want to say you are sorry you did not ask him. You say:
A "I wish I asked him before."
B "I wish I would have asked him before."
C "I wish I had asked him before."
The interviewer asks: "Was it really your fault? The market was difficult that year." You say:
A "The market was difficult for everybody. The promotion was my decision, and so was the mistake."
B "Yes, you are right, the market was the real problem that year."
C "Partly. The client also changed the schedule twice."
She asks what you learned. Which answer gives her evidence?
A "I learned that communication is very important."
B "Nobody is promoted now without a ninety-day handover, and the two engineers promoted that way are still with us."
C "I still think about it every day, and I feel terrible about it."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Tell me about a time you failed. Take two minutes, and choose a failure that really cost something. Own it in the first sentence, say the regret only once -- with wish, if only or regret -- and spend at least half of the time on what you do differently now.
