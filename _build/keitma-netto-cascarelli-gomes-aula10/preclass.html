Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

The Interview Answer That Says Enough

Eight minutes into an interview, one question decides everything. Key words: hiring manager, shortlist, hands-on, scope, achievement, notice period, to step up, strength. Structure: relative clauses -- who, that, where, whose.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

“I am a senior accountant.” Four words, all of them true, and the interviewer has learned almost nothing. Now the same person, one sentence later: “I am the accountant who runs the close for Brazil, in a team that covers six countries, and I am the person the auditors come to when a number needs explaining.” Same job. Completely different picture. A title says what you are called; a clause says what you own.

The structure is simple and it has four members. Who attaches to a person: the accountant who runs the close. That or which attaches to a thing, a team, a process: a team that covers six countries. Where attaches to a place: an office where nine languages are spoken. And whose attaches to possession: a client whose invoices always arrive late. When the noun is the object rather than the subject of the clause, English lets you drop the word entirely: the person the auditors come to needs no who at all, and adding one is not wrong, only heavier.

One trap catches almost every learner, and it comes from translating: the relative word IS the subject, so it must not be repeated. The person who he prepares it has two subjects for one verb. Say the person who prepares it. And one detail of punctuation carries meaning: commas turn the clause into extra information, the kind you could remove without losing the sentence — my manager, who has been there twelve years, signs it off. Without commas, the clause is doing the identifying, and removing it would leave the listener not knowing which person you mean.

1. What does the text say is the difference between a title and a clause?
A A title says what you are called; a clause says what you own.
B A title is formal and a clause is informal.
C A title is for a CV and a clause is only for speaking.
2. When can you leave the relative word out completely?
A Whenever the sentence is getting too long.
B When the noun is the object of the clause, not its subject.
C Only after where and whose.
3. What do the commas do in “my manager, who has been there twelve years, signs it off”?
A They mark the clause as the part that identifies which manager.
B They turn the clause into extra information you could remove without losing the sentence.
C They are optional and change nothing.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

A claim is an adjective. A clause is a fact with a shape.

WordWhat it attaches toExample
whoa personthe accountant who runs the close
that · whicha thing, a team, a processa team that covers six countries
wherea placean office where nine languages are spoken
whosepossessiona client whose invoices always arrive late
no word at allwhen the noun is the object of the clausethe person the auditors come to
commasextra information, removableMy manager, who has been there twelve years, signs it off.
the trapthe relative word IS the subject — do not repeat itNot the person who he prepares it. Say who prepares it.
the one that travels with ityou are responsible for something, and you started in a yearI am responsible for the close, and I started in 2020.

Never: the person who he prepares it · my manager, that has been there · responsible of the close · I started at 2020

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

“Tell me about your current role.” The strongest answer is:
A I am a senior accountant in a shared service center.
B I am the accountant who runs the close for Brazil, in a team that covers six countries.
C I am the accountant who I run the close for Brazil.
“Tell me about a strength.” The strongest answer is:
A I am organised and detail-oriented.
B I am the person who finds the difference before the auditor does, and last year that saved us three weeks of review.
C I think I am a fast learner, people say that about me.
You want to add a detail about your manager that is not identifying. You say:
A My manager that has been there twelve years signs it off.
B My manager, who has been there twelve years, signs it off.
C My manager who he has been there twelve years signs it off.
“When could you start?” You say:
A My notice period is thirty days, and I would honour it.
B I am not sure, I have to check with my company.
C Immediately, if you want.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. You are on the shortlist. Describe your current work to the hiring manager: who comes to you and with what, which decisions are yours, what happens in your process when you are away, one achievement with a number, and your notice period. Finish with one question back.
