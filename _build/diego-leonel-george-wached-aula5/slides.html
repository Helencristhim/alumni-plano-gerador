Plain English is not simpler English. It is the same idea carried by a different structure — and the structure is a relative clause.

The Clause That Explains

Grammar Discovery

What Joins These Two Ideas?

"It is a condition that stays with you for life."

"Your immune system is a defence which is working in the wrong place."

"You are the kind of patient who reads before the appointment."

"The cream that your sister gave you is not the one I would use."

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Explanation, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one patient leaflet

Dialogue

What Is It, Doctor?

O
You said psoriasis. I looked it up last night and I stopped reading. What is it, in words I can understand?
D
Let me reassure you about one thing first: it is not contagious. Nobody can catch it from you.
O
That was the question I was afraid to ask. My daughter is eight.
D
To put it simply: your skin is building itself too fast, and the redness you see is inflammation, which is your own body reacting.
O
Reacting to what? Something I ate? Somebody told me it was dirty skin.
D
That is the most common misconception, and it is wrong. It is your immune system, which is a defence that is working in the wrong place.
O
Can you cure it?
D
There is no cure, and I am not going to pretend there is. It is chronic. But we can manage it well, and long quiet periods are normal.
Comprehension

About Mrs. Okafor

1. What did she do the night before, and why did she stop?
She looked psoriasis up online and stopped reading, because what she found frightened her.
2. Which question was she afraid to ask, and what made it urgent for her?
Whether it is contagious. Her daughter is eight.
3. What had somebody told her about the cause, and what does the doctor call that?
That it was dirty skin. The doctor calls it the most common misconception and says it is wrong.
Listening 1 · A Dermatologist

Explaining Without Frightening

A dermatologist on the order in which she says things. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she always say first, and why?
Whatever takes the fear away — usually that it is not contagious and not cancer. She says a frightened patient does not hear the explanation at all.
2. What is her rule about technical words?
She uses one, at most, and only after the plain version. The technical word is a label to look up later, not the explanation itself.
3. What does she say about the word cure?
Never to promise one that does not exist. She says patients forgive a hard truth and never forgive a promise that fails.
Listening 2 · A Patient

What I Understood, and What I Did Not

A patient repeating, at home, what her doctor told her. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the one sentence she remembers word for word?
That her skin is building itself too fast. She says it is the only thing she could repeat to her husband that evening.
2. What did she misunderstand about remission?
She thought it meant the illness had gone, and only understood later that it means a quiet period, and that a relapse is part of how it behaves.
3. What does she say she wishes the doctor had asked her?
Whether she had understood. She says she nodded the whole time and understood about half.
Real Document

The Leaflet You Hand at the Door

UNDERSTANDING PSORIASIS
information for patients · page 1 of 2
What it is. A condition that makes the skin build itself faster than it can shed.
Why it happens. The immune system, which normally defends you, reacts in the wrong place.
Can I pass it on? No. It is not contagious, and nothing you did caused it.
Is there a cure? Not yet. It is chronic, which means it stays with you.
What can be done. Treatment that keeps it quiet, and long periods of remission for most people.
If it comes back. A relapse is not a failure — it is part of how this condition behaves.
Written for patients by the clinic. This is a teaching document for this lesson, in the format American clinics use; it is not a source of clinical guidance.
1. Every line of this leaflet uses the same structure. What is it, and why does it suit a patient document?
A plain noun followed by a relative clause that explains it — a condition that makes, the immune system which defends, chronic which means. It keeps each idea to one sentence, so the reader never has to hold two.
2. Which line answers a question the patient was afraid to ask, and how does it do it?
Can I pass it on. It answers no first, then adds that nothing the patient did caused it — reassurance before explanation.
3. The last line says a relapse is not a failure. Why is that sentence in a patient leaflet at all?
Because patients read a relapse as their own fault or as the treatment failing, and stop the treatment. Naming it in advance is what keeps them on it.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can explain a chronic condition in ninety seconds with at most one technical word.
I glue a relative clause to the noun it defines, with who for people and that or which for things.
I never say a condition that it stays, or a disease chronic.
I reassure before I explain, and I never promise a cure that does not exist.
I know the words: chronic, to manage, inflammation, contagious, a misconception, a remission.
