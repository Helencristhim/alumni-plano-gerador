In Plain English -- Explaining a Diagnosis Without Jargon

The patient asks what it is, and every technical word you know makes it worse. Key words: chronic, to manage, a cure, the immune system, inflammation, harmless, contagious, to reassure, a misconception, a relapse, a remission, to put it simply. Structure: defining relative clauses -- who, that, which.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Chronic -- lasting a long time, often for the rest of a person’s life
"It is a chronic condition, which means it stays with you."
To manage -- to keep a condition under control without making it go away
"We cannot cure it, but we can manage it very well."
A cure -- a treatment that makes an illness go away for good
"There is no cure yet, and I am not going to pretend there is."
The immune system -- the part of the body that defends it against infection
"Your immune system is doing its job in the wrong place."
Inflammation -- the redness, heat and swelling the body itself produces
"The redness is inflammation, which is the body reacting."
Harmless -- not able to cause any damage
"The spot is harmless. I am telling you that before you ask."
Contagious -- able to pass from one person to another
"It is not contagious. You cannot give it to anybody."
To reassure -- to say something that takes away somebody’s fear
"Before anything else, let me reassure you about one thing."
A misconception -- a belief that many people hold and that is not true
"The most common misconception is that it is dirty skin."
A relapse -- the return of an illness after a quiet period
"A relapse is not a failure. It is part of how this behaves."
A remission -- a period when the illness is quiet and the signs are gone
"She has been in remission for two years, and that is the goal."
To put it simply -- the phrase that announces the plain version is coming
"To put it simply: your skin is building itself too fast."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A patient asks the question that no amount of reading prepares you for: what is it, doctor? And the words that come to mind first are the ones that help least. Plain English is not simpler English. It is the same idea carried by a different structure, and the structure is a clause glued to a plain word.

Watch how the leaflet does it. Psoriasis is a condition that makes the skin build itself faster than it can shed. The noun is plain: a condition. Everything technical went into the clause that follows it. The redness is inflammation, which is your own body reacting — again, plain noun, then the clause. It is chronic, which means it stays with you. That last one is the workhorse: which means turns any technical word into an explanation, in the same breath.

Two clauses, two words. Who points at a person: you are the kind of patient who reads before the appointment. That and which point at a thing: the cream that your sister gave you, the defence which is working in the wrong place. And the clause sits glued to the noun it defines, with no comma and no repeated subject: never a condition that it stays with you.

The order matters as much as the grammar. To reassure comes first — it is not contagious, and the spot is harmless — because a frightened patient hears nothing else until that sentence arrives. Only then the mechanism, then the misconception to correct, and last the honest part: there is no cure, but we can manage it, and a relapse after a long remission is not a failure.

1. Why does the leaflet say "a condition that makes the skin build itself too fast" instead of naming the mechanism?
A Because the technical name is difficult to pronounce.
B Because the plain noun carries the idea and the relative clause carries the explanation, so the patient never has to hold two sentences at once.
C Because leaflets are not allowed to use technical terms.
2. "It is chronic, which means it stays with you." What is the job of which means here?
A It softens the bad news.
B It repeats the same information twice for emphasis.
C It turns a technical word into its own explanation, in the same sentence.
3. Why does the doctor say the condition is not contagious BEFORE explaining what it is?
A Because it is the shortest sentence.
B Because a frightened patient does not take in the explanation until the fear is answered.
C Because contagion is the only thing that matters clinically.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Defining Relative Clauses -- Who, That, Which

GRAMMAR

The clause goes glued to the noun it defines. No comma, no repeated subject -- the relative pronoun already IS the subject.

JobFormExample
Defining a thingthat / which + verba condition that stays with you
Defining a personwho + verbthe patient who reads before coming
Where it goesglued to the noun it definesNot a condition, stays with you.
No commadefining clauses take no commathe cream that she gave you
Never repeat the subjectthe relative pronoun already is itNot a condition that it stays.
Object clausethat is optional as objectthe cream (that) she gave you
Explaining a wordX, which means...It is chronic, which means it stays with you.

Never: a condition that it stays with you · the cream who your sister gave you · a disease chronic · inflammation is the redness, which your body produces (defining clause takes no comma) · the patient which reads before coming

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A frightened patient asks what it is. Your first sentence is:
A It is a chronic autoimmune condition of the epidermis.
B Let me reassure you about one thing first: it is not contagious.
C It is complicated. I will explain later.
You want to define the condition in one sentence, in plain words. You say:
A It is a condition that makes your skin build itself too fast.
B It is a condition that it makes your skin build too fast.
C It is a disease chronic of the skin.
The patient says a friend told her it comes from dirty skin. You answer:
A Your friend is not a doctor.
B That is the most common misconception, and it is wrong. It is your immune system, which is a defence reacting in the wrong place.
C It is not that. It is autoimmune.
She asks whether you can cure it. You cannot. You say:
A We will get rid of this, do not worry.
B There is no cure, and I am not going to pretend there is. But we can manage it well.
C Cure is a strong word in medicine.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Choose any condition you treat and explain it to somebody who left school at fifteen: what it is, why it happens, whether they can pass it on, whether it can be cured, and what happens next. Ninety seconds, at most one technical word, and at least four relative clauses.
