Five Minutes, One Message -- Presentation Skills

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Outline -- a short plan that lists the main points of a talk in the order you will say them
"Send me the outline of your talk before Thursday."
Hook -- an opening line that tells the audience at once why the talk matters to them
"Your first sentence is the hook -- make it about them, not you."
Signpost -- to use a short phrase that shows the audience where you are in the talk and what comes next
"Always signpost each new section so nobody gets lost."
Headline -- the one-sentence main message of a slide or a talk, written as a full statement
"Every slide needs a headline, not just a title."
Rehearse -- to practice a talk out loud, with the timing, before you give it for real
"I always rehearse the opening aloud twice."
Pace -- the speed at which you speak or move through a talk
"Slow your pace when you give a number."
Filler -- a word or sound, such as um or basically, that fills a silence but adds no meaning
"Every filler you cut makes you sound more certain."
Attention span -- the length of time a person can stay focused on something
"On a Friday afternoon the board has a short attention span."
Call to action -- a clear request at the end of a talk that tells the audience what to do or decide
"End with a clear call to action, not a summary."
Handout -- a printed page with the details, given to the audience so the slides do not have to hold them
"Put the full costs in a one-page handout."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

On Thursday night Maria Claudia writes the outline for Friday. She has five minutes with the family board, and she knows their attention span after lunch is short. She starts with a hook: "The June consignment will arrive two weeks late unless we choose a route today." To begin with, she will give the good news: the May container has been cleared. Having said that, June is still at risk, because the carrier changed the route. As a result, the lead time is now five weeks. In other words, the parts will reach the plant two weeks late. That brings me to the options, she writes -- rerouting or expediting -- and she will signpost each one so nobody gets lost. The costs go into a handout, not onto the slide. She will rehearse it twice, at a slower pace than feels natural, and she will cut every filler. Finally, she will end with a call to action: "Approve one option today."

1. Why does she start with the hook and not with the history of the shipment?
A Because the history is not important.
B Because the board has to know in the first seconds why they are listening.
C Because she has no time to rehearse.
2. What does "Having said that" tell the board?
A That a contrast is coming -- the bad news after the good news.
B That the talk is finished.
C That she is repeating the last sentence.
3. How will the talk end?
A With a summary of every point.
B With "thank you for listening".
C With a call to action: "Approve one option today."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Discourse Markers

GRAMMAR

A listener cannot scroll back, so good speakers use short phrases that say what kind of sentence is coming before it arrives. Each marker has a job: to start or order the points, to add a contrast, to show a result, to say something more simply, or to move to the next part. Put the marker at the start of the sentence, usually followed by a comma.

JobMarkersExample
start / orderto begin with, first of all, finallyTo begin with, the May container has been cleared.
move to a new topicturning to, let me turn toTurning to the costs, rerouting is cheaper.
contrasthaving said that, even so, on the other handHaving said that, June is still at risk.
resultas a result, which means thatAs a result, the lead time is five weeks.
say it more simplyin other words, to put it simplyIn other words, the parts will be late.
go to the next partthat brings me toThat brings me to the decision.

Key point: three traps. First, "said that" → "having said that". Second, "as a result" + comma, but "as a result of" + noun: "as a result of the new route". Third, "in other word" → "in other words" -- always plural.

Stage 2: A Five-Minute Update in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the parts of a five-minute update in the correct order.

?You give the first point, starting with a marker such as "to begin with", so the room knows the talk has begun.
?You end with a call to action, ask for the decision by name, and then you stop talking.
?You write one sentence that the audience must remember, and you build the whole outline around it.
?You signpost the move to each new point, so that anyone who drifted can find the way back.
?You open with a hook that tells the room why these five minutes matter to them.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best sentence for each situation.

You have five minutes with the board. Which opening is best?
A "So, basically, I want to share some logistics updates with you."
B "The June consignment will arrive two weeks late unless we choose a route today."
C "Let me start with the history of our relationship with Pittsburgh."
You gave the good news. Now you need the bad news. Which is best?
A "Having said that, the June consignment is still at risk."
B "Said that, June is at risk."
C "In other word, June is at risk."
You are at the end of your talk. Which is best?
A "That is all, thank you."
B "To sum up everything again: May, June, the carrier, the options..."
C "So what I need from you today is a decision between the two options."
