Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. Do not translate — the example sentence is the meaning.

To fit in -- to feel accepted by a group of people
"It took me six weeks to fit in, and then it happened suddenly."
Awkward -- uncomfortable and slightly embarrassing
"The first lunch was awkward, and then it never was again."
Culture shock -- the tired, confused feeling of living in a very different culture
"Culture shock has a shape: excitement, then irritation, then normal life."
To stand out -- to be very noticeable, different from everybody around you
"My accent makes me stand out, and honestly I quite like it."
Blunt -- saying exactly what you think, very directly, sometimes too much
"People in Amsterdam were blunt, and at first I thought they were angry."
To take something the wrong way -- to understand something as an insult when it was not meant that way
"I don't want to take it the wrong way, so I asked her what she meant."
To hesitate -- to pause before doing something because you are not sure
"Don't hesitate. If you wait, the moment to speak is gone."
Worth it -- good enough to justify the effort or the money
"Six months away from everybody you know is hard. It is worth it."
To make up your mind -- to finally decide, after thinking about it
"I couldn't make up my mind about the trip until March."
Tempting -- something you would really like to do, even if it is a bad idea
"Staying in bed on a cold Tuesday is very tempting."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nobody warns you about week six. The first weeks are loud and new and you say yes to everything. Then the noise stops and suddenly the silence at dinner feels awkward, the weather feels personal and you miss food you did not even like at home. That is culture shock, and it has a shape: excitement, irritation, normal life. If I knew that on day one, I would worry a lot less about week six.

The other thing nobody tells you is that people are more blunt here than in Sao Paulo. Somebody says what they think and I spend an hour deciding whether they were rude. So now I ask. If I took everything the wrong way, I would have no friends by October. Asking what somebody meant costs one sentence and saves a whole week.

Nico arrived two months before me and gave me two pieces of advice. If he started again, he would speak more in the first month, because he stayed quiet until November and by then he was already the quiet one. And he would say yes to more things. Staying in bed on a cold Tuesday is tempting, but the invitations are where everything happens. If I were a new student next year, that is exactly what I would tell her.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Possible, or Only in Your Head?

GRAMMAR

Last lesson: things that can really happen. This one: things that cannot, or probably will not.

FormUseExample
Real and possible (first)if + present, will + verbIf I miss the bus, I'll walk.
Imaginary (second)if + past, would + verbIf I lived in Puglia, I'd swim every day.
The past is not the pastIn the second conditional the past form only means this is not real. Both halves move back one step, and nothing else changes.
If I wereWith to be, English uses were for everybody in this sentence. "If I were you" is the standard way of giving advice.
would, could, mightwould = I'm sure. could = one option. might = maybe. All three take the base verb, with no to.
The trapNever would in the if half. "If I would have time" does not exist. It is "if I had time".

One question decides it: can this really happen? Yes, first conditional. No, second. That is the whole decision.
