Lesson 19 -- Pre-class

Projections and Deadlines -- Putting a Date on It

The post-mortem ended with two changes and two dates. Now the committee wants the whole plan. Key words: projection, milestone, lead time, slippage, roll out, cut-off date, commit to, backlog, provisional, horizon. Structure: future forms (will, going to, present continuous) and the deadline prepositions by, until and within.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Projection -- a number for a future period, worked out from what you know today
"The projection for the fourth quarter is flat, on today's assumptions."
Milestone -- a fixed point in a plan where one named result has to be finished
"The first milestone is the alert going live on the risk desk."
Lead time -- the time between asking for something and having it ready
"The lead time on a change to the alert is six weeks."
Slippage -- the time a plan loses when one step finishes later than planned
"One week of slippage on the system change moves every date after it."
To roll out -- to put a change into use across a team or a system, step by step
"We are rolling the alert out desk by desk from the first of September."
Cut-off date -- the last day something is accepted for the current period
"The cut-off date for the quarterly figures is the twenty-eighth."
To commit to -- to promise a date or a result in public, so that people can hold you to it
"I will commit to the first milestone, and not to the last one."
Backlog -- the work that is waiting and has not been started yet
"The backlog on Risk Systems is eleven changes long."
Provisional -- not final, and still able to change before the real number arrives
"These figures are provisional; I am confirming them on Friday."
Horizon -- the length of time a plan or a number looks ahead
"On a twelve-month horizon the exposure is flat."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The post-mortem is written, and the committee now wants the plan behind it. There are four milestones on one page, and the deputy line is closed already. The second one is the alert: the decision was taken last week, so we are going to roll it out desk by desk from the first of September, and the risk desk goes first. The lead time on a change to the alert is six weeks, and the backlog on Risk Systems is eleven changes long, so one week of slippage there moves every date after it. That is why I will commit to the first milestone in the room and not to the last one. The third line is the quarterly number. The cut-off date is the twenty-eighth, and the figures stay provisional until then; the confirmed version will be on the chair's desk by the twenty-ninth. On a twelve-month horizon the projection is flat, and I will say so as soon as I have the closing figures — not before. The last line is already in the diary: Internal Audit is reviewing the new alert on the fourth of November at nine.

1. "We are going to roll it out from the first of September." Why "going to" and not "will"?
A Because "will" cannot be used with a date.
B Because the decision was already taken before this conversation.
C Because the sentence is about a rollout, and a rollout always takes "going to".
2. "The confirmed version will be on the chair's desk by the twenty-ninth." What does "by" mean here?
A That it stays on the desk the whole time up to the twenty-ninth.
B That it arrives on the twenty-ninth at the latest, and it can arrive earlier.
C That it arrives exactly on the twenty-ninth and on no other day.
3. "I will say so as soon as I have the closing figures." Why is there no "will" in the second half?
A After "as soon as", "when" and "until", English keeps the present and puts the "will" in the other half.
B Because the figures are provisional, and provisional facts take the present.
C Because one sentence can only carry one verb in the future.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Future Forms

GRAMMAR

English has three everyday futures, and the one you choose tells the room how much of the plan was already decided before you opened your mouth.

FormWhen you use itExample
will + base verbA decision you make as you speak, a promise, or a prediction.I will send the confirmed number on Friday.
going to + base verbA plan already decided before this conversation, or evidence you can see now.We are going to roll it out desk by desk.
present continuousA fixed arrangement: a date, and usually a time, already in the diary.I am presenting the plan on Tuesday at nine.
by · until · withinby = not later than that day. until = the whole time up to that day. within = inside that period.It is on your desk by the twenty-ninth.
no will after when / as soon as / untilThe time half takes the present and the will lives in the other half: "I will confirm it when I have the figures" — never "when I will have". Three errors to avoid: "send it until Friday", "as soon as I will have", and "Tomorrow I go to the committee at nine".

Quiz

A "Fine, I am going to send it on Friday."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Choose one thing you have to deliver this quarter: a report, a model, a limit review or a system change. Present it out loud to somebody who will hold you to it. Say what the milestones are and when each one lands, what the lead time is, what was already decided before today, which date you commit to and which one you do not, and what is still provisional. Use will for what you decide as you speak, going to for what was already decided, and the present continuous for anything already in your diary. Take your time.
