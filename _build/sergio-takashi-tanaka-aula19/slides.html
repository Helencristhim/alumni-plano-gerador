Chapter 3: The Code

Three Futures

will · going to · present continuous

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Fine — I will send you the confirmed number on Friday."

"We are going to roll the alert out desk by desk."

"The committee is meeting on the fourth at nine."

"I will confirm the figures as soon as I have the cut-off number."

All four lines are about the future, and no two use the same form. What decides which one you say — and why does the last one have no will in the first half?

FormWhen you use itExample
will + base verbA decision you make as you speak, a promise, or a prediction.I will send the confirmed number on Friday.
going to + base verbA plan already decided before this conversation, or evidence in front of you now.We are going to roll it out desk by desk.
present continuousA fixed arrangement: a date, and usually a time, already in the diary.The committee is meeting on the fourth at nine.
by · until · withinby = not later than that day. until = the whole time up to that day. within = inside that period.The alert is live by the first, not "until the first".
no will after when / as soon as / untilThe time half takes the present; the will lives in the other half: "I will call you as soon as I have the number" — never "as soon as I will have". Same for when, before, after and until.

The form is not decorative: it tells the room how much of the plan was already decided before you opened your mouth.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I will send you the plan until Friday."
"I will send you the plan by Friday."
"I will confirm it when I will have the cut-off number."
"I will confirm it when I have the cut-off number."
"Tomorrow I go to the committee at nine."
"Tomorrow I am going to the committee at nine."

By is a deadline; until is a length of time. After when, as soon as and until, English keeps the present and puts the will in the other half. And a meeting already in your diary takes the present continuous, never the present simple.

Listening

Marina on Dates

A voice note the night before. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does Marina say happens to a plan with only one date on it?
The committee treats the whole thing as one promise, so the first slip kills the plan. She wants milestones, so that a late step is visible early.
2. Which two dates does she say he should not put in the same sentence?
The date he controls and the date another team controls — because the room hears both as his promise.
3. What does she tell him to say about a number that is not confirmed yet?
To call it provisional out loud, and to name the day it becomes final — never to present it as if it were the final figure.
Listening 2

Paulo on Promises

A message from the credit desk. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. According to Paulo, what does a room hear when you say "we are going to"?
That the decision was already taken before the meeting — the plan exists and you are reporting it, not inventing it in the room.
2. Which form does he say makes a date sound already booked, and why?
The present continuous, because it is the form people use for a diary entry: a date, a time, and somebody else expecting you.
3. What is the one preposition mistake he says costs money?
Saying "until Friday" when you mean "by Friday": until promises the whole week, by promises a deadline.
