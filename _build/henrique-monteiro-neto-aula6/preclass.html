Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

The Weight of Words — Official Language Decoded

A communique of two hundred words, four days of argument, and everything it was written not to say. Key expressions: a communique, to reaffirm, to take note of, a caveat, wording, to water down, binding, to be subject to, in good faith, to fall short of, a deadlock, non-committal, a face-saving formula, to sign off on, an undertaking, to be couched in, a loophole, open to interpretation. Structure: nominalisation and formal register — how English turns an action into a thing, and what disappears when it does.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

A communique -- an official statement issued after a meeting between parties
"The communique ran to two hundred words and took four days."
To reaffirm -- to state formally, again, a commitment that already existed
"They reaffirmed a principle nobody had questioned, which is the point."
To take note of -- to acknowledge something formally without approving or committing to it
"The council took note of the report, which is not the same as accepting it."
A caveat -- a warning or condition attached to a statement to limit it
"Every figure in that annex arrives with a caveat."
Wording -- the exact choice of words, as opposed to the general meaning
"They agreed on the policy in an hour and on the wording in four days."
To water down -- to weaken a text by removing what was strong in it
"The final draft had been watered down almost beyond recognition."
Binding -- legally obliging the parties to do what the text says
"Nothing in paragraph six is binding, and everybody signing it knew that."
To be subject to -- to depend on a further approval or condition before it takes effect
"The transfer is subject to parliamentary approval."
In good faith -- with honest intentions, though without any guarantee of the result
"Both sides undertook to negotiate in good faith, and both meant it."
To fall short of -- to be less than what was needed, expected or promised
"The text falls short of what the smaller countries had asked for."
A deadlock -- a situation in which neither side is able to move
"By Wednesday night the talks were in complete deadlock."
Non-committal -- deliberately giving no clear answer either way
"His reply was studiously non-committal, and it was meant to be."
A face-saving formula -- wording that lets both sides claim they did not lose
"What broke the deadlock was a face-saving formula about timing."
To sign off on -- to give the final approval that lets something proceed
"Three capitals had to sign off on a single adverb."
An undertaking -- a formal promise to do a particular thing
"The undertaking is real, and the deadline attached to it is not."
To be couched in -- to be expressed in a particular kind of language, usually to soften it
"The refusal is couched in the language of regret."
A loophole -- a gap in the wording that allows the obligation to be avoided
"The loophole is in the definition, not in the commitment."
Open to interpretation -- capable of meaning more than one thing, sometimes on purpose
"The phrase is open to interpretation, which is why it survived."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The communique is two hundred words long and it took four days. The policy had been settled on the first afternoon; the four days went on the wording, which is where the argument actually lives. Read it once and it says a great deal. Read it twice, counting the verbs, and you notice how few of them have anybody attached.

The parties reaffirm a commitment nobody had questioned, which tells you it was never at issue. They take note of the report from the smaller delegations, a phrase which acknowledges that the report exists and obliges the reader to nothing whatever. And there has been, we are told, a marked improvement in cooperation — a sentence in which cooperation improves entirely by itself.

Paragraph six repays a second reading. It contains an undertaking, which is real, and a deadline, which is subject to a review that has not been scheduled. Nothing in it is binding. The draft had been watered down twice by Wednesday, and by Thursday the talks were in deadlock over a single adverb that three capitals eventually had to sign off on. The loophole, if there is one, is in the definition rather than in the promise.

What broke the deadlock was a face-saving formula about timing. The result falls short of what the smaller states asked for and is couched in language warm enough for them to present it at home as progress. Officials were non-committal afterwards, with the usual caveat that talks were continuing in good faith. The key phrase remains open to interpretation, and that, one negotiator said later, is exactly why it survived.

1. "There has been a marked improvement in cooperation." What has this sentence removed?
A The date.
B The person or body responsible for the improvement.
C The word cooperation.
2. What is the difference between to take note of and to accept?
A There is none — they are synonyms in official texts.
B Take note of acknowledges that something exists without committing to it.
C Take note of is stronger and legally binding.
3. The deadline is described as "subject to a review that has not been scheduled". What does that mean in practice?
A That the deadline is fixed and will be reviewed afterwards.
B That the review already happened.
C That the deadline does not take effect until something that has no date happens.
4. Why does the text say the key phrase survived because it is open to interpretation?
A Because ambiguity was the only version every party could sign.
B Because nobody read it.
C Because it was translated badly.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Nominalisation — the Action Becomes a Thing

GRAMMAR

Nominalisation turns a verb or an adjective into a noun. It is what makes formal English compact and readable — and it is also what allows a sentence to describe an action without saying who performed it.

FormWhat it doesExample
verb → noundecide → decision · improve → improvement · withdraw → withdrawal · refuse → refusal · fail → failure · comply → compliance. There is no single rule; these are learned one at a time."They decided to postpone it." →
"The decision to postpone it was taken."
adjective → nounable → ability · reluctant → reluctance · willing → willingness · absent → absence."They were reluctant to sign." →
"Their reluctance to sign was obvious."
There has been
+ noun phrase		The standard opening of institutional English. Something changed, and the sentence contains nobody who changed it."There has been a marked improvement in compliance."
Failure to + verb
The absence of + noun		Names something that did NOT happen as though it were an event, so that a consequence can be attached to it. Standard in rules and contracts."Failure to notify will result in suspension."
The pattern after the nounAfter decision, attempt, failure, refusal, willingness: to + verb, never for. After many others: of + noun."The refusal to comment" · "the withdrawal of the proposal"

The judgement to carry away: nominalisation is not a trick, it is compression, and any serious document needs it. It becomes a trick at one precise point — when the actor who has disappeared is the one who would otherwise be responsible. Mistakes were made is grammatical, polite, and doing something.
