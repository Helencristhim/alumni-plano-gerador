Chapter 3: The Code

When an Action Becomes a Thing

And the person who did it quietly disappears

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"The decision to postpone the inspection was taken in March."

"There has been a marked improvement in compliance since January."

"The withdrawal of the proposal came without explanation."

"Failure to notify the committee will result in suspension."

Say each of these as a plain sentence with a person in it. What disappears when you turn it back?

FormWhat it doesExample
verb → noun
decide → decision		The action becomes a thing that can be dated, measured and discussed — and that needs no subject."The decision to postpone was taken in March."
(Who took it? The sentence does not say.)
adjective → noun
able → ability		Turns a quality into a countable object, which is what allows it to be argued about in a report."The ability to verify remains limited."
There has been
+ noun phrase		The workhorse of institutional English. Something changed and nobody changed it."There has been a marked improvement in compliance."
Failure to + verb
The absence of + noun		Names a non-event as if it were an event. Extremely common in rules, contracts and warnings."Failure to notify will result in suspension."
The cost:
who did it?		Every nominalisation buys formality and pays for it with an actor. That trade is the whole subject of this lesson."Mistakes were made" vs "We made a mistake."

Nominalisation is not dishonest in itself — it is how any serious document keeps a long argument readable. It becomes dishonest at exactly one point: when the actor who has disappeared is the one who would otherwise be responsible.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"The decision was taken for postpone the inspection."
"The decision to postpone the inspection was taken."
"There has been a improve in compliance since January."
"There has been an improvement in compliance since January."
"The withdraw of the proposal came without explanation."
"The withdrawal of the proposal came without explanation."

Two mechanical traps and one habit. After a noun like decision, attempt or failure, English uses to + verb, never for. And the noun form has to be the real one: improve gives improvement, withdraw gives withdrawal, refuse gives refusal — there is no shortcut, and this is worth a page in your notes.

Dialogue

The Woman Who Writes the Adverbs

I
People imagine my job is translation. It is closer to demolition. I am given a sentence four countries have approved and asked whether it survives in three languages.
H
And when it does not survive, who decides what changes — you or the delegations?
I
I say what breaks. They decide what to do about it. Last month I flagged a phrase that was open to interpretation in two of the three, and it went back for another day.
H
Is the ambiguity ever deliberate? I have always assumed it is, and I have never had anybody in the room to ask.
I
Frequently. Ambiguity is sometimes the only thing everyone can sign. What I object to is a loophole nobody has noticed, which is a different animal entirely.
H
The accidental one. Because the deliberate one at least has an author who can be argued with.
I
Exactly. And there is a third kind, which is the sentence written so that no author exists at all. There has been a deterioration. By whom? The text will not say.
H
That is the one I find hardest to read honestly, because it is grammatical, it is polite, and it is doing something.
Comprehension

About Ingrid

1. How does Ingrid describe her job?
Closer to demolition than to translation — she tests whether a sentence survives in three languages.
2. What happened to the phrase she flagged last month?
It went back for another day, because it was open to interpretation in two of the three languages.
3. What does she object to, as opposed to deliberate ambiguity?
A loophole that nobody has noticed.
4. What is the third kind of sentence she describes?
One written so that no author exists at all — "there has been a deterioration", with no one responsible.
Listening 1

The Statement Itself

A spokesperson reading a prepared text. Sound first — no text.

1. What does the statement say has taken place?
A comprehensive review, and a marked improvement in cooperation.
2. What is the commitment subject to?
A further review, for which no date is given.
3. What does the spokesperson say about responsibility?
Nothing at all — no person or body is named anywhere in the text.
Listening 2

The Same Thing, Said Plainly

A former negotiator, off the record. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say they actually argued about for four days?
The wording, and at the end a single adverb.
2. Why does he say ambiguity is sometimes necessary?
Because it is occasionally the only text everybody is able to sign.
3. What does he say he regrets?
The paragraph with an undertaking and no date, which he helped write.
