Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Eight clauses from the back of the document. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Force majeure -- an event outside anyone's control that excuses performance
"The supplier is trying to invoke a force majeure clause."
Cure period -- the time you are given to fix a failure before it becomes a breach
"The side letter is the only place the cure period is written down."
Liquidated damages -- an amount fixed in advance as compensation for a specific failure
"Late delivery triggers liquidated damages of fifty thousand a week."
Entire agreement -- the clause saying the contract is the whole deal, and nothing outside it counts
"The entire agreement clause may exclude the side letter."
Severability -- the clause keeping the rest of the contract alive if one part fails
"There is no severability provision at all, which is unusual."
Boilerplate -- the standard clauses at the back that everyone copies and nobody reads
"It is the boilerplate that decides the case three years later."
Recital -- the opening paragraphs that explain why the parties are signing
"The recitals tell a story about the deal that no longer matches it."
Notices -- the clause saying where and how a formal message must be sent
"The notices clause still lists an address the company left two years ago."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the note on the award. The structures in bold decide where you look, without adding a word of information.

Meridian and Cortez negotiated the commercial terms for six weeks. The clauses at the back — the boilerplate — were copied from a previous deal in a single afternoon.

Cortez missed eleven deliveries and Meridian claimed liquidated damages. Cortez argued that a side letter gave it a longer cure period. What the tribunal never reached was that question: the entire agreement clause was so wide that the side letter fell outside the contract. It was one sentence at the back that decided the case — not the deliveries, and not the damages formula.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Cleft sentences — move the focus, not the volume

Neither form adds information. Both decide where the listener looks.

  • It-cleft — It is / It was + the thing + that...
    Points at one element and excludes the others.
    "It was the entire agreement clause that decided the case, not the deliveries."
  • What-cleft — What + subject + verb + is / was...
    Names the topic first, the content after.
    "What they failed to disclose was the lien."

Two things to watch. The that in the it-cleft is not optional in formal speech. And the what-cleft takes a singular verb: What concerns me is..., never are.

This is the alternative to speaking faster or louder. When a discussion drifts, you do not raise the volume — you change where the sentence points.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Say each one in NORMAL volume. The structure carries the emphasis — if you push with your tone as well, it sounds like an accusation.

It was the entire agreement clause that decided the case.
What they failed to disclose was the lien.
What I find most concerning is the absence of a severability provision.
It is the notices clause that we need to correct first.
