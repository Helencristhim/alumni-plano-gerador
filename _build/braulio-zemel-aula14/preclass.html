Lesson 14 -- Pre-class

Cinema and Culture -- Saying More Than That You Liked It

Twelve words for what a film does to you, and the five openings that turn a verdict into a conversation. Key expressions: compelling, ambiguous, portrayal, undertone, tension, to resonate, genre, critique, to evoke, layered, understated, to unfold. Structure: hedging language for degrees of certainty.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. None of these describes a plot -- they all describe an effect. These twelve stay here in your material.

Compelling -- so interesting that you cannot look away from it
"The first twenty minutes are so compelling that nobody in the room moved."
Ambiguous -- open to more than one reading, on purpose
"The ending is deliberately ambiguous, and the director refuses to explain it."
Portrayal -- the way a person or a subject is shown on screen
"The portrayal of the mother is the most honest thing in the film."
Undertone -- a quiet feeling running underneath what is being said
"There is a political undertone running under every dinner scene."
Tension -- the unease between two forces that cannot both win
"The tension between the two brothers is never resolved, and that is the point."
To resonate -- to keep affecting somebody long after it has finished
"Films about leaving work late in life resonate with me in a way they did not before."
Genre -- a family of films defined by its rules and its style
"It looks like a thriller, but it breaks every rule of the genre."
Critique -- a judgement that explains the reasons behind itself
"Her critique of the film is longer than the film deserved and better written."
To evoke -- to bring a feeling or a memory to the surface
"A single piano note is enough to evoke the whole decade."
Layered -- carrying more than one level of meaning at the same time
"It is a layered film: three viewings and you are still finding things."
Understated -- powerful precisely because it is kept quiet
"The performance is so understated that you only notice it afterwards."
To unfold -- to develop slowly, one step at a time
"The story takes ninety minutes to unfold, and it needs every one of them."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a moment at every dinner where somebody asks what you thought of something, and the honest answer is short. You liked it. It was good. In Portuguese that answer can carry a whole opinion, because the tone does the rest of the work. In English it does not carry anything, and the conversation stops there, politely.

The reason is not vocabulary. It is that an English opinion is expected to arrive unfinished. It strikes me that the ending is deliberately ambiguous. One could argue that the portrayal of the mother is the only honest thing in the film. To some extent the whole genre is built on that idea. Each of those is a firm opinion, and each of them hands the other person somewhere to go.

This catches people out in an unexpected way. A speaker who is careful and precise in his own language often produces the flattest sentences in his second one, because the machinery he uses to be careful has not been translated yet. He knows exactly what he thinks about the tension in the film and the political undertone under it, and what comes out is that it was very good.

The machinery is small. Five openings and a habit of naming one specific thing. It strikes me that, one could argue that, to some extent, there is a sense that, I tend to think that. None of them means you are unsure. They mean you are still talking, which is the only thing an English dinner table is really asking of you.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging

GRAMMAR

Five openings that let you say exactly what you think and still leave the other person somewhere to go. Hedging in English is not uncertainty and it is not politeness -- it is what an opinion sounds like when it expects an answer.

FormWhat it doesExample
It strikes me that...Presents a firm opinion as your impression, which invites an answer instead of ending the exchange."It strikes me that the ending is ambiguous."
One could argue that...Puts an argument on the table without owning it yet, so you can test it before you commit."One could argue that it is his best film."
To some extent...Concedes a limit first, which makes everything after it sound more confident."To some extent, although the second half changes that."
There is a sense that... / It would appear that...Reports something that is in the room rather than only in you."There is a sense that he does not trust the audience."
The trapsOne hedge is a signal; four in a row is somebody who does not want to be quoted. And could, like every modal, takes the bare verb.NOT "maybe possibly a little bit good"
NOT "One could to argue"

A practical note. The fastest of the five is to some extent, because it does two jobs at once: it agrees with part of what you just heard and it announces that something else is coming. When a conversation is moving faster than you are, those three words buy you a whole sentence of thinking time and sound like generosity.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best option for each situation.

Somebody at a dinner asks what you thought of a film you admired. The strongest answer is:
A It was very good. I liked it a lot.
B It strikes me that it is a much smaller film than it pretends to be.
C Sorry, my English is not good enough to explain it.
You disagree with somebody, but you want the conversation to continue. You say:
A No, that is wrong.
B To some extent, although I tend to think the slowness is the point.
C Maybe. I do not know. Possibly.
You want to test an idea without committing to it yet. You open with:
A One could argue that the war is not really the subject.
B Everybody knows the war is not really the subject.
C In my opinion, for me, I believe the war is not the subject.
Which sentence is grammatically correct?
A One could to argue that it is his best film.
B It strikes to me that it is his best film.
C It would appear that it is his best film.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is scoring it.

Record about ninety seconds. Choose a film or a documentary you have seen more than once. Say in two sentences what it is about, name the one scene that does the real work, and say what it left you with. Use at least three of the five openings from this lesson, and do not use the word good at any point.
