Chapter 3: The Code

The Word in the Middle

who / which / that / where

Grammar Discovery

What Joins the Two Facts?

Look at the red word. What kind of noun comes just before it?

"The artist who painted this canvas was born in Baltimore."

"The window, which takes up half of the picture, looks out on a landscape."

"The cup that everybody notices first is orange."

"The room where the sisters are sitting is almost empty."

WordComes afterExample
whoa personthe artist who painted this
whicha thingthe window, which takes up half of the picture
thata person or a thing, when the information is essential (no comma)the cup that everybody notices first
wherea placethe room where the sisters are sitting
commasExtra information takes commas. Essential information takes none.The window, which..., looks out. · The cup that everybody notices is orange.

Two traps: nothing is repeated after the joining word. Not the room where the sisters are sitting there, and not the woman who she is standing in the doorway. The joining word already IS the subject.

Listening 1 · The Audio Guide

Stop Number Four

1. What does the guide ask you to do first, and what do you see from there?
Step back about two meters. From there the canvas reads as one grey block with a single orange point, which is the cup on the table.
2. Which part of the painting was finished twice?
The hands of the two girls. Up close you can see every brushstroke on them.
3. What is the shape in the doorway, and when did it come back?
It is not a shadow. It is a woman who was painted over by the artist, and she came back in 1998, when the canvas was restored.

1. What time does the museum close, and what time is the last entry?
It closes at five thirty, and the last entry is at four forty-five.
2. Why is photography not allowed on the third floor?
Because several of those works are on loan from other collections, and their owners do not permit it.

At the coat check, which is behind the ticket desk on the right, next to the elevators. The service is free.
Listening 3 · An Opinion

Why Museums Are So Tiring

Sound first — no text on screen.

0:000:00
1. Why does she think people find museums exhausting?
Not because of the art. Because they try to look at everything, four hundred works in two hours, and by the third room the brain has closed.
2. What is her rule, and which three works does she choose?
She picks three and lets the rest go: one portrait, one landscape, and one thing that simply looks striking to her.
3. How long does she stay in front of each one, and what does she say about that time?
Ten minutes each, which she says is a very long time, and something always happens in the last two minutes.
Real Document

The Wall Label

ROOM 4 · AMERICAN PAINTING
Audio guide: stop 4

Clara Whitfield

American, 1861–1934

The Sisters at the Window

1889 · Oil on canvas · 71 × 54 cm

Restored 1998 · On loan from the Baltimore Museum of Fine Art until March

A commission that the family never hung. Whitfield depicts the two daughters of a mill owner in the room where they spent most of that winter, in the muted grey and sand of her later era. Look for the orange cup, which is the only striking color on the canvas, and for the figure in the doorway, who appeared again when the painting was restored.

1. Does this museum own the painting? How do you know, and until when is it here?
No. The label says it is on loan from the Baltimore Museum of Fine Art, and it stays until March.
2. What do the two dates 1861 and 1934 mean, and what does 1889 mean?
1861 and 1934 are the years the artist was born and died. 1889 is the year of this canvas.
3. Find the two relative clauses in the last paragraph. What does each one add?
The orange cup, which is the only striking color, and the figure in the doorway, who appeared again when the painting was restored. One adds a fact about a thing, the other about a person.
