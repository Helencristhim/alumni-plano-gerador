Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Say It and Back It Up -- Holding One Position for Two Minutes

Anybody can say what they think in five seconds. The hard part is holding the same idea for two minutes and making it stand up. Key words: a point of view, evidence, a drawback, a trade-off, commitment, to back something up, to make a case for something, in the long run, to have an impact on something, a counter-argument. Structure: clauses of reason and result.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A point of view -- the position you take on a question, together with the reasons behind it
"My point of view is that a season abroad teaches you more than a year of classes."
Evidence -- the facts and examples that show a statement is true
"The evidence is simple: I arrived in August and by December nobody translated for me."
A drawback -- the part of a good decision that still costs you something
"The main drawback of four training sessions a week is that you study late at night."
A trade-off -- what you give up in order to get something else you want more
"It is a trade-off: less free time, but a level of English you cannot buy in a classroom."
Commitment -- the time and effort you agree to give to something, week after week
"A club team is a real commitment. You cannot show up only when you feel like it."
To back something up -- to give a fact or an example that makes what you said stronger
"Do not just say the team helped you. Back it up with one thing that happened."
To make a case for something -- to give the reasons why something should be done or should continue
"I can make a case for sport at school in about ninety seconds."
In the long run -- over a long period, when the final result is what counts
"In the long run, the language you use every day is the one that stays with you."
To have an impact on something -- to change something in a way that people can actually see
"Two games a week had a bigger impact on my listening than any app I ever used."
A counter-argument -- the strongest thing the other side could say against your position
"The obvious counter-argument is that my grades dropped, and I have to answer that."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A student is asked whether a season with a club team abroad is worth the hours it takes. He does not start with it depends. He starts with a point of view: five months on a team teach you more than a year of classes. Then he gives the reason, and the word that opens a reason is because. He improved fast because he spoke English at training, on the bus and at home. Since nobody on the team spoke his language, there was no easy way out. Notice that since does the same job as because, only it comes first, before the claim.

The second half of an argument does the opposite job. It does not explain why; it says what followed. Training took four evenings a week, so he studied after ten at night. The whole season ran in English, which is why he stopped translating in his head. In a written paragraph the same idea is often carried by as a result. All three point forward, to the consequence, while because and since point backward, to the cause.

Two traps catch almost every Portuguese speaker here. The first is because of, which takes a noun and never a full sentence: because of the championship, he missed two weeks of school. The second is the phrase the reason is because, which sounds right and is not. Say the reason is that. Get those two right and the rest is repetition: a claim, a reason, a piece of evidence, one honest drawback, and an answer to the counter-argument before anybody has to ask for it.

1. "He improved fast because he spoke English at training." What does the word "because" open here?
A The consequence of the first idea.
B The cause, which is the reason behind the claim.
C An example that backs the claim up.
2. According to the text, what is the difference between "since" and "because"?
A They do the same job, but "since" usually comes first, before the claim.
B "Since" opens a consequence and "because" opens a cause.
C "Since" is only used for time and never for reason.
3. The text warns against "the reason is because". What should you say instead?
A The reason is because of.
B The reason is so.
C The reason is that.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Clauses of Reason and Result

GRAMMAR

Two jobs, two sets of words: one opens the cause, the other opens the consequence.

JobFormExample
Reason, after the claimclaim + because + subject + verbI improved fast because I trained in English.
Reason, before the claimSince / As + subject + verb, + claimSince the coach spoke only English, I had to listen.
Reason as a nounbecause of / due to + noun phraseBecause of the championship, I missed two weeks.
Result, in speechreason + so + resultThe games were on Saturdays, so I studied on Sunday.
Result, more formalreason. As a result, / Therefore, + resultThe season ran in English. As a result, my listening changed.
Result as a comment..., which is why + subject + verbNobody translated for me, which is why I stopped.
Question about a reasonWhy...? / What is the reason that...?What is the reason that you left the team?

Never: because of I trained every day · Since the season was long, so I missed classes · the main reason is because the language was everywhere · I joined the club for practice my listening.

Stage 2: Put the Two-Minute Answer in Order

Order

Somebody has just asked whether a season abroad is worth a term of bad grades. Put your answer in a logical order.

?"My point of view is that a season abroad teaches you more than a year of classes."
?"I say that because nobody on the team ever waited for me to translate."
?"Let me back that up: two hours of English, four times a week, for five months."
?"There is a real drawback, and it is a trade-off I would make again."
?"The strongest counter-argument is my grades, so let me answer that directly."
?"In the long run I got the grades back, and the English stayed."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Somebody asks whether a season abroad is worth it. You want to open with a position, not with it depends. You say:
A "It depends of the person and the situation, I think."
B "My point of view is that a season abroad teaches you more than a year of classes."
C "For me is very good, everybody should do."
You want to give the reason behind your position. You say:
A "Because of I spoke English at training every day."
B "I say that because nobody on the team ever waited for me to translate."
C "The reason is because the people speak only English there."
The other person asks for something concrete. You say:
A "Let me back that up: two hours of English, four times a week, for five months."
B "Believe me, it is true. Sport builds character."
C "I already said it, so I do not need to explain again."
You know the other person is about to bring up your grades. You get there first. You say:
A "My grades are not important for this conversation."
B "Nobody can say anything against my point of view."
C "The strongest counter-argument is my grades, so let me answer that directly."
