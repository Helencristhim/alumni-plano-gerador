Chapter 3: The Code

Why You Think It, and What Follows From It

Two jobs, two sets of words

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Do?

"I joined the club team because I wanted to hear English all day."

"Since nobody on the team spoke Portuguese, I had no easy way out."

"Training took four evenings a week, so I studied after ten at night."

"The whole season ran in English, which is why five months beat five years."

The red words and the blue words both connect two ideas, but they point in opposite directions. One of them opens the CAUSE. The other opens the CONSEQUENCE.

JobFormExample
Reason, after the claimclaim + because + subject + verbI joined the team because I wanted to hear English.
Reason, before the claimSince / As + subject + verb, + claimSince nobody spoke Portuguese, I had no way out.
Reason as a nounbecause of / due to + noun phraseBecause of the championship, I missed two weeks.
Result, in speechreason + so + resultTraining took four evenings, so I studied late.
Result, more formalreason. Therefore, + result / As a result, + resultThe season ran in English. As a result, my listening changed.
Result as a comment..., which is why + subject + verbEverything ran in English, which is why it worked.
The two trapsnever because of + full clause · never the reason is becauseNot because of I trained. Say because I trained. Not the reason is because. Say the reason is that.

In one line: because and since open the cause, so and which is why open the consequence.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Somebody Who Disagrees With You

One conversation, two recordings, one real document

Dialogue

Campus Sports Blog, Thursday Afternoon

M
So you played a full season here and studied at the same time. Everyone says that is impossible. What is your point of view?
V
It is not impossible, it is a trade-off. I gave up my evenings, and I got a level of English I could not have got in a classroom.
M
I am not sure I buy that. Plenty of people take a language course and never touch a ball. Can you back that up?
V
Sure. In a course you speak for maybe twenty minutes a week. At training nobody waits for you, so you talk for two hours, four times a week. That is the evidence.
M
Fair. But my grades went down in my first season, and I know I am not the only one. Is that not a serious drawback?
V
It is, and I am not going to pretend it is not. Mine dropped too. But in the long run I got the grades back, and the English stayed.
M
That is the honest answer, and honestly it is the one nobody gives me. Most people just tell me sport builds character.
V
Because that is easy to say and impossible to prove. A number is harder to argue with. And you — how many hours a week does hockey take from you?
Comprehension

About Megan

1. What does Megan say everybody thinks about playing and studying at the same time?
That it is impossible. She opens by saying everyone tells her it cannot be done.
2. What is Megan’s own experience with her first season, and why does she bring it up?
Her grades went down in her first season, and she says she is not the only one. She brings it up as the drawback, to test whether the argument survives it.
3. How does Megan disagree, and what does she say about the answers other people give her?
She says she is not sure she buys it and asks for something to back it up. She says most people just tell her sport builds character, and that this is the answer she never gets.
Listening 1 · The Athletics Director

What a Season Actually Costs You

A university athletics director on a campus podcast. Sound first — no text.

1. What number does she give for the hours, and why does she say she gives it first?
Around twenty hours a week once travel is counted. She says she gives it first because families always find out later, and it is better to hear it from her.
2. What does she say happens to most first-year students’ grades, and what happens after that?
They go down in the first term. She says they come back up in the second year, and that the students who leave usually leave in that first bad term.
3. What is the argument she says she refuses to use, and what does she use instead?
She refuses to say that sport builds character, because it cannot be measured. She uses graduation rates and the fact that her athletes finish their degrees at a higher rate than the campus average.
Listening 2 · The Other Side

Why He Left the Team

A second-year student explaining a decision he still defends. Sound first — no text.

1. What reason does he give for leaving, and what reason does he say people expect to hear?
He left because he wanted the engineering degree more than he wanted the season. People expect to hear that he was not good enough, and he says that was not the reason.
2. What does he say about his first year on the team?
He does not regret it. He says his English and his confidence came from that year, and that he would do the first year again.
3. What does he say is the real question, instead of whether sport is good or bad?
How long you keep doing it. He says one season is an investment and four is a career decision, and that people argue about the wrong thing.

1. Two lines on this sheet are not about playing at all. Which are they, and why would the program put them on a soccer form?
Study hall, five required hours, and the rule that you have to pass every course. They are on the form because the program is protecting the degree, not only the team.
2. The sheet says travel happens every second week. What does that change about the twenty-hour figure?
It means twenty hours is an average, not a fixed number. In an away week the load is higher, and in a home week it is lower.

They lose their place in the next match. The program claims its athletes finish their degrees at a higher rate than the campus average.

Five Sentences for Any Argument

I say that because nobody on the team ever waited for me to translate.
