Chapter 4: The Code

The Plan, and the Thought Experiment

One word apart

Grammar Discovery

Which Two Could Actually Happen?

"If I get the offer in March, I will accept it the same week."

"If the tuition were free, I would go tomorrow."

"If I miss the deadline, there will not be a second chance this year."

"If I had another year, I would apply to six places instead of two."

Look only at the red verbs, the ones right after if. Two are present and two are past — and none of the four sentences is about the past at all.

JobFormExample
A real, likely futureIf + present simple, + will / will not + baseIf I get the offer, I will accept it.
An imagined or unlikely situationIf + past simple, + would / would not + baseIf I had another year, I would apply to six places.
The verb to be, imaginedIf + were (all persons, in careful English)If the tuition were free, I would go tomorrow.
Either orderIf..., result · result if... (no comma when if comes second)I would apply to six places if I had another year.
AskingWhat will you do if...? · What would you do if...?What would you do if the answer were no?
Advice, imaginedIf I were you, I would + baseIf I were you, I would apply to both.
The one rule that fixes everythingnever will or would immediately after ifNot if I would have the money. Say if I had the money.

In one line: present after if means it might really happen; past after if means you are only imagining it.

Real Document

A Conditional Offer, Line by Line

CONDITIONAL OFFER OF ADMISSION
undergraduate · September intake
ApplicantVictor Garcia Ferreira de Oliveira
Statusconditional — not final
Conditional onlanguage certificate, by 1 March
Tuitionfull fee, unless a scholarship is awarded
Scholarship decisionseparate letter · April
Depositdue 15 May · non-refundable
If conditions are not metthe offer expires automatically
Note to applicants: a conditional offer is a real offer. Every year a number of students treat it as a maybe, miss the March date, and lose a place that was already theirs. If you cannot meet a condition, write to us before the date rather than after it.
1. Three dates on this letter do three different jobs. What happens on each of them?
1 March is when the certificate has to arrive or the offer expires. April is when the scholarship decision arrives, in a separate letter. 15 May is when the deposit is due, and it cannot be refunded.
2. The letter says tuition is the full fee "unless a scholarship is awarded". Why does that sentence matter to somebody deciding?
Because the deposit is due in May and the scholarship letter comes in April, so the money decision has to be made after one answer and before the other. Planning for the full fee is the only safe assumption.
3. According to the note, what mistake do students make every year, and what does the university tell them to do instead?
They treat a conditional offer as a maybe, miss the March date and lose a place that was already theirs. The university tells them to write before the date rather than after it if they cannot meet a condition.
Listening 1 · The Counsellor

How Many Places Should You Apply To?

A university counsellor at an information evening. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say about families who apply to only one or two places?
She says they are betting the whole year on a small number, and that if one answer is no in April there is nothing left to do until the following September.
2. What are the three kinds of application she says a list should contain?
One or two long shots, three or four where the applicant matches what the university asks for, and at least one that is almost certain and affordable.
3. What does she say is the mistake people regret most, and what is her advice about it?
The application they did not send, usually the long shot they decided not to try. She says if you would regret not sending it, send it, because the cost of applying is an evening and the cost of not applying is a year.
Listening 2 · The One Who Said No

Why He Turned It Down

A student defending a decision that everybody questioned. Sound first — no text.

1. What offer did he turn down, and what did people assume about his reason?
A place with a partial scholarship at a well-known university. People assumed he was scared, or that his family had said no.
2. What was his actual reason, and how does he explain the numbers?
The scholarship covered tuition but not the city. He says that if he had taken it, he would have been working most of the week and studying with what was left, which is not the year he wanted.
3. What does he say he would do differently, and what does he say he would not change?
He would apply to more places, because two was not a list. He would not change the decision itself, and he says he would make the same one again with the same information.
