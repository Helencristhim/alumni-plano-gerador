Vocabulary

To present with -- to come to the doctor with a particular problem
"A 58-year-old man presents with a lesion on his nose."
Nonhealing -- not getting better or closing over time
"A nonhealing ulcer always needs a closer look."
Unremarkable -- normal, with nothing important to report
"His past medical history is unremarkable."
A finding -- something that an exam or a test shows
"The most important finding is the rolled border."
A pertinent negative -- a symptom the patient does not have, but that matters
"No fever is a pertinent negative in this case."
A differential -- a list of the possible diagnoses
"Basal cell carcinoma is first on my differential."
To narrow down -- to reduce a list to fewer options
"Three good questions narrow down the list."
A workup -- the tests and exams done to reach a diagnosis
"The workup includes a biopsy and blood tests."
To worsen -- to become worse
"The itching has worsened since he started the new soap."
To resolve -- to go away completely, for a symptom or a lesion
"The rash resolved after two weeks of treatment."
The next best step -- EXPRESSION — what the doctor should do next, in exam language
"The next best step is a shave biopsy."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A Step 3 vignette is a timeline, and the present perfect carries it. When something started in the past and is still true, the vignette uses it: a 58-year-old man has had a lesion on his nose for six months. When it counts how many times, or gives a result, it uses the simple form: the lesion has bled twice, two biopsies have been unremarkable.

When the vignette wants you to see an activity that is still going on, it uses the continuous: he has been applying an ointment for three weeks, the plaque has been growing since April. Verbs of state — have, be, know — never go into the continuous, so a patient has had a rash, never has been having it.

Portuguese uses the present tense here, and that is why Brazilian doctors often say he has this lesion since six months. In English the tense is the present perfect, for takes a period (for six months) and since takes a point in time (since April). The question is always How long have you had this rash?

1. "The lesion ___ twice this week." Which form completes the sentence?
A has been bleeding, because the patient is still worried about the lesion
B has bled
C is bleeding, because it is happening this week
2. Why does a vignette say "has been applying an ointment"?
A Because the patient is still applying it: it is an activity that is still going on.
B Because the ointment is finished and the vignette only wants the result.
C Because apply is a state verb.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A He has this rash since six months, and it is getting worse every week.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect Simple and Continuous

GRAMMAR

The continuous shows an activity that is still going on. The simple counts, gives a result, or describes a state.

JobFormExample
State still truehas / have + past participleHe has had the lesion for six months.
How many times / resulthas / have + past participleIt has bled twice. We have done two biopsies.
Activity still going onhas / have + been + -ingShe has been using a cream since March.
Negativehas not / have not + past participleThe lesion has not resolved.
QuestionHow long have you + past participle?How long have you had this rash?
for / sincefor + a period / since + a pointfor six months · since April

Never: he has this lesion since six months · she is using the cream since March · it has been bleeding twice · how long do you have this rash? · he has been having a rash

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You ask a patient about the timeline of a rash. You say:
A How long have you had this rash?
B How long do you have this rash, and did it start on your arms or on your legs first?
C Since when you have the rash?
The attending asks for the short version of a case. You start with:
A He is a man of fifty-eight years who comes with a wound that is not closing since six months.
B Man, nose, wound.

A He is applying an ointment since three weeks.
B He has been applying an ointment for three weeks.

The last line of the vignette asks for the next best step. You say:
A I would prescribe a stronger steroid cream for two more weeks and wait to see if the lesion resolves.
