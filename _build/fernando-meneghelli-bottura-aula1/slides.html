Chapter 3: The Code

Finished and Dated, or Still True Today?

Two options. That is the whole decision.

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I worked at Publicis for nine years."

"I have worked in agency finance for over twenty years."

"We closed the shared services project in 2021."

"I have never reported to a board that spoke only Portuguese."

Two of them describe a closed chapter. Two describe something still true today. Which is which?

FormUseExample
Past simple
I worked / we closed		A closed chapter with a date, a place or a company that is overI joined Publicis in 2017.
We closed the project in 2021.
Present perfect
I have worked		A chapter that is still open, or experience with no date attachedI have worked in agency finance for over twenty years.
I have led three consolidations.
The time markersin 2017, last year, two years ago, when I was at WPP point to the past simple. for, since, ever, never, already, so far point to the present perfect.
for vs sincefor plus a length of time: for nine years. since plus a starting point: since 2017.
Why it matters in an interviewThe present perfect is what turns a list of jobs into a track record. "I have managed the P and L of nine agencies" is a claim about you today. "I managed it in 2019" is a story about the past.

One question, two answers. Is the chapter closed, or is it still open? When you are not sure, choose the past simple with a date and keep going — a finished answer beats a perfect tense you never say.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I work in agency finance since 2005."
"I have worked in agency finance since 2005."
"I have joined Publicis in 2017."
"I joined Publicis in 2017."
"I was at the helm of finance since nine years."
"I was at the helm of finance for nine years."

A closed date takes the past simple. An open stretch of time takes the present perfect. And since needs a starting point, while for needs a length.

Chapter 4: The Screening Call

Five Minutes on the Phone

An executive search consultant, a CFO role, and no warning

Dialogue

The Call from the Search Firm

C
Fernando, thanks for taking the call. I am Claire Doyle, from the search firm. Before we go into detail, could you walk me through your career?
F
Of course. I have spent most of my career in advertising groups, and I have been at the helm of finance for fourteen years.
C
And the most recent one? I see a group here, nine years, which is a long tenure in this market.
F
I joined that group in 2017 and I left at the end of last year. I spearheaded the consolidation of the back office there, across nine agencies.
C
Good. Now, our client is Belgian, and the reporting line runs to Brussels. How comfortable are you working in English day to day?
F
Let me make sure I answer that properly. For five years I reported directly to a CFO in New York, in English, every single day. So the answer is yes, and I can give you an example.
C
Please do. And then tell me what you bring to the table that a local candidate would not.
F
Twenty years inside agency economics. I know where the money hides in this industry, and I have already laid that groundwork once, in a group of a similar size.
Comprehension

About the Call

1. Who is Claire Doyle, and what does she ask for first?
She works for the search firm, and she asks him to walk her through his career.
2. Where is the client based, and where does the reporting line run?
The client is Belgian, and the reporting line runs to Brussels.
3. What is the last thing Claire asks him to explain?
What he brings to the table that a local candidate would not.
Listening

A Voicemail from Claire

She is confirming the next step. Sound first — no text.

1. Who will be on the call, and what is each of them responsible for?
The Group CFO in Brussels, who owns the numbers, and the HR director, who runs the process.
2. What does Claire ask him to prepare?
Two examples of a turnaround he has led, with figures.
3. What is the detail she mentions at the very end?
The whole conversation will be in English, and the Group CFO tends to interrupt with follow-up questions.
