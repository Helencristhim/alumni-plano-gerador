Chapter 3: The Code

What Happened, and What Still Stands

Two pasts, and English keeps them apart

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"The company used the song in March."

"My client has never been paid for it."

"She wrote to them on the fourteenth of July."

"They have not answered her yet."

Two of the red verbs are one word, and two of them carry have. Look at the blue part: it appears on one side only, and that is the whole clue.

JobFormExample
A finished moment, and you name itpast simpleThey used the song in March.
The story, step by steppast simple, one event after anotherShe wrote, they ignored it, she called a lawyer.
The situation as it stands nowhave / has + past participleThey have not answered her.
A period that is still openpresent perfect, with this year / so farWe have met twice this month.
Experience, with no dateever / never + present perfectHave you ever mediated a case like this?
Since and forpresent perfect — never the present tenseI have worked on this case since January.
The trapa named finished time kills the present perfectNot we have signed it yesterday. Say we signed it yesterday.

In one line: if you say when, the verb is one word. If the moment does not matter, the verb carries have.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Session, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one case sheet

Dialogue

The First Five Minutes of a Mediation

M
Good morning. My name is Marcus Reed and I am the mediator today. Before we start: I am not a judge. I will not decide who is right.
A
Good morning. I am Ana Tavares and I represent the songwriter, Helena Braga. We came here in good faith.
M
Thank you. Take three minutes and tell me what happened. Not what you want yet. What happened.
A
In March the company used one of her songs in a short film. Nobody asked her. She found the film in July, she wrote to them twice, and they have not answered her.
M
And what is her claim? Give me the number and the thing that is not a number.
A
A fee for the use, and her name on screen. She has never asked for the film to come down.
M
Good. Then we already have common ground, and that is more than most rooms start with. If we get to a deadlock on the number, I will stop us and we will come back to it.
A
That works for us. Before the other side speaks, may I ask you one thing? How long have you been mediating cases like this one?
Comprehension

About Marcus

1. How does Marcus introduce himself, and what does he say he is not?
He gives his name and says he is the mediator. He says he is not a judge and that he will not decide who is right.
2. What exactly does Marcus ask for in his second turn, and what does he tell them to leave out for now?
He asks for three minutes on what happened. He tells them to leave out what they want for the moment.
3. What does Marcus say he will do if the two sides stop moving on the number?
He says he will stop them and they will come back to it later.
Listening 1 · The Mediator

What I Do and What I Do Not Do

A mediator opening a session, explaining his own role. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say the room usually expects him to do, and what does he actually do?
People expect him to decide who is right, like a judge. He says he only helps the two sides build an answer they can both sign.
2. What is the one rule he gives about interrupting, and what reason does he give for it?
Nobody interrupts the other side. He says the first version of the story is the only one that comes out whole, and once it is broken people start defending instead of explaining.
3. What does he say about the sides who arrive believing the case is simple?
They are the ones who take longest, because they have never had to say the story out loud to somebody who was not already on their side.
Listening 2 · The Student

My First Mock Mediation

A law student talking about the first simulation she did in English. Sound first — no text.

1. What did she prepare before the simulation, and what happened to it?
She wrote her opening statement out word for word and learned it. After the first question she could not use any of it, because the question was not the one she had prepared for.
2. What does she say she kept doing in the middle of her own sentences, and what did it cost her?
She kept rewriting the sentence in her head to avoid a verb she was not sure about. She says it cost her the point she was making, because by the time the sentence came out it said much less than she meant.
3. What advice did the person running the session give her afterwards?
To say the sentence wrong and keep going, because a wrong verb never lost anybody an argument, and a sentence that stops in the middle always does.
Listening 3 · Radio Report

The Cases That Never Reach a Judge

A radio report on why almost every dispute is settled before trial. Sound first — no text.

1. What figure does the report give about cases that go to trial, and what does the reporter say people get wrong about it?
Fewer than five in a hundred reach a trial. People read that as a failure of the courts, when it is closer to the opposite: the system is built so that most cases end before that point.
2. According to the report, what changed in the last thirty years?
Mediation moved from something a judge suggested at the end to something the parties do at the start, and in many places it is now a step you have to take before a court will hear you.
3. What does the mediator quoted at the end say the job actually is?
She says the job is not to find the truth. It is to find the smallest thing both sides can agree on and build from there.
Real Document

The Sheet on the Table Before Anybody Speaks

MEDIATION — CASE SUMMARY
confidential · for the parties and the mediator only
MediatorMarcus Reed
First partyHelena Braga, songwriter
Represented byAna Ladeia Tavares
Second partyNine Doors Films
Timelinefilm released 2 March · song identified 9 July
The claima fee for the use, and a credit on screen
Statusno reply from the second party since July
Both parties attend voluntarily and in good faith. Neither party has asked for the film to be taken down. Anything said in this room stays in this room, and cannot be used later if the case goes to court.
1. Two lines on this sheet talk about time, and they do it in opposite ways. Which are they, and what is the difference?
"Timeline: film released 2 March, song identified 9 July" gives finished dates. "Status: no reply since July" describes something that is still true this morning.
2. The sheet says neither party has asked for the film to be taken down. Why would a mediator want that written at the top?
Because it is common ground. It tells both sides, before anybody speaks, that the film itself is not what they are arguing about.
3. According to the last paragraph, what happens to what is said in the room if the case later goes to court?
It cannot be used. Whatever is said in the mediation stays in that room.
