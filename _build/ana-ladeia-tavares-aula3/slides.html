Chapter 3: The Code

One Half Then, One Half Now

The old decision, and this morning

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"If she had read clause four, she would not be tied to it today."

"If they had offered two years in 2020, we would not be in this room."

"If the label were bigger, they would have settled this years ago."

"If she trusted them less, she would have asked a lawyer at nineteen."

In the first two, the blue half is in the past and the red half is this morning. In the last two, it is the other way round. Last week both halves lived in the same time. These four do not.

JobFormExample
Past cause, present resultif + had + participle → would + base verbIf she had read it, she would not be tied to it today.
Present cause, past resultif + past simple / were → would have + participleIf the label were bigger, they would have settled it.
The words that give it awaytoday, now, still, at this moment, any more...she would not be in this room today.
Were, not wasin careful English, were for every person in the if halfIf I were her lawyer, I would have said no.
Possible, not certaincould / might in either halfShe might not be on this label at all.
Questionwould + subject + base verb / would + subject + have + participleWould we be here at all if they had said two years?
The trapdo not send both halves to the past when one of them is nowNot she would not have been tied to it today. Say she would not be tied to it today.

In one line: each half sits in its own time. Ask when the cause happened, then ask when the result is felt.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Room, at Full Speed

One private conversation, three recordings, one position sheet

Dialogue

Two Lawyers in the Corridor

D
Before we go back in. Off the record, what does your client actually want out of this afternoon?
A
Out of the deal. If somebody had explained the renewal to her in 2020, she would not be having this conversation at twenty-five.
D
Nobody hid it. It was clause four and it was binding the day she signed it. And you know I cannot let her walk, because it sets a precedent for eleven other artists.
A
Then let us not talk about walking. A two-year term with the same royalty is on the table, and it costs you no precedent at all.
D
That is more interesting. And what would you give me for it? Because if I go back in there with nothing, I am worse off than when I came out.
A
The back payments. She drops them entirely. That is a real concession, and if the label were bigger you would have offered this yourself two years ago.
D
You are not wrong. Give me ten minutes with my client, and please do not put that last sentence to her in the room.
A
I will not. And Daniel, before you go back in — how many of those eleven artists have asked you the same question this year?
Comprehension

About Daniel

1. What reason does Daniel give for refusing to let the artist out of the deal?
Not the money. He says it would set a precedent for eleven other artists on the label.
2. What does Daniel say he needs before he goes back into the room, and how does he explain it?
He needs something to take back. He says that if he walks in with nothing, he is worse off than when he came out.
3. What does Daniel ask her not to do, and what does that tell you about the sentence she used?
He asks her not to say the last sentence in front of his client. It tells you the sentence was true and uncomfortable: it named a weakness of the label without accusing anybody.
Listening 1 · The Music Lawyer

Nobody Argues About the Term

A music lawyer on the one number in a deal that everybody ignores. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say young artists argue about, and what does he say they should be arguing about instead?
They argue about the percentage. He says the term is the number that decides everything, because a bad percentage for two years costs almost nothing and a fair percentage for ten years can cost a career.
2. What does he say about automatic renewal clauses, and why do they survive?
They are legal and normal and almost nobody reads them. They survive because the person signing is usually twenty and is being told, honestly, that everybody signs the same thing.
3. What does he say about the moment when leverage changes hands?
That it happens on one specific day, and neither side notices at the time. The artist starts earning more than she costs, and from that morning on the label needs her more than she needs the label.
Listening 2 · The Artist

I Was Nineteen and Everybody Was Nice

A songwriter on the contract she signed at nineteen and still lives with. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say about the room where she signed, and what does she say she did not do?
Everybody was kind and the room was pleasant, and she did not ask a single question, because asking would have looked like she did not trust them.
2. What does she say she understood at nineteen, and what does she say she did not understand?
She understood the percentage perfectly. She did not understand that the three years would renew themselves unless she wrote a letter, and nobody said that part out loud.
3. What does she say she would tell somebody who is nineteen now?
Not to be braver, because she says bravery is not the problem. To take the contract home, and to ask one boring question: what has to happen for this to end.
Listening 3 · Radio Report

The Day the Leverage Changed Sides

A radio report on how bargaining power moves over the life of a contract. Sound first — no text.

1. What does the report say most people get wrong about leverage?
That they treat it as a property of a person, when it is a property of a moment. The same two people can swap positions without either of them changing at all.
2. According to the report, what has changed for artists in the last fifteen years, and what has not?
Distribution has changed: an artist can now reach an audience without a label. What has not changed is the length of the contracts, which were written for a world where the label owned the only road to the listener.
3. What does the negotiation researcher quoted at the end say about long contracts?
That a contract long enough to outlive the reason it was signed will end up in a room like this one, and that the only question is whether the two sides get there while there is still goodwill left.

1. What is the one thing on this sheet that is not open to negotiation, and what is offered in exchange for it?
A fixed end date with no renewal. In exchange, the back payments are dropped entirely.
2. The last line says their stake is the precedent, not the money. How should that change the way the offer is worded?
It has to look like a normal renegotiation rather than a release, because a release is the thing that would bind them with the other eleven artists.

Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Three Minutes, No Notes

Scenario: Open the renegotiation, from nothing. Say where the deal came from, what it is costing your client this morning, what you are holding out for, what you will give in exchange, and why today is a better day for the other side than next year. Do not accuse anybody of anything.

No keywords, no notes, three minutes. If a sentence comes out wrong, finish it anyway.
