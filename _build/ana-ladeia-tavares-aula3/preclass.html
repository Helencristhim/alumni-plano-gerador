Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

When Past and Present Collide -- The Old Deal That Is Still Costing

She signed it at nineteen, for three years, and nobody explained what automatic renewal meant. Six years later she is still in it. Key words: leverage, a concession, to backtrack, a precedent, to undermine, the stakes, a fallback, to hold out for, binding, goodwill, to be worse off, on the table. Structure: mixed conditionals.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Leverage -- the advantage that makes the other side more likely to say yes
"Their leverage is time. Every month she stays in the deal, they lose nothing."
A concession -- something one side gives up so that the talk can move forward
"Dropping the back payments was a concession, and it bought us the term."
To backtrack -- to go back on something you have already said or agreed to
"They backtracked on the term twenty minutes after they had offered it."
A precedent -- an earlier decision that other people are now expected to follow
"They will not release her early, because it would set a precedent for everyone else on the label."
To undermine -- to weaken something slowly, often without saying so and sometimes without meaning to
"Every month they delay undermines the argument that the deal is working for her."
The stakes -- what each side stands to win or lose if the talk fails
"The stakes are not equal here, and everybody in the room knows it."
A fallback -- the option you keep for the moment the main plan fails
"Our fallback is a shorter term with the same royalty. We do not open with it."
To hold out for -- to refuse to accept anything less than one particular thing, even when it costs you time
"She is holding out for the rights to the first album, and nothing else matters to her."
Binding -- legally forcing you to do what you agreed, whether you still want to or not
"The renewal was automatic and binding, and that is the whole case."
Goodwill -- the willingness of the other side to be helpful, built up over time and easy to lose
"We have six years of goodwill with this label and one afternoon to spend it."
To be worse off -- in a worse position than before, usually after something you agreed to
"If she signs this version, she is worse off in three years than she is today."
On the table -- available to be discussed or accepted, right now, in this room
"A two-year term is on the table. The back payments are not."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A songwriter signed an exclusive distribution deal at nineteen. Three years, renewing automatically unless she wrote to cancel it. Clause four was binding from the day she signed, and nobody read it out loud to her. Six years later she is still in the deal, and two lawyers are standing in a corridor deciding what to take back into the room. If somebody had explained clause four to her in 2020, she would not be having this conversation at twenty-five.

Look at that sentence again. The half with if is in the past, exactly as it was last week. The other half is not: it is this morning, and it takes would plus the plain verb, not would have. That is what a mixed conditional does — each half sits in its own time. The same shape works in the other direction: if the label were bigger, they would have settled this years ago. Here the cause is now (the label is small today) and the result is in the past.

This is not a decoration. It is the structure that lets you say what the other side got wrong without accusing anybody, and that is why it opens a renegotiation. The lawyer offers a real concession — the back payments, dropped entirely — and puts a two-year term on the table, because a release would set a precedent for eleven other artists and would be refused. One warning: do not send both halves to the past out of habit. Not she would not have been tied to it today, but she would not be tied to it today.

1. "If somebody had explained clause four to her, she would not be here today." Why "would not be" and not "would not have been"?
A Because "be" is an irregular verb and has no participle form.
B Because the result is felt now, this morning, even though the cause is in the past.
C Because the sentence is negative.
2. "If the label were bigger, they would have settled this years ago." Which half is in the present?
A The half with "if": the label is small now, and that is the cause.
B The half with "would have settled": the settlement is happening now.
C Neither. Both halves are in the past.
3. According to the text, why does this structure open a renegotiation instead of a fight?
A Because it is more formal, and formal language always calms people down.
B Because it hides what really happened.
C Because it lets you name what the other side got wrong without accusing anybody of it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Mixed Conditionals

GRAMMAR

Each half sits in its own time. Ask when the cause happened, then ask when the result is felt.

JobFormExample
Past cause, present resultif + had + participle → would + base verbIf she had read it, she would not be tied to it today.
Present cause, past resultif + past simple / were → would have + participleIf the label were bigger, they would have settled it.
The words that give it awaytoday, now, still, at this moment, any more...she would not be in this room today.
Were, not wasin careful English, were for every person in the if halfIf I were her lawyer, I would have said no.
Possible, not certaincould or might in either halfShe might not be on this label at all.
Questionwould + subject + base verb, or would + subject + have + participleWould we be here at all if they had said two years?
The trapdo not send both halves to the past when one of them is nowNot she would not have been tied to it today. Say she would not be tied to it today.

Never: if she had read it, she would not have been tied to it today · if the label would be bigger · if they had offered two years, we are not here now · if I were her lawyer, I would ask for it in 2020.

Reflection
