Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Dry -- not sweet at all, which is what most table wine is
"I usually go for something dry, but not too heavy."
Bold -- a flavour that is strong and fills the mouth, rather than light and quiet
"That is a bold red. It would be too much with fish."
Sparkling -- with bubbles in it, the kind of thing somebody opens at the start of an evening
"They opened something sparkling before dinner, and everybody relaxed."
A vintage -- the year the grapes were picked, which is printed on the bottle
"It is a good vintage. That year the weather did everything right."
A grape variety -- the type of grape a wine is made from, such as Malbec or Chardonnay
"I have never tried this grape variety before. What is it like?"
A pairing -- putting a particular wine with a particular dish so that both taste better
"The classic pairing is red meat and a bold red, but I have had better surprises."
The finish -- the taste that stays in your mouth after you swallow
"Nice long finish. That is the part I always notice first."
Tasting notes -- the short description on the back of the bottle, or what somebody says about a wine
"I never read the tasting notes first. I would rather guess and be wrong."
To go well with -- to be a good combination with another food or drink
"It goes surprisingly well with cheese, and nobody expects that."
To break the ice -- to say the first thing that makes two strangers comfortable together
"The glass in your hand is there to break the ice. That is its whole job."
Not really my thing -- a polite and honest way of saying you do not like something
"Sweet wine is not really my thing, but I have never said no to a good one."
To be into -- to be genuinely interested in something and follow it closely
"I am really into wine. Not the labels, more the history and the regions."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody sits down next to you at a company dinner. They have already opened the red and the food has not arrived yet. The safest first move is not a fact about grapes. It is a question: have you ever tried a wine from this region? Notice what is not in that sentence. It does not say when, and it does not have to, because the question is about experience at any point in a life. That is the whole job of this tense.

The answers work the same way. I have never been to Portugal, so I would not know. I have just tried it, and it is drier than I expected. Quite bold, actually, and it would go well with the beef. Four small words do most of the work here. Ever asks about any time in a life. Never is already negative, so it never takes a second not. Just means a very short time ago. Already means sooner than expected, and yet lives in negatives and questions, at the end of the sentence.

Then comes the moment where English changes lane. As soon as you say WHEN, you leave this tense and take the past simple. We tried that pairing at a dinner last December. I read the tasting notes after we finished the bottle. That is the only real trap, and it catches almost everybody. The rest is easier than it looks: say whether you like it, say what it reminds you of, and hand the question back. Say that very sweet wine is not really my thing, or that you are genuinely into the history and the regions, and you have broken the ice without pretending to know anything. That is what a good table sounds like. Not one that says I have tried it last year, but one that says I tried it last year, and then asks a question.

1. Why is "Have you ever tried a wine from this region?" better than "Did you ever try a wine from this region?" at a dinner?
A Because the question is about experience at any point in a life, and that is what this tense is for.
B Because "did" is impolite in English.
C Because "try" cannot be used in the past simple.
2. What is wrong with "I have not never been to Portugal"?
A "Portugal" needs an article.
B "Never" is already negative, so the sentence does not take a second "not".
C "Been" should be "gone".
3. According to the text, what makes English change lane into the past simple?
A The sentence becoming a question.
B The subject changing from I to we.
C Saying WHEN it happened, for example "last December".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Ever, Never, Just, Already and Yet

GRAMMAR

This tense is about experience, not about dates.

WordWhat it doesExample
everat any time in your life, in a questionHave you ever tried a wine from this region?
nevernot once in your whole life — already negative, so no second "not"I have never been to Portugal.
justa very short time agoI have just tried it.
alreadysooner than expected, in a positive sentenceThey have already opened the red.
yetnot until now — negatives and questions, and it goes at the ENDThe food has not arrived yet. Have you ordered yet?
Where they sitever, never, just and already go between "have" and the verbI have just tried it · I have never been there
The trapthe moment you say WHEN, you leave this tenseNot I have tried it last year. Say I tried it last year.

Never: did you ever try this? · I have not never been there · I have tried it last December · the food has yet not arrived · I have already tried it yesterday.

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

Somebody has just sat down next to you at a company dinner. Put your side of the first two minutes in a logical order.

?"Luiz. Good to meet you."
?"Have you ever been to this part of the country before?"
?"I have just tried the red, and it is drier than I expected."
?"Quite bold, actually. It would go well with the beef."
?"I am really into wine, but more the history and the regions than the labels."
?"And you, what are you into when you are not working?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A guest you have just met is holding a glass. You want to open the conversation. You say:
A "This is a Cabernet Sauvignon from a limestone soil at four hundred metres."
B "Have you ever tried a wine from this region before?"
C "Did you ever drink the wine of here?"
Somebody asks what you think of the wine and you have taken one sip. You say:
A "I have just tried it. It is drier than I expected, and quite bold."
B "I have tried it one minute ago and I found it dry."
C "I am trying it just now, it is dry I think."
You are offered a very sweet dessert wine and you do not want it. You say:
A "No. I do not like this."
B "Very sweet wine is not really my thing, so I will stay with the red. Thank you."
C "I am sorry, the sweet wine is bad for me."
You want to say you went to a tasting in December. You say:
A "I have been to a tasting last December."
B "I went to a tasting last December."
C "I have already been to a tasting in the last December."
