Since, For and Ago -- Your Background in Three Sentences

How to say how long you have been doing what you do, and why the tense answers the question before the numbers arrive. Key expressions: a career, a background, to join, to be promoted, a degree, ever since, to specialise in, to keep in touch, a turning point, to take on. Structure: present perfect versus past simple with for, since and ago.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These ten stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

A career -- the series of jobs you do through your working life
"My whole career has been in the same industry."
A background -- your education and your work history
"Tell me a little about your background."
To join -- to start working for a company
"I joined the bank eleven years ago."
To be promoted -- to be given a more important job in the same company
"She was promoted twice in four years."
A degree -- the qualification you get when you finish university
"I finished my degree in the nineties and I have studied ever since."
Ever since -- from that moment until now, without stopping
"I have worked with investments ever since."
To specialise in -- to work mainly in one particular area
"I specialise in clients who invest outside the country."
To keep in touch -- to continue speaking to somebody from time to time
"We have kept in touch since he left the bank."
A turning point -- the moment when something important changed
"That meeting was the turning point of my career."
To take on -- to accept a new responsibility or a new client
"I have taken on about forty families in eleven years."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Janderson joined the bank eleven years ago and he has worked with investments ever since. He finished his degree in the nineties, went straight into the financial sector, and has never left it. His whole career has been in one industry, which is unusual and which he only noticed when somebody asked him about his background in a meeting.

He specialises in clients who hold money outside the country. He has taken on about forty families in eleven years, and the number is small on purpose: forty families that he knows well are worth more than four hundred he does not. He was promoted twice along the way and both times he asked to keep the same clients.

The turning point was not a promotion. It was a meeting, about four years ago, where a foreign client asked him a simple question in English and he could not answer it. Somebody else answered for him. He has thought about that afternoon more often than he admits, and it is the reason he is sitting here twice a week now.

He has kept in touch with two colleagues who left the bank, and both of them work abroad now. They write to him in English. He reads the messages easily, he has read them easily for years, and until recently he answered them in Portuguese. That has changed.

1. "He joined the bank eleven years ago." Why the past simple?
A Because ago counts back from today and always takes the past simple.
B Because joining is a short action.
C Because the bank no longer exists.
2. "He has worked with investments ever since." What does this tell you?
A That he stopped and started again.
B That it began then and it is still true today.
C That it lasted only a short time.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A I work here since 2015.
B I have worked here since 2015.
C I have worked here since eleven years.
4. According to the text, what was the turning point?
A His second promotion.
B A meeting where he could not answer a client's question in English.
C Finishing his degree in the nineties.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- For, Since and Ago

GRAMMAR

Three small words that split the same eleven years into two different tenses. The rule is mechanical, and once it is automatic you never think about it again.

FormWhat it saysExample
for + a periodHow long it has lasted. Present perfect."I have worked here for eleven years."
since + a starting pointWhen it began: a year, a date, an event. Present perfect."since twenty fifteen"
"since my daughter was born"
ago + past simpleHow far back the start was, counted from today."I joined eleven years ago."
NOT "I have joined eleven years ago"
How long have you...?The question for the whole period. It always takes this tense."How long have you worked there?"
Still true or finishedThe tense answers before the numbers do."I have worked there for two years." (still there)
"I worked there for two years." (left)
The Portuguese trapPortuguese uses the present for something that continues. English does not.NOT "I work here since 2015"
"I have worked here since 2015."

In a first meeting with a client this distinction is not decoration. I have worked with this for eleven years and I worked with this for two years are two completely different offers, and the only thing that separates them is the tense.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are in a first meeting with a client who is deciding whether to work with you. Choose the best thing to say.

You started at the bank in 2015 and you are still there:
A I work here since 2015.
B I have worked here since 2015.
C I am working here since 2015.
You want to give the length of time, not the starting year:
A I have worked here since eleven years.
B I have worked here for eleven years.
C I have worked here eleven years ago.
You want to count back from today:
A I have joined the bank eleven years ago.
B I joined the bank eleven years ago.
C I join the bank eleven years ago.
You want to ask about her whole period in the business:
A Since when are you in this business?
B How long have you been in this business?
C How much time you are in this business?
You want to say what kind of client you work with:
A My professional focus is directed towards a specific segment of clients.
B I specialise in clients who invest outside the country.
C I am specialist of clients that invest outside.
