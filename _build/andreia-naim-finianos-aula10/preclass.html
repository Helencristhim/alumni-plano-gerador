Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

Connecting the Dots -- Describing the Client You Actually Want

A company that cannot describe its best client in one sentence cannot find the next one. Key words: a segment, a use case, a reference, to tailor, a pain threshold, a champion, churn, a footprint, a case study, to narrow down, to move the needle, right in the sweet spot. Structure: non-defining relative clauses and the relative adverbs where and whose.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A segment -- a group of clients similar enough to be sold to in the same way
"Our best segment is state groups whose filing is monthly."
A use case -- one specific situation in which a client actually uses the product
"The use case we lead with is always the audit."
A reference -- a client who agrees to talk to another client about working with you
"That account, which took nine months to close, is now our strongest reference."
To tailor -- to change something so that it fits one client instead of all of them
"We tailor the deck to one person, and we say who that person is."
A pain threshold -- the point at which a problem stops being tolerable and somebody acts
"Their pain threshold is the audit, not the price."
A champion -- the person inside the client who argues for you when you are not in the room
"The champion is almost never the person who signs."
Churn -- the share of clients who leave over a period
"They send the same deck to everybody, and then explain the churn as a market problem."
A footprint -- how much of a market, or how many countries, a company is present in
"Sao Paulo, where most of our footprint is, is not the whole market."
A case study -- a short written story of one client, used to sell to the next one
"One good case study is worth more than any number of features."
To narrow down -- to reduce a long list to the few that are actually worth the time
"Each clause narrows the list down until what is left is findable."
To move the needle -- to make a difference big enough to show up in the numbers
"Another feature will not move the needle. A named champion will."
Right in the sweet spot -- exactly the client the product was made for, with nothing to adapt
"A group of that size, with an internal auditor, is right in the sweet spot."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ask a commercial team who their best client is and you will usually get an adjective: big, loyal, easy. None of those is a segment, because none of them tells anybody where to go on Monday morning. A segment is a sentence somebody else can act on: the groups whose filing is monthly, the companies that already have an internal auditor, the operations where one person signs off on everything.

Look at what those sentences do. Each one has a clause that narrows the list. Take the clause away and the sentence stops pointing at anybody in particular — which is exactly why it has no commas. Now compare it with this: "Our biggest account, which renewed in March, is right in the sweet spot." Here you already know which account is meant, so the clause is only adding a detail. Delete it and nothing is lost except the detail. That clause takes two commas, and it can never start with that.

The difference is not decoration. A team that describes its clients precisely knows its pain threshold, knows which use case opens the conversation, knows who the champion is — the person one level below the signature, whose team lives with the problem every day. A team that cannot describe them sends the same deck to everybody, calls it tailoring, and explains its churn as a market problem. One good case study about a client in your own segment will move the needle further than another feature ever will — and you cannot write that case study until you can write the sentence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Non-Defining Relative Clauses, where and whose

GRAMMAR

No commas narrows the list. Two commas only add a detail. If you can delete the clause and still know who you meant, it takes commas.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Describe your own client base out loud, as if somebody who has never met your company had to go and find the next client from what you said: which segment you win in and why, which one you keep losing and why, who the champion usually is, what your churn tells you, and the one thing that would move the needle next year. Use at least six relative clauses, and make at least two of them the kind that only adds a detail.
