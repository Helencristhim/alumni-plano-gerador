Chapter 3: The Code

Reporting Without Owning

The grammar every newsroom in the world runs on

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"It is thought that the file was copied twice."

"He is said to have known about it for months."

"The minister is believed to be considering resignation."

"The document is understood to have been withdrawn on Friday."

Four claims, and in not one of them can you say who is making the claim. Where did the speaker go?

FormWhat it doesExample
It is said / thought /
believed / understood
+ that + clause		The impersonal form. The claim is reported and nobody is named as making it. This is the default in a first report."It is thought that the file was copied twice."
Subject + is said /
believed + to + verb		The personal form: the subject of the claim moves to the front, which makes the sentence read as being ABOUT that person."He is said to know the family well."
is said + to have
+ past participle		When the reported event is EARLIER than the report. This is the form most often got wrong, and the meaning changes completely."He is said to have known for months."
is believed + to be
+ -ing		A situation in progress at the moment of reporting. Standard whenever the story is still moving."She is believed to be considering resignation."
Why journalists use itIt reports a claim without asserting it, and without naming who made it. That is legitimate when the source is real and cannot be named — and it is a hiding place when the source is thin."It is widely reported that…" (by whom, on what basis?)

This structure is not dishonest, and journalism could not function without it. But it does one specific thing every time it is used: it moves the claim away from the person saying it. Reading well means asking, every time, who exactly is doing the thinking, saying or believing.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"He is said that he knew about it for months."
"He is said to have known about it for months."
"It is thought the file to have been copied twice."
"It is thought that the file was copied twice."
"The minister is believed considering resignation."
"The minister is believed to be considering resignation."

One structural rule holds all three together. It is said THAT + a full clause, or somebody is said TO + a verb — never the two mixed. And after to you must choose the time: to know (now), to have known (before), to be considering (in progress).

My job is to ask one question about every sentence that reaches me. Not is it true. Who is saying it, and would they say it with their name on.

Because it is both. The existence of a claim is a fact and reporting it is legitimate. What is not legitimate is doing it in language that reads like confirmation.

2. What does she say a screenshot is not?

That they use them every day, and once a week one of them is a hiding place.
Listening 1

The Careful Sentence

A bulletin reporting a claim. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the official said to have done?
Attended a meeting he was not supposed to attend, and arrived late.
2. Who is quoted, and under what condition?
A source close to the department, on condition of anonymity.
3. What does the bulletin say about evidence?
That the claim remains unsubstantiated and has not been confirmed.
Listening 2

What the Editor Would Not Run

A standards editor, in a training session. Sound first — no text.

1. What is her first test for an anonymous source?
That she herself knows who it is, and that a second person can confirm it.
2. Which sentence does she say she would refuse?
"It is widely reported that" — when the only reports are each other.
3. What does she say about the official silence?
That silence lends credence, and that people who refuse to comment rarely understand this.
