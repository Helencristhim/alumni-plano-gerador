It Is Said That — Attribution, Rumour and Responsibility

Three days from a private message to a front page, and who owns the claim at each step. Key expressions: on condition of anonymity, to attribute to, a source close to, off the record, unsubstantiated, to circulate, to gain traction, to debunk, a retraction, to vouch for, a leak, to go on record, speculation, to lend credence to, a denial, widely reported, to stand by, to be economical with the truth. Structure: reported and impersonal passive structures — it is said that, he is said to have, is believed to be, is understood to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

On condition of anonymity -- speaking only if the name is not published
"Two officials described the meeting on condition of anonymity."
To attribute to -- to say that something came from a particular person or cause
"The quote was attributed to a person who denies having said it."
A source close to -- somebody who knows the person or the process but is not named
"A source close to the negotiations described the mood as bleak."
Off the record -- said on the understanding that it will not be published at all
"Everything useful he told me was off the record, which is the problem."
Unsubstantiated -- not supported by any evidence that anybody has produced
"It remains an unsubstantiated claim, repeated in four countries."
To circulate -- to pass from person to person, gathering people who believe it
"The screenshot circulated for two days before anybody checked it."
To gain traction -- to start being taken seriously and to spread further
"The story gained traction the moment a serious outlet mentioned it."
To debunk -- to show publicly that a claim is false
"It was debunked within a week and it is still being shared."
A retraction -- a formal statement withdrawing something already published
"The retraction ran on page nineteen, which tells you everything."
To vouch for -- to guarantee personally that somebody or something is reliable
"I would vouch for the reporter and not for the source."
A leak -- information released deliberately by somebody who should not have released it
"Every leak has an author and a purpose, and both are worth asking about."
To go on record -- to say something publicly, with your name attached to it
"Nobody would go on record, and that in itself was the story."
Speculation -- guessing presented as analysis, with nothing solid behind it
"By Thursday it was pure speculation dressed in the language of reporting."
To lend credence to -- to make a claim seem more believable than it was
"The official silence lent credence to a story that had none."
A denial -- a statement saying the claim is untrue
"The denial was carefully worded, and the wording is the interesting part."
Widely reported -- published in many places — which is not the same as verified
"It is widely reported and it has never been confirmed by anybody."
To stand by -- to continue to support something you published, under pressure
"The editor stands by the story, and she has read the file."
To be economical with the truth -- to mislead by leaving things out rather than by lying
"The statement was accurate, and economical with the truth."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The claim began in a private message on a Sunday evening, and by Monday afternoon a screenshot was circulating. It is said that a named official attended a meeting he was not authorised to attend; he is believed to have arrived after it had begun. No evidence was attached and none has been produced since, which is why the claim remains unsubstantiated eight months later.

On Tuesday a small site published it, and the wording repays study. A source close to the department was quoted, on condition of anonymity, confirming only that the meeting had taken place. The official is understood to have been elsewhere that afternoon, according to a second account nobody has been able to check. Every sentence is technically defensible. Not one of them asserts anything, and together they read like a report of established fact.

On Wednesday a serious newspaper mentioned it, and the mention was the event. The claim gained traction not because anything new appeared but because repetition in a trusted place lends credence to almost anything. Nobody involved would go on record. The denial, when it came, was carefully worded, and the wording was itself reported. By Thursday it was widely reported and, in the strict sense, entirely unconfirmed.

There has been no retraction, because nothing was ever asserted. The editor stands by the piece and will tell you, correctly, that every sentence in it is true. She would not vouch for the source, and she has not been asked to. It has not been debunked either, since there is nothing solid enough to disprove. The whole episode was accurate and economical with the truth, and both of those things are the point.

1. "He is believed to have arrived after it had begun." Why to have arrived rather than to arrive?
A Because the arrival is earlier than the report.
B Because the sentence is a question.
C Because the subject is singular.
2. What is the effect of the sentences described as "technically defensible"?
A They assert nothing, and together they read like established fact.
B They accuse the official directly.
C They are grammatically incorrect.
3. Why does the text say the mention by the serious newspaper "was the event"?
A Because the newspaper produced new evidence.
B Because repetition in a trusted place lends credence, even with nothing new.
C Because the newspaper retracted it.
4. Why has there been no retraction?
A Because the claim was proved true.
B Because nothing was ever asserted, so there is nothing to withdraw.
C Because the newspaper refused to publish one.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Passive Reporting — Saying It Without Saying It

GRAMMAR

These structures report a claim without asserting it and without naming who made it. Journalism could not function without them, which is exactly why it is worth being able to see them working.

FormWhat it doesExample
It is said / thought /
believed / understood
+ that + CLAUSE		The impersonal form: a full clause follows, with its own subject and verb. Nobody is named as the source of the claim."It is thought that the file was copied twice."
Subject + is said /
believed + TO + verb		The personal form. The person moves to the front, and the sentence starts to read as being about them rather than about the claim."He is said to know the family well."
… to HAVE + participleThe reported event is EARLIER than the report. Getting this wrong changes the meaning completely, and it is the commonest slip in the whole structure."He is said to have known for months."
(not: he is said to know for months)
… to BE + -ingA situation in progress at the moment of reporting. Standard while a story is still moving."She is believed to be considering resignation."
… to have been
+ participle		Passive AND earlier at the same time. It sounds heavy and it is completely standard in this register."The document is understood to have been withdrawn."

Never mix the two frames. He is said THAT he knew does not exist: either it is said that he knew, or he is said to have known. And the reading habit worth building: every time you meet one of these, ask who is doing the thinking, saying or believing. Sometimes there is a real answer that cannot be printed. Sometimes there is no answer at all.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are talking about a story that may or may not be true. Choose the sentence that says exactly what you mean.

You want to report, impersonally, that the file was copied:
A "It is thought the file to have been copied twice."
B "It is thought that the file was copied twice."
C "It is thought that the file to be copied twice."
You want to say that his knowing happened BEFORE the report:
A "He is said that he knew for months."
B "He is said to know for months."
C "He is said to have known for months."
You want to describe something in progress right now:
A "She is believed to be considering resignation."
B "She is believed considering resignation."
C "She is believed that she considers resignation."
Somebody says the claim must be true because it is everywhere:
A "Yes, it is widely reported, so it is confirmed."
B "It is widely reported and it has never been verified — those are different things."
C "It has been debunked, so it is unsubstantiated."
You want to say that a statement misled by leaving things out:
A "It was a denial."
B "It was accurate, and economical with the truth."
C "It was attributed to a source close to the department."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Think of one claim about a public figure or an institution that you half believe and could not source. In about ninety seconds, do three things: state it as a plain assertion you would own, then restate it using two of today's reporting structures, and finally say what single piece of evidence would move you from one version to the other. Notice how much more credible the careful version sounds — with exactly the same evidence behind it, which is none.
