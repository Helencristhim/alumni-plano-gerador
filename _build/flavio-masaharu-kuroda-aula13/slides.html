How Sure Is He, and Which Way?

"Harbrook is likely to close before the twentieth."

"The Verdana expansion is expected to slip to October."

"Ingrid is unlikely to sign this quarter."

"New revenue rose by ten per cent in June and has leveled off since."

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Forty Seconds and One Follow-Up

One Monday call, two recordings, one forecast sheet

Dialogue

Monday with Claire

C
Morning, Flavio. Where are we for the quarter?
F
The commit is nine hundred and twenty thousand, against a target of one million. We are likely to fall short by about eighty.
C
Thank you for saying it first. What happened to Verdana?
F
The expansion is expected to slip to October. Their board meets later than we thought, not a problem with the deal.
C
And is there any upside?
F
Ingrid, in Germany. If she signs this month, we exceed the target. But honestly, she is unlikely to sign before October.
C
So what is your worst case?
F
Eight hundred and fifty, if Harbrook moves too. I want to firm up their close date on Wednesday, and I will send you the update the same afternoon.
Comprehension

About Claire

1. How does Claire react to the number below target?
She thanks him for saying it first, and then asks what happened to Verdana. She is not upset about the gap; she wants the reason.
2. What four things does Claire ask about?
Where the quarter is, what happened to Verdana, whether there is any upside, and what the worst case is.
3. Why does Claire ask for the worst case after hearing about the upside?
Because she needs both ends of the range to plan. The upside alone does not tell her how bad the quarter could be.
Listening 1 · The VP

One Number, or Two?

A sales VP on how he runs the weekly forecast. Sound first — no text.

1. What are the two numbers he says the CEO needs?
The commit, the deals you are almost certain to close, and the best case, everything that could close if the right things happen.
2. Why does he say a number that is on track but falling is worrying?
Because the direction matters more than the position: a number that is short but rising is less worrying than one that is on track but falling every week.
3. What are the four things in his forty-second rule?
The number, how sure you are, which way it is moving, and the one deal that is likely to change it.
Listening 2 · The CFO

The Report She Reads Twice

A CFO on which heads of sales she trusts. Sound first — no text.

1. Which heads of sales does she say she can trust?
Not the ones with the biggest numbers, but the ones who tell her what is unlikely before she has to ask.
2. What changes when she discovers a slipped deal on the last day of the month?
It is too late to plan around it, and the problem is no longer the deal but the person who did not tell her.
3. What kind of report does she read twice?
The honest one: the run rate has leveled off, next month looks thin, and here is what we are going to do about it.

Every line has a number and a word of probability, and only one line needs action this week. The commit is below target, and it says so in the second line, not at the bottom. The upside is written down but not counted: a deal that is unlikely this quarter belongs on the sheet, never inside the commit.
1. Which line needs action this week, and what is the action?
Harbrook: the close date is the twentieth and he has to firm it up on Wednesday, because if Harbrook moves, the quarter falls to the worst case.
2. Why is Ingrid on the sheet but not inside the commit?
Because she is unlikely to sign this quarter. The upside is written down so the CFO knows it exists, but it is not counted.
3. Say the Verdana line as a full sentence.
The Verdana expansion is expected to slip to October, because their board meets later than we thought.
