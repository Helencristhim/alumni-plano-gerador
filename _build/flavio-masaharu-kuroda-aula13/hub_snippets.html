The Weekly Forecast -- Reporting the Number

One number is not a forecast: the CEO needs to know how sure you are and which way it is moving. Key words: a forecast, on track, to fall short, to slip, a close date, the upside, a run rate, to level off, to firm up, the worst case. Structure: probability and trend language.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A forecast -- what you really expect to sell in a period, based on the deals you have
"The forecast for the quarter is nine hundred thousand."
On track -- moving at the right speed to reach the target
"We are on track for the quarter, with three weeks to go."
To fall short -- to not reach a target or an amount you expected
"We are likely to fall short by about eighty thousand."
To slip -- to move to a later date than planned
"The Verdana expansion is expected to slip to October."
A close date -- the date you expect a deal to be signed
"The close date for Harbrook is the twentieth."
The upside -- the extra result you could get if things go well
"The upside is Ingrid: if she signs, we exceed the target."
A run rate -- what you will reach in a year if the current speed continues
"At the current run rate, we finish the year at three point six million."
To level off -- to stop rising or falling and stay at the same level
"New leads rose in June and have leveled off since."
To firm up -- to make a date, a number or a plan more certain
"I want to firm up the Harbrook date before Friday."
The worst case -- the result if everything that can go wrong goes wrong
"In the worst case, we close the quarter at eight hundred."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every Monday the CFO asks the same question: where are we for the quarter? One number does not answer it. A good forecast gives the number, says how sure you are, says which way it is moving, and names the deal that could change it. It takes forty seconds.

English has a small scale for how sure you are. If a deal is likely to close, it is probable. If it is expected to slip, the plan or the data now says it will be late. If it is unlikely to sign, you are telling the CFO not to count on it. All three work the same way: be + likely / expected / unlikely + to + base verb. Without the to, the sentence breaks: is likely close is not English. When you use probably, put it after will: we will probably fall short.

The second part is the direction. Numbers rise, fall or level off. The size of the change takes by: revenue rose by ten per cent. The speed takes an adverb after the verb (it rose sharply) or an adjective before a noun (a sharp rise).

The most important sentence is often the uncomfortable one. "We are likely to fall short by eighty thousand" said on Monday is a plan. The same number discovered on the last day of the month is a problem with the person, not with the deal. Say the worst case before anybody asks for it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- How Sure, and Which Way

GRAMMAR

Say the number, say how sure you are, say which way it is moving.

JobFormExample
Almost certainis certain to / will definitely + verbHarbrook is certain to renew.
Probableis likely to / will probably + verbIt is likely to close.
What the plan or data saysis expected to + verbIt is expected to slip.
Improbableis unlikely to + verbShe is unlikely to sign.
Directionrise, fall, level offLeads have leveled off.
Size of the changeverb + by + amountIt rose by ten per cent.
Speedverb + adverbIt rose sharply.
Word orderwill + probably + verbWe will probably fall short.

Never: is likely close · we probably will fall short · it increased ten per cent · it rose sharp
