Lesson 22 -- Pre-class

The Long Arm of the Law

A Brazilian company opens an office in the US, and the anti-bribery rules come with it: gifts, dinners, agents and the gray areas. Key expressions: a bribe, a kickback, a facilitation payment, a foreign official, hospitality, lavish, an intermediary, to turn a blind eye, a quid pro quo, to self-report, extraterritorial, strict liability, to crack down on, a gray area. Structure: inversion for emphasis -- not only, never, rarely, only when, under no circumstances.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to the same world: bribes, gifts, the gray areas, and what the law does about them.

A bribe -- money or a gift offered to make somebody act dishonestly in your favor
"The agent offered the inspector a bribe, and the inspector reported him."
A kickback -- a secret payment returned to the person who helped you win a contract
"The supplier paid a kickback to the manager who chose its proposal."
A facilitation payment -- a small payment to an official to speed up a routine action
"Our policy does not allow a facilitation payment, not even a small one."
A foreign official -- a person who works for the government of another country, or for a company it owns
"An engineer at a state-owned oil company counts as a foreign official."
Hospitality -- meals, travel or entertainment that a company offers to clients or partners
"Reasonable hospitality is normal, but it has to be recorded."
Lavish -- much more expensive or generous than necessary
"A lavish dinner for an official is very hard to explain to an auditor."
An intermediary -- a third party, like an agent or a consultant, who acts between a company and others
"Every intermediary has to pass a background check before we sign."
To turn a blind eye -- to pretend not to notice something that is wrong
"The manager knew about the payments and decided to turn a blind eye."
A quid pro quo -- something given in exchange for something else, often a favor for a favor
"There was no written quid pro quo, but everybody understood the deal."
To self-report -- to tell the authorities about your own wrongdoing before they find it
"The board decided to self-report the problem to the authorities."
Extraterritorial -- applying to people or actions outside the country that made the law
"The extraterritorial reach of the law surprises many foreign companies."
Strict liability -- legal responsibility even when there was no intention or fault
"Under strict liability, the company pays even if no director knew."
To crack down on -- to start dealing with something much more strictly than before
"The authorities promised to crack down on bribery in public contracts."
A gray area -- a situation where the rules are not clear
"Gifts to a client's family are a gray area, so ask before you send one."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Pedro's company is opening an office in the US, and the compliance team has asked him to draft the first version of a gifts and hospitality policy in English. His first sentence is plain: Employees should not offer cash to officials in any situation. It is correct, but it sounds like a suggestion. So he rewrites it: Under no circumstances should employees offer cash to officials. The negative phrase goes to the front, and the auxiliary jumps before the subject, as in a question.

He does the same with the second paragraph. Not only does the policy apply to the US office; it also applies to every agent who works for us. Notice that only the first part is inverted. And when a sentence has no auxiliary, he adds one: We rarely approve a gift above one hundred dollars becomes Rarely do we approve a gift above one hundred dollars.

The trickiest one is only when. Pedro's first try is Only when the check is complete we will sign, which is wrong. The inversion goes in the second clause, after the condition: Only when the check is complete will we sign the contract.

His manager reads the draft and gives him one piece of advice: use inversion where the rule really matters, not in every sentence. A policy that shouts all the time is a policy nobody reads.

1. Why does Pedro rewrite his first sentence?
A Because the first sentence had a grammar mistake that his manager would have to correct before the policy went out.
B Because the first sentence sounded like a suggestion, not a firm rule.
C Because policies in the US cannot use the word should.
2. In Not only does the policy apply to the US office; it also applies to every agent, which part is inverted?
A Only the first part, after not only.
B Only the second part, after the semicolon, where the bigger point is.
C Both parts.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A Rarely we approve a gift above one hundred dollars.
B Rarely do we approve a gift above one hundred dollars.
C Rarely approve we a gift above one hundred dollars, according to the policy.
4. What advice does the manager give Pedro?
A To use inversion in every sentence, so that the policy sounds formal and nobody can say it is a suggestion.
B To avoid inversion in policies, because it is too formal.
C To use inversion only where the rule really matters.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Inversion for Emphasis

GRAMMAR

In formal English, a negative or limiting word at the start of the sentence changes the word order: the auxiliary comes before the subject, as in a question. The meaning does not change. The weight does.

PatternWhen to use itExample
Under no circumstances / On no account + auxiliary + subjectA rule with no exceptions.Under no circumstances should employees accept cash.
Not only + auxiliary + subject ..., (but) ... alsoAdds a second, bigger point. Only the first part is inverted.Not only is it illegal; it is also bad business.
Never / Rarely / Seldom + auxiliary + subjectHow often something happens, with force.Rarely do auditors accept a vague invoice.
Only when / Only if / Only after + clause, + auxiliary + subjectThe inversion goes in the second clause.Only when the check is complete will we sign.
Nor / Neither + auxiliary + subjectAdds another negative point.Brazilian law does not allow it, nor does the UK law.
No auxiliary in the normal sentenceAdd do, does or did, as in a question.We rarely check. / Rarely do we check.

Register check: inversion belongs to policies, contracts, reports and speeches. In a relaxed conversation, the normal order sounds more natural. One inversion in the right place is worth more than five everywhere.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You work in compliance at a Brazilian company that is opening an office in the US. Choose the answer a professional would actually give.

1. A colleague in the US office asks if he can give a client a box of chocolates for the holidays. The client works for a private company:
A Yes, that is reasonable hospitality, as long as no decision is pending and you record it.
B No. Under no circumstances can anybody in the company give any gift to anybody, for any reason, at any time of the year.
C Yes, gifts are never a problem if they cost less than a dinner.
2. You are writing the policy. Which sentence is correct?
A Under no circumstances employees should pay an official.
B Under no circumstances should employees pay an official.
C Under no circumstances should pay employees an official.
3. An agent asks for a small payment to speed up a routine permit. You answer:
A That is a facilitation payment. The FCPA has a narrow exception, but Brazilian law does not, so our policy prohibits it.
B No problem, small payments are always legal.
C That is a kickback, so we have to self-report it to the authorities in Brazil and in the US before the end of the day.
4. You want to add a second, bigger point. Which sentence is correct?
A Not only it is illegal; it also damages trust.
B Not only is illegal it; it also damages trust.
C Not only is it illegal; it also damages trust.
5. Your director says: Enforcement in the US is weaker now. Can we relax the rules? You answer:
A Yes, the authorities will not crack down on anybody for the next few years, so it is safe to relax the rules for now.
B I'd be careful. The law has not changed, conduct from today can be investigated later, and Brazilian law still applies.
C Yes, the FCPA was cancelled in 2025.
