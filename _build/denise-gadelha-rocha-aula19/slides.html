Chapter 1: The Half Second

The Point You Had, and Never Made

Not because you did not know it. Because by the time you opened your mouth, somebody else was already talking.

Where the Turn Is Lost

Three Ways to Lose a High-Stakes Room

Late by half a second

you knew the answer and were still building the first clause when the gap closed

In, then out

you started, somebody came over the top, and you stopped — because stopping felt more senior than fighting

Never came back

the meeting moved on, and half of your answer is still sitting in your notes

None of the three is about English ability. All three are about timing, and timing can be bought with fixed phrases you never have to build.

Chapter 3: The Code

Come In, Hold, Return, Yield

The grammar of the turn, in four moves

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

Can I come in on that? The renewal season closes in April.

Just to build on what Philippe said, June is after the decision, not before it.

If I could just finish the point. Two more sentences and it is yours.

Coming back to what I was saying about the limits, the exposure has grown by a third.

The coloured part of each one carries no information at all. Cut it and the fact survives. So what is it for, and why would anyone say it?

MoveThe phraseWhy it works
Come in"Can I come in on that?" · "Can I come in here?"asks for the turn and takes it in the same breath. The question mark is a formality; nobody answers it
Build"Just to build on what Philippe said," · "Picking up on that,"attaches you to the last speaker, so entering costs him nothing. Almost impossible to refuse
Hold"If I could just finish the point." · "Two more sentences and it is yours."names an END. That is the whole difference from "Let me finish", which is a door closing in someone's face
Return"Coming back to what I was saying about the limits," · "Before we move on, I still owe you the second half of that answer."reclaims a turn you lost five minutes ago without accusing anybody of taking it
Yield"I would like to hear Milan on this." · "Philippe, you were closer to that renewal — go ahead."giving the floor away on purpose is the move of the person running the room, not of the person losing it
Steer"That is worth its own conversation — can I bring us back to capacity?"closes a tangent by promising it a future, so nobody is dismissed
The interference trapYour own reflex, translated word for word, comes out as Just a minute or Let me talk — and both land as a reprimand in English. The English move never pushes the other speaker back: it attaches to him, or it names an end.

The Executive Point

The Gap Is Half a Second Long

In your first language

He stops. The opening is already in your mouth before you have decided what the point is. You start, and the sentence assembles itself while you speak.

Twenty-two years of meetings built that reflex. You never think about it.

In English

He stops. You know exactly what you think. You build the first clause — and the gap has already closed. Somebody else is three words into the next point.

Nobody interrupted you. You were late by half a second, and half a second is the whole gap.

This is not a fluency problem, and no amount of vocabulary fixes it. It is fixed by five openings that require no thought — you say one, and the sentence arrives while you are already talking.

Chapter 4: The Steering Committee

Philippe, Southern Europe

He talks a great deal, he means no harm, and the hour ends at the hour.

Dialogue

Two Sentences and It Is Yours

P
So my recommendation is that we hold the Colombian limits where they are and revisit them in June. Simple, and the group will accept it.
D
Can I come in on that? Just to build on what you said about June: the renewal season closes in April, so June is after the decision, not before it.
P
That is fair. Although the group calendar was built around June for a reason, and I would not want us to open that up now.
D
Understood, and I am not asking to move the calendar. What I am asking for is one threshold, so that the April renewals do not sit and wait for a meeting. The exposure in Colombia has grown by a third since we set those limits, and
P
And Mexico is exactly the same story, which is why one date for everything is cleaner. If we start moving one market we end up with four calendars, and then the reporting alone becomes a project, and you know what happened last time we tried that in Madrid
D
If I could just finish the point. Two more sentences and it is yours.
P
Of course. Go ahead. I have cut in on you twice now, and I have taken most of the airtime again, which my wife tells me I do at dinner as well.
D
Coming back to what I was saying about the limits: they cover the exposure we had in February, not the exposure we have now. So I am asking for one threshold on Colombia, and Mexico is an aside I am happy to take after this. Before we decide, I would like to hear Milan on it.
P
Then let us do that. Put the threshold in writing and I will ask Milan for a read before Thursday.
Comprehension

About Philippe

1. What is his original recommendation for the Colombian limits?
Hold them where they are and revisit them in June, because the group will accept it.
2. What does he say will happen if one market moves off the group calendar?
Four separate calendars, and reporting that becomes a project on its own.
3. What does he admit about the way he has been talking?
That he has cut in on her twice and taken most of the airtime again.
4. What does he agree to do before Thursday?
Ask Milan for a read on the threshold, once she has put it in writing.
Listening

Stop Composing

A man who chairs meetings in three languages, on the size of the gap. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. How long does he say the gap between two speakers is in English?
About two tenths of a second — and he calls it a measurement, not a cultural preference.
2. What does he say happens while you build the first clause in a second language?
The gap closes and somebody else is already talking. Nobody interrupted you; you were simply late.
3. What does he tell people to do instead of composing the sentence first?
Keep four or five openings that need no thought at all, and say one of them before you know what comes next.
4. What does he say always arrives, and what does not come back?
The sentence always arrives. The gap does not come back.
Listening 2

Somebody Counted

A woman who measured her own weekly call for six weeks. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What exactly did they count over the six weeks?
Who spoke, for how long, and how many times each person was cut in on.
2. What had the two most interrupted people stopped doing?
Starting sentences at all. They waited to be asked, and mostly they were not.
3. What does she say about the senior man with the most airtime?
He was the most surprised person in the room. He was not dominating anyone on purpose — he simply started faster.
4. What does she say actually changed the room, and what did not?
Putting a number on a feeling changed it. Talking about respect did not.

The Words You Will Need On Monday

Spot the Error

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "Sorry, can I to come in on that point?"
"Sorry, can I come in on that point?" (after can, the verb takes no to — and this is the phrase you will say most often, so it has to be automatic)
2. "Just to build in what Philippe said, the renewal season closes in April."
"Just to build on what Philippe said, the renewal season closes in April." (you build ON what somebody said — build in is a different verb altogether)
3. "He interrupted to me twice before I finished the point."
"He interrupted me twice before I finished the point." (interrumpir A alguien carries a personal a in Spanish; in English interrupt takes the person directly, with no preposition)
4. "Philippe took the word before I could start."
"Philippe took the floor before I could start." (tomar la palabra, and its twin in your first language, is take the floor — take the word means nothing in English)

1. What does the map show about the person with the most airtime?
He has forty percent of the hour and was not cut in on once. The speaker who dominates a discussion is usually the one nobody interrupts — which is why he never feels it happening.
2. Why is a map like this harder to argue with than a complaint?
Because it accuses nobody. It is arithmetic: seven minutes against twenty-four, five interruptions against none. A feeling can be denied; a column of numbers has to be answered.
