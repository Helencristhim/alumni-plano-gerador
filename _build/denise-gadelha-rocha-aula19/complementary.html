Lesson 19 -- Taking the Turn

Series
Suits -- any conference-room scene, watched with the sound of the turn

Forget the plot for once and watch the mechanics. Almost every negotiation scene is a fight for the floor, and the writers give the winners the same four moves you practised today: they come in on a pause, they attach their point to the last speaker, they name an end when somebody tries to take the turn back, and they hand it away the moment it costs them nothing.

Tip: pick one six-minute scene and count. How many times does each character cut in, and what does the one with the least airtime do differently? Write down the exact words used to come back to an unfinished point.

Podcast
Think Fast, Talk Smart -- Stanford GSB, the episodes on speaking without preparation

The whole show exists for the problem in today's lesson: what to say when there is no time to prepare the sentence. Matt Abrahams keeps coming back to the same finding, which is that fluent speakers are not faster thinkers -- they have structures ready and start talking inside them. That is exactly what a fixed opening is.

Tip: choose one episode and listen for the host's own openings when he takes the turn back from a guest. He does it constantly and never once says "let me finish". Collect three of his phrases and bring them to class.

YouTube
Celeste Headlee -- 10 Ways to Have a Better Conversation (TED)

Headlee interviewed people for a living, which means her job was managing turns under pressure with a clock running. Watch it for the half of the lesson we did not have time for: yielding well. She is very precise about what happens to a conversation when one person stops leaving room, and about why the listener who takes the floor back with a question keeps everybody on side.

Tip: she gives away the floor several times inside her own talk. Notice how, and write the two sentences you could say on Monday to hand a question to the person who should be answering it.
