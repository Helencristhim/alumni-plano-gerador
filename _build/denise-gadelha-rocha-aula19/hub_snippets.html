The Language of Leadership Under Uncertainty -- Discourse Strategies for High-Stakes Conversations

How to take the turn, hold it when somebody cuts in, and come back to a point you lost. Key words: to cut in, to talk over someone, to hold the floor, to yield, to chime in, airtime, to dominate, to steer, aside, tangent, to reel someone in, crosstalk. Structure: discourse management and turn-taking -- the four fixed moves that buy you the half second you keep losing.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These twelve words describe what actually happens in a meeting, as opposed to what the agenda says happens.

To cut in -- to start speaking while somebody else still has the turn
"He cut in before I had finished the sentence about the renewal."
To talk over someone -- to keep speaking while the other person is still speaking, so that neither is heard
"They talked over each other for a full minute and the point was lost."
To hold the floor -- to keep the turn and finish what you came to say
"I held the floor long enough to give the number and the driver behind it."
To yield -- to hand the floor to somebody else on purpose, before it is taken from you
"I would rather yield to Milan here than answer for a market I do not price."
To chime in -- to add a short comment to a discussion that is already running
"Chime in early, even with one sentence, or the topic closes without you."
Airtime -- the share of a meeting a person actually speaks for
"Philippe had two thirds of the airtime and did not notice."
To dominate -- to take so much of a discussion that other people stop trying
"He does not mean to dominate the call. He simply never stops for breath."
To steer -- to move a discussion towards the point you need decided
"I steered us back to capacity before the hour ran out."
Aside -- a short remark off the main line of the discussion, which comes back on its own
"That is an aside, and I will come back to it after the renewal."
Tangent -- a subject the conversation drifts onto and does not come back from
"We were four minutes into a tangent about the Milan office."
To reel someone in -- to bring a speaker back to the subject without cutting them off
"Reel him in with a question, not with a raised hand."
Crosstalk -- two or more people speaking at once, so that nobody is understood
"On a bad line, crosstalk costs you the whole exchange."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Pay attention to the phrases in bold: none of them carries any information, and all of them do the work.

Philippe had been speaking for four minutes about the group calendar when he paused for breath. Denise did not wait for a better moment, because there is no better moment: she said "Can I come in on that?" and kept going. Then "Just to build on what you said about June" — which attaches her point to his instead of pushing it away, and which is why nobody in the room felt she had cut in at all.

Halfway through her number Philippe started again. This is the moment most people lose, because stopping feels more senior than fighting. She did not raise her voice. She said "If I could just finish the point. Two more sentences and it is yours." The phrase works because it names an end: he is not being shut out, he is being asked to wait for something specific. He apologised, admitted he had taken most of the airtime again, and gave her the floor back.

Five minutes later the discussion had drifted onto the Milan office — a proper tangent, not a short aside — and half of her answer had never been given. She did not say that she had been interrupted, which would have made the meeting about her. She said "Coming back to what I was saying about the limits" and finished. Then she did the one thing that separates the person participating from the person running the room: she gave the turn away on purpose. "I would like to hear Milan on this before we decide." Seven minutes of speaking in a sixty-minute call, and the decision was hers.

1. Why does "Just to build on what you said about June" work better than "I disagree with June"?
A It attaches her point to his, so entering costs him nothing and refusing her would look strange.
B It is more polite, and politeness is what senior meetings reward.
C It hides her disagreement, which is safer with a director.
2. What makes "Two more sentences and it is yours" stronger than "Let me finish"?
A It is longer, and length signals authority in English.
B It names an end. He is not being shut out; he is being asked to wait for something specific and short.
C It is softer, so it avoids the confrontation altogether.
3. Why does she say "Coming back to what I was saying" rather than "You interrupted me earlier"?
A Because she is not certain that he interrupted her.
B Because directors do not accept criticism in front of other people.
C Because the second version makes the meeting about her and the first one simply takes the turn back. The phrase has no "you" in it at all.
4. She spoke for seven minutes out of sixty and the decision was hers. What does the account suggest about airtime?
A Holding the floor is not about speaking more. It is about finishing the sentences that decide something, and giving the rest away.
B Airtime does not matter, so there is no reason to measure it.
C She should have taken as many minutes as Philippe did.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Discourse Management and Turn-Taking

GRAMMAR

Four moves, and a fixed phrase for each one. The phrase carries no content on purpose: there is nothing to build, so you can say it before you know what comes next.

MoveThe phraseWhy it works
Come in"Can I come in on that?" · "Can I come in here?"asks for the turn and takes it in the same breath. Nobody answers the question, and nobody is meant to
Build"Just to build on what Philippe said," · "Picking up on that,"attaches your point to the last speaker, so entering costs him nothing. Almost impossible to refuse
Hold"If I could just finish the point." · "Two more sentences and it is yours."names an end. That is the entire difference from "Let me finish", which is a door closing in somebody's face
Return"Coming back to what I was saying about the limits," · "Before we move on, I still owe you the second half of that answer."reclaims a turn you lost five minutes ago with no "you" in the sentence, so nobody is accused of anything
Yield"I would like to hear Milan on this." · "Philippe, you were closer to that renewal — go ahead."giving the floor away on purpose is the move of the person running the room, never of the person losing it
Steer"That is worth its own conversation — can I bring us back to capacity?"closes a tangent by promising it a future, so the other speaker is postponed rather than dismissed
The formNote what these phrases are made of: can I + bare infinitive (never can I to), just to build on, if I could just + bare infinitive, and coming back to with an -ing form opening the sentence. Fixed forms, learned whole.
Why it mattersIn a meeting in your second language you do not lose the turn because you have nothing to say. You lose it because you spend half a second building the first clause, and half a second is the whole gap. These phrases need no building, so they buy that half second back.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

Philippe has been talking for four minutes and pauses for breath. The fastest way into the discussion is:
A "Can I come in on that? Just to build on what you said about June, the renewals close in April."
B "Sorry to interrupt, I know you have not finished, but if you do not mind I would like to say something."
C Wait until he finishes completely, then raise the point if there is still time.
You are halfway through your number and he starts speaking over you. The strongest thing to say is:
A "Let me finish, please."
B "If I could just finish the point. Two more sentences and it is yours."
C "Sorry, go ahead." Then wait for another opening.
Five minutes later the room has moved on and half of your answer was never given. You say:
A "You interrupted me earlier and I never finished my point."
B "Before we move on, I still owe you the second half of that answer."
C Nothing. The moment has passed, and going back would sound difficult.
The discussion has been on the Milan office for six minutes and the meeting ends in eight. You say:
A "That is worth its own conversation. Can I bring us back to capacity before the hour?"
B "We are wasting the meeting on something that is not on the agenda."
C "Can we please stop talking about Milan?"
The question is about pricing, and the person who should answer it has said nothing for twenty minutes. The move that shows seniority is:
A Answer it yourself, quickly, to keep the meeting moving.
B "I would like to hear Milan on this before we decide."
C Wait, and hope he comes in on his own before the decision is taken.
You recorded your weekly call and counted: two people had eighty percent of the minutes. The most useful way to say that in English is:
A "Two of us had eighty percent of the airtime." It is arithmetic, and nobody has to be accused of anything.
B "They talked over everybody for the whole hour."
C "Nobody ever lets me speak in that meeting."

Series
Suits -- any conference-room scene, watched with the sound of the turn

Forget the plot for once and watch the mechanics. Almost every negotiation scene is a fight for the floor, and the writers give the winners the same four moves you practised today: they come in on a pause, they attach their point to the last speaker, they name an end when somebody tries to take the turn back, and they hand it away the moment it costs them nothing.

Tip: pick one six-minute scene and count. How many times does each character cut in, and what does the one with the least airtime do differently? Write down the exact words used to come back to an unfinished point.

The whole show exists for the problem in today's lesson: what to say when there is no time to prepare the sentence. Matt Abrahams keeps coming back to the same finding, which is that fluent speakers are not faster thinkers -- they have structures ready and start talking inside them.
