Chapter 3: The Code

I Would Rather, You Had Better

Preference, warning and resignation, in three short forms

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I would rather talk about the policy than about the currency."

"You had better check that figure before you quote it."

"The market is closed until Monday, so we may as well walk."

"I would rather they did not ask me about my portfolio."

Look at the verb straight after each of the three forms. One small word that English normally insists on is missing from all of them. And the fourth sentence does something different again.

FormWhat it saysExample
would rather + bare verbYour own preference, right now. Never to after it."I would rather talk about the policy."
would rather + subject + PASTA preference about what SOMEBODY ELSE does. The past tense here is not about the past."I would rather they did not ask."
had better + bare verbStrong advice with a consequence behind it. It is NOT polite, and it is not it would be better."You had better check that figure."
may as well + bare verbThere is no better option, so we might as well do this. Resignation, said lightly."We may as well walk."
The trapsNo to after any of the three. And had better is stronger than it looks: use it about facts, not about people you have just met.NOT "I would rather to talk."
NOT "You had better to check."

All three are short, and that is the point. In a conversation about the economy the person who sounds certain is not the one with the longest sentence.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I would rather to talk about the policy."
"I would rather talk about the policy."
"You had better to check that figure."
"You had better check that figure."
"I would rather that they do not ask me about it."
"I would rather they did not ask me about it."

Two rules cover all three. After would rather, had better and may as well the verb is bare — no to, ever. And when the preference is about somebody ELSE, English steps the verb into the past, exactly as it does after wish. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

A Number About the Market, Not About Me

M
Do people in Brazil talk about money as openly as they talk about politics?
B
About the economy, constantly. About their own salaries, almost never.
M
That holds almost everywhere in my experience. The exception is people who have just made money.
B
And when somebody asks you directly what you used to earn? I would rather they did not, but they do.
M
They do, usually about twenty minutes in and usually after the second glass.
B
So what do you say?
M
I answer with a number about the market instead of a number about me. Nobody notices, and the conversation gets better.
B
Then I may as well start collecting numbers about the market.
Comprehension

About Margaret

1. What does Margaret say holds almost everywhere?
That people talk about the economy constantly and about their own salaries almost never.
2. Which group does she name as the exception?
People who have just made money.
3. When does she say the direct question usually arrives?
About twenty minutes in, and usually after the second glass.
4. What is her own way out of it, and what is the result?
She answers with a number about the market instead of a number about herself -- nobody notices, and the conversation gets better.
Listening

Three Things Before You Eat

An economist at a dinner, talking fast. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does he say the number everybody quoted last week actually was?
A valuation, not a price -- and a valuation is somebody's opinion with a decimal point in it.
2. What does it mean when people say a market is liquid?
That you can sell without moving the price -- which is a different question from whether the thing is worth anything.
3. Why does he refuse to use the word bubble?
Because every bubble is obvious afterwards and invisible from the inside, and anybody who claims to see one from the inside is selling something.
Listening 2

Money in the Third Person

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

1. Which subjects does she say are open, and which are closed?
Open: the economy, the market, the currency, the policy. Closed: how much you have, what you paid for the house, what you earned.
2. In what way does she say the question is rude?
In a quieter way -- it puts the other person in a position where every possible answer is uncomfortable.
3. What is the rule she gives, in one line?
Talk about money in the third person -- and the only person who may safely bring up your finances is you.
