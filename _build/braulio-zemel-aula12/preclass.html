Lesson 12 -- Pre-class

Money, Power and Success -- What Gets Said at the Table and What Does Not

How to talk about the economy for an hour without putting a single number of your own on the table, and how to leave the question when it arrives. Key expressions: a venture, an asset, liquidity, a valuation, to consolidate, a portfolio, fiscal, a yield, the downside, to hedge, a windfall, a bubble. Structure: would rather, had better and may as well -- preference, warning and resignation, all with a bare verb.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

A venture -- a new business project with real risk attached to it
"It was a venture, not an investment, and everybody knew the difference."
An asset -- anything you own that has value and could be sold
"On paper it is an asset. In practice nobody wants to buy it."
Liquidity -- how easily something can be sold without moving its price
"Liquidity is not the same as value. It is only the exit."
A valuation -- somebody's estimate of what a thing is worth, not its price
"A valuation is an opinion with a decimal point in it."
To consolidate -- to combine several things into one stronger unit
"Three banks consolidated into one and the queues got longer."
A portfolio -- the whole collection of things somebody has invested in
"Nobody at a dinner table has ever been asked about their portfolio twice."
Fiscal -- relating to what a government takes in and spends
"It is a fiscal problem, not a monetary one, and the two get confused every day."
A yield -- what an investment pays you back, as a percentage
"If you cannot explain where the yield comes from, you are the yield."
The downside -- the bad side of an idea, said out loud before somebody else says it
"The upside is obvious. Tell me the downside and I will believe you."
To hedge -- to protect yourself against being wrong, at the cost of winning less
"He hedged, which is why he is still here and the others are not."
A windfall -- money that arrives unexpectedly and that nobody planned for
"The commodity boom was a windfall, and windfalls are spent badly everywhere."
A bubble -- a price that everybody agrees on until the morning they do not
"Every bubble is obvious afterwards and invisible from the inside."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a rule at every international dinner table and nobody states it. Money is an open subject in the third person and a closed one in the first. The fiscal deficit, the valuation of a company nobody in the room owns, whether the market has enough liquidity to survive a bad week: all of that is conversation. What you earned, what you paid, what your portfolio looks like: none of that is.

The question arrives anyway, usually twenty minutes in. And this is where the three short forms earn their place. I would rather talk about what the job did to my weekends than about what it paid. I would rather they did not ask, but they do, so there is no point being surprised by it. You had better have an answer ready, because inventing one at the table always sounds like an evasion, and an evasion sounds worse than a number.

Notice the shape of those three. After would rather, had better and may as well, the verb comes bare, with no to in front of it. And when the preference is about somebody ELSE, the verb steps into the past: I would rather they did not ask, never do not ask. That step backwards is the same one English makes after wish, and it means the same thing: this is not real, it is what I would prefer.

The rest is vocabulary you already half own from reading. A windfall is money nobody planned for and it is spent badly in every country. A bubble is obvious afterwards and invisible from inside. And if you cannot explain where a yield is coming from, you had better assume you are the yield. Those three sentences will hold a table for ten minutes, and not one of them is about you.

1. "I would rather they did not ask." Why the past tense?
A Because it happened yesterday.
B Because the preference is about somebody else, and English steps the verb backwards.
C Because ask has no present form after rather.
2. What do would rather, had better and may as well have in common?
A The verb after them is bare -- no to.
B They are all polite ways of giving advice.
C They all take a verb ending in -ing.
3. According to the text, why should you have an answer ready?
A Because the question is rude and you should refuse it.
B Because inventing one at the table sounds like an evasion, and that sounds worse than a number.
C Because everybody at the table already knows the answer.
4. What is a windfall in this text?
A Money nobody planned for -- and it is spent badly in every country.
B A price that everybody agrees on until they do not.
C What an investment pays you back as a percentage.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Preference, Warning and Resignation

GRAMMAR

Three short forms that let you take a position without a long sentence. All three take a bare verb, and one of them is stronger than it looks.

FormWhat it saysExample
would rather + bare verbYour own preference, right now. Never to after it."I would rather talk about the policy."
would rather + subject + PASTA preference about what somebody ELSE does. The past tense here is not about the past."I would rather they did not ask."
had better + bare verbStrong advice with a consequence behind it. It is not politeness, and it is not it would be better."You had better check that figure."
may as well + bare verbThere is no better option, so we might as well do this. Resignation, said lightly."We may as well walk."
The trapsNo to after any of the three. And had better is heavier than it sounds -- use it about facts, not about people you have just met.NOT "I would rather to talk."
NOT "You had better to check."
NOT "I would rather that they do not ask."

A practical note. All three are short on purpose, and shortness is what makes them sound certain. In a conversation about the economy the person who convinces the table is almost never the one with the longest sentence.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a dinner and the conversation has turned to money. Choose the best thing to say.

You prefer to discuss the policy rather than the currency:
A "I would rather to talk about the policy."
B "I would rather talk about the policy."
C "I would rather talking about the policy."
You want somebody to stop asking about your finances:
A "I would rather they did not ask me about it."
B "I would rather that they do not ask me about it."
C "I would rather they will not ask me about it."
Somebody asks directly what you used to earn. The best move is:
A Give the exact figure so that nobody thinks you are hiding it.
B "Enough to stop, which is the only number that matters now."
C Say nothing and change the subject abruptly.
A number nobody can defend has just been quoted as a fact. That number is:
A a valuation
B liquidity
C a windfall
Nobody is going to settle the argument tonight. You would say:
A "We may as well move on."
