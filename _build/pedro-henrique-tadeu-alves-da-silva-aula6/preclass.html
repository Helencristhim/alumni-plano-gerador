Rules of the Room

They hand you two printed pages on your first day, and almost nothing that matters is in them. Key words: etiquette, to cut in, a deadline, to be on call, overtime, a heads-up, to flag something, mandatory, to be off limits, a workaround. Structure: must came from inside the building, have to came from outside it, should came from nobody -- and do not have to is not a prohibition.

Vocabulary

Etiquette -- the unwritten rules people follow in a particular place
"Studio etiquette is never printed, and it is the only thing anybody judges you on."
To cut in -- to start speaking before the other person has finished
"You should not cut in while somebody is listening back to a take."
A deadline -- the day and hour when something has to be delivered, and after which it is late
"Is this deadline fixed, or is this deadline a preference?"
To be on call -- to be available to work at short notice, even outside your hours
"Being on call is not the same as working, and it is not free either."
Overtime -- the hours you work beyond the normal day, usually paid differently
"We worked overtime for a deadline that turned out to be a preference."
A heads-up -- a short warning given early, so that nobody is surprised later
"Send a heads-up before you are late, not after."
To flag something -- to point out a problem early so that somebody can deal with it
"You should flag it early, in one sentence, before it becomes expensive."
Mandatory -- required by a rule, with no choice about it
"A mandatory deadline should be treated as mandatory. The other kind should not."
To be off limits -- not allowed to be touched, used or entered
"Studio two is off limits after eleven, and that one is actually printed."
A workaround -- an unofficial way of getting past a problem without really fixing it
"A workaround that only one person understands is a bomb with a long fuse."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every studio hands you two printed pages on the first day, and almost nothing that matters is in them. You must wear the pass. You must not bring food into the control room. Studio two is off limits after eleven. Those are real, but you can read them in four minutes and then forget them, because the room enforces them for you. The rules that decide whether you are invited back are the etiquette, and nobody will ever tell you what they are.

English marks where a rule comes from, out loud. Must usually comes from inside the building: the studio decided it. Have to often comes from outside it — the broadcaster, the client, the contract. We have to deliver on Thursday because somebody else owns the slot. Should is advice, and it is the form the unwritten rules arrive in, which is exactly why they are easy to ignore. You should flag a problem the same day, in one sentence. You should not cut in while somebody is listening back. Can and may are permission, and the difference between them is mostly how formal the room is.

Two traps. The first is that must not and do not have to look similar and mean opposite things: you must not stay is a prohibition, and you do not have to stay means it is simply not required. The second is that none of these modals ever takes to, and none of them is followed by -ing. Not you must to wear, not we have to delivering. And one piece of vocabulary that carries its own rule: if you build a workaround, you must tell somebody, because a mandatory deadline, a night of overtime and a weekend on call are all survivable, but a workaround nobody knows about is not.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Obligation, Prohibition and Advice

GRAMMAR

One decision: who decided this, and is it forbidden or simply not required?

JobFormExample
Obligation from insidemust + base verbYou must wear the pass. We must write it in the handover notes.
Obligation from outsidehave to / has to + base verbWe have to deliver on Thursday. He has to be on call one weekend in four.
Prohibitionmust not / cannot + base verbYou must not bring food in. You cannot enter studio two after eleven.
No obligation (NOT prohibition)do not have to + base verbYou do not have to stay tonight. It is allowed; it is just not required.
Adviceshould / should not + base verbYou should flag it the same day. You should not cut in during playback.
Permissioncan (normal) · may (formal)Can I use studio two? May I take five minutes of your time?
The trapno to, no -ing, and two opposites that look alikeNot you must to wear, not we have to delivering. And must not is the opposite of do not have to.

Never: you must to wear the pass · we have to delivering on Thursday · you should to flag it · it is optional so you must not stay · he must works this weekend

Stage 2: Put the Briefing in Order

Order

Somebody starts on Monday and knows nothing. Put your briefing in a useful order.

?"The printed rules are the easy ones: you must wear the pass and you must not bring food into the control room."
?"The rules that matter are unwritten, and nobody will ever tell you what they are."
?"The first one: you should not cut in while somebody is listening back to a take."
?"The second one: you should flag a problem early, in one sentence."
?"And if you are going to be late, you have to send a heads-up before you are late, not after."
?"And you, what are the unwritten rules where you work?"
