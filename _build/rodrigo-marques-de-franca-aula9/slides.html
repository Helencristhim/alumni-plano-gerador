Lesson 9 · The Report

What They Told Me

The meeting is over and somebody has to be told what happened. The hard part is not the memory. It is keeping what they said apart from what you concluded.

Chapter 3: The Code

Everything Moves One Step Back

The tense, the person, and the time word

Grammar Discovery

What Changed on the Way?

“We cannot accept that cap.” → She said that they could not accept the cap.

“I will send the mark-up tomorrow.” → He told me he would send it the next day.

“Have you spoken to the board?” → She asked whether I had spoken to the board.

“Send it before Friday.” → He asked me to send it before Friday.

Compare each original with its report. Three things change every single time, and one of them is not a verb at all.

In one line: everything moves one step away from the room — the tense, the person, and the day.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Report, at Full Speed

One client, two recordings, one file note

Dialogue

Reporting to the Client

M
I have four minutes before my next call. What happened?
R
Three things. They said they could accept twelve months on the cap. They were adamant that fraud stays outside it. And they asked whether we had already spoken to the board.
M
Did they say twelve, or did they say they could live with twelve?
R
Her words were that twelve was something she could take to her client. So it is not agreed. I am relaying that as she said it, because the difference matters on Friday.
M
Good. And what do you think is actually going on?
R
That part is my reading, not theirs. She was guarded about the timetable and open about everything else, and she downplayed the delay twice. My reading is that their board meets before ours does.
M
Did she actually say that, or is that you?
R
That is me. She never mentioned a board meeting. She hinted at a date twice and walked it back both times, and the note I send you tonight will say exactly that, in two separate paragraphs.
Comprehension

About Marta

1. How much time does Marta have, and what does she ask for first?
Four minutes, before her next call. She asks what happened, with no preamble.
2. Her second question separates two things. What are they, and why does the distinction matter?
Whether they said twelve, or said they could live with twelve. The first would be an agreement; the second is only something the other lawyer can take to her client.
3. She asks twice, in different words, whether something was said or concluded. What is she protecting herself from?
From acting on a conclusion as if it were a fact. By Friday nobody would remember which of the two it had been.
Listening 1 · The Client

Three Sentences, In That Order

An in-house lawyer saying what she wants after a meeting. Sound first — no text.

1. What are her three sentences, in order?
What was agreed, what was not agreed, and what you think they will do next.
2. Why does she say people leave out the third one, and what is her answer to that?
Because it is a judgement rather than a record, and nobody wants to be wrong in writing. She would rather have the lawyer wrong in writing than silent.
3. What does she have to ask for every single time, and why?
That the lawyer separate what they said from what he concluded. Both are useful, they are not the same thing, and by Thursday nobody remembers which one it was.
Listening 2 · The Visible Seam

They Told Me X, and My Reading Is Y

Somebody describing the sentence that changed how he reports. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the sentence he now uses, and what does it have that the old way did not?
They told me X, and my reading of it is Y. Two clauses, with a visible seam between them.
2. What does he say used to happen instead, and what goes into the compressed version?
He compressed: four things somebody said, plus their tone, plus the fact that they arrived late and looked tired, all turned into one confident sentence.
3. Why does he call being usually right the dangerous part?
Because the one time in eight when he is wrong, nobody can reconstruct how the conclusion was reached, including him.
Real Document

The Note That Goes on the File

FILE NOTE — MEETING
Project Meridian · call of 14 March · prepared same day
WHAT THEY SAID
They said they could accept a cap of twelve months. They were adamant that fraud remains outside it. They asked whether we had spoken to the board.
WHAT WAS NOT AGREED
Nothing. Counsel said only that twelve was a number she could take to her client.
MY READING
She was guarded about the timetable and downplayed the delay twice. I believe their board meets before ours. She did not say this.
NEXT STEP
She undertook to revert by Friday. I flagged the notice period; it was not picked up.
Note: one remark was made off the record and is not recorded above.
1. Why are What they said and My reading in separate sections rather than in one paragraph?
Because a report that mixes them turns a conclusion into a fact. Separated, the client can rely on the first section even if the second turns out to be wrong.
2. The section headed What was not agreed says Nothing. Why is that line worth writing at all?
Because without it a reader assumes the twelve-month cap is settled. Saying that nothing was agreed is the whole point of the section.
3. What does the last line protect, and what would happen without it?
It records that something was said off the record and deliberately not written down. Without it, a later reader would assume the note is complete.
