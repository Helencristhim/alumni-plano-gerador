Chapter 3: The Code

How Much and How Many

Two boxes, and the words that go with each one

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"How many people are coming?"

"How much money do we need?"

"There were only a few tables free."

"There was only a little cash on the table."

Two of these ask the same question and two of them give the same answer. Something about the nouns decides which version you get. What is it?

FormWhat it saysExample
Countable: one, two, threeYou can put a number in front. It has a plural.one bill, two bills
a receipt, three receipts
Uncountable: no number, no pluralIt is a substance or an idea. It never takes a and never takes an S.money, cash, change, time
NOT "two moneys"
how many / how muchMany counts. Much measures."How many people?"
"How much money?"
a few / a littleA small amount. A few counts, a little measures."a few tables"
"a little cash"
The words that work with bothSome, any, plenty of and a lot of never ask you to choose."plenty of time" · "plenty of tables"
"a lot of money" · "a lot of people"

The eight that catch everybody: money, cash, change, information, advice, news, luggage and furniture. English refuses to count all eight, and Portuguese counts most of them.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"How many money do we need for the trip?"
"How much money do we need for the trip?"
"He gave me an important information."
"He gave me an important piece of information."
"There were only a little tables free."
"There were only a few tables free."

Much goes with things you cannot count and many with things you can. Information, advice and news are uncountable in English even though Portuguese counts them — to count one, English borrows a container: a piece of information. And a small amount is a few for countable things, a little for the rest. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Comprehension

About Grace

1. What does Grace offer before the bill?
The dessert menu.
2. What two options does she give for the bill?
All together, or split it.
3. What does she say about the service charge?
It is ten per cent and it is already on the bill.
4. What does she ask him at the end?
Whether he needs a receipt for the company.
Listening

Ten Per Cent, or Nothing at All

A consumer radio segment. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does the report say about tipping in Japan?
That leaving a tip can be taken as an insult.
2. What is normal in the United States, according to the report?
Between eighteen and twenty-two per cent, and it is not optional.
3. What is the practical advice for a traveller?
Ask a local, or look at the bottom of the bill, because in many countries the service is already there.
Listening 2

The Expenses Message

A voice message about a trip budget. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does she ask him to keep?
Every receipt, including the small ones.
2. How much money is left in the budget?
About a third of it.
3. What does she say about paying in cash?
That he should avoid it, because the company cannot process a payment with no receipt.

The Habit

Two Boxes, Out Loud

Before the next class, look at anything on your desk and say out loud whether English counts it or not: how many pens, how much paper. Sixty seconds, ten nouns.

Nobody sorts these two boxes by thinking. You sort them by hearing yourself do it, a hundred times.
