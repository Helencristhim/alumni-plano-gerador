Where This Takes You -- The Goals Essay

Every application asks for the same page: what you will do with this, and by when. Key words: a statement of intent, to pivot, a horizon, to professionalize, a road map, to be due to, a deliverable, to future-proof, to bridge a gap, incremental, a ceiling, scope. Structure: future forms -- will, going to, present continuous and future perfect.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A statement of intent -- the written page in which you say what you plan to do and why
"The statement of intent is eight hundred words, and six hundred of them are about the future."
To pivot -- to change direction while keeping most of what you have already built
"We are not starting again. We are pivoting towards renovation, with the same team."
A horizon -- the distance ahead that a plan is made for, in years
"Our planning horizon used to be one project. Now it is three years."
To professionalize -- to replace what one person does by instinct with a process anybody can follow
"Professionalizing the company means my judgement stops being the only system."
A road map -- the ordered set of steps between where you are and where you say you are going
"A goal without a road map is a wish with a date on it."
To be due to -- to be scheduled to happen at a particular time, whether you decided it or not
"The interviews are due to start in October, so the essay is due in September."
A deliverable -- a specific thing you promise to produce, with a date attached to it
"The capstone has one deliverable, and the date is not negotiable."
To future-proof -- to build something now so that it still works when conditions change
"Writing the governance down was the cheapest way to future-proof the company."
To bridge a gap -- to connect two things that do not yet meet
"The program is how I bridge the gap between what I run and what I was trained for."
Incremental -- happening in small steps rather than in one jump
"The change was incremental, which is why nobody resisted it."
A ceiling -- the limit you reach that stops you going further without changing something
"I could see the ceiling before I hit it, and that is why I am applying now."
Scope -- how wide a job or a project reaches, and where it stops
"The scope of my job doubled in 2011 and the title took two more years."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A statement of intent is the only page of an application written entirely in the future, and English has four ways of writing it. They are not stylistic alternatives: each one tells the reader how much has already been decided. Will is the weakest. It predicts: "the market will be harder in 2028 than it is now." Going to is a decision already taken, whether or not anything has been arranged: "I am going to professionalize the company either way."

The present continuous is the strongest of the three, and it is the one Portuguese speakers reach for least. It means the thing is in the diary: "I am applying in October." Note what it needs -- a time expression. Without one it means now, not later. Close to it sits to be due to, which says the same but hands the decision to somebody else: "the interviews are due to start in October." Fixed timetables take the present simple: "the residency starts on the fourth."

The fourth form does something the other three cannot. Put by in front of a future date and the sentence has to look back from it: "by 2029 I will have handed the site work to somebody else." That is what turns a horizon into a road map, and it is the form an admissions reader is looking for, because it forces a deliverable instead of an ambition. Two traps to avoid. Never use a future form after when, if, before or as soon as: "when I finish the program, I will be forty-nine." And never write will for something already arranged -- it reads as a wish, and the reader will treat it as one.

2. Why does the text say the future perfect is what an admissions reader is looking for?

3. What does the text say about the words when, if, before and as soon as?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- How Decided Does It Sound?

GRAMMAR

Will predicts, going to has decided, the present continuous has a date, and the future perfect looks back from one.

JobFormExample
A predictionwill + base formThe market will be harder in 2028.
A decision already takenbe going to + base formI am going to professionalize the company.
An arrangement with a datepresent continuous + time expressionI am applying in October.
Finished by a future pointby + date, will have + participleBy 2029 I will have handed it over.
Scheduled by somebody elsebe due to + base formThe interviews are due to start in October.
A fixed timetablepresent simpleThe residency starts on the fourth.
After when, if, before, as soon aspresent simple, never a future formWhen I finish the program, I will be forty-nine.

Never: I will apply in October (if it is arranged) · next year I professionalize the company · by 2029 I will hand it over · when I will finish the program · I am applying, with no time expression, meaning later.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose what you would actually say.

The application is already submitted and the interview date is in your diary. You say:
A "I will interview in October."
B "I am interviewing in October."
C "I interview in October, probably."
A reader asks what happens if you are not admitted. You want to say the plan survives. You say:
A "I am going to professionalize the company either way."
B "I will professionalize the company, I think."
C "I professionalize the company anyway."
You want to say the site work will be finished before 2029 arrives. You say:
A "By 2029 I will hand the site work over."
B "By 2029 I will have handed the site work over."
C "By 2029 I am handing the site work over."
You are talking about the moment the program ends. You say:
A "When I will finish the program, I will be forty-nine."
B "When I finish the program, I will be forty-nine."
C "When I am going to finish the program, I will be forty-nine."

Lesson 10 — Where This Takes You

Talk
TED -- Dan Gilbert, "The psychology of your future self" (7 min)

A Harvard psychologist on why the person you will be in ten years is almost never the person you predict today. Seven minutes, and it is the most useful argument against writing a goals essay full of predictions.

Tip: after the talk, say out loud which line of your own road map is a prediction and which one is a decision. The two need different verbs.
