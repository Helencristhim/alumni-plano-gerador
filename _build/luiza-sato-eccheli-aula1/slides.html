Chapter 3: The Code

The Past That Is Still Going On

Four sentences about the same eleven years, and why none of them uses the simple past

Grammar Discovery

Why Not Just I Studied?

"I have been studying English since I was seven."

"I have read about forty novels in English this year."

"My brother has lived in London for three years."

"I have been preparing for the TOEFL for two months, and I have finished one practice test."

All four sentences talk about the past. So why does none of them say studied, read or lived? Look at the red forms and the blue forms: which ones focus on the activity, and which ones focus on the result?

JobFormExample
An activity that started in the past and is still going onhave / has been + verb-ingI have been studying English since I was seven.
A result, a number, a finished amounthave / has + past participleI have read forty novels this year.
A state that is still true (live, know, be)have / has + past participle, not -ingI have known her since school.
The starting pointsince + a momentsince I was seven · since 2019
The length of timefor + a periodfor eleven years · for two months
A finished time in the pastsimple past, never present perfectI went to Scotland last year.
The trapnever I study English since; never I have been knowingNot I study since I was seven. Say I have been studying.

In one line: If it started in the past and is still true, use have: -ing for the activity, the past participle for the result.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Call Before the Application

One conversation, two recordings, one checklist

Dialogue

Ten Minutes with the Exchange Office

M
Hi, Luiza, I am Mark Ellison, and I coordinate exchange students at the law school here. Before we look at your file, tell me a little about your English.
L
Sure. I have been studying English since I was seven, but honestly, I learned most of it online, watching American videos.
M
That is more common than you think. And have you ever studied abroad?
L
Only for one month, in Scotland, last year. It was short, but it gave me a head start, because now I know what a class in English feels like.
M
Good. You should know that our exchange program is quite competitive. Every year about two hundred students apply for thirty places.
L
I know. My brother did the same exchange, and when he applied, the cut-off score for the best universities was above ninety.
M
The scale has changed since then, but the idea has not. The score is the first requirement, and it only gets you shortlisted. What makes a student stand out is the interview.
L
Then that is what I want to work on. I read a lot, but when I speak about technical things, I sometimes stumble over the words.
M
You have time. The deadline is in two years, so if you practice every week, you will keep up with the classes easily.
L
That is exactly why I started now. And which host universities do law students usually choose?
Comprehension

About Mark

1. What is Mark’s job at the law school?
He coordinates exchange students.
2. According to Mark, how many students apply every year, and for how many places?
About two hundred students apply for thirty places.
3. What does Mark say has changed, and what does he say the score does?
The scale has changed. The score is the first requirement and only gets you shortlisted; the interview is what makes a student stand out.
4. When is the deadline, and what does Mark say will happen if she practices every week?
In two years. If she practices every week, she will keep up with the classes easily.
Listening 1 · A Podcast

What the Committee Remembers

A former admissions reader on exchange interviews. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does she say the score stops being interesting in the interview?
Because everyone who reaches the interview already has a good score.
2. What did the strongest candidates have, and what did they not necessarily have?
They had a clear story. They did not necessarily have the most impressive track record.
3. What did the committee care about, instead of accent?
Whether they had to ask the candidate to repeat themselves.
Listening 2 · Info Session

Two Years Before You Go

An exchange office explains the timeline. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does he say most students come to the office too late?
Because they come six months before the deadline.
2. What are the three parts of the application, and what band do the most competitive universities expect?
The language requirement, the grades from the first two years, and a short interview in English. They expect an overall band of at least four and a half, sometimes five.
3. What are his two practical pieces of advice, and what does he say a semester abroad is not?
Take a practice test this semester and choose three host universities. He says a semester abroad is not a vacation.
