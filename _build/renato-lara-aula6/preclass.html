Lesson 06 — Pre-class

House Rules

Eight house words (curfew, chores, bin, laundry, share, spare key, lock up, help yourself) and the modals of permission and obligation: can, can't, have to — and why "don't have to" is not the same as "can't".

Stage 1.1: House Words

Vocabulary

Tap Listen, then say the word out loud. Eight words, two minutes.

Curfew — the time when you have to be at home
"On school nights the curfew is nine thirty."
Chores — the small jobs everybody does at home
"Everybody in this house has chores."
Bin — the container where you put rubbish — Australians never say trash can
"The bins go out on Tuesday night."
Laundry — the clothes you have to wash
"You do your own laundry on Sundays."
Share — to use something together with somebody
"You share the bathroom with Jack."
Spare key — the extra key that stays in the house for you
"Your spare key is on the hook by the door."
Lock up — to close all the doors with a key before you go
"The last person out has to lock up."
Help yourself — take what you want — you do not have to ask
"There is fruit on the table. Help yourself."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the note. Then answer the questions.

Welcome! A few things. The kitchen is open all day, so help yourself — you don't have to ask. You can use the bikes in the garage any time. You have to put the bins out on Tuesday night and you have to do your own laundry on Sunday. On school nights you have to be home by nine thirty, but at the weekend you can stay out later if you send a message. You don't have to cook, although you can help if you want. One thing you can't do: leave the back door open, because of the dog. The last person out has to lock up. Your spare key is on the hook.
What are the two obligations every week?
A The bins on Tuesday and his own laundry on Sunday.
B Cooking dinner and locking up.
Does he have to cook?
A No, and he is not allowed to.
B No, but he can help if he wants.
What is the difference between "you don't have to cook" and "you can't cook"?
A The first one is free choice; the second one is forbidden.
B There is no real difference.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Can, can't, have to, don't have to

Four ways to talk about rules. The verb after all of them is bare: can go, have to go.

can → it is allowed. You can surf before school.
can't → it is forbidden. You can't take the car.
have to → it is obligatory. You have to lock up. (he / she / it: has to)
don't have to → it is not necessary, but you may. You don't have to cook.
? Can I use the kitchen? / Do I have to do my own laundry?
Careful: never "can to", never "cans", never "don't can". It is can, can't, and that is it.

Stage 4: In the house

Quiz
You want permission to use the kitchen. What do you ask?
A Can I to use the kitchen?
B Can I use the kitchen?
Dinner is cooked for you, but helping is welcome. How do they say it?
A You don't have to cook.
B You can't cook.
Talking about your host brother's obligation. Which sentence is correct?
A He have to lock up.
B He has to lock up.
Somebody invites you out on a school night. The curfew is 9:30. What do you say?
A Sorry, I have to be home by nine thirty.
B Sorry, I don't have to be home by nine thirty.

Stage 5: The rules of your house

Reflection
An Australian friend is going to stay at your place for a week. Explain the house, out loud, for 30 seconds. What can he do freely? What is forbidden? What does he have to do? And what does he NOT have to do? Use all four: can, can't, have to, don't have to.
