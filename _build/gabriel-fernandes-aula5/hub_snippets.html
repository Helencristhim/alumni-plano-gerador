Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A draft -- a version you write knowing it is not the final one
“The fourth draft was half the length of the first, and it was better.”
To edit -- to change what you have already written, in order to improve it
“Writing is choosing the words. Editing is taking half of them out again.”
A turning point -- the moment after which things were not the same
“The week I lost money was a turning point, and it did not feel like one at the time.”
To reflect on -- to think carefully about something that already happened
“Telling the story is easy. Reflecting on it is the half that people skip.”
An insight -- the thing you understood that you could not have understood before
“I want the insight that only you could have had, not the one from the internet.”
A trait -- a part of your character that shows up again and again
“One trait, shown three times, beats a list of ten adjectives.”
To narrow down -- to cut a wide idea to the one thing you will actually write about
“He narrowed it down to one Saturday, and the essay got better and shorter.”
Vivid -- described so clearly that the reader can see it
“Fourteen unsold boxes is vivid. A difficult week is not.”
To proofread -- to read your own text looking only for mistakes
“I proofread it out loud, and both times I caught something my eyes had walked over.”
A cliche -- a phrase so used that it has stopped meaning anything
“This taught me the value of hard work is a cliche, and it tells the reader nothing.”
Concise -- saying it in fewer words without losing anything
“Concise is not short. Short can lose things; concise does not.”
An anecdote -- a short true story you tell in order to make a point
“Open with an anecdote, not with a definition of the thing you love.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of talking about the past, and the difference is not how long ago it was. It is whether the time you are naming is closed or still open. I started the stall in 2024. The year 2024 is finished, the door is shut, and the past simple is the only form that fits. I have run it since 2024. That period began in 2024 and has not ended, because I still have the stall. The door is open, and English marks that with have plus the past participle.

The signal words are the fastest way in. Ago, last year, in March, yesterday, when I was nine all close the door and demand the past simple. Since, for, ever, never, so far, already, until now keep it open and demand the present perfect. Learn the words and the tense usually follows on its own, which is why they are worth memorising before the rule is.

There is a second use that has nothing to do with time. I have never asked my parents for money. Nobody is naming a moment here; the point is the experience up to now, and the door is open because my life is not over. The same with a result that still counts: I have learnt to write the number down matters because I am still doing it. Say I learnt it in March and you have turned it into a fact about March.

Two mistakes are almost universal among Portuguese speakers, and they are opposites. The first is putting have with a closed time: I have started the stall last year is wrong, because last year has already shut the door. The second is putting the past simple with since: I run it since 2024 is wrong, because since holds the door open and needs have. If you learn to catch only these two, most of your writing is already fixed.

1. According to the text, what decides which of the two tenses you use?
A How long ago the event happened.
B Whether the time you are naming is closed or still open.
C Whether the sentence is about you or about somebody else.
2. Why does the text say "I have never asked my parents for money" uses the present perfect?
A Because it happened a long time ago.
B Because nobody is naming a moment: it is the experience up to now, and life is not over.
C Because the sentence is negative.
3. What are the two mistakes the text calls almost universal, and how does it describe them?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect vs Past Simple

GRAMMAR

A closed time takes the past simple. An open time takes have plus the participle.

JobFormExample
A moment that is overpast simpleI started the stall in 2024.
Something still goinghave / has + participleI have run it ever since.
Words that close the doorago, last, in 2024, yesterday, whenI lost money in March.
Words that keep it opensince, for, ever, never, so far, alreadyI have counted every box since.
Experience at no fixed timehave + participleI have never asked my parents for money.
The result that still countshave + participleI have learnt to write the number down.
The first trapnever have with a closed timeNot I have started it last year. Say I started it last year.
The second trapnever past simple with sinceNot I run it since 2024. Say I have run it since 2024.

Never: I have started it last year · I run it since 2024 · I have lost money in March · Since then I write it down

Stage 2: Put the Writing in Order

Order

This is the order in which a personal statement actually gets written. Listen first, then put the lines in order.

?“You cut every sentence that could belong to somebody else.”
?“You narrow down from everything you have done to one thing that actually changed you.”
?“You read it out loud and you proofread it once more before you send it.”
?“You write the anecdote in the past, in as much detail as you can remember.”
?“You say what you have understood since, and that part is in the present perfect.”

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give. Read all three before you decide.

1. The year is named and it is finished. You say:
A I have started the stall in 2024.
B I started the stall in 2024.
C I have been starting the stall in 2024.
2. The word is since and you still have the stall. You say:
A I run it since 2024.
B I ran it since 2024.
C I have run it since 2024.
3. You are describing an experience with no date attached. You say:
A I have never asked my parents for money to buy stock.
B I never asked my parents for money to buy stock, until now.
C I am never asking my parents for money to buy stock.
4. You are writing the second half of a personal statement, about what you understood. You say:
A This taught me the value of hard work, and I grew as a person.
B I have written down what I am assuming before I decide anything ever since.
C Since then I write down what I am assuming before I decide anything.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Tell one turning point of your own out loud, as if it were the opening of your personal statement. Put the story in the past simple, with at least one detail so vivid that I could see it. Then say what you have understood since, in the present perfect, and make sure that sentence could not belong to anybody else. Before you record, say it twice and keep the version that sounded right.
