Chapter 3: Two Pasts

Four Sentences, Two Pasts

One that is over, and one that is still going

Grammar Discovery

Which Two Are Finished?

"I started the stall in 2024."

"I have run it since then."

"I lost money on one Saturday in March."

"I have counted every box ever since."

The orange ones name a time that is CLOSED: in 2024, on one Saturday in March. English uses the past simple and the door shuts. The red ones use a time that is still OPEN: since, ever since. English uses have plus the participle, and the sentence reaches into today. This is why an essay needs both: the story is closed, and what you understood is not.

JobFormExample
A moment that is overpast simpleI started the stall in 2024.
Something still goinghave / has + participleI have run it ever since.
Words that close the doorago, last, in 2024, yesterday, whenI lost money in March.
Words that keep it opensince, for, ever, never, so far, alreadyI have counted every box since.
Experience at no fixed timehave + participleI have never asked my parents for stock money.
The result that still countshave + participleI have learnt to write the number down.
The first trapnever have with a closed timeNot I have started it last year. Say I started it last year.
The second trapnever past simple with sinceNot I run it since 2024. Say I have run it since 2024.

In one line: a closed time takes the past simple, an open time takes have plus the participle, and the essay needs one of each.

Listening 1 · Somebody Who Reads Them

The Smallest Thing, Told Properly

Nine hundred personal statements a year. Sound first — no text.

1. Which essays does she remember, and which does she not?
Never the ones about the biggest thing that happened to somebody. She remembers the ones about the smallest thing, told properly.
2. What example does she give, and what detail does she still remember?
Somebody wrote about the week a market stall lost money, and she still remembers the number of unsold boxes. That is what a vivid detail does.
3. What does almost everybody get wrong in the second half?
They tell the story and then stop, or they end with a sentence that could belong to anybody. She wants the insight only that person could have had, and she wants to hear they are still carrying it.
Listening 2 · Somebody Who Wrote One

The Fourth Draft Was Half the Length

From a list of everything to one Saturday. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the first draft about, and what one question changed it?
It was about everything he had ever done, and it was terrible. His teacher asked him: which of these do you still think about?
2. What did he find hardest, and why?
Not the writing. Saying what he had understood, in the present, because he kept slipping back into the past while the story itself was in the past.
3. What did he do at the end, and what happened both times?
He read the whole thing out loud to his mother, twice, and both times he caught something his eyes had walked straight over.

Real Document

Personal Statement — First Draft

Personal Statement — Draft 1
Gabriel Reis Medeiros Fernandes · Campinas, Brazil · 214 words

On one Saturday in March I made forty boxes of cake and sold twenty-six. It was the hottest day of the month, and I had decided the number on the Thursday, because I thought a hot Saturday would bring more people. Cake does not keep. The fourteen boxes that came home with me were not stock, they were a loss, and I had made them with my own hands.

I have run that stall at a fortnightly market since 2024. I have never asked my parents for the money to buy the ingredients, and I have kept the accounts myself from the first week. Before that Saturday I set the number by feel. I have not done that again, and not only with cake: I have started writing down what I am assuming before I decide anything, whether it is a route in a flight simulator or how long a piece of homework will take.

What I understood was not that hard work matters. It was that a number you pick before you look is a guess wearing a suit, and that the guess costs somebody something. Usually it costs me. On the Saturday in March it cost me fourteen boxes, and I have counted every one since.

Every fact in this draft came from something you told me across five lessons. Nothing here was invented.
1. Find the anecdote and find the insight. Which paragraph is which, and which tense does each use?
Paragraph 1 is the anecdote, in the past simple: made, sold, had decided. Paragraph 3 is the insight, in the present perfect: I have counted every one since. Paragraph 2 does both, because it links the two.
2. Find the sentence that refuses a cliche. What does it refuse, and what does it say instead?
"What I understood was not that hard work matters." It refuses the cliche Claire named, and replaces it with something only he could have said: a number you pick before you look is a guess wearing a suit.
3. What would you change? Tell me one thing that is not true about you, or one thing that is missing.
There is no right answer here. This is your draft: the chapel on Saturdays, the swimming, the flight plans and Harvard are all still outside it, and one of them may belong in draft two.

Go Deeper · Optional

Two Tenses You Already Used Today

Both of these are in your draft, and nobody taught you either of them.

The past before the past. "I had decided the number on the Thursday." The Saturday is already in the past, and this happened before it. English stacks one step further back with had plus the participle, and you used it correctly in your own draft.

The one that stresses the going-on. "I have been keeping the accounts since the first week." Almost the same as have kept, but it puts the weight on the doing rather than on the result. Use it when the effort is the point.

Your turn. Take one sentence from your draft and write it three ways: past simple, past perfect, and present perfect continuous. Then tell me which one you would keep, and what each one makes the reader feel.
