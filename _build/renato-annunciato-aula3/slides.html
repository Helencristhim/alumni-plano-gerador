Chapter 3: The Code

We Have Grown, or We Grew?

The tense that reports a period

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Year to date we have reached ninety-four percent of the target."

"In March the team hit the quota for the first time."

"Since January our market share has risen two points."

"Last quarter we missed the forecast in Rio de Janeiro."

Two sentences use have. Two do not. What is different about the time in each one?

FormWhen you use itExample
have / has + past participleThe period is still open, or the result matters right nowYear to date we have reached ninety-four percent.
past simpleThe time is finished and named, or both sides know which time you meanIn March the team hit the quota.
Markers that pull haveyear to date · so far · this quarter · since January · over the last six months · already · not yet
Markers that block itlast quarter · in March · in 2025 · yesterday · two weeks ago · when I joined
Ongoing since a dateStarted in the past, still going on: have been + -ingI have been working on the forecast since Monday.
Two trapsNever We have hit the target in March — a named finished month takes the past simple. And never Since January we grew — since always takes the present perfect.

The Structure

Three Sentences, Thirty Seconds

Build the report once and use it every quarter, with the numbers changed.

1 · The headline number

"Year to date we have reached ninety-four percent of the target."

2 · What drove it

"Growth came from the clinics in the interior, where two consultants outperformed their quota. The shortfall is in Rio de Janeiro."

3 · What happens next quarter

"Next quarter I am moving three large accounts into the pipeline, and I expect to close the gap by December."

Number → reason → next. Never the market conditions first.

Chapter 4: Claire Wants the Number

The Quarterly Review

Claire Whitfield · Global Commercial Director

Dialogue

Give Me the Number

C
Renato, good afternoon. We have fifteen minutes. Give me the region, and start with the number.
R
Good afternoon, Claire. Year to date we have reached ninety-four percent of the target.
C
Good. And where did that come from?
R
Growth came from the clinics in the interior. Two consultants outperformed their quota there, and our market share has risen two points since January.
C
Two points is real. So what is not working?
R
Rio de Janeiro. Last month we missed the forecast there, and the whole shortfall sits in that one city. I wanted to say that before you asked.
C
Thank you. Is the revenue recoverable this year, or are we closing the year below?
R
Recoverable. There are three large clinics in the pipeline for the fourth quarter. If two of them close, we hit the target in December.
C
That is the clearest report I have had today. Send me the three clinics by name and I will keep the target where it is.
Comprehension

About Claire

1. What does she ask him to start with?
The number — she has fifteen minutes and wants the region, number first.
2. How does she react to the two points of market share?
She says two points is real, and immediately asks what is not working.
3. What does she want to know about the revenue?
Whether it is recoverable this year, or whether they are closing the year below target.
4. What does she decide at the end, and why?
She keeps the target where it is, because it was the clearest report she had had that day. She asks for the three clinics by name.
Listening

What a Director Wants to Hear First

Forty reviews a quarter. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he say separates a good report from a bad one?
Not the result. A terrible number can still come with a very good report.
2. What does he want first, and what does he not want?
The number first, then the reason. Not the market, the weather, or the competitor.
3. What happens when somebody starts with ten minutes of context?
He already knows what is coming, and the context turns into an apology.
4. What does he say about a finished quarter and an open year?
A finished month takes the past. A year that is still moving needs language that shows it. Managers who mix them make him ask the same question three times, and then he stops trusting the numbers.
Listening 2

The Number She Hid for Six Weeks

Same shortfall, two very different conversations. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. By how much did she miss the quota, and how long did she wait?
Nineteen percent, and she waited six weeks.
2. Why did she wait? Was it deliberate?
No. She kept expecting the pipeline to close and the shortfall to disappear before anybody had to hear about it.
3. What did her director actually care about?
Not the nineteen percent — that he had spent six weeks telling his own boss the region was fine.
4. What does she do now, the moment she sees a shortfall coming?
She says it, with a date and a plan: what they have done so far, what did not work last month, and what she is changing.

"Last quarter we missed the quota." (last quarter is a closed period)
