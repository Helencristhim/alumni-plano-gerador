Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A plateau -- a period when you stop improving even though you keep practising
"Month four is a plateau, and almost everybody quits there."
To keep something up -- to continue doing something regularly, without stopping
"Two hours a week is not much. Keeping it up for a year is everything."
A benchmark -- a standard you measure your progress against
"Without a benchmark, every hard week feels like going backwards."
Incremental -- happening in small steps rather than all at once
"The change is incremental, which is exactly why you cannot feel it."
To fall behind -- to make less progress than you should, or than others do
"I fell behind for eight years and I did not notice until I needed it."
To stick with something -- to continue with something difficult instead of giving up
"The people who stick with it are not the ones who found it easy."
Rusty -- out of practice, so not as good as you used to be
"My Spanish is rusty rather than bad, and those are different problems."
A milestone -- an important point in a longer process
"Lesson ten is a milestone. It is not the end of anything."
To level off -- to stop increasing and stay at the same level
"Everybody improves fast at first, and then it levels off."
To push yourself -- to make yourself do something harder than is comfortable
"If the lesson is comfortable, you are not pushing yourself."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nothing is new in this lesson. Six structures came in over the last six lessons, one at a time, each with a whole hour to itself. Today they arrive together, mixed and unlabelled, which is the only condition that resembles a real conversation. In a meeting nobody announces that the next sentence needs a conditional. You have about half a second to choose, and choosing is the skill this lesson measures.

Here they are in one paragraph. If the file is not closed by Thursday, we will lose the window — a real condition with a real consequence, and never will after if. If I had more time, I would rewrite the whole process — not real, so the verb steps back and would stays out of the if half. The review has been completed — the tense lives in be, and nobody is named because nobody needs to be.

And the other three. I did not use to sleep the night before those calls — no D after did, and this is about a habit that ended. She asked me to escalate it, and he said he would send the numbers — the verb steps back one, tell takes a person and say does not. Ana, who liaises with the auditors, is covering for him — two commas, so this only adds a fact about somebody already named.

The four errors that came back most often across the six lessons are worth one last look, because they are the ones that survive translation: will after if, would on the if side of a second conditional, said me instead of told me, and the echo pronoun in a relative clause. None of them stops anybody from understanding you. All four are what separates English that works from English that sounds finished, and that distinction is the entire point of the last ten lessons.

1. According to the text, what makes today different from the last six lessons?
A The structures are harder than they were before.
B The structures arrive mixed and unlabelled, so choosing between them is the skill being measured.
C There is no grammar in this lesson at all.
2. Why does the text say would stays out of the if half?
A Because the situation is real and has a deadline.
B Because the situation is not real, so the verb steps back and would belongs only in the other half.
C Because would can never be used with if in any sentence.
3. What does the text say about the four recurring errors?
A They make the speaker impossible to understand.
B They only appear in writing, never in speech.
C They do not block understanding, but they separate English that works from English that sounds finished.

GRAMMAR

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the sentence that works.

A deadline is real and the consequence is real. You say:
A "If the file will not be closed by Thursday, we lose the window."
B "If the file is not closed by Thursday, we will lose the window."
C "If the file would not be closed by Thursday, we would lose the window."
You are giving advice about something that is not going to happen. You say:
A "If I were you, I would raise it before the meeting."
B "If I would be you, I raise it before the meeting."
C "If I am you, I will raise it before the meeting."
You are reporting what a colleague promised in a meeting. You say:
A "He said me that he will send the numbers on Friday."
B "He said he would send the numbers on Friday."
C "He told that he would send the numbers on Friday."
You want to identify one colleague out of several. You say:
A "The colleague, who handles the monthly filings, is the one you want."
B "The colleague who handles the monthly filings is the one you want."
C "The colleague who I spoke to him handles the monthly filings."
