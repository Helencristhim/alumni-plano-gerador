What Will Happen Next? -- Predictions and Plans

Choose between the three everyday futures: will for a prediction or a decision made right now, going to for evidence you can point at or an intention you already had, and the present continuous for an arrangement with a time and another person. Key words: forecast, hype, speculation, leak, plausible, far-fetched, teaser, fan theory, sequel, reboot, odds, in the works.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. You already read most of these every week, in comments and headlines.

Forecast -- a statement about what is likely to happen, based on data
"Every forecast said the game was coming in March, and every one was wrong."
Hype -- loud excitement built up around something before it arrives
"The hype is already too big to risk a bad launch."
Speculation -- talk about what might be true, with nothing confirmed
"Everything after that frame is pure speculation."
Leak -- information published before the company wanted it public
"The leak came from a job listing, not from the studio."
Plausible -- reasonable enough to be believed, even without proof
"A prequel is plausible. Time travel is not."
Far-fetched -- so unlikely that it is hard to take seriously
"That theory is far-fetched, and half the comment section believes it."
Teaser -- a very short video released to build excitement before the trailer
"The teaser is eighty seconds long and it shows almost nothing."
Fan theory -- an explanation invented by viewers, not confirmed by the writers
"That is not a fan theory. That is what the background tells you."
Sequel -- a film or game that continues the story of an earlier one
"I am going to say the sequel is set before the first game, not after it."
Reboot -- a new version of an old series, started again from the beginning
"Nobody asked for a reboot, and they are making one anyway."
Odds -- how likely something is, compared with the alternative
"The odds are that they will delay it at least once."
In the works -- already being made, but not announced or finished
"A second team has something in the works with the same engine."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the post and answer the questions. Watch how the writer changes form every time the reason changes.

The announcement dropped last night, so here is the actual state of things. The teaser is eighty seconds long and shows almost nothing, which is deliberate. Based on the two frames at the end, it is going to be a prequel, not a continuation. That is not a fan theory; it is what the architecture in the background tells you, and you can go and look at it yourself.

Now for the things that are still speculation. The studio has not confirmed a release window, so anybody telling you it is coming in March is guessing. My own prediction is that they will delay it at least once, because they always do and because the hype is already too big to risk a bad launch. That is an opinion, and I am labelling it as one.

What I do know is what is in the works, because it was in the job listings: a second team is building something with the same engine. That came out as a leak, not as an announcement.

As for me, I am not staying up for the next stream. I am playing football on Saturday morning and my brother is driving me at seven. I will watch the recording on Sunday like a normal person, and I will probably be the last one in the group chat to find out.

1. Why does the writer use "it is going to be a prequel" instead of "it will be a prequel"?
A Because he can point at evidence — the architecture in the last two frames.
B Because he is more certain than he would be with "will".
C Because "will" cannot be used for films and games.
2. Why is "they will delay it" the right form in the second paragraph?
A Because a studio decision is always expressed with "will".
B Because it is a prediction with nothing behind it except his opinion, and he says so.
C Because the delay has already been arranged with a date.
3. Why is "I am playing football on Saturday morning" in the present continuous?
A Because it is an arrangement: there is a time, and another person is involved.
B Because it is happening at the moment of writing.
C Because football is a repeated habit.
4. What is the difference between what he calls evidence and what he calls speculation?
A Evidence comes from the studio and speculation comes from fans.
B Evidence is what most people believe and speculation is what only he believes.
C Evidence is something you can go and look at yourself; speculation is a guess with nothing to point at.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Will, Going To, Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

The form is easy. Choosing is the lesson. Ask where the future you are describing actually lives.

FormUse it whenExample
will + bare verbYou are predicting or deciding right now, with only your opinion behind it."I think they will delay it." / "Fine, I will watch the recording."
going to (evidence)You can point at the reason: a teaser, a leak, the clouds."Look at that frame. It is going to be a prequel."
going to (intention)The plan was already in your head before this conversation started."I am going to finish the first game again."
present continuousIt is an arrangement: fixed time, usually another person involved."I am playing football on Saturday." / "We are watching it at seven."
The testIs the plan only in your head, or already in your calendar? In your head is "going to". In the calendar is the present continuous.
The tie-breakerWhen two forms still look possible, choose the present continuous. It is the one you use least and the one that sounds most natural.
The trapAfter when, if and as soon as, English drops the future: "as soon as it comes out", never "as soon as it will come out".
