Chapter 3: What They Said

Every Verb Takes One Step Back

And the question stops being a question

Grammar Discovery

Said, and Repeated

“We are interested.”

He said they were interested.

“We have reviewed the proposal.”

She said they had reviewed the proposal.

“We will confirm on Friday.”

He told me they would confirm on Friday.

“Can you send the numbers?”

She asked whether we could send the numbers.

Every green verb took one step back into the red one. In the last pair something else happened too: the question stopped being a question and got a whether.

They saidYou reportExample
presentpastare → were interested
present perfect / pastpast perfecthave reviewed → had reviewed
will / can / maywould / could / mightwill confirm → would confirm
a yes-or-no questionasked if / whether, normal orderCan you send it? → asked whether we could send it
a what / when questionkeep the word, drop the inversionWhat is the price? → asked what the price was
say vs telltell somebody · say somethinghe told me · he said that — never he said me
time and place move tootomorrow → the next dayhere → there · this → that

In one line: step every verb back, and turn the question into a statement. If what they said is still true today, keeping the present is also correct — but the step back is never wrong.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Twenty Minutes After

One debrief, two recordings, one email

Dialogue

The Debrief, Ten Minutes After the Call

M
So how did it go? Did they say yes?
A
Not exactly. He said they were interested, but that they could not commit before they had seen the audit report.
M
So they approved it in principle.
A
That is close, and I would put it slightly differently. Interested is not approved. I do not want to put words in his mouth: the caveat was the whole sentence.
M
Fair. Did he say when the audit report lands?
A
He told me it would be ready by the end of the month, and he asked whether we could send the migration plan before that.
M
And what is the takeaway for the board?
A
That the takeaway is a date, not a decision. I will send a recap tonight and I will read the sentence back to him tomorrow, so nobody has to paraphrase me either.
Comprehension

About the Debrief

1. What does Marco assume after the first answer, and how does Andreia handle it?
He assumes they approved it in principle. She says that is close and she would put it slightly differently — interested is not approved.
2. What two things does Marco ask about after that?
When the audit report lands, and what the takeaway is for the board.
3. What are the two things Andreia says she will do, and why does the second one matter?
Send a recap tonight, and read the sentence back to him tomorrow. The second one matters because it stops anybody — including him — from paraphrasing her later.

Not a Lie. Slightly Wrong.

The most expensive thing he has done in this job. Sound first — no text.

1. What did the client actually say, and what did he tell his own team?
The client said they were comfortable with the timeline. He told his team they had approved the timeline.
2. Why does he say those are not the same sentence?
Comfortable is a feeling; approved is a decision.
3. What does he do now, and how long does it take?
He writes the sentence down while they are still saying it, in their words, and reads it back to them before repeating it to anybody. He says it takes eleven seconds.
Listening 2 · A Commercial Director

So What You Are Saying Is

What to do when somebody summarises you and gets it slightly wrong. Sound first — no text.

1. What happens in every second meeting, and why does she say it is usually not malicious?
Somebody says "so what you are saying is" and then says something you did not say. She says they are compressing, and the compression drops the caveat.
2. Why does it matter if you let it go once?
Because from that moment the room is working with their version, not yours, and it becomes the record.
3. What does she say when she is the one summarising somebody else, and what happens?
She says "tell me if I have got this wrong". About a third of the time they do tell her, and she says that third is where the whole deal lives.
Real Document

The Email You Send the Same Night

Subject: Recap — call of 14 October
from: Andreia Naim Finianos  ·  to: Mr Chen, cc: internal team

Thank you for your time this morning. So that we are all working from the same page, here is what I understood:

• You said your team were interested in the pilot, and that you could not commit before you had seen the audit report.

• You told me the report would be ready by the end of the month.

• You asked whether we could send the migration plan before then. We can, and I will send it on Friday.

• We agreed that pricing would only be discussed after the pilot scope is fixed.

If I have got any of this wrong, please correct me before Friday.

1. Four of these lines report somebody else and one does not. Which one is different, and what is it doing?
The last line. It is not reporting anything — it is inviting a correction before the email becomes the record.
2. Why does the second bullet say "could not commit" rather than "cannot commit"?
Because it is reporting what he said in the meeting, so the verb steps back one: cannot becomes could not.
3. What would be lost if the first bullet just said "your team approved the pilot"?
The caveat, which was the whole sentence. Interested is not approved, and the audit report condition would disappear from the record.

The Correction

“So What You Are Saying Is...”

1. You said: "We are interested, but not before the audit." → They repeat: "So you approved it."
"That is close, and I would put it slightly differently. We said we were interested. The audit comes first."
2. You said: "It might take twelve weeks." → They repeat: "So it will be ready in twelve weeks."
"Not quite. I said it might take twelve weeks. I would rather not commit to a date before the scope is fixed."
3. You said: "We cannot move the price." → They repeat: "So there is no flexibility at all."
"On the price, no. On the timeline there is, and I would rather we spent the time there."
4. You said: "Their team had never used this kind of system." → They repeat: "So their team is not ready."
"I would not go that far. I said they had not used one before, which is a training question, not a capability one."

Every one of these is somebody being helpful and dropping the caveat. Correct all four out loud, and keep the room warm while you do it.

Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

A Story Where Somebody Else Talks

Scenario: At dinner, tell a story in which somebody else said something that surprised you — family, a friend, a taxi driver, anybody. Report at least three things they said and one thing they asked you. Nothing at stake, and that is the point: this is where the verb has to move without you thinking about it.

Keywords:

she said she was... he told me he had... and then she asked me whether... what would you have said?
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Three Minutes, No Notes

Scenario: Dictate the recap email out loud, as if you were sending it the same night to the partner with your own team copied in. Report what they said, what they asked, what was agreed and what was explicitly not agreed — and finish with the sentence that invites them to correct you before Friday.

No keywords, no notes, three minutes.
