What Did They Actually Say? -- Reporting Without Misquoting

Repeating what somebody said to you, in English, without changing it. Key words: a recap, to relay, to paraphrase, a takeaway, to misquote, verbatim, a caveat, to clarify, a stakeholder, to debrief, to water something down, to put words in somebody's mouth. Structure: reported speech and backshift.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A recap -- a short summary of what was said, given afterwards to people who need it
"I will send a recap tonight so nobody has to remember it."
To relay -- to pass a message from one person to another without changing it
"I relayed exactly what he said, including the part I did not like."
To paraphrase -- to say what somebody said, in your own words, keeping the meaning
"Let me paraphrase and you tell me if I have got it wrong."
A takeaway -- the one thing people should remember from a meeting
"The takeaway was that the audit report comes before any commitment."
To misquote -- to report somebody's words wrongly, changing what they meant
"I would rather ask twice than misquote him to my own board."
Verbatim -- using exactly the words that were said, with nothing changed
"I wrote his sentence down verbatim while he was still saying it."
A caveat -- a warning that limits how far a yes can be taken
"He said yes, with one caveat, and the caveat is the whole sentence."
To clarify -- to make something clearer when it could be understood in two ways
"Can I clarify one thing before I take this back to my team?"
A stakeholder -- anybody whose decision or opinion affects the project
"The person who was not in the room is usually the stakeholder that matters."
To debrief -- to go through what happened right after a meeting, with your own team
"Give me ten minutes to debrief before anybody writes anything down."
To water something down -- to repeat a message in a weaker form than it was said
"He did not say maybe. Do not water it down when you repeat it."
To put words in somebody's mouth -- to say that somebody said something they did not say
"That is close, and I would put it slightly differently."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The most expensive mistake in this job is not a lie. It is a recap that is slightly wrong. A client said they were comfortable with the timeline, and what got relayed to the delivery team was that they had approved it. Comfortable is a feeling. Approved is a decision. Six weeks later the work had been built to a date nobody had signed off, and the client was completely right.

So the discipline is simple. Write the sentence down verbatim while the person is still saying it. Then, when you debrief your own team, keep the shape: he said they were interested; he told me the report would be ready by the end of the month; he asked whether we could send the migration plan first. Every verb steps back one, and the question stops being a question. And whatever you do, keep the caveat — the limit on the yes is usually the only part that matters.

The other half of the skill is what to do when somebody paraphrases you and gets it wrong. It is rarely malicious: they are compressing, and the compression drops the caveat or waters the message down. But if it goes unchallenged once it becomes the record, and every stakeholder downstream works from their version. The sentence that fixes it is short and it costs nothing: that is close, and I would put it slightly differently. Then say your version. Nobody has ever taken offence, and it is a great deal cheaper than being misquoted in a document you did not write.

1. "He said they were interested." The client actually said "we are interested." Why is it were?
A Because they are no longer interested.
B Because when you report what somebody said, every verb steps back one: are becomes were.
C Because they is plural.
2. "He asked whether we could send the migration plan first." What happened to the original question?
A It stopped being a question: whether was added, the word order became normal, and can became could.
B Nothing changed except the tense.
C The question mark was simply removed.
3. According to the text, why does it matter to correct a wrong summary the first time?
A Because the person is usually being dishonest.
B Because if it goes unchallenged once it becomes the record, and everybody downstream works from their version.
C Because correcting people establishes authority in a meeting.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech

GRAMMAR

Step every verb back one, and turn the question into a statement.

They saidYou reportExample
presentpastare → were interested
present perfect / pastpast perfecthave reviewed → had reviewed
will / can / maywould / could / mightwill confirm → would confirm
a yes-or-no questionasked if / whether, normal orderasked whether we could send it
a what / when questionkeep the word, drop the inversionasked what the price was
say vs telltell somebody · say somethinghe told me — never he said me
time and place move tootomorrow → the next dayhere → there · this → that

Never: He said me that... · She asked what is the price · He said they will confirm (about a meeting that is over) · They approved it (when what they said was: we are comfortable with it)

Stage 2: Put the Recap in Order

Order

You are dictating the recap email the same night. Put it in a logical order.

?"Thank you for your time this morning. Here is what I understood."
?"You said your team were interested in the pilot."
?"You said you could not commit before you had seen the audit report."
?"You asked whether we could send the migration plan before then."
?"We can, and I will send it on Friday."
?"If I have got any of this wrong, please correct me before Friday."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The partner said "we are interested". You report it to your team. You say:
A "He said me they are interested."
B "He said they were interested."
C "He told they were interested."
He asked "what is the price?". You report the question. You say:
A "He asked what is the price."
B "He asked what the price was."
C "He asked me what was the price?"
The client said "we are comfortable with the timeline". Your colleague says "so they approved it". You say:
A "That is close, and I would put it slightly differently. They said they were comfortable with it."
B "Yes, exactly."
C "No, you are wrong, that is not what happened at all."
You are about to repeat something important to your board. Before you do, you say to the partner:
A Nothing — you took good notes.
B "Let me read that back to you before I take it to my team."
C "I hope I understood you."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Dictate the recap email out loud, as if you were sending it the same night to a partner with your own team copied in. Report what they said, what they asked, what was agreed and what was explicitly not agreed, and finish with the sentence that invites them to correct you. Step every verb back one, keep every caveat, and never report anything stronger than it was said.
