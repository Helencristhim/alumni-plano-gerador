Arrivals — The First Two Hours in a Country You Do Not Know

A missed connection, a closed border and the two hours after landing that decide the whole trip. Key expressions: a layover, to clear immigration, to be bumped off a flight, a red-eye, jet lag, a transit visa, onward travel, to be stranded, a shuttle, to haggle, a tourist trap, off the beaten track, to wing it, to get your bearings, a stone's throw from, to turn in, a curfew, to take something in your stride. Structure: participle clauses — having cleared, arriving, given, faced with, with the border closed.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

A layover -- a wait between two flights, long enough to be a problem
"A nine-hour layover is not a break, it is an unpaid shift."
To clear immigration -- to pass through passport control successfully
"It took ninety minutes to clear immigration and four to lose the bag."
To be bumped off a flight -- to be removed from a flight you had booked, because it was oversold
"Having been bumped off the evening flight, he took the red-eye."
A red-eye -- an overnight flight that arrives early in the morning
"The red-eye is cheaper and you pay for it the following day."
Jet lag -- the tiredness that comes from crossing several time zones
"Jet lag going east is worse, and everybody who travels knows it."
A transit visa -- a permit needed only to pass through a country, not to stay
"Nobody mentions the transit visa until you are at the desk."
Onward travel -- the rest of the journey after the point you have reached
"They want proof of onward travel before they will let you in."
To be stranded -- to be stuck somewhere with no way of continuing
"Four hundred passengers were stranded and the desk had two people."
A shuttle -- a vehicle that runs a short route repeatedly, usually to a hotel or terminal
"The shuttle runs every twenty minutes and stops at midnight."
To haggle -- to argue about a price until both sides accept one
"In some countries not haggling is the rude thing to do."
A tourist trap -- a place that exists mainly to take money from visitors
"The square is a tourist trap and it is still worth twenty minutes."
Off the beaten track -- away from the places most visitors go
"Off the beaten track is where you need the language, not in the hotel."
To wing it -- to improvise, without a plan or preparation
"I book the first night and wing the rest, which works about half the time."
To get your bearings -- to work out where you are and which way to go
"Give yourself an hour to get your bearings before you decide anything."
A stone's throw from -- very close to something, within an easy walk
"The hotel is a stone's throw from the station and nobody tells you which exit."
To turn in -- to go to bed, especially earlier than usual
"I turned in at nine and slept eleven hours, which is the correct response to a red-eye."
A curfew -- a rule requiring people to be indoors after a certain hour
"There was a curfew that week and the taxi driver mentioned it first."
To take in your stride -- to deal with something difficult calmly, without drama
"She took a cancelled flight and a closed border in her stride."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every journey has two hours that decide how the rest of it feels. Having landed, you queue. Not knowing the layout, you follow the person ahead of you, who does not know it either. Clearing immigration takes anything between four minutes and two hours, and there is no way to know in advance which of the two it will be.

Given a delay of any size, your onward travel stops being a plan and becomes a question. Faced with a closed transfer desk, most passengers do the worst possible thing: they begin to optimise. At two in the morning, having slept for three hours on a red-eye, with jet lag arriving on schedule, they compare taxi fares and haggle over twenty euros.

The experienced traveller does the opposite, and it looks like extravagance. Being stranded at that hour, they take the first safe option at any price, find a bed, and turn in. Every real decision waits until the morning, having got their bearings and eaten something. With the border closed and no information available, a hotel for two nights costs less than a bad decision taken at three.

One more thing, and it is why this is a language lesson rather than a travel column. In those two hours you will speak to five or six people: a shuttle driver, somebody at a desk, a stranger who says the hotel is a stone's throw away and does not mention which exit. Several of them will be speaking English as a second language, exactly as you are. The travellers who ask a clear question, take the answer in their stride and ask a second one end up somewhere different from those who go quiet and point — and it has almost nothing to do with how much vocabulary they have.

1. "Having landed, you queue." What does this form tell us?
A That the landing is finished before the queueing begins.
B That the two things happen at the same time.
C That the landing is still in progress.
2. "Not knowing the layout, you follow the person ahead." What is the -ing form replacing?
A After you had known
B Because you do not know
C Although you know
3. "Given a delay of any size…" What kind of word is given behaving like here?
A A main verb.
B A preposition, introducing the circumstance.
C A question word.
4. Why does the text say the experienced traveller's choice "looks like extravagance"?
A Because they pay more in order to stop making decisions while exhausted.
B Because they always stay in expensive hotels.
C Because they refuse to haggle anywhere.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Participle Clauses — Two Events, One Sentence

GRAMMAR

A participle clause joins two events without a conjunction and without repeating the subject. It is what makes written English compact, and it is one of the clearest signals of a fluent speaker in conversation.

FormWhat it doesExample
Having + past participleThe first action is complete before the second starts. Replaces after he had…. Both halves must share a subject."Having cleared immigration, he looked for the shuttle."
-ing (present participle)Simultaneous actions, or the first as the reason for the second. Replaces as, while, because. The negative goes in front: not knowing."Arriving at midnight, you see nothing."
"Not knowing the city, I took a taxi."
Given · Faced with
Left · Seen from
Compared with		Past-participle openings with a passive sense. Given is the most common and takes NO preposition after it."Given the delay…"
"Faced with a closed desk…"
With + noun + participleAdds a circumstance that has its own subject — the legitimate way out of the same-subject rule."With the border closed, travel was impossible."
"With everybody arguing, nothing moved."
The dangling participleIf the participle does not belong to the subject of the main clause, the sentence says something absurd. English speakers hear this instantly, even when they cannot name it.NOT "Having cleared immigration, the shuttle was waiting."

The test that catches every error: after writing the participle, ask who is doing this? and check that the answer is the subject of the main clause. If it is not, either change the main clause or use with + noun + participle, which is allowed its own subject on purpose.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are in a terminal at two in the morning. Choose the sentence that is both correct and useful.

You want to say the first action finished before the second:
A "Having cleared immigration, the shuttle was waiting."
B "Having cleared immigration, he found the shuttle waiting."
C "Having clear immigration, he found the shuttle."
You want to give the reason with an -ing form:
A "Not knowing the city, I took a taxi."
B "Knowing not the city, I took a taxi."
C "Don't knowing the city, I took a taxi."
You want to open with the circumstance:
A "Given to the delay, the connection failed."
B "Given of the delay, the connection failed."
C "Given the delay, the connection failed."
You are at the desk and the queue is not moving. The most useful thing to say is:
A "When will the flight go?"
B "Can you rebook me tonight, on any airline, to anywhere within two hundred kilometres?"
C "This is completely unacceptable."
You want a circumstance with its own subject:
A "With the border closed, travel was impossible."
B "Closing the border, travel was impossible."
C "Having closed the border, travel was impossible."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

In about ninety seconds, narrate it from the aircraft door to the moment you had somewhere to sleep, using at least four participle clauses. Then finish with one sentence about what you would decide differently, and one about what you would refuse to decide at all at that hour.
