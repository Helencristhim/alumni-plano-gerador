Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

More Than It Was Good -- The Second Sentence

Somebody asks how it was, you say it was good, and the conversation dies right there. Key words: a layover, to get around, a local, crowded, a highlight, worth it, to end up, to hang out, to be into, a heads-up. Structure: reason and result with because, so and that is why.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A layover -- the time you wait in an airport between two flights
"We had a four-hour layover in Panama."
To get around -- to move from place to place inside a city
"We get around by bus, because parking is impossible."
A local -- a person who lives in the place you are visiting
"Ask a local, because the guidebook is two years old."
Crowded -- full of people, with very little space
"The beach is crowded on Sunday, so we go on Tuesday."
A highlight -- the best part of a trip or of an experience
"The highlight was the boat, and my mother agrees."
Worth it -- good enough to pay for the money, the time or the trouble
"The ticket was expensive, but it was worth it."
To end up -- to finish somewhere, or with something, that you did not plan
"We wanted a coffee, and we ended up staying two hours."
To hang out -- to spend relaxed time with somebody, with no plan
"We hang out by the pool in the morning, and that is enough."
To be into something -- to like something a lot and follow it closely
"I am into books, so I always travel with two of them."
A heads-up -- a short warning that gives you time to get ready
"Just a heads-up: the last bus leaves at nine."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody you have just met asks how the weekend was, and you answer: "It was good." The sentence is correct, it is polite, and it kills the conversation, because the other person has nothing to hold on to. The repair is not a bigger vocabulary. The repair is one more sentence, and English gives you three little words to open it.

The first one is because, and it opens the reason: "It was good, because we finally got out of the city." After because comes a full sentence, with a subject and a verb. There is a shorter cousin, because of, and it is the one Brazilians get wrong: after because of comes a noun and nothing else. "We arrived at midnight because of the layover" is right. "Because of the flight was late" is not.

The second word is so, and it opens the result: "The beach was crowded, so we went back on Tuesday." Notice the order. The reason comes first, and the consequence comes after so. The third is that is why, and it does the same job with more weight: "My mother does not drive, and that is why we get around by bus." Never say for that here. It does not exist in English, and it is a direct translation from Portuguese.

One last thing. You can put the reason first: "Because it was crowded, we left early." And when the question is simply "Why?", the whole answer is a because sentence: "Why October?" — "Because my mother is free that week." Two sentences instead of one, and the person in front of you suddenly has somewhere to go.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reason and Result

GRAMMAR

Because opens a reason. So opens a result. Because of takes a noun and stops there.

JobFormExample
Give the reasonbecause + subject + verbIt was good, because we got out of the city.
Give the reason in two wordsbecause of + noun onlyWe arrived late because of the layover.
Give the resultreason, so + resultThe beach was crowded, so we left.
Point at the resultand that is why + resultShe does not drive, and that is why we take the bus.
Answer a direct Why?Because + full sentence"Why October?" — "Because my mother is free then."
The order can flipBecause + reason, + resultBecause it was crowded, we left early.
In speechthat is why becomes that’s whyAnd that’s why we take the bus.

Never: because of the beach was crowded · for that we travel in October · I liked because is quiet · the bus was late, so we take a taxi

Quiz
