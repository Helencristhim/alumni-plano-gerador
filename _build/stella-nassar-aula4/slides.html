Tell or Show

The Same Nerves, Two Sentences

TELLING

"I was extremely nervous before my first portfolio review."

SHOWING

"By the time my name was called, my hands had left two damp prints on the cover of my portfolio."

The first sentence reports a feeling. The second one lets the reader feel it, and trusts the reader to do the work.

Chapter 4: The Style Code

The Sentence That Lifts

Fronting and inversion: word order as emphasis

Discover the Style

What Happened to the Word Order?

"Never had I stood so still in front of anything."

"Rarely does a first sentence survive the first draft."

"Only later did I understand what the painting had asked of me."

"So quiet was the gallery that I could hear my own hesitation."

Rewrite each sentence in normal order. What exactly changes in the verb, and why does the original sound more deliberate?

FormWhat it doesExample
negative adverbial + auxiliary + subjectlifts a moment by opening with its rarityNever had I stood so still.
restrictive adverbial + auxiliary + subjectdelays the main clause until the condition is clearOnly later did I understand. Not until the last room did I notice.
So / Such + adjective or noun + verb + subjectintensifies a quality and prepares a resultSo quiet was the gallery that...
fronting without inversionbrings a detail or place forward for emphasisInto that silence walked my teacher. Stranger still was the label.
The C2 trapoveruse. One inversion in an essay sounds deliberate; three sound like a costume. And never invert a positive adverb: "Often did I visit" is archaic, not elegant.

Chapter 5: Voices on the Draft

Openings, Out Loud

A counselor review, a novelist and a writing workshop

Dialogue

Mr. Brooks Reads the Opening

D
I read your new opening twice. Do you want the good news or the useful news first?
S
The useful news. The good news I can probably survive without.
D
Your first paragraph explains why museums matter to young people. It is well written, and I have read it a thousand times.
S
I know. It sounded tentative even while I was typing it.
D
Now look at your third paragraph. You describe a guard who kept moving you back from a painting, and you kept inching forward.
S
That was real. He must have warned me four times.
D
That is your first sentence. Start there, in medias res, with the guard and the line on the floor.
S
So I cut two paragraphs? That feels like losing half the essay.
D
You lose two paragraphs anyone could write and keep the one only you could. Rarely does a cut feel good, but this one will.
S
Then I will open with the guard, and let the reader figure out why I kept stepping over the line.
Comprehension

About Mr. Brooks

1. What does Mr. Brooks say about the first paragraph of the opening?
It explains why museums matter to young people and is well written, but he has read it a thousand times.
2. Which part of the draft does he want Stella to use as her first sentence?
The scene from the third paragraph, with the guard who kept moving her back from the painting and the line on the floor.
3. How does he answer Stella when she says the cut feels like losing half the essay?
She loses two paragraphs anyone could write and keeps the one only she could, and he says this cut will feel good.
Listening 1

Starting in the Middle

A novelist talks about beginnings on a writing podcast. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. What does the novelist say most beginning writers do in their first pages, and why?
They explain who the characters are and how they got there, because they are afraid the reader will be lost.
2. According to her, what does starting in medias res require from the writer?
Trusting the reader: the reader can live with a little confusion if something is clearly happening.
3. What warning does she give about in medias res openings?
The action must matter. Starting with a car chase that has nothing to do with the story is a trick, and readers notice.
Listening 2

Show, Don't Tell

The director of a university writing center leads a workshop. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. What example does the speaker use to contrast telling and showing?
Telling is "I was nervous"; showing is "My hands left damp prints on the portfolio."
2. Why does he say showing is more persuasive in an application essay?
Because the reader draws the conclusion alone, and people believe conclusions they reach themselves.
3. What limit does he put on the rule?
Not every sentence should show. Reflection has to tell the reader what the scene meant, or the essay becomes a list of pretty moments.
