Chapter 3: The Code

Question Tags and Short Responses

The two-word machinery that keeps a conversation moving

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"You've been to Lisbon, haven't you?"

"You're not from here originally, are you?"

"I'm really into food." — "So am I."

"I lived in Liverpool for a year." — "Did you? What was that like?"

None of the orange parts is really asking for information. So tell me: what are they doing, and where do they get their verb from?

FormUseExample
Positive sentence → negative taginvite agreement, warmly"That was a hidden gem, wasn't it?"
Negative sentence → positive tagcheck something you half-know"You're not from here, are you?"
The tag copies the auxiliarybe · have · do · the modal"You can cook, can't you?"
So + auxiliary + I · Neither + auxiliary + Iagree in one beat"I'm into food." — "So am I."
Echo: auxiliary + you?show interest, hand the turn back"Did you? What was that like?"

A tag is not a question — it is an invitation. A short response is not an answer — it is a pass back. Take the auxiliary out of the sentence in front of you and both tools build themselves: have → haven't you, am → so am I, did → did you?

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"You like Italian food, no?" (the Portuguese tag, translated)
"You like Italian food, don't you?"
"You are not from here, aren't you?" (negative sentence, negative tag)
"You're not from here, are you?"
"I do not eat much meat too." (agreeing with a negative using too)
"Neither do I."

The rule is mechanical, so it is learnable in one evening: the tag flips the sentence, and it borrows the sentence's own auxiliary. Positive takes negative, negative takes positive — and too never agrees with a negative; neither does.
