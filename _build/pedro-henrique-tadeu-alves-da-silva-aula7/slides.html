If the Cue Runs Long

In a recorded session a mistake is a delay. Live, a mistake is a decision, and you have about four seconds to make it.

Chapter 1: First Words

Three Rules of Your Room

Say them again, faster. Use at least three of these: etiquette, a deadline, on call, overtime, a heads-up, to flag, mandatory, off limits, a workaround.

Then today’s question: what is the worst thing that has ever gone wrong during a live show you worked on?

Today’s Goal

Four Seconds, Three Moves

1. The Words

a fallback, to go live, a glitch, to drop out, a backup, to patch, redundancy...

2. One Choice

what always happens, and what happens only if

3. Your Plan

the likely problem, the first four seconds, and who has to know

Preparation is not about preventing problems, because you cannot. It is about deciding, in a quiet room three days earlier, what you will do when each of them happens.

One Question

What Is the Most Likely Thing to Go Wrong Tonight?

Not the dramatic one. The boring, ordinary one that actually happens.

Then the second question: what exactly would you do in the first four seconds, and who would you tell before you did it?

Chapter 3: The Code

What Always Happens, and What Happens Tonight

Two kinds of if, and only one of them takes will

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"If you unplug it, the signal stops."

"If the track drops out tonight, I will patch the backup."

"When a cue runs long, the picture cuts first."

"If she starts in the wrong key, we will let it run."

The red half is the same shape in all four. Look at the blue half: twice it is a plain verb, twice it has will, and the difference is not grammar — it is whether the sentence is about tonight or about always.

JobFormExample
Always true (a law of the room)If / When + present, presentIf you unplug it, the signal stops. When a cue runs long, the picture cuts.
One specific future situationIf + present, will + base verbIf the track drops out, I will patch the backup.
The order can be reversedno comma when if comes secondI will patch the backup if the track drops out.
If or whenif = maybe · when = certainIf it rains, we move inside. When we go live, the door closes.
Other endings, same rulecan / might / must / imperativeIf it drops out, call me. If it drops out, we might lose eight bars.
Unless= if notWe will not abort unless the key is wrong.
The trapnever will in the if halfNot if the track will drop out. Say if the track drops out.

In one line: the if half always stays in the present; only the other half can look at the future.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Contingency Call, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one sheet

Dialogue

Three Days Before, In a Quiet Room

N
Right. Walk me through what happens if it all goes wrong on Sunday. Start with the music.
P
If the backing track drops out, I patch the backup from the second machine. That takes about four seconds, and the band keeps playing over it.
N
Four seconds of silence on air is quite a lot. Who talks?
P
Nobody talks. When the music stops, the host looks at the camera and waits — that is the rule, and it never changes. If she improvises, the downtime sounds twice as long.
N
Good. And if the singer comes in in the wrong key? That happened in Manchester and nobody had decided anything.
P
If it is wrong in the first four bars, we abort and go again. After that we let it run, because stopping costs more than the mistake. We do not troubleshoot on air.
N
And the worst case? Give me the one that keeps you awake.
P
A laptop that decides to install something at eight fifty-nine. It is not dramatic and it is the most likely thing in the building. If that happens, we have full redundancy: second machine, same session, already loaded.
N
You have thought about this more than the last three people I asked. Send me the sheet tonight.
P
I will send it after the rehearsal. And if anything changes on Saturday, who needs to know before I change it?
Comprehension

About Nina

1. What does Nina ask for at the very start, and what does she say about four seconds?
She asks him to walk her through what happens if it all goes wrong on Sunday, starting with the music. She says four seconds of silence on air is quite a lot.
2. Nina mentions a specific show. What happened there, and why does she bring it up?
In Manchester the singer came in in the wrong key and nobody had decided anything in advance. She brings it up because an objection with a real example is harder to dismiss.
3. What does Nina say about the last three people she asked, and what does she ask him to do?
That he has thought about this more than they did. She asks him to send her the sheet tonight.
Listening 1 · The First Week

A Mistake Live Is a Decision

The conversation everybody has in their first week of live television. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the difference she draws between a recorded session and a live show?
In a recorded session a mistake is a delay. In a live show a mistake is a decision, and you have about four seconds to make it.
2. What does she say preparation is actually for, and what three questions does she give as examples?
Not preventing problems, because you cannot, but deciding calmly in advance what you will do when each one happens. If the backing track drops out, who speaks first? If the singer starts in the wrong key, do we let it run or abort? If the cue runs long, does the picture cut or does the music fade?
3. Why does she insist that the answers are written down?
Because nobody thinks clearly at nine on a Sunday night, and the version of you sitting in the quiet room is much smarter than the version that will be standing there.
Listening 2 · Three Lines

Nobody Saw Me Think. They Saw Me Move.

A ninety-second habit, repeated before every live show for six years. Sound first — no text.

1. What are the three lines he writes before every show?
What is the single most likely thing to go wrong tonight, what will he do in the first four seconds if it does, and who needs to know before he does it.
2. What does he say the answer to the first question almost never is, and what is it usually?
Never the dramatic one: never the power, never the building. Usually a laptop that has decided to install something, or a musician who changed a key on Friday and told one person.
3. How often has the thing he wrote down actually happened, and why does he still call the habit a success?
Maybe eleven times in six years. He calls it a terrible success rate for a prediction and a fantastic one for a habit, because on those eleven nights nobody saw him think -- they just saw him move.

Note: we do not troubleshoot on air. We switch, we finish the song, and we find the cause in the break. Worst case, eight bars with no music and the host keeps talking.
1. Two rows are marked Always and two are marked Tonight. What is the grammatical difference between them?
The Always rows have no will: if the music stops, the host waits. The Tonight rows describe one specific future situation, so the second half takes will: if the track drops out, I will patch machine 2.
2. Why is a boring problem written in the Most likely row, instead of a dramatic one?
Because the dramatic failures almost never happen, and the ordinary one does. Planning for the likely problem is what makes the response fast.
3. According to the note, what is the rule about finding the cause of a problem?
Not on air. Switch, finish the song, and troubleshoot in the break.

Chapter 6: Your Turn

The Same Call, Three Times

Guided > Semi-free > Free

Role-Play 1 of 3 — Guided

Walk Me Through Sunday

Scenario: Nina asks the same four questions: what happens if the music drops out, who speaks, what happens if the key is wrong, and what the worst case is. Every answer must be a complete conditional. No maybe.

Keyword chips:

patch the backup four seconds abort and go again worst case
Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Now You Run the Call

Scenario: You are the one asking now, and the person on the other side has not decided anything. Get three decisions out of them using if questions, and do not accept "we will see" as an answer. Finish by saying who you will tell.

Keywords:

What happens if...? Who decides in the moment? Do we abort or let it run? I will write it down.
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Your Show, No Notes

Scenario: Take a real show you have worked on. Give the contingency briefing in English: two rules that are always true, two decisions for that specific night, the most likely problem, and who you tell first. Finish with the worst case, in one sentence.

No keywords, no notes, two minutes. Every answer is a complete conditional.
