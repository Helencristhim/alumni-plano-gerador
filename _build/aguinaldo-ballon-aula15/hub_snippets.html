Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Small talk -- light, polite conversation about everyday topics
"Small talk about the weather can open a big business conversation."
To break the ice -- expression: to say something that starts a conversation and makes people relaxed
"He broke the ice with a joke about the long keynote."
To mingle -- to move around at a party and talk to different people
"Don't stay with your colleagues. Mingle."
Common ground -- interests or experiences that two people share
"We found common ground: we both worked on port projects."
Rapport -- a friendly relationship in which people understand each other
"Good rapport makes a negotiation much easier."
An elevator pitch -- a very short description of who you are and what you do
"Your elevator pitch should take less than thirty seconds."
A contact -- a person you know who can help you professionally
"She has excellent contacts in the Canadian pension funds."
A referral -- a recommendation of a person to somebody else
"Most of my clients came from a referral."
To keep in touch -- to continue to communicate with somebody
"Let's keep in touch after the conference."
To excuse yourself -- to leave a conversation or a place politely
"After ten minutes, he excused himself to find his colleague."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

After the forum, there is a reception on the terrace. Aguinaldo sees a woman from the morning panel. He breaks the ice: "It was a great panel, wasn't it?" She smiles and agrees.

He checks what he knows about her: "You manage a fund in Toronto, don't you?" Then: "You haven't invested in Brazil yet, have you?" They find common ground: both worked on port projects. The rapport is good.

After ten minutes, they exchange cards and promise to keep in touch. Then he excuses himself to mingle. Notice: a positive sentence takes a negative tag, and a negative sentence takes a positive tag.

1. How does Aguinaldo break the ice?
A He gives her his business card and his elevator pitch before saying anything else.
B With a question tag about the panel.
C He asks her directly how much money her fund has invested in Latin America this year.
2. Why does he say "have you" and not "haven't you" in his third question?
A Because the sentence is negative.
B Because "have you" is always more polite than "haven't you" in American English.
C Because the question is about the future, and the future takes a positive tag.
3. Which question is correct?
A "You manage a fund in Toronto, isn't it? I saw your name on the list."
B "You manage a fund in Toronto, doesn't you? I saw your name on the list."
C "You manage a fund, don't you?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Question Tags

GRAMMAR

A small question at the end of a sentence invites the other person to agree and to talk. Positive sentence, negative tag; negative sentence, positive tag.

JobFormExample
Positive sentence, negative tag..., isn’t it? / don’t you? / wasn’t it?It was a great panel, wasn’t it?
Negative sentence, positive tag..., is it? / have you? / do you?You haven’t been to Brazil, have you?
Repeat the auxiliary or bebe / have / will / can + pronounYou’ll be in Lisbon, won’t you?
No auxiliary? Use do, does or didpresent simple and past simpleShe kept us on time, didn’t she?
Intonationvoice down = I expect you to agree; voice up = a real questionIt’s cold today, isn’t it? (down)
The trapthe tag matches the verb and the subject; never isn’t it for everythingNot You work here, isn’t it? Say don’t you?

Never: You work here, isn’t it? · It was great, was it? · You haven’t been, haven’t you? · She kept us on time, didn’t he?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to start a conversation about the panel. You say:
A "It was a great panel, wasn't it?"
B "Hello. My name is Aguinaldo Ballon and I am a lawyer. Here is my business card."
C "It was a great panel, was it? I think the moderator was excellent this afternoon."
You think the man works for a pension fund. You check:
A "You work for a pension fund, isn't it? I think I saw your name on the list."
B "You work for a pension fund, don't you?"
C "You work for a pension fund, doesn't he? I think I saw your name on the list."
You think she has not been to Rio before. You say:
A "You haven't been to Rio before, haven't you? It is a beautiful city for a conference."
B "You have been to Rio before, have you? It is a beautiful city for a conference."
C "You haven't been to Rio before, have you?"
You need to leave the conversation politely. You say:
A "Okay, bye. I have to go now because I want to talk to more important people."
B "It was great to meet you. Let's keep in touch."
C "Excuse me, you are taking too much of my time, so I need to go and find my colleague."
