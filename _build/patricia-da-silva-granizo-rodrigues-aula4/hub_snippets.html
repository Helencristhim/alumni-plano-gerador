Talking Numbers

The ten words a budget conversation runs on, the comparative structures that make a figure land, and the one-page note that goes out before the American people call. Key words: attrition, benchmark, forecast, variance, to allocate, cost per hire, baseline, run rate, flat, to offset. Structure: comparatives and approximation for trends.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read it inside a real sentence. Say the numbers out loud too -- figures are where fluent speakers slow down.

Attrition -- the rate at which people leave the company over a given period
"Attrition closed the quarter at fourteen per cent."
Benchmark -- an outside figure you compare your own number against
"The market benchmark for our sector is around nine per cent."
Forecast -- what you expect a number to be at a future date
"Our forecast for December is twelve, and I will explain why."
Variance -- the gap between what was budgeted and what was actually spent
"The variance on recruitment is ninety thousand, and it is not a surprise."
To allocate -- to decide which part of a budget goes where
"We allocated most of the training budget to the two new sites."
Cost per hire -- the total cost of recruiting divided by the number of people hired
"Cost per hire is almost twice the benchmark, and agencies are the reason."
Baseline -- the starting figure that later numbers are measured against
"Take January as the baseline, because that is when the new process started."
Run rate -- what a current figure becomes if it continues at the same pace for a year
"At this run rate we finish the year eleven per cent over budget."
Flat -- unchanged from the previous period
"Engagement is flat, which after a restructure is a good result."
To offset -- to reduce the effect of one cost or loss with a gain somewhere else
"The agency spend is partly offset by the drop in overtime."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Attrition closed the quarter at fourteen per cent, three points higher than the same quarter last year and slightly above the market benchmark of around nine. Cost per hire is almost twice that benchmark, and the reason is agencies rather than volume.

The variance on recruitment is ninety thousand, marginally smaller than the figure we forecast in March, and it is partly offset by a fall in overtime. Engagement is flat. Headcount sits at just under two hundred and has not moved since April. At the current run rate we close the year roughly eleven per cent over budget.

1. Slightly above the market benchmark. What does slightly add?
A It says the writer is not certain of the figure.
B It says HOW far above, which is the question the reader asks next.
C It makes the sentence more polite.
2. Why does the note say almost twice that benchmark instead of much higher?
A Because the two numbers are far apart, and a proportion is clearer than a difference.
B Because much higher is grammatically wrong with cost.
C Because finance always prefers multiplication.
3. Just under two hundred rather than one hundred and ninety-six. Why?
A Because the exact figure is confidential.
B Because approximating on purpose is safer and faster than an exact figure you may have to correct.
C Because English does not use exact figures in reports.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparison and Approximation

GRAMMAR

A figure on its own invites one question: compared to what? Answering that in the same breath is the whole skill. And approximating on purpose is a strength, not a hedge -- as long as you are precise about being approximate.

FormWhat it doesExample
comparative + thancompares two figures directly, and is the first thing a director asksAttrition is three points higher than last year.
much / slightly / marginallysays HOW different, which is the second thing they askIt is slightly higher than the benchmark.
twice / half / a third ofproportion instead of difference, for numbers far apartCost per hire is almost twice the benchmark.
roughly / just under / in the region ofapproximation that is precise about being approximate -- safer than a wrong exact figureWe are at just under two hundred.
the trapmore big, more cheaper and the double of are the three that mark a non-native speaker instantly. Say bigger, cheaper, twice.

Stage 2: Build the Three-Minute Update

Order

The order an American executive team expects to hear it in.

?Attrition closed the quarter at fourteen, three points higher than last year.
?We have moved the training budget to the first ninety days, and Ana owns it.
?We are not losing people to money -- we are losing them in their first ninety days.
?The one I do not have yet is exit-interview data by site, and I will have it Thursday.
?Two thirds of the leavers had less than a year of service, and it is one site.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Read each sentence out loud, record it and listen back. Numbers are where fluent speakers slow down -- say them until they come out at speed.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

One right answer each. Read the situation before you look at the options.

You open the numbers section of the call. Which sentence works?
A Attrition is a bit of a concern this quarter.
B Attrition closed the quarter at fourteen per cent, three points higher than last year.
C The attrition is more high than the last year.
D We have some attrition data I can send afterwards.
The CFO asks about a number you did not prepare. What is the professional answer?
A Guess, so that the room keeps moving.
B I do not know, and I will have it by Thursday.
C That is not in my scope.
D It should be in the file somewhere.
Your recruitment budget is over by ninety thousand and nobody has noticed. What do you do?
A Wait to be asked, so as not to draw attention to it.
B Say it yourself, with the cause and what offsets it.
C Put it in the appendix of the file.
D Report it at the end of the year, when the picture is complete.
You have eleven true numbers and three minutes. What do you bring?
A All eleven, quickly.
B One claim, and only the numbers that support it.
C The three that look best.
D The three that are easiest to explain in English.
