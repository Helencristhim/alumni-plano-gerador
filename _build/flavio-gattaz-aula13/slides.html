Lesson 13 · The Leadership Question

Tell Me About a Time You Led

Everybody prepares this question and almost nobody lands it. The story is rarely the problem. The order is.

Warm-Up

The File Spoke for You. Now You Speak.

Last time every claim on the page had to be verifiable, and the writer was an office rather than a person. Today there is no page, no passive and no second draft.

In sixty seconds: one moment at the company when you had to decide something nobody else wanted to decide. Start wherever you like — I am going to time where you start.

Today’s Goal

Making the Point Land

1. The Words

a judgment call, composure, a precedent, a ripple effect...

2. One Structure

moving the important part to the front, and what the verb does next

3. Your Turn

the same story three times, and a panel that pushes back

A committee is not scoring the outcome. It is scoring the decision — who made it, what it cost, and what it set in motion. The candidate who opens with the decision is already ahead of the candidate with the better story.

Then answer this: in that sentence, is there a decision, or is there a description?

Chapter 3: The Code

Putting the Important Part First

Fronting, inversion, and the little word that adds weight

Grammar Discovery

What Moved, and What Happened to the Verb?

"We had never stopped a pour before." → "Never before had we stopped a pour."

"We kept the crew and we finished ahead." → "Not only did we keep the crew, but we also finished ahead."

"I understood the real problem only after the audit." → "Only after the audit did I understand the real problem."

"I told them twice." → "I did tell them, twice."

Everything in blue is the neutral sentence. Everything in red carries emphasis, and it was bought in one of two ways: something moved to the front and the auxiliary jumped in front of the subject, or a did was added where there was none.

ToolFormExample
Front itmove the important part to the front, verb unchangedThat call was mine, and I would make it again.
Invert itnever / not once / at no point / only after → auxiliary before the subjectNot once did anybody leave the site.
Not only... but alsoinversion in the first half, normal order in the secondNot only did we keep the crew, but we also finished ahead.
Add a do, does or didput it before the plain verb; the plain verb loses its endingNot I did told them. Say I did tell them.
Order of informationold news first, new and heavy news lastThe man who stopped that pour was a twenty-three-year-old engineer.
Where the beat fallsemphasis is a pause and a stressed word, never volumeIt was not the concrete. It was the schedule.

In one line: move it, invert it, or add a did — one of the three per sentence, never all three at once.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Question, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one scoring form

Dialogue

Six in the Morning, Forty Men

C
Let me ask you the question every school asks, and I will ask it the plain way. Tell me about a time you had to lead people who did not want to be led.
F
At six in the morning I stopped a concrete pour with forty men already on the site. The lab result on the Saturday batch had come back wrong at two.
C
Who made that call?
F
I did. It was a judgment call and the report was borderline. Not once did I ask my uncle first, and that was the expensive part.
C
Why is that the expensive part, and not the lost day?
F
Because I set a precedent without meaning to. Only after the audit did I understand that I had also decided who is allowed to stop a pour in that company.
C
And the forty men on the slab. What did you say to them?
F
I leveled with them. The concrete was wrong, the day was lost, and they would be paid for it. Not only did they stay, but two of them told me afterwards that they had seen it and said nothing.
Comprehension

About Claire

1. How does Claire introduce the question, and what does she say about the way she is asking it?
She says it is the question every school asks and that she will ask it the plain way: a time he had to lead people who did not want to be led.
2. After he describes the stopped pour, what is the only thing Claire wants to know next?
Who made the call. She goes straight to the decision-maker, before anything about the site or the schedule.
3. What does Claire never ask about in the whole conversation?
The result. She asks who decided, why that part was expensive and what he said to the crew, and she never asks how the project ended.
Listening 1 · A Committee Member

What the Question Is Measuring

Nine years on an admissions committee, on the answer everybody rehearses. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say she is listening for, and what is she not listening for?
She is listening for a moment when something was going wrong and a person decided. She is not listening for a promotion, a title, or a project that went well.
2. What does she say about a story with no cost in it?
That in nine years she has never given a high mark to one, because a decision that cost nothing is a task, and a task is not leadership.
3. What is the third thing she listens for, and what do most candidates get wrong about it?
The order. She wants the decision first and the context second, and candidates spend ninety seconds on background and fifteen on what they actually did.
Listening 2 · A Candidate

The Second Time I Told It

A candidate on the same story, told in two different orders. Sound first — no text.

1. How did he tell the story the first time, and how long did the background take?
In order from the beginning, like a report: two minutes on the client, the engineer and the contract, and thirty seconds on what he actually did.
2. Where did he start the second time, and what happened to the background?
At the worst minute: stopping a pour at six in the morning with forty men on site. Everything else became background and took forty seconds instead of two minutes.
3. What did he change about the ending, and what result does he report?
He used to finish with the good result; now he finishes with what the decision set in motion and says the part that went badly before anybody asks. He says nobody has second-guessed him since.

1. Five things are scored on this form. Which of them can only be scored in the first fifteen seconds?
Whether there was a decision or only a task. The note records the exact second the decision arrived, so a candidate who opens with context loses that line before he has finished the background.

Owning the call without being asked, and naming the cost before the interviewer did.
3. The form has no line for the outcome. What does that tell you about how to build the answer?
That the result is not what is being scored, so it belongs at the end in one sentence, and the decision, the cost and the effect on people take the time.

The First Fifteen Seconds

Scenario: The interviewer asks for a time you had to lead people who did not want to be led. Give ninety seconds: the decision first, the background second, the cost third, and what it set in motion at the end. Use at least one inverted sentence.

Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

A Different Story

Scenario: Same question, different story — the club, a client, a supplier, anything that is not the concrete. The interviewer will interrupt you twice. Answer the interruption and go back to your line without starting again from the background.

Keywords:

to level with only after a ripple effect I did tell them
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

A Panel That Pushes Back

Scenario: Two minutes with an interviewer who doubts every point you make. Defend the decision without apologizing for it, hold your composure, and never answer a challenge by making the story longer.

No keywords, no notes, two minutes.
