Lesson 13 -- Pre-class

The Leadership Question -- Tell Me About a Time You Led

Everybody prepares this question and almost nobody lands it. The story is rarely the problem: the order is. Key words: to set the tone, to rally somebody, to take the heat, composure, a judgment call, a precedent, to level with somebody, to second-guess somebody, a ripple effect, to sign off on something, discretion, resolve. Structure: emphasis and information order -- fronting, inversion after never and only, and the emphatic did.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To set the tone -- to decide, by the way you behave, how everybody around you behaves
"He set the tone in the first ten minutes, and nobody raised their voice for the rest of the meeting."
To rally somebody -- to bring people back behind something at the moment they are ready to give up
"Rallying twenty men at six in the morning is a different skill from writing a good plan."
To take the heat -- to accept the blame in public so that the team does not have to
"The engineer was right and I was the one who signed, so I took the heat in front of the client."
Composure -- staying calm and controlled at the moment everybody is watching you
"Nobody scores you on how you feel. They score you on the composure they can see."
A judgment call -- a decision the facts alone do not make for you
"The lab report was borderline. From there it was a judgment call, and it was mine."
A precedent -- the first decision of its kind, which every later one is compared to
"I did not only stop that pour. I set a precedent about who is allowed to stop one."
To level with somebody -- to tell somebody the uncomfortable truth plainly, without softening it
"I leveled with the crew on the slab: the concrete was wrong, the day was lost, and they would be paid."
To second-guess somebody -- to question a decision after it is made and the result is already known
"Anybody can second-guess a call on Monday. I had eleven minutes and half a lab report."
A ripple effect -- the second and third consequences that follow one decision
"The pour cost us a day. The ripple effect cost us a subcontractor and gave us a rule."
To sign off on something -- to give the final approval that makes something official
"Three people can recommend it. Only one signs off on it, and that is the one they ask about."
Discretion -- the freedom to decide for yourself inside limits somebody else has set
"Below thirty thousand I have full discretion. Above it, my uncle and I decide together."
Resolve -- the decision to keep going when stopping would be easier and cheaper
"It took six months and most of the margin. It needed resolve, and not a better plan."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every admissions panel asks for a time you led, and the story is almost never the problem. The problem is where the weight falls. English has three ways of moving the weight, and the first one costs nothing: you take the important part and put it at the front of the sentence. "I would make that call again" becomes That call I would make again. Nothing else changes. The verb stays where it was, and the listener hears the noun first because you put it first.

The second way is the one Portuguese speakers get wrong, and it is worth learning properly. When a negative or limiting expression goes to the front — never, never before, not once, at no point, only after, not only — the auxiliary jumps in front of the subject, exactly as it does in a question. "We had never stopped a pour before" becomes Never before had we stopped a pour. "Nobody left the site" becomes Not once did anybody leave the site. In Portuguese the sentence works without any of that, which is why "Never before we had stopped" sounds correct to a Brazilian ear and wrong to everybody else. With not only, the inversion happens in the first half only: Not only did we keep the crew, but we also finished ahead.

The third way is the smallest: add a do, a does or a did in front of a plain verb that had none. "I told them twice" becomes I did tell them, twice. Be careful with the past: the tense now lives in the did, so the main verb goes back to its plain form. And underneath all three sits the ordering rule that the whole interview depends on: old information first, new and heavy information last. A committee is not scoring the outcome. It is scoring the decision — so the decision goes at the front of the answer, and the result, which almost nobody needs, goes at the end in one sentence.

1. According to the text, what happens after a negative expression like "not once" is moved to the front?
A Nothing changes; the rest of the sentence keeps statement order.
B The auxiliary jumps in front of the subject, exactly as it does in a question.
C The main verb moves to the end of the sentence.
2. What does the text say happens to the main verb when you add an emphatic "did"?
A It goes back to its plain form, because the tense now lives in the "did".
B It keeps its past ending, so both words carry the past.
C It takes an -ing ending.
3. What does the text say about where the result of the story belongs?
A At the front, because it is the strongest part of the answer.
B Nowhere; the result should never be mentioned.
C At the end, in one sentence, because the panel is scoring the decision and not the outcome.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Emphasis and Information Order

GRAMMAR

Move it, invert it, or add a did -- one of the three per sentence, never all three at once.

ToolFormExample
Front itmove the important part to the front; the verb does not changeThat call was mine, and I would make it again.
Invert itnever / never before / not once / at no point / only after → auxiliary before the subjectNot once did anybody leave the site.
No auxiliary? Use dida simple past with no auxiliary borrows did for the inversionOnly after the audit did I understand the cost.
Not only... but alsoinversion in the first half, normal order in the secondNot only did we keep the crew, but we also finished ahead.
Emphatic do, does, didput it before a plain verb; the plain verb loses its endingNot I did told them. Say I did tell them.
Order of informationold news first, new and heavy news lastThe man who stopped that pour was a twenty-three-year-old engineer.

Never: never before we had stopped · not only we kept the crew · only after the audit I understood · I did told them · at no point anybody questioned it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The panel asks for a time you led. Which opening sentence is the strongest?
A "So, my company has three sites in Sao Paulo and about twenty-five employees."
B "At six in the morning I stopped a concrete pour with forty men already on the site."
C "I have always believed that leadership is about trust and communication."
You want to say that this had never happened before, with emphasis. You say:
A "Never before we had stopped a pour."
B "Never before had we stopped a pour."
C "Never before did we had stopped a pour."
The interviewer doubts that you warned the client. You want to insist, politely. You say:
A "I did warned the client, in writing."
B "I warned the client, really."
C "I did warn the client, in writing, and I still have the message."
She asks what the decision changed afterwards. Which answer gives her what she is scoring?
A "It set a precedent. Any engineer on my sites can stop a pour now, without my signature."
B "We delivered the building on time and the client was very happy with us."
C "Nothing changed, because the concrete was replaced the following week."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Tell me about a time you had to lead people who did not want to be led. Take ninety seconds, and build it in this order: the decision in the first sentence, the background second, what it cost third, and what it set in motion at the end. Use at least two of today's structures -- a fronted phrase, an inversion after never, not once or only after, or an emphatic did.
