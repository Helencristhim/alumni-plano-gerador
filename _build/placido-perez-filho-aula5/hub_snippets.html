The Supplier Call -- Firm and Polite

A call with a supplier abroad: price, payment and delivery. Key words: a down payment, payment terms, a batch, a counteroffer, to meet someone halfway, a firm offer, to push back, leverage, a deal-breaker, to lock in, to follow up, a sticking point. Structure: diplomatic language.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A down payment -- the part of the price you pay at the start, before the rest
"They asked for a thirty percent down payment."
Payment terms -- the rules about when and how a buyer must pay
"Our usual payment terms are sixty days."
A batch -- a group of products that are made or sent together
"Could you send the order in two batches?"
A counteroffer -- a new offer that you make after you receive an offer you do not accept
"They rejected our price and sent a counteroffer."
To meet someone halfway -- to accept part of what someone wants if they accept part of what you want
"If you lower the price a little, we can meet you halfway on the volume."
A firm offer -- an offer that will not change and that you can accept now
"Is this a firm offer, or can we still talk?"
To push back -- to say no to something, or to argue against it, politely
"When they asked for cash in advance, we pushed back."
Leverage -- something that gives you more power in a negotiation
"A two-year contract gives us leverage."
A deal-breaker -- one thing that is so important that, without it, there is no deal
"For us, a lead time over twelve weeks is a deal-breaker."
To lock in -- to fix a price or a condition so that it cannot change
"We want to lock in this price for six months."
To follow up -- to contact someone again to continue something you started
"I’ll follow up with an email after the call."
A sticking point -- a problem that stops two sides from reaching an agreement
"The only sticking point is the down payment."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Katrin sends Plácido an offer with a forty percent down payment. He answers: “I was wondering if we could talk about the payment terms. I’m afraid forty percent is a bit too much for us.”

He pushes back politely: “Would it be possible to send the order in two batches?” Katrin makes a counteroffer and they meet halfway: thirty percent and two batches.

The only sticking point is the price. Plácido wants to lock in the price for six months. “We might be able to,” says Katrin. He promises to follow up by Thursday.

1. Why does Plácido say “I was wondering if” and not “I want”?
A Because it is a softer, more polite way to ask for the same thing.
B Because he is talking about the past.
C Because he is not sure what he wants.
2. How do they meet halfway?
A No down payment and one batch.
B Thirty percent down payment and two batches.
C Forty percent and a lower price.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A “Would it be possible lock in the price?”
B “Would be possible to lock in the price?”
C “Would it be possible to lock in the price?”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Diplomatic Language -- Firm and Polite

GRAMMAR

In English negotiation, past forms and modals make a request softer without making it weaker. Use I’m afraid to say no politely, and always give a reason.

JobFormExample
A big requestI was wondering if you could + verbI was wondering if you could lower the price.
Is it possible?Would it be possible to + verb?Would it be possible to lock in this price?
Less directmight / could instead of will / canWe might need more time.
Soften a criticismit seems that... / a little / slightlyIt seems that the price is a little high.
Say no politelyI’m afraid + negativeI’m afraid that’s a deal-breaker for us.
The trapI was wondering is a request now, not a storyI was wondering if... = a polite request today.

Never: Give me a discount · I was wondering if you can · Would it be possible lock in · I afraid

Stage 2: Put the Call in Order

Order

?"Thirty percent and two batches? That seems fair."
?"Would it be possible to start with twenty percent?"
