Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

The Week That Does Not Fit -- Saying No Without Apologising

Somebody asks for two hours you do not have, and the long apology you improvise is not politer than one clear sentence. Key words: a deadline, a clash, a slot, a workload, to prioritize, to reschedule, to push something back, to fit something in, to take something on, to double-book, to be stretched thin, overcommitted. Structure: the buffer, the clear no, and the real alternative.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Five of the twelve are expressions.

A deadline -- the moment after which something is late, and there is no version of it that is not late
"I am afraid I do need an answer before Friday. That is a real deadline."
A clash -- two things booked at the same hour, where only one of them can actually happen
"We have a clash next week and I need to sort it out before Friday."
A slot -- a defined piece of time in a diary, kept for one thing and no other
"There is a slot on Thursday afternoon, same room, same length."
A workload -- the total amount of work somebody is carrying, which can grow while the job stays the same
"My workload has doubled and my hours have not."
To prioritize -- to decide what gets done first when there is not enough time for all of it
"Prioritizing is not choosing what matters. It is choosing what waits."
To reschedule -- to move something that was already agreed to a different day or time
"I would much rather reschedule something once, properly."
To push something back -- to move something to a later date, keeping it on the list
"We push the lab session back to Thursday afternoon, same room."
To fit something in -- to find room for something in a week that had no room in it
"I cannot fit it in this month, but I could in April."
To take something on -- to accept new work on top of what you are already carrying
"I am afraid I cannot take that on this month."
To double-book -- to promise the same hour to two different people, by accident
"I have double-booked myself twice this month, and both times on a Monday."
To be stretched thin -- doing so many things at once that none of them gets done properly
"It ends with me stretched thin and everybody slightly disappointed."
Overcommitted -- having agreed to more things than the hours in your week can hold
"Being overcommitted is not a personality. It is a diary problem with a fix."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Daniel asks Isabel for two extra afternoons a week, starting Monday, for six weeks. She has a lab placement four mornings a week, lectures until eleven every evening, and an outreach programme on Monday afternoons. The two hours he wants are not in her week. What she says is: I am afraid I cannot take that on before April, but I could do it then.

Three parts, in that order, and nothing else. I am afraid is the buffer: it tells him a no is coming, before it arrives. I cannot take that on is the no itself, said plainly, so nobody is left guessing. And but I could in April is the alternative, which is the part that stops it being a door closing. Notice what is not there: a reason. She never mentions the workload, the placement or the lectures.

That is not rudeness, it is arithmetic. An excuse invites a negotiation, because every reason is something the other person can try to solve for you. When she does need to explain, she keeps it to the shape of the diary rather than the shape of her life: Monday afternoons are a clash, and she would rather not double-book herself and let him down halfway through. And when she wants something changed, the same grammar goes the other way: would it be possible to push the whole thing back a month, and could we keep the same slot on Thursdays? A person who says all of that in four sentences is not being difficult. She is being easy to plan around.

1. "I am afraid I cannot take that on." Is she actually frightened?
A Yes. She is nervous about her supervisor’s reaction.
B No. It is a buffer, and its only job is to warn the listener that a no is coming.
C No, it is simply an old-fashioned way of saying sorry.
2. Why does the text say that giving no reason is kinder, not ruder?
A Because an excuse invites a negotiation: every reason is something the other person can try to solve.
B Because reasons are considered private in English-speaking workplaces.
C Because the other person is not really interested in the answer.
3. "Would it be possible to push the whole thing back a month?" What is this doing?
A Refusing the request a second time, more firmly.
B Asking for a change, with the same softened grammar pointed the other way.
C Asking permission to leave the project completely.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Shape of a Polite No

GRAMMAR

Buffer, clear no, real alternative — and no reasons.

UseFormExample
The bufferI am afraid… / Unfortunately…I am afraid I cannot take that on.
A soft preferenceI would rather not + base verbI would rather not commit to Tuesdays.
Asking for a changeWould it be possible to + base verbWould it be possible to push it back?
The alternativebut I could / but I can do X instead…this month, but I could in April.
Softer than cancould / would in place of can / willCould we look at Thursday?
LengthOne sentence beats fourA long excuse is not politer. It is an opening.

Never: I would rather not committing · Would it be possible push it back · No, I am very busy · Sorry, I have lectures and the placement and my project, so I really cannot.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

Your supervisor asks for two extra afternoons a week. You cannot. You say:
A "No. I am very busy."
B "I am afraid I cannot take that on before April, but I could do Wednesdays then."
C "Sorry, I have lectures until eleven and the placement and my project, so I really cannot."
You want a deadline moved by one week. You ask:
A "Would it be possible push the deadline back a week?"
B "Would it be possible to push the deadline back a week?"
C "I want that you push the deadline back a week."
You do not want to commit to Tuesdays, but Thursday works. You say:
A "I would rather not committing to Tuesdays."
B "I would rather not commit to Tuesdays, but Thursday works."
C "I prefer not Tuesdays, Thursday is better for me, if possible, maybe."
Somebody insists and asks what exactly you have on. The strongest reply is:
A "A full week, honestly. Rather than list it, let me tell you what I can do instead."
B A full list of everything in your diary, day by day.
C "Fine, I will find a way to fit it in."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

The Bear (O Urso)

Thirty minutes an episode, and every one of them is people negotiating time and workload under pressure, at speed, in overlapping American English. It is the hardest listening on this page and the most useful, because nobody in it speaks in full careful sentences and neither does anybody in a real lab.

Tip: one episode with English subtitles. Write down two moments where somebody refuses something, and decide whether they used a buffer or not.
