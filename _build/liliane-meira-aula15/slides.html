When the Answer Is No

How to refuse an invitation without closing the door.

Warm-Up

After the Review, the Invitations

Last time you read two critics on the same show. A strong review brings emails: a talk, a new commission, a residency. Some of them you cannot accept.

Think of the last invitation you said no to. How did you say it — and did the person ever ask you again?

One Question

Which No Would You Rather Get?

A: “No, I can’t. I’m too busy.”

B: “Thank you for thinking of me. I’m afraid I can’t make a new piece in six weeks. Would it work if I sent you an existing one?”

Both answers say no. Which one would make a curator invite you again, and why?

Chapter 3: The Code

Softening the No

Polite refusal and counter-proposal

Grammar Discovery

What Makes a No Polite?

"I'm afraid I can't make a new piece in six weeks."

"I'd rather not rush a work about patients."

"Would it work if I sent you an existing piece instead?"

"How about recording a short video introduction?"

What do the red words have in common? Look at the verb that comes after each one.

MoveFormExample
Refuse softlyI'm afraid + I can't / I won't be able toI'm afraid I can't take this on.
Thank firstThank you for + -ing / I'm flattered that...Thank you for thinking of me.
PreferenceI'd rather (not) + base verbI'd rather not rush it.
Counter-proposal: questionWould it work if + past simple?Would it work if I sent you an older piece?
Counter-proposal: suggestionHow about / What about + -ing?How about showing it in the autumn?
DistancingI was wondering if + couldI was wondering if we could move it.
The trapno to after I’d rather; -ing after How aboutnot I’d rather to wait, not How about to meet

In one line: thank, refuse softly, give one reason, and offer something else.

Chapter 4: Real Conversations

Thank You, But...

One conversation, two recordings, one email

Dialogue

A Call from Lisbon

D
Liliane, thanks for joining. I'll get straight to it: we're opening a show called Night Shift in Lisbon in six weeks, and we'd love a new piece from you.
L
Thank you for thinking of me. I'm really flattered, and the title is perfect for my work.
D
So is that a yes?
L
I'm afraid I can't make a new piece in six weeks. I record in hospitals, and every recording needs consent. I'd rather not rush that.
D
I understand. Consent is non-negotiable, of course. But we really want your voice in the show.
L
Would it work if I sent you an existing piece instead? I have a twelve-minute sound work from a night ward in São Paulo.
D
That could work. Could you also come to Lisbon for the opening talk?
L
Can I take a rain check on the talk? I have a prior commitment in Basel that week. How about recording a short video introduction?
D
That's a gracious offer. Let's do that, and let's leave the door open for a new commission next year.
L
I'd love that. Next year I'll have the bandwidth to do it properly.
Comprehension

About Daniel

1. What is Daniel organizing, where, and when?
A group show called Night Shift, in Lisbon, in six weeks.
2. What does Daniel say about consent?
That it is non-negotiable, of course. He still wants her voice in the show.
3. What does Daniel propose at the end of the call?
To do the video introduction, and to leave the door open for a new commission next year.
Listening 1 · A Gallerist

Helping Artists Say No

Somebody who represents fifteen artists, on the refusals that work. Sound first — no text.

1. What are the two bad ways artists say no, according to her?
They say yes to everything and overcommit, or they disappear and never answer the email.
2. What are the three parts of a good no?
Thank the person, give the real reason in one sentence, and make a counter-proposal.
3. What happens to the artists who refuse well?
They are invited again, year after year.
Listening 2 · A Hospital Arts Director

An Honest No in April

Somebody who invites artists to work in a public hospital, on the refusals he remembers. Sound first — no text.

1. How many artists does he invite every year, and how many say no?
About thirty. Around half of them say no.
2. What did the painter do that he still remembers?
She answered the same day, said she was flattered, explained a prior commitment until March and asked to talk again in the spring.
3. What would he rather hear in April?
An honest no, instead of a broken yes in June.
Real Document

The Email That Says No

Re: Weekly sound workshops on Ward 7
From: Liliane Meira · To: Marta Okafor, Arts Coordinator, St. Agnes Hospital

Dear Marta,

Thank you for thinking of me for the workshop program. I am genuinely flattered, and I love the idea.

I’m afraid I can’t commit to a weekly workshop: I am traveling with two art groups this semester, and I’d rather not start something I cannot finish.

Would it work if we started with one workshop a month, from August? I would also need a small fee for materials; that part is non-negotiable for me.

If the monthly format works, we could review it together in December.

Warm regards,
Liliane Meira

1. Which sentence is the refusal, and what makes it soft?
I'm afraid I can't commit to a weekly workshop. It is softened by I'm afraid, by the thank-you before it and by one clear reason after it.
2. What is her counter-proposal?
One workshop a month, starting in August, with a small fee for materials.
3. How does she leave the door open?
She suggests reviewing the monthly format together in December.

A Request of Your Own
