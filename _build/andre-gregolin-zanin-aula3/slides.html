Chapter 3: Already, Just, Yet

Where Are You Inside Today?

The routine is the map. Three words are the pin.

Grammar Discovery

Every Day, or Today So Far?

"I get up at twenty to six and I eat breakfast on the bus."

"By seven I have already had breakfast and I have already been on a bus for forty minutes."

"I have just finished a four-hour block, and I am going straight into another one."

"It is nine at night and I have not started the biology unit yet."

The red sentences say what happens every day. The green ones say where you are inside today: what is already done, what has just happened, and what has not happened yet.

JobFormExample
The routine itselfpresent simple + always, usually, neverI get up at twenty to six.
Done, and earlier than expectedalready, between have and the verbBy seven I have already had breakfast.
Finished seconds agojust, between have and the verbI have just finished a four-hour block.
Not done, and still expectedyet, at the very end, in negatives and questionsI have not started the unit yet.
Later than expected, still not donestill, before the negativeI still have not had a real day off.
Marking the point in the dayby + a time · by the time + a clauseBy the time I get home, the day is gone.
The trapalready and just go INSIDE, yet goes at the ENDNot I have finished already. Say I have already finished.

In one line: the routine is the map. Already, just and yet are the pin that says where you are on it.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Somebody Asks How Your Day Has Been

One conversation, three recordings, one week on paper

Dialogue

So, How Has Your Day Been?

H
You look destroyed. Have you eaten anything today, or is that a stupid question?
A
I have just finished an eight-hour block. I have already had lunch, at eleven, standing up.
H
Eleven. And you started at what time?
A
Twenty to six. Every day. It is exhausting, but it is a trade-off — one year for the next six.
H
I could not do that. Honestly. How do you cope with it?
A
You stop asking whether you feel like it. That is the whole answer. The hard part is keeping up with the reading, not understanding it.
H
And you still have not had a proper day off since when?
A
March. But in the long run that is the point, I think. And you — what does a normal Tuesday look like for you?
Comprehension

About Hannah

1. What does Hannah notice first, and what does she immediately ask?
She notices he looks destroyed, and she asks whether he has eaten anything today.
2. What does she say about doing the same routine herself?
She says she could not do it, honestly, and asks how he copes with it.
3. What does she want to know at the end of her last question?
She wants to know since when he has not had a proper day off.
Listening 1 · A Voice Note

Nineteen Hours, Six Days

A first-year architecture student in Chicago, recording between two classes. Sound first — no text.

1. What time does she get up, and what has she already done before the first class?
She gets up at five, and she has already been in the building for an hour, because the good desks go early.
2. What has she not done since September, and what has she stopped doing about it?
She has not slept a full eight hours since September, and she has stopped pretending that is normal.
3. What does she say the actual skill is?
Not surviving one impossible week, but doing an ordinary Tuesday forty times in a row, at the same standard.
Listening 2 · A Podcast

Both Ends of the Candle

A sleep researcher on students who study early and late. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the image behind the phrase, and what happens to the candle?
You light the top and the bottom, and the candle lasts half as long.
2. What mistake does he say students make with their day?
They protect the middle, which the timetable already protects, and take the hours off the two ends, which the body cannot rebuild.
3. What does he ask them to do, and what does he say almost nobody has done?
He asks them to decide in advance which end they are lighting. Almost nobody has decided: they have been taking it from both and calling that discipline.
Listening 3 · Campus Radio

What the Timetable Does Not Show

A short campus radio piece on what students trade away. Sound first — no text.

1. What single question did the survey ask?
What have you stopped doing since the year began — not what have you started.
2. What were the three answers, in order?
Sport came first by a distance, then seeing people who are not on your course, and then cooking, which surprised the researchers.
3. What is the point the piece makes at the end?
Not that the trade-offs are wrong, but that almost nobody had said them out loud, and a trade-off you have not named is very hard to review.

It says Sunday is the day off, and the words are on paper — which suggests it is not really one.

The column of what stopped when this started. It matters because it is the one that decides whether the year is survivable.
