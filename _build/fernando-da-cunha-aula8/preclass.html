Lesson 08 -- Pre-class

Reading the Room -- The No That Was Never Said

A meeting goes well, everybody is warm, and six weeks later nobody has called. Nothing changed: you were told no in the room. Key words: to hedge, reservations, lukewarm, a concession, tentative, to park something, a sticking point, to defer to somebody, non-committal, a red flag, to read between the lines, to take something on board. Structure: modal verbs as stance markers.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To hedge -- to avoid committing yourself fully, so that you can move later
"He hedged for twenty minutes and I heard agreement. He was not agreeing."
Reservations -- doubts about a plan, which the person may or may not state openly
"She has reservations about the volume, and she has not said so once."
Lukewarm -- showing only weak enthusiasm, without ever being negative
"The response was lukewarm, which in that room is a no."
A concession -- something you give up in order to get agreement on something else
"The concession was the delivery window. It cost us almost nothing."
Tentative -- not final, and offered in a way that invites you to change it
"A tentative date is not a date. It is an invitation to confirm."
To park something -- to set an issue aside on purpose, to come back to it later
"Let us park the price and agree the volume first."
A sticking point -- the one issue on which agreement keeps failing
"Payment terms were the sticking point in every meeting that year."
To defer to somebody -- to accept somebody else’s judgement instead of pressing your own
"I would rather defer to your team on the artwork than argue about it."
Non-committal -- deliberately saying neither yes nor no
"His answer was non-committal, and non-committal in writing is a decision."
A red flag -- a sign that something is wrong, which you notice before you can prove it
"Forty per cent upfront from a new supplier is a red flag."
To read between the lines -- expression: to understand what somebody means but has not said
"If you cannot read between the lines, you will book a second meeting that will not happen."
To take something on board -- expression: to accept a point and let it change what you do
"I have taken that on board, and the second version of the proposal shows it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A supplier flies eleven hours, the meeting is warm, and at the end the buyer says this is really interesting and that they will circle back. Six weeks later nobody has. Nothing changed in those six weeks: the refusal happened in the room, and it was never a word. In this business culture a decline is marked by temperature rather than by content. The vocabulary stays positive and the enthusiasm drops, so a lukewarm answer is a no with good manners, and a non-committal answer in writing is a decision.

Hearing it is half the skill. The other half is producing it, because the same register is expected of you, and a flat refusal lands harder in English than the speaker intends. The layer that does this work is made of modal verbs. We may have to revisit the delivery window is the same fact as we cannot deliver in June, with a door in it. That might well be the sticking point says likely without saying certain. They are bound to ask about the tariff says certain, but marks it as your judgement rather than a fact. I would rather park the price is a preference, and it is softer than I want.

Two mechanical points. After would rather the verb has no to: I would rather park it, never I would rather to park it. And be bound to and may have to both keep their to: they are bound to ask, we may have to revisit. Take the modals out of any of these sentences and the facts survive intact; what disappears is you — how sure you are, and where you stand. That is why a speaker who never uses them is read as blunt, and why four or five of these patterns, used until they are automatic, do more for how you are heard than any amount of new vocabulary.

2. What is the difference between "we cannot deliver in June" and "we may have to revisit June"?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Verbs as Stance Markers

GRAMMAR

The modal is where the speaker is standing. Take it out and only the facts are left.

JobFormExample
A polite warning of a changemay have to / might have toWe may have to revisit the window.
Likely, and you are saying so carefullymight well / may wellThat might well be the sticking point.
Certain, in your judgementbe bound to / be sure toThey are bound to ask about the tariff.
A preference, softer than I wantwould rather + plain verbI would rather park the price.
A guess offered as a guessI would imagine / I would sayI would imagine they have a second quote.
A general pattern, not a promisetend toBuyers tend to ask about volume first.
The trapno to after would rather, and no would in the if halfNot I would rather to park. Say I would rather park.

Never: I would rather to park the price · they are bound ask about the tariff · that might well to be the sticking point · we may have revisit the window · I would rather that we park it.

Stage 2: Put the Debrief in Order

Order

You have landed after a day of visits and your team wants to know how it went. Put the debrief in a logical order.

?"Two meetings today, and the warm one is not the useful one."
?"The first buyer was positive throughout and asked us for nothing."
?"She finished with really interesting, we will circle back, and no date."
?"The second was harder and ended with a request for a sample and a reference."
?"They are bound to ask about the tariff, so I want an answer ready before Friday."
?"We may have to revisit the delivery window for them, and I would rather know now."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A buyer ends the meeting warmly and asks you for nothing. Your report says:
A "It went very well, they loved it."
B "Warm, but they asked us for nothing, which is bound to mean no."
C "They said it was interesting, so we should expect an order."
You want to warn a client that a date may move, without committing. You say:
A "We cannot deliver in June."
B "We may have to revisit June, and I would rather say so now."
C "June is impossible for us, sorry."
You want to state a preference softly. You say:
A "I would rather to park the price."
B "I would rather park the price and agree the volume first."
C "I prefer that we park the price, please."
You want to say you are sure they will raise the tariff, as a judgement. You say:
A "They are bound to ask about the tariff."
B "They are bound ask about the tariff."
C "They will ask about the tariff, certainly, one hundred per cent."
