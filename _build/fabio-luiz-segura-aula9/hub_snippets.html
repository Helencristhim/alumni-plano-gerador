Three lines, two weeks later, that decide whether the flight was worth it. Key words: action item, next steps, mutual interest, due diligence, value proposition, recap, to loop somebody in, to touch base, thread, partnership opportunity. Structure: reported speech -- repeating somebody's words back to them.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An action item -- one specific task, with an owner and a date attached to it
"If it has no owner and no date, it is not an action item, it is a hope."
Next steps -- what each side will do before you speak again
"Two lines of next steps at the bottom of the email, and nobody has to guess."
Mutual interest -- the thing both sides want, which is why the conversation continues
"Without a mutual interest you do not have a partner, you have a supplier."
Due diligence -- the checking a company does before it commits to anything
"Due diligence happens before the decision. After it, it is called a surprise."
A value proposition -- the one sentence that says what somebody gets, and why it is worth it
"A value proposition is not what we do. It is what they get."
A recap -- a short summary of what was agreed, sent so nobody remembers it differently
"The recap is four lines and it has saved me more arguments than any contract."
To loop somebody in -- to add a person to a conversation that started without them
"I am looping in Marina, who owns the compliance side."
To touch base -- to make brief contact, without asking for anything, to keep a relationship warm
"I touch base twice a year with everybody I want to be able to call."
A thread -- the chain of messages on one subject, kept together
"Keep it in the same thread. A new email loses everything that came before."
A partnership opportunity -- a way of working together that neither side could do alone
"If either of us could do it alone, it is not a partnership opportunity, it is a purchase."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The strongest sentence in a follow-up email is not one of yours. It is one of theirs, repeated back accurately: you mentioned that most of the delay sat in the correspondent chain. That sentence does something no compliment can do, which is prove that you were listening. And to write it, you need one structure: reported speech.

The mechanism is a single step backwards in time. What they said in the present simple becomes the past simple: “our platform supports the new format” becomes you mentioned that your platform supported the new format. Will becomes would: “the pilot will be ready by March” becomes she said that the pilot would be ready by March. Can becomes could, and the past simple becomes the past perfect: “we did this in Colombia” becomes she mentioned that they had done this in Colombia. Nothing in the world changed; only your position in relation to it did.

Two verbs carry almost all of this, and English is strict about how they behave. Tell needs a person straight after it: he told me that, never he told that. Say refuses one: she said that, never she said me that. In Portuguese the same verb covers both patterns, which is why this is the single most audible mistake in this area — and it appears in writing as well as in speech, where it stays on the page for everybody to read.

Why any of this matters outside a grammar exercise: the tense shift is what separates a claim from a quotation. Your platform supports the new format is you stating a fact about their product, and you cannot know that. You mentioned that your platform supported the new format is you repeating what they told you. The first one can be wrong. The second one cannot, because you are only reporting.

Stage 2: Put the Follow-Up Email in Order

Order

Two days after the conference, you are writing to somebody you met. Put the email in a logical order.

?"You mentioned that most of the delay sat in the correspondent chain, not in the platform."
?"We measured exactly that across two corridors over ninety days."
?"You also said that you had done this with a bank in Colombia."
?"I would like the introduction you offered."
?"Next steps: you send the compliance answer by Tuesday, I send the figures on Friday."
?"I am looping in Marina, who owns compliance on our side. Same thread."

Talk
Aaron Painter — “Listen to This! How listening can transform work” (TEDx)

The business case for the habit this lesson is built on. He argues that listening is what earns you the second meeting, and he is specific about what listening actually looks like when somebody is doing it well.

Tip: he gives concrete signals of a good listener. Pick the one you do least and use it in your next call — then write the follow-up and see whether it is easier to draft.
