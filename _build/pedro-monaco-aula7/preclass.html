Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

Going Deeper — The Second and Third Sentence

A conversation in which every answer is correct and half of them stop too early. Key expressions: to put it another way, as it turns out, in fairness, more often than not, a case in point, to some extent, by and large, in practice, to take a step back, the upshot, bear in mind, all things considered, on balance, to bring it back to. Structure: discourse markers — the four jobs a second sentence can do, and where the marker goes.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen exist for one purpose: to open a second sentence when the first one is already finished.

To put it another way -- to say the same thing again in different words
"Let me put it another way: the clause protects them, not us."
As it turns out -- used to introduce something you discovered later
"As it turns out, the delay had nothing to do with the filing."
In fairness -- used to admit a point that works against your own argument
"In fairness, nobody on their side had seen that structure before."
More often than not -- in most cases, though not always
"More often than not, the delay is the bank and not the lawyer."
A case in point -- a clear example of exactly what you have just said
"A case in point is the bank account, which took four months."
To some extent -- partly, but not completely
"To some extent that is true, although not in this contract."
By and large -- generally speaking, when you look at the whole picture
"By and large, the American side has been reasonable with us."
In practice -- what actually happens, as opposed to what is written
"In theory it is thirty days; in practice it is closer to sixty."
To take a step back -- to stop and look at the wider situation before continuing
"Let me take a step back and explain why the clause exists at all."
The upshot -- the final result, once everything is taken into account
"The upshot is that nothing can be signed until the board meets."
Bear in mind -- remember this while you consider the rest
"Bear in mind that the governing law is not ours."
All things considered -- after weighing everything up
"All things considered, it was a good outcome for a first negotiation."
On balance -- when the arguments on both sides are compared
"On balance, I would rather keep the cap and lose the discount."
To bring it back to -- to return to the original question or point
"To bring it back to your question, the answer is still no."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a kind of answer that is completely correct and still fails. Somebody asks how the negotiation went and you say it went well. Nothing is wrong with it. The problem is that it is finished: there is nothing in it for the other person to build on, no reason, no example, nothing that shows how you think. In an exam it gives the examiner nothing to award marks for. In a meeting it makes the room move on.

The fix is not more vocabulary. It is deciding, in advance, that every answer has three parts. First the answer. Then ONE of these: the reason (mainly because...), the example (a case in point is...), or the concession (in fairness..., that said...). Then the consequence, which is what closes it: the upshot is..., on balance..., all things considered...

The concession is the move worth practising most, because it is the one that sounds senior. Saying the point that works against you — before the other person says it — does not weaken your position; it shows that you have already thought about it. To some extent that is true, although not in this contract. In fairness, nobody had seen that structure before. Both of those are stronger than a flat disagreement, and both buy you the right to keep talking.

Two small warnings. Markers are not decoration, so never use two in a row: however, on the other hand, that said... is three promises and no content. And beware of actually, which does not mean at the moment — it means in fact. For time, English uses currently or at present. That single word has confused more Brazilian professionals than any grammar point in this course.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Discourse Markers and the Three-Move Answer

GRAMMAR

Nothing here is new grammar. It is a decision made before you open your mouth: answer, then one of the four moves, then close. Three sentences is roughly forty seconds, which happens to be exactly what a TOEFL speaking task is looking for.

FormWhat it doesExample
the reasonWhy it is true. The default second move, and the one that costs nothing to prepare: mainly because, the reason being, largely down to."It went well. Mainly because we had the structure agreed first."
the exampleOne concrete case. Stronger than a reason, because it cannot be argued with: a case in point, take the bank account, for instance."The process is slow. A case in point is the bank account."
the concessionThe point against you, said before somebody else says it: that said, in fairness, to some extent, admittedly. This is what makes an answer sound senior."We got most of it. That said, the cap is still open."
the consequenceWhat follows from all of it. This is the move that CLOSES an answer instead of letting it fade: the upshot is, on balance, all things considered."The upshot is that nothing can be signed this month."
where the marker goesFront position with a comma is the safe default and works for all of them. Mid position sounds more written; end position is speech only. Never two markers in a row."That said, the cap is open."
"The cap, however, is open."

One habit for this week: after every answer you give in English, count to two in your head before you let yourself stop. If a second sentence has not started by then, use mainly because and one will.

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to a complete three-move answer, then put the parts in the order they were said.

?The question is asked: how is the American operation going?
?The answer comes first, in one short sentence: slower than planned.
?A reason or an example follows: the bank account alone took four months.
?A point against the speaker is conceded: in fairness, nobody had seen that structure before.
?The consequence closes it: the upshot is that nothing can be signed until the cap is agreed.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Somebody has just asked you a question. Choose the answer that does not stop too early.

"How did the negotiation go?"
A "It went well."
B "It went well. Mainly because we had the structure agreed before anybody talked about money."
C "Good, good, very good."
You want to admit a point that works against your own argument:
A "You are completely right, then."
B "In fairness, nobody on their side had seen that structure before."
C "That is not true at all."
You want to close an answer instead of letting it fade:
A "So, yeah."
B "And that is more or less it, I think."
C "The upshot is that nothing can be signed until the cap is agreed."
You want to say what is happening at this moment:
A "Actually we are in the third round of negotiation."
B "Currently we are in the third round of negotiation."
C "In the actuality we are in the third round."
You want to give one concrete example of a slow process:
A "Everything is slow, in general, all of it."
B "A case in point is the bank account, which took four months."
C "For example many things, like documents and other things."
