Chapter 3: The Code

Three Moves, Every Time

What the second sentence is actually for

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"It went well. Mainly because we had the structure agreed before anybody talked about money."

"The process is slow. A case in point is the bank account, which took four months."

"We got most of what we wanted. That said, the cap is still open."

"The clause is unusual. The upshot is that nothing can be signed this month."

Each of these is one short answer followed by one more sentence. The second sentence does a different job every time. What are the four jobs?

FormWhat it doesExample
the reasonWhy it is true. The default second move, and the one that costs nothing to prepare: mainly because, the reason being, largely down to."It went well. Mainly because we had the structure agreed first."
the exampleOne concrete case. Stronger than a reason, because it cannot be argued with: a case in point, take the bank account, for instance."The process is slow. A case in point is the bank account."
the concessionThe point against you, said before somebody else says it: that said, in fairness, to some extent, admittedly. This is what makes an answer sound senior."We got most of it. That said, the cap is still open."
the consequenceWhat follows from all of it. This is the move that CLOSES an answer instead of letting it fade: the upshot is, on balance, all things considered."The upshot is that nothing can be signed this month."
where the marker goesFront position with a comma is the safe default and works for all of them. Mid position sounds more written; end position is speech only. Never two markers in a row."That said, the cap is open."
"The cap, however, is open."

None of this is new grammar. It is a habit: answer, then choose ONE of the four moves and say it out loud before you stop. Three sentences is the target, and three sentences is roughly forty seconds, which is exactly what a TOEFL speaking task asks for.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"In the other hand, the cap is still open."
"On the other hand, the cap is still open."
"Despite of the delay, we signed on time."
"Despite the delay, we signed on time."
"Actually we are in the third round of negotiation."
"Currently we are in the third round of negotiation."

The first is a preposition that never changes: it is on the other hand, always. The second is despite with no of — the of belongs to in spite of, and mixing the two is how the mistake is born. The third is the false friend that costs the most: actually means "in fact", not "at the moment". For "at the moment" English uses currently or at present.

Chapter 4: Going Deeper

Eight Words and a Silence

Alice has done this interview four hundred times

Dialogue

And Why Do You Say That?

A
So, how did the negotiation go in the end?
P
It went well. Mainly because we had the structure agreed before anybody talked about money.
A
That is a better answer than most people give me. What was the hardest part?
P
The bank account, which is not what anybody expects. More often than not it is the compliance checks and not the lawyers.
A
And you would say the process is slow overall?
P
To an extent. A case in point is that account, which took four months, although by and large the American side has been reasonable.
A
So if you had to give me one sentence for the board, what would it be?
P
The upshot is that nothing can be signed until the cap is agreed. All things considered, I would rather wait a month than sign that version.
Comprehension

About Alice

1. What does Alice say about the first answer Pedro gives?
That it is a better answer than most people give her.
2. What does she ask about after the first question?
The hardest part of the negotiation.
3. What does she want to check about the process?
Whether he would say the process is slow overall.
4. What does she ask for at the end?
One sentence he would give the board.
Listening 1

Forty Seconds, Not Eight

A speaking examiner on the answer that loses marks for being correct. Sound first — no text.

1. Who does he say surprises him, and who does not?
Not the candidates with the widest vocabulary — the ones who do not stop after the first sentence.
2. Why is a short answer a problem for an examiner?
Not because it is short as such, but because there is nothing in it to assess: no complex sentence, no linking, no evidence that an idea can be developed.
3. What are the three things he tells people to practise?
Answer the question; then say why or give one example; then say what follows from it — about forty seconds.
Listening 2

The Person Who Says the Second Thing

A partner on what gets somebody invited back into the room. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say happens after an accurate one-sentence answer?
The room moves on, and six months later that person is surprised not to be in the smaller meeting.
2. What does she say the problem is NOT about?
Not confidence and not English — it is that a one-sentence answer gives the room nothing to build on.
3. What does she say about cultures that train you out of it?
She accepts it: saying more than you were asked can read as showing off — but in an American meeting the second sentence is where you exist.
