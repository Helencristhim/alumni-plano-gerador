Navigating Uncertainty -- Risk, Climate Trends and Market Projections

How to be exact about a number nobody can promise. Key words: volatility, downside, tail risk, hedge, mitigation, resilience, threshold, contingency, stress-test, base case, leading indicator, price in. Structure: relative clauses -- and the comma that decides whether you mean all of them or only those.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words that let you be precise about a risk you cannot predict.

Volatility -- how far and how fast a number moves around its trend
"The volatility is in the south, not in the corporate book."
Downside -- the part of a projection that goes against you
"The downside is one bad season at limits we set in February."
Tail risk -- the rare event at the far end of the distribution, which decides the year when it arrives
"We are well priced for the average year. The tail risk is what I want to discuss."
To hedge -- to take a second position that limits the loss if the first one goes wrong
"We hedged the southern exposure with reinsurance we bought in April."
Mitigation -- work done in advance so that a risk does less damage
"Mitigation is what we buy in April. Contingency is what we write in September."
Resilience -- how well something keeps working after it has been hit
"The question is not whether we are exposed. It is what our resilience looks like in a bad year."
Threshold -- the level at which something has to change, agreed before you get there
"I would rather agree the threshold now than argue about it in February."
Contingency -- the plan you keep ready for the outcome you hope to avoid
"Our contingency plan, which nobody has read since 2023, assumes a single event."
To stress-test -- to run a plan against a deliberately bad scenario to see where it breaks
"We stress-tested the southern book against two events in one season."
Base case -- the scenario you actually plan against, between the optimistic one and the bad one
"Eleven percent is the base case, and I will tell you what sits on either side of it."
Leading indicator -- a signal that moves before the thing you actually care about
"Broker submissions are our leading indicator. They turned in July."
To price in -- to build an expected risk into the number before the risk arrives
"What we have not priced in is a year in which two events arrive together."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Pay attention to which clauses carry commas and which do not.

Group Risk did not ask Denise for a forecast. He asked how much confidence sat behind the number, which is a different question and a harder one. She gave him a range instead of a figure: nine to thirteen percent, with eleven as the base case. Almost all of the volatility sat in one place, and she said where.

The downside was one bad season in the agricultural book, which sits in the region where the season has moved by three weeks. That much was already priced in. What was not priced in was a year in which two events arrive together, at the limits that we set in February — not every limit in the book, only those. That is the tail risk, and she named it before anyone found it.

She had stress-tested it in June. Capacity in the south breaks first, at around forty percent of the aggregate, so that is the threshold she asked the committee to agree. The mitigation had been bought in April; the contingency plan, which nobody has read since 2023, still assumes a single event, and she said so. Broker submissions are the region's leading indicator, and they turned in July.

1. "At the limits that we set in February" — how many limits is she talking about?
A Only the ones set in February. The clause has no commas, so it identifies which limits she means.
B All of the limits in the book, with February as extra information.
C It is impossible to tell without asking her.
2. "The contingency plan, which nobody has read since 2023, still assumes a single event." What do the commas do here?
A They separate two plans, only one of which has been read.
B They mark the clause as extra information about a plan that is already identified. Remove it and the sentence still stands.
C They are optional punctuation and change nothing.
3. Why does she give a range rather than the single figure Group Risk could take to the group?
A A range with a named driver is defensible next quarter; a single figure she cannot support becomes a promise nobody made.
B Because she has not finished the analysis and needs more time.
C Because ranges are the standard format for every executive number.
4. Why does she ask the committee for a threshold instead of asking it to approve the projection?
A Because the projection is too uncertain to present at all.
B Because a projection can only be believed or doubted, while a threshold is a decision the committee can take now, before the pressure arrives.
C Because thresholds are easier to explain than percentages.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses for Technical Precision

GRAMMAR

The comma is not punctuation. In a risk note it is the difference between all of them and only those.

FormWhat it doesExample
that / which — no commas (defining)tells the room which ones; remove it and the sentence loses its subjectThe limits that we set in February were built for another season.
, which ... , — commas (non-defining)adds information about something already identified; removable. Never that hereThe southern portfolio, which we stress-tested in June, is the one that worries me.
who / whosepeople and organizations; whose = belonging to themThe broker whose model we use has changed the return period.
where / whenplace and time — shorter than in which / on whichThe season when the losses arrive has moved by three weeks.
preposition + whichthe register of a board note: formal, exact, never casualthe threshold at which we stop; the basis on which we priced it
Dropping the pronounwhen it is the object of a defining clause, you can leave it out entirelya season (that) we no longer expect
The two trapsThe relative pronoun is the object — never repeat it (the portfolio which we tested it). And never use that after a comma.
Why it matters here"The limits that were revised in February" = only those. "The limits, which were revised in February," = all of them. Same eleven words, two different requests to the committee.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

Group Risk asks for your growth number for next year. The strongest opening is:
A "Nine to thirteen, and eleven is the base case. The volatility sits in the agricultural book in the south."
B "Around eleven, but it is very hard to say at this stage."
C "Eleven percent." Give the single figure and defend it later.
You want to say that only the February limits are the problem, not the whole book. You say:
A "The limits, which were revised in February, no longer reflect the exposure."
B "The limits that were revised in February no longer reflect the exposure."
C "The limits, that were revised in February, no longer reflect the exposure."
There is one southern portfolio, and you want to add that it was tested in June. You say:
A "The southern portfolio, which we stress-tested in June, is the one that worries me."
B "The southern portfolio which we stress-tested it in June is the one that worries me."
C "The southern portfolio, that we stress-tested in June, is the one that worries me."
The committee asks what the worst outcome is. The most useful answer names:
A Every scenario the model produced, in order of severity.
B The tail risk that is not priced in, and what breaks first when it arrives.
C That it is impossible to say, because the climate is unpredictable.
You genuinely do not know how the season will develop. The strongest thing to give the room is:
A The threshold at which you would change your view, and the indicator you are watching.
B "It could go either way at this point."
C A precise figure, so that the meeting can move on.
You bought reinsurance in April to reduce the damage a bad season would do. In English that is:
A Contingency — a plan for what you do afterwards.
B Mitigation — work done in advance so the risk does less damage.
C A leading indicator — a signal that moves early.
