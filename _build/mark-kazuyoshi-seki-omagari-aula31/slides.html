Four Sentences About the Same Trip

Three Stand on Today

"I will book the flights tonight."

A decision taken now, looking forward from here.

"We are flying on the fourth of July."

An arrangement that is already in the calendar.

"It is going to be expensive."

A prediction you can already see coming from here.

"By the time we land, we will have been in the air for twenty-six hours."

A day that has not arrived yet, looking back at what is already over.

You already own the first three. The fourth one is the only tense in English that moves you to a future day and lets you look backwards from it, and it is the one a plan is built out of.

Chapter 3: Finished Before It Starts

Stand There, Look Back

Grammar Discovery

Four Sentences With a Deadline

By July I will have saved enough for the flights.

We will not have finished the itinerary by Friday.

By the time we land, we will have been in the air for twenty-six hours.

Will you have booked the hostel by then?

Every sentence names a day that has not arrived yet, and every sentence says what is already over by the time you get there. Now look at what comes immediately after "will" in all four, and then at the verb inside "by the time we land".

PieceFormExample
the tense itselfwill have + past participle — identical for every person"I will have saved" / "she will have saved" / "they will have saved"
the deadline: byby + a point in time, or by then"By July..." / "By Friday..." / "...by then?"
the deadline: by the timeby the time + present simple — never will in that half"By the time we land, we will have been in the air for twenty-six hours."
negative and questionwill not have + participle · Will + subject + have + participle"We will not have finished it." / "Will you have booked it?"
alreadygoes between will and have, for the part that surprises"By the time you wake up, I will already have packed."
by against untilby is a point and the thing is finished; until is a stretch and the thing goes on: "I will have booked it by Friday" against "I will keep looking until Friday".

The Tool

Three Places It Goes Wrong

Trap 1 · after by the time, the present

The main half already says the sentence is about the future, and English refuses to say it twice. "By the time we land, we will have been in the air for twenty-six hours" — never "by the time we will land".

Trap 2 · by is a point, until is a stretch

"I will have booked it by Friday" means done, Friday at the latest. "I will keep looking until Friday" means the looking runs all the way to Friday and then stops. Portuguese uses one word for both, and that is exactly where it goes wrong.

Trap 3 · have never moves, the participle never changes

It is "she will have saved" for every person — never "will has saved", and never "will have save". The tense is carried by will have, so the verb after it stays in the participle and does nothing else.

A plan is a promise with a date on it. Take the date away and you are back to "I want to go one day", which is the sentence this whole lesson exists to replace.

Comprehension

About Nina

1. Why does she tell him to book the flights this week?
Because she traveled in peak season too, and by April half the hostels were already fully booked.
2. What does she say about the flight with the seven-hour layover?
That he should take it: it is horrible, but by the time he gets there he will have saved almost a thousand.

It turned into a detour that she thought was better than the museums.
Listening

The Day You Look Back From

Your teacher on the form, the two traps and the word that surprises. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does the future perfect do that "will", "going to" and the present continuous do not?
It stands at a point in the future and looks back at what is already finished by then, instead of standing at today and looking forward.
2. What tense comes after "by the time", and why?
The present simple. The main half already puts the sentence in the future, and English refuses to say it twice.
3. What is the difference she gives between "by Friday" and "until Friday"?
"By" is a point and the thing is finished at the latest then; "until" is a stretch and the action runs all the way to Friday and stops.
4. Where does she say to put "already", and what is it for?
Between "will" and "have" — "I will already have packed" — for the part of the sentence that surprises the listener.

Fix the Sentence

Say the correction out loud first, then click

1. "By the time we will land, we will have been in the air for twenty-six hours."
"By the time we land, we will have been in the air for twenty-six hours." (after "by the time", the present simple — never "will")
2. "By July she will has saved enough for the flights."
"By July she will have saved enough for the flights." ("have" never becomes "has" after "will")
3. "We will have book the hostel before the end of the month."
"We will have booked the hostel before the end of the month." (will have takes the past participle, never the bare verb)
4. "I will have booked everything until Friday."
"I will have booked everything by Friday." ("by" is the deadline point; "until" would mean the booking goes on all week)
5. "By the time you wake up, I will have already packed my bag, already."
"By the time you wake up, I will already have packed my bag." ("already" goes between "will" and "have", and it is said once)
The Real Thing

An Itinerary With Deadlines

Trip plan — four deadlines
Mark · Two weeks, three cities
Departure
4 July
This SundayBy Sunday night I will have booked the flights, layover included.
End of MarchBy the end of March I will have paid for every hostel, because in peak season they fill up first.
By 30 JuneBy the end of June I will have saved the whole budget for food and trains, and I will not have asked anybody for it.
Departure dayBy the time we set off, the itinerary will have been printed, with one empty afternoon in every city.
Your cardFour deadlines for a trip you actually want to take. A real date in every line, and one line in the negative.
1. Why does the last line say "by the time we set off" and not "by the time we will set off"?
Because after "by the time" English uses the present simple — the other half of the sentence has already put the whole thing in the future.
2. What does a deadline add to a plan that a list of places does not have?
A point you can be judged against: on that date the task is either finished or it is not, so the plan stops being something you intend and becomes something you can check.
