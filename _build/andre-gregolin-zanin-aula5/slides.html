Chapter 3: Rule or Conclusion?

A Rule, or a Conclusion?

The same word does both, and only the context tells you which

Grammar Discovery

What Is the World Telling You to Do?

"You have to register before the deadline. It is compulsory."

"You should apply to both, honestly. And you do not have to decide today."

"That must be exhausting, waking up at twenty to six every day."

"He cannot be serious about that. He might be joking."

The same little words, two completely different jobs. The blue ones tell somebody what the world requires. The green ones tell somebody what you have worked out — and nothing at all about the rules.

JobFormExample
An obligation from outsidehave to + bare verbYou have to register before November.
No obligation at alldo not have to + bare verbYou do not have to decide today.
Adviceshould · ought to + bare verbYou should apply to both.
A possibilitymight · may · could + bare verbHe might get in if he keeps this up.
A conclusion you are almost sure ofmust + bare verbThat must be exhausting.
A conclusion you have ruled outcannot + bare verbThat cannot be easy.
The trapmust not is not do not have toYou must not come = forbidden. You do not have to come = optional.

In one line: ask what the sentence is doing. If it is about the rules, it is blue. If it is about your reasoning, it is green.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

Two People Who Disagree Well

One conversation, three recordings, one official notice

Dialogue

You Cannot Be Serious

H
For what it is worth, I think a gap year would do you more good than another year of this.
A
You cannot be serious. A gap year here is not what it is in the United States.
H
Go on. What do people actually do with one?
A
Mostly the same exam, one year later. So it is not really a gap. I would argue that it is just the same year with worse timing.
H
Okay, that is fair. But you must be exhausted. Nobody can keep this up for eleven months.
A
Some people cannot, and they stop in September. I might be one of them, honestly. I do not know yet.
H
See, that is the first honest thing you have said about it. Why do you not say that more often?
A
Because it sounds like an excuse in Portuguese. And you — what would you do, if you were me?
Comprehension

About Hannah

1. What does Hannah suggest, and how does she soften it before saying it?
She suggests a gap year would do him more good, and she softens it by opening with for what it is worth.
2. How does she react when he disagrees with her?
She does not defend the idea. She says go on and asks what people actually do with a gap year, and later she says that is fair.
3. What does she say is the first honest thing he has said, and what does she ask about it?
That he might be one of the people who stop in September. She asks why he does not say that more often.
Listening 1 · A Voice Note

I Am Not Entirely Sure, But

A student explaining a position she is only half certain of. Sound first — no text.

1. What is her main claim, and how sure does she say she is about it?
That the exam measures the wrong thing. She says she is not entirely sure, and calls part of it speculation on her part.
2. Why does she say the exam cannot measure what people claim?
Because a test that lasts one morning cannot see whether somebody can work for six years.
3. What are the two different claims she says people collapse into one?
That the exam measures the wrong thing, and that it should be abolished. She does not rule out that it is the least bad system anybody has built.
Listening 2 · A Podcast

Sounding Sure, and Being Sure

A debate coach on confidence that is not a performance. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say people get wrong about confidence?
They treat it as volume. He says it is calibration, not volume.
2. What does he say an audience works out in about ninety seconds?
That a speaker who says every sentence with the same certainty is not confident, just loud.
3. What do his students never believe until they try it?
That saying I am not certain about this does not cost you authority — it buys you the authority you need for the next sentence.
Listening 3 · World Service

Compulsory, Optional, and In Between

A short radio piece on rules countries choose to enforce. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the argument of the countries that make voting compulsory?
That a democracy in which only the motivated vote is not a democracy, but a survey of the motivated.
2. What do the numbers show about turnout?
Turnout is higher in compulsory systems, which surprises nobody, but the gap is smaller than most people assume.
3. What does the piece say happens when the fine is very low?
The obligation is effectively a suggestion with paperwork.
Real Document

The Notice That Says Must Four Times

NATIONAL ENTRANCE EXAM · CANDIDATE NOTICE
registration · second call · please read in full
CandidateAndré Gregolin Zanin
RegistrationCandidates must register online before 20 November
PhotographA recent photograph is required
Second languageCandidates may choose English or Spanish
Electronic devicesDevices must not be taken into the room
ArrivalCandidates do not have to arrive before 11:30
Four of these lines use a modal verb, and no two of them mean the same thing. One is an obligation, one is a prohibition, one is a permission and one is the absence of an obligation. Reading a notice like this correctly is a language skill before it is anything else.
1. Which line is a prohibition, and which one is only the absence of an obligation?
Devices must not be taken into the room is a prohibition. Candidates do not have to arrive before 11:30 is the absence of an obligation.
2. What does the line about the second language allow, exactly?
It allows candidates to choose. May means permission, not obligation, so either English or Spanish is acceptable.
3. According to the note at the bottom, what does reading a notice like this require first?
It is a language skill before it is anything else, because no two of the modal lines mean the same thing.

Chapter 6: Your Turn

Your Position, Three Times

Role-Play 1 of 3 — Guided

Hold the Position

Scenario: You said something you believe. The other person disagrees, politely, twice. Hold your position both times without raising your voice — and concede one point that is genuinely fair.

Keyword chips:

I would argue that for what it is worth that is fair, but I would not rule that out
Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Ask Before You Argue

Scenario: Somebody states an opinion about your country that you think is wrong. Before you answer it, ask two questions to find out what they actually mean — then answer only the part you disagree with.

Keywords:

What exactly do you mean by…? Are you saying that…? an assumption speculation
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Two Minutes, No Structure

Scenario: Two minutes on any of the three statements from today, or on one of your own. Take a clear position, mark how sure you are at least twice, and finish with the strongest sentence, not the longest.
