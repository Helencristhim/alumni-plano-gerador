Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

Say It Like You Mean It -- Modals, Opinion, and How Sure You Sound

The same six little words give orders and draw conclusions, and only the context tells you which. Key words: conviction, speculation, obligation, an assumption, to deduce, compulsory, inevitable, to rule something out, for what it is worth, I would argue that. Structure: modal verbs for obligation, advice, possibility and deduction.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Conviction -- a belief you hold strongly enough to defend when somebody pushes back
"She said it with real conviction, and nobody argued."
Speculation -- saying what might be true when you do not actually know
"That is speculation, not a conclusion. We do not have the numbers yet."
Obligation -- something you have to do because a rule or a person requires it
"Registering before November is an obligation, not a suggestion."
An assumption -- something you treat as true without ever checking it
"You are making an assumption about why he left."
To deduce -- to work something out from the evidence in front of you
"From the way he answered, I deduced that he had not read it."
Compulsory -- required by a rule, with no choice about it
"Voting is compulsory in Brazil and optional in most of Europe."
Inevitable -- certain to happen, whatever anybody does about it
"A bad week in October is inevitable. Planning for it is not."
To rule something out -- to decide that one possibility is definitely not the answer
"I would not rule it out yet."
For what it is worth -- marks an opinion as offered, not imposed, before you give it
"For what it is worth, I would apply to both."
I would argue that -- opens a strong opinion while showing it is a position, not a fact
"I would argue that consistency matters more than talent."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A modal verb is a small word that goes in front of another verb and changes what the whole sentence is doing. The first job is about the world outside you. You have to register before November, because a rule says so. You should apply to both, because that is my advice. You do not have to decide today, because nothing is forcing you. Devices must not be taken into the room, because that one is forbidden. Notice the pair in the middle: must not and do not have to look similar and mean opposite things, and confusing them turns a prohibition into a free choice.

The second job has nothing to do with rules at all. Here the same words say how sure you are about something that is already true. That must be exhausting: I am not ordering anybody to be exhausted, I am concluding it from the evidence. He cannot be serious: I have ruled it out. He might be joking: it is possible, and I am not committing. Same words, opposite direction. In the first job the world tells you what to do. In the second you tell the world what you have worked out.

Three habits are worth fixing today, and all three come from Portuguese. First, nothing goes between a modal and the verb, and the verb keeps no to: you should apply, never you should to apply. Second, can is about ability and permission, not about how likely a fact is — when you mean it is possible that he is right, say he might be right. And third, the most stubborn error in Brazilian English: agree is a verb, not an adjective. I agree with you. Never I am agree.

1. What is the difference between must not and do not have to?
A They mean the same thing, but must not is more formal.
B Must not is forbidden. Do not have to means there is no obligation at all.
C Must not is about the past and do not have to is about the future.
2. In “That must be exhausting”, what is must doing?
A Giving an order to be exhausted.
B Drawing a conclusion from evidence, with no rule involved.
C Asking a polite question.
3. The text calls one error the most stubborn in Brazilian English. Which?
A Saying he might be right instead of he can be right.
B Saying I am agree instead of I agree.
C Saying compulsory instead of obligatory.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals: The Rules, and Your Reasoning

GRAMMAR

Ask what the sentence is doing. If it is about the rules, it is one job. If it is about what you worked out, it is the other.

JobFormExample
An obligation from outsidehave to + bare verbYou have to register before November.
No obligation at alldo not have to + bare verbYou do not have to decide today.
Adviceshould · ought to + bare verbYou should apply to both.
A possibilitymight · may · could + bare verbHe might get in if he keeps this up.
A conclusion you are almost sure ofmust + bare verbThat must be exhausting.
A conclusion you have ruled outcannot + bare verbThat cannot be easy.
The trapmust not is not do not have toYou must not come = forbidden. You do not have to come = optional.

Never: You should to apply · You must not come (when you mean it is optional) · He can be right · I am agree with you

Stage 2: Put the Argument in Order

Order

You are disagreeing with somebody, politely and well. Put your answer in a logical order.

?"For what it is worth, I am not entirely sure about that."
?"What exactly do you mean by it measures the wrong thing?"
?"That is fair, and I would not rule it out."
?"But I would argue that a single morning cannot measure six years."
?"So it must be measuring something else, and that is my whole point."
?"What would you put in its place, if it were up to you?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A notice says devices must not be taken into the room. This means:
A You can leave them outside if you prefer.

C There is no rule about devices.
You want to say that being tired is your conclusion, not your instruction. You say:
A You must be tired.
B You have to be tired.
C You should be tired.
You want to disagree without ending the conversation. The strongest answer is:
A No. That is wrong.
B That is fair, but I would argue that the comparison is not equal.
C I am agree with you, but no.
You think it is possible that he is right, but you are not sure. You say:
A He can be right.
B He must be right.
C He might be right.
