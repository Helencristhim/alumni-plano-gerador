The Place That Changed Me -- Describing Somewhere With Precision

The sentence that says which place, and why that one, without starting a second sentence. Key words: vibrant, diverse, a scene, a community, urban, a district, a landmark, buzzing, to stumble upon, to feel at home. Structure: relative clauses with who, where, which and that — and the pronoun that must not come back a second time.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Vibrant -- full of life, color and noise, in a way you notice the moment you arrive
"It is a vibrant street, and it is loud at eleven at night on a Tuesday."
Diverse -- made of many different kinds of people, in the same few blocks
"It is the most diverse neighborhood I have ever walked through."
A scene -- everybody in one city who is into the same thing, and the places they go
"Austin has a music scene that runs from Wednesday to Sunday."
A community -- a group of people in a place who look out for each other
"There is a community there, and it takes about four months to be let into it."
Urban -- belonging to a city, and to the way a city feels
"I want somewhere urban. I have tried quiet and quiet is not for me."
A district -- a part of a city with a name and a character of its own
"The whole district was warehouses twenty years ago and it is cafes now."
A landmark -- the one thing in a city that everybody photographs
"The landmark is the reason you go, and it is never the reason you stay."
Buzzing -- busy and full of energy, with something happening everywhere you look
"The square was buzzing at midnight and nobody there was a tourist."
To stumble upon -- to find something good by accident, without looking for it
"We stumbled upon a place with nine tables and no sign, and we went back twice."
To feel at home -- to feel that you belong somewhere, even when you do not live there
"I felt at home in about four days, and I still cannot explain why."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

You already know how to say "I love that city." What you need now is the sentence that says which city, and why that one, without starting a second sentence. That is what a relative clause does: it hangs extra information off a noun and keeps it in the same breath.

Four words do almost all of it. Who is for people: "The woman who helped me at the airport was incredibly kind." Where is for places: "This is the hotel where I stayed for a week." Which is for things, and it is the slightly more formal one: "The app which I use to find flights is called Hopper." And that works for both people and things, and it is what Americans actually say out loud: "The city that I have always wanted to live in is Austin."

Two traps, and both of them are Portuguese sneaking in. First: the pronoun does not come back a second time. "The city that I have always wanted to live in" — not the city that I have always wanted to live in it. Second: where is only for a place, never for a thing. "The hotel where I stayed" is right; the app where I book is not.

And there is a version with commas, which you only need to recognize for now: "Austin, which is in Texas, has a huge tech scene." Those commas change the job of the clause — without them the clause tells you WHICH one, with them it is a side remark about a thing you had already identified. Listen for the tiny pause. It is the whole difference.

1. Which sentence is wrong, and why?
A The city that I have always wanted to live in is Austin.
B The city that I have always wanted to live in it is Austin.
C The hotel where I stayed for a week was on that corner.
2. When do you use where instead of that?
A When the noun is a place.
B When the sentence is in the past.
C When you want to sound more formal.
3. "Austin, which is in Texas, has a huge scene." What do the commas do?
A They mark a side remark about something already identified, instead of telling you which one.
B They mean the sentence is a question.
C They are optional and change nothing.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Who, Where, Which, That

GRAMMAR

Who for people, where for places, which and that for things. And the pronoun never appears twice in the same clause.

JobFormExample
For a personwho (or that)The woman who helped me was incredibly kind.
For a placewhereThis is the hotel where I stayed for a week.
For a thingwhich (or that)The app which I use is called Hopper.
For both, and what you actually hearthatThe city that I want to live in is Austin.
The trap: no second pronoun… to live in, not in itThe city that I want to live in.
The trap: where is only a placenot for an app, a book or a listthe app that I book with, not where I book
A side remark (recognition)…, which …, …Austin, which is in Texas, has a huge scene.
The useful openerThe thing that … / What I love most about … is …The thing that really got me was the noise.

Never: The city that I want to live in it · The app where I book my flights · The woman which helped me · The place what changed me · Austin which is in Texas has a scene (with no commas, when it is a side remark)

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

Somebody has asked you to describe a place without naming it. Put your side in a logical order.

?"Okay -- tell me about the place, and do not start with the name."
?"It is a street that is nine blocks long and does not go anywhere."
?"There is a laundromat that has been open since 1962."
?"And a woman who knows the name of every dog on that street, and none of the owners."
?"The building where I stayed had a fire escape that everybody used as a balcony."
?"The thing that got me was not the street. It was being somewhere nobody was waiting for me."
