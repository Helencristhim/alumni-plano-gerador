Chapter 3: The Code

Five Steps Under Pressure

One answer, five tools, one order

Grammar Discovery

One Answer, Five Steps

1. "Sorry, could you tell me what you mean by reliable?"
2. "So you're asking if the answers were correct, is that right?"
3. "I'm not 100% sure, but I think they might be reliable."
4. "That's because a nurse read every question to the patients."
5. "If you're interested, I'll send you the protocol."
Click to reveal the rule
A hard question has five steps. 1. Repair: an indirect question, subject + verb (what you mean, not what do you mean). 2. Check: So you're asking if... is that right? Steps 1 and 2 give you time to think. 3. Hedge: might / may / could + verb, I think. 4. Explain: a connector of cause, because, that's because, so. 5. Invite: if + present, will + verb. You do not need all five every time — but this is the order.

Listening 1

Scene 1: The Registration Line

Read the questions, then listen.

1. Where is Emre from, and what does he do?
He is from Ankara. He is a neurologist, and he also does some research on pain.
2. How does he know that Dejair is from Brazil?
He saw it on Dejair's badge.
3. What does he want to know?
Where the workshop on nerve damage is going to be. He thinks it starts at eleven.
Listening 2

Scene 2: Two Questions at Once

Read the questions, then listen.

1. What is the problem with the questionnaire?
Some patients cannot read very well, so the moderator wants to know if the answers were reliable.
2. What is her own question about?
Time: does the effect last longer than six months, or does it go down?
3. How long does Dejair have to answer?
About three minutes. She asks him to keep it short.
Listening 3

Scene 3: One Doubtful Number

Read the questions, then listen.

1. Which number surprises her, and why?
Seventy percent. In her study in Moscow, with a similar drug, they saw about forty percent.
2. What are her two worries about the method?
Who measured the pain (the patient or a doctor), and that there was no control group.
3. Does she say the number is wrong?
No. Maybe the patients or the dose are different, but he needs to explain the difference.
