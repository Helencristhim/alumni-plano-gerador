Lesson 20 -- Pre-class

The Formal Presentation -- Ten Minutes

The plan has a date on every line. Now the chair wants it standing up, in ten minutes. Key words: rehearsal, slot, overrun, delivery, filler, digress, circle back, emphasis, aside, closing remark. Structure: cleft sentences for emphasis -- "What the committee needs is one number" and "It was the manual step that failed".

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Rehearsal -- the practice run you do before the real thing, alone or with one colleague
"One rehearsal out loud is worth four hours of editing the slides."
Slot -- the amount of time you are given to speak, and nobody gives you more
"My slot is ten minutes, so I write for eight."
To overrun -- to speak for longer than the time you were given
"If I overrun, the head of credit loses her four minutes, not mine."
Delivery -- the way you speak: your pace, your pauses and your voice, not the content
"The content was right; it was the delivery that lost the room."
Filler -- an empty sound or word you make while you are thinking
"Every filler is a place where a pause would have worked better."
To digress -- to leave your main line and start talking about something else
"I digressed for two minutes on the model, and the ten minutes were gone."
To circle back -- to come back to a point you left earlier, on purpose
"Let me finish the dates, and I will circle back to your question."
Emphasis -- the extra weight you put on the words that matter most
"Three sentences carry the emphasis. Everything else is said plainly."
Aside -- a short remark outside the main line, that you signal and then leave
"One aside, and then I am back on the plan: the backlog is eleven changes long."
Closing remark -- the last sentence you leave the room with, chosen before you start
"Write the closing remark first, and then write the nine minutes before it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The chair read the plan and wrote back one line: ten minutes, Tuesday, standing. A ten-minute slot is not ten minutes of material, so I am writing for eight; if I overrun, the four minutes I take come out of the credit desk. In the rehearsal last night I counted every filler and replaced each one with a pause, which is the only part of my delivery I can still fix in a day. Then I chose the three sentences that carry the emphasis, and I built them so that the important part falls at the end. The first one opens the talk: what this committee has to decide today is one thing, and nothing on the other three pages changes that. The second one corrects the room: it was the manual step that failed, not the model. The third one is the refusal: what I am not going to do is promise a date I do not control. Everything else comes out flat, on purpose, because a fourth heavy sentence cancels the other three. If somebody interrupts, I answer in one line and say I will circle back, so that I do not digress and lose my place; one aside is allowed, signalled and closed in a sentence. And I wrote the closing remark before I wrote anything else, because what the room carries out of the door is the last thing it hears.

1. "What this committee has to decide today is one thing." Why does the writer build the sentence this way?
A Because a presentation has to begin with a question word.
B Because it moves the important part to the end of the sentence, where the room is still listening.
C Because "what" makes the sentence more polite than "this committee has to decide one thing".
2. "It was the manual step that failed, not the model." What is the "It" at the front doing?
A It refers back to the plan mentioned in the sentence before.
B Nothing useful, so it can be left out in speech.
C It opens the structure that picks one part out of the sentence, and English cannot start it without the "It".
3. Why does the writer say that everything else "comes out flat, on purpose"?
A Because a fourth heavy sentence would cancel the three that matter.
B Because the other sentences are less true than the three heavy ones.
C Because the committee only listens to the first three sentences of any talk.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences for Emphasis

GRAMMAR

A cleft sentence is one idea cut into two halves, so that the part you care about lands at the end. English uses it when a plain sentence would let the room look away.

FormWhen you use itExample
What + subject + verb ... + is / was + the pointTo drop the important part at the end of the sentence, where the room is still listening.What the committee needs is one number.
What I am going to do is + base verbTo announce an action before you take it. No to after is, and no second subject.What I am going to do is give you four dates.
It + is / was + the part you pick + that ...To pick ONE part out and say: this one, not the others. Often followed by not ...It was the manual step that failed, not the model.
the plain sentenceSame facts, no weight. This is what ninety per cent of a ten-minute talk should sound like.The committee needs one number.
two trapsAfter What ... the verb is always singular: "What we need is two more weeks", never "are". And the front It is not optional: "It was the manual step that failed", never "Was the manual step that failed". Use the structure three times in ten minutes; a fourth one and nothing stands out.

Stage 2: Put the Ten Minutes in Order

Order

Put the steps of a ten-minute presentation in the correct order.

?Correct the one thing the room has wrong, picking that part out with it was and that.
?Land on the closing remark you wrote before the rest, and then stop talking.
?Open with what the room has to decide today, and put the decision at the end of the sentence.
?Take the interruption in one line, finish the point you were on, and circle back.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best option for each moment in the committee room.

You open your ten minutes and you want the room to hear that there is only ONE decision today. What do you say?
A "You have to decide one thing today, and there are four pages here."
B "What you have to decide today is one thing, and I will come to it first."
C "What you have to decide today are one thing and four pages."
A director says the model was wrong. It was not: the model was fine and nobody ran it. How do you correct the room?
A "Was the manual step that failed, not the model."
B "The manual step failed and the model also worked."
C "It was the manual step that failed, not the model."
Somebody interrupts at minute three with a question you can answer later. What do you say?
A "Let me finish this line, and I will circle back to your question."
B "Sorry, I cannot answer questions until the end of my slot."
C "Good question. So, about the model, and the assumptions behind it..."
The chair asks you to commit to November for the whole rollout, and part of it belongs to another team. What do you say?
A "What I am not going to do is I will not promise November."
B "What I am not going to do is promise you a date I do not control."
C "November is possible, probably, if the other team helps us."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Take something you will really have to present this quarter and give it a ten-minute slot. Record the first two minutes out loud: open by saying what the room has to decide, then name the lines of your plan and their dates. Somewhere in those two minutes, use "What ... is" once and "It was ... that" once, and say everything else plainly. Finish by reading the closing remark you would leave the room with. Take your time.
