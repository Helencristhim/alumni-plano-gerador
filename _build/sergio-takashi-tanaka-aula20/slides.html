Chapter 3: The Weight

The Sentence That Lands

What ... is · It was ... that

Grammar Discovery

Read and Notice

"What the committee needs from this page is one number."

"What I am not going to do is promise a date I do not control."

"It was the manual step that failed, not the model."

"What changed this quarter was the lead time, and nothing else."

Look at the orange words. Where does the new information sit — at the start or at the end?

FormWhat it doesExample
What + subject + verb ... + is / was + the pointSplits one sentence in two and drops the important part at the END, where the room is still listeningWhat the committee needs is one number.
What I am going to do is + base verbAnnounces the action before you do it, so the room knows what is coming. No to after isWhat I am going to do is give you four dates.
It + was / is + the part you pick + that ...Picks ONE part out of the sentence and says: this one, not the others. Often followed by not ...It was the manual step that failed, not the model.
the plain sentenceSame facts, no weight. This is what the other ninety per cent of your ten minutes sounds likeThe committee needs one number.
two trapsAfter What ... the verb is always singular: "What we need is two more weeks", never "are". And the It is not optional in English: "It was the manual step that failed", never "Was the manual step that failed".

Use it three times in ten minutes. A fourth one and the room stops hearing any of them.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"What the committee wants are one number and one date."
"What the committee wants is one number and one date."
"Was the manual step that failed, not the model."
"It was the manual step that failed, not the model."
"What I am going to do is I will give you four dates."
"What I am going to do is give you four dates."

After What ... the verb stays singular, whatever comes after it. The It at the front is not optional. And after is you go straight into the verb: give, not "I will give".

Listening

Marina on the Night Before

A voice note before the committee session. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. How much material does Marina tell him to write for a ten-minute slot?
Eight minutes of material, not ten. Everybody speaks slower standing up than sitting at a rehearsal, and the room forgives a thin minute at the end. It does not forgive overrunning, because the four minutes he steals come out of the next speaker.
2. How many sentences does she say should carry the emphasis, and why not more?
Three. If everything is heavy, nothing is: the fourth one cancels the first three, and the room stops hearing any of them. Everything else goes out plainly, on purpose.
3. What does Marina say about the last sentence?
Write the closing remark first, before the other nine minutes, and never end with "that is it" or "any questions". The last sentence is the one they carry out of the room, so it should be the ask, said in full.
Listening 2

Paulo on the Interruption

A colleague from the credit desk. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does Paulo say a pause does that a filler cannot?
A pause buys the same two seconds of thinking time and sounds like a decision, while a filler sounds like the speaker is looking for the word. He says the room hears confidence in the silence and hesitation in the sound.
2. What does Paulo tell him to do when somebody interrupts in the middle of the ten minutes?
Answer in one line, say out loud that he is coming back to it, finish the point he was on, and then circle back. Never abandon the line he was on, because he will not find it again with eight people watching.
3. According to Paulo, what is the danger of an aside?
An aside is fine if he signals it and closes it in one sentence. The danger is digressing without saying so: the room follows him off the line, and when he comes back nobody knows which part was the plan and which part was the story.
