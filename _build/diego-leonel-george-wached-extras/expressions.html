Everyday Expressions 01

Yes, No, and Not Right Now

The six short answers you need every day: agreeing to a plan, turning something down without sounding rude, and buying yourself time before you answer.
0%
▼

Stage 1: The expressions

Listen

Tap Listen to hear the expression on its own, then hear it inside a real sentence. The card turns green once you have listened.

Sounds good -- you agree with the plan the other person just suggested
“Sounds good. I'll see you at the front desk at seven.”
Works for me -- the time or the plan fits your schedule too
“Thursday at two works for me, if it works for you.”
I'd rather not -- a polite no to something you do not want to do
“I'd rather not drive at night, if that's all right.”
I'm good -- a soft no, thank you: you do not need anything more
“More coffee? I'm good, thanks.”
Let me get back to you -- you need time before you give your answer
“Let me get back to you after I check the schedule.”
No rush -- there is no hurry, so the other person can take their time
“No rush, send it to me whenever you finish.”
Careful. All six are informal and completely normal at work. The one to watch is "I'm good": it means no, thank you, not "I am fine". If you want to accept, say "Yes, please".

Everyday Expressions 02

When Something Goes Wrong

How Americans admit a small mistake, and how they tell you to stop apologising. Short, light and said fast, so that the moment passes.
0%
▼

Stage 1: The expressions

Listen

Tap Listen to hear the expression on its own, then hear it inside a real sentence. The card turns green once you have listened.

My bad -- a light way to admit a small mistake that was yours
“My bad, I sent that message to the wrong group.”
It's all good -- there is no problem, so the other person can relax
“It's all good, we still have time to fix it.”
No worries -- a friendly answer to sorry or to thank you
“No worries, it happens to everyone.”
It happens -- mistakes like this are normal and nobody is in trouble
“You forgot the file? It happens.”
I'm on it -- you are starting that task right now
“I'm on it. I'll call the lab in two minutes.”
Let me double-check -- you will confirm the detail before you say it for sure
“Let me double-check the dose before you give it.”
Careful. "My bad" is for small mistakes between colleagues. If the mistake is serious, or you are talking to a patient or a family, say "I'm sorry" or "I apologise" instead.

Everyday Expressions 03

How You Feel, and Small Talk

The hallway conversation: how tired you are, how full your day is, and the two or three lines that answer a greeting without stopping to talk.
0%
▼

Stage 1: The expressions

Listen

Tap Listen to hear the expression on its own, then hear it inside a real sentence. The card turns green once you have listened.

I'm beat -- very tired, with no energy left at all
“I'm beat. That shift was twelve hours long.”
I'm swamped -- you have far too much work at the same time
“I'm swamped today. Can we talk tomorrow?”
Long day? -- small talk to somebody who looks tired
“Long day? You look like you need a break.”
I could use a coffee -- a light way to say that you would like one
“I could use a coffee before rounds.”
How's it going? -- an everyday hello, not a real question about your life
“Hey, how's it going?”
Same here -- you feel the same way, or you did the same thing
“Same here, I slept badly too.”
Careful. "How's it going?" is a greeting, not a question about your life. Two or three words back is enough: "Good, you?" or "Not bad, and you?".

Everyday Expressions 04

Making Sense of Things at Work

What people say when the information fits, when a decision was right and when a subject has to wait. These six carry a lot of the talk in a meeting.
0%
▼

Stage 1: The expressions

Listen

Tap Listen to hear the expression on its own, then hear it inside a real sentence. The card turns green once you have listened.

That checks out -- the information fits what you already know, so it makes sense
“The dates match the chart, so that checks out.”
Good call -- that was a smart decision
“Good call. That test saved us two days.”
A heads-up -- a warning in advance, so that nobody is surprised
“Just a heads-up: the clinic closes early on Friday.”
Circle back -- come back to a subject later, when there is time for it
“Let's circle back to that after the meeting.”
Keep me posted -- tell me the news as soon as you have it
“Keep me posted if anything changes overnight.”
Off the top of my head -- from memory, without checking, so it may not be exact
“Off the top of my head, I think it was three weeks ago.”
Careful. "Circle back" and "keep me posted" are office English, and hospitals use them too. They are neutral, so you can say them to your boss.
