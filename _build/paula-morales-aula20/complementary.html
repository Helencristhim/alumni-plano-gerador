Learn English Tenses: 4 ways to talk about the FUTURE (engVid, with Emma, free)

It is the pair that still slips: going to and will. Emma puts the two side by side on the board, with everyday examples, and explains exactly the difference you saw in class — what was already booked against what you decide as you speak. She also shows two other future forms you will hear around; you do not need to use any of them yet, only to recognize them.

Tip: watch it with English subtitles (the video's own CC). Do one thing only: every time she writes an example, pause and say ONE sentence of your own about next week — one with going to (something already in your calendar) and one with will (a promise you are making right now). Open channel, no sign-up.

Learn English Tenses: Past Simple, Past Continuous, Past Perfect, or Present Perfect? (engVid, with Rebecca, free)

Rebecca puts the past forms in the same table and shows, with a drawn timeline, why a fire started and the team was loading are not the same thing — the point and the line, exactly as you saw in class. It is the same contrast explained in another voice and with other examples.

Tip: watch only the first two blocks (past simple and past continuous) and ignore the last two — past perfect and present perfect come later in your course, and looking at them now only gets in the way. After watching, close the video and tell the morning of the Santos fire out loud: one sentence with what happened, one with what was happening. Open channel, no sign-up.

Let's Learn English — Level 1, Lesson 52: Taking Chances (VOA Learning English, free)

It is the last lesson of a 52-lesson course, and that is why it is the perfect companion to your lesson 20: Anna looks back over the whole year and tells her own story — what she used to do, what happened, what she is today and what she is going to do next. Five minutes, American English spoken slowly on purpose, with subtitles on screen. It is the same move you will make in Miami, made by somebody else.

Tip: watch it once without stopping. Then close the video and do the same thing out loud, about yourself: five sentences about your twenty lessons — where you started, what happened in the middle, what you can do today and what comes in November. You do not have to understand every word of the video; the goal is to hear somebody putting past, present and future into a single story. The page has the video and the text — VOA is an American public service, open and with no sign-up.
