Chapter 3: The Code

Can, or Could

One syllable between a promise and an open door

Grammar Discovery

Which of These Promises Something?

"We can waive the setup on a three-year term."

"We could look at the term instead of the price."

"That might work, but I would need to check the margin."

"I will send you the revised figures on Monday."

Ask each one the same question: if I say this, what have I promised? The red ones commit you. The blue ones open a door and leave it open.

JobFormExample
A commitmentcan / will + bare verbWe can waive it. I will send it Monday.
An open door, no promisecould / might / may + bare verbWe could look at the term.
Softening anythingwouldI would need to check the margin.
A preference between two thingswould rather + bare verb + thanI would rather change the scope than the number.
Never two modals togetheruse be able to for the secondNot I will can; say I will be able to.
Never to after a modalcould, might, would + bare verbNot we could to look
Two words, not onemay be (verb) vs maybe (adverb)It may be possible. Not It maybe works.
Attach a conditionif / provided thatWe could, if the term moves to three years.

In one line: can gives it away, could offers to talk about it. Every concession you make should buy something.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Objection, at Full Speed

One conversation, two recordings, one prep sheet

Dialogue

Holding the Price with Ingrid

I
I will be direct with you. It is too expensive.
F
Thank you for saying it early. Can I ask what you are comparing it with?
I
With what we spend today, which is a licence and part of two people. Your setup alone is more than that licence.
F
Then the sticking point is the setup, not the platform. That is useful. Let me break that number down before we talk about it.
I
Go ahead, but I should tell you now that I have no room above fifty thousand in the first year.
F
Understood, and I would rather work with that than pretend I did not hear it. We could look at the term instead of the price: on three years the setup is waived, and year one comes in under your number.
I
Three years is a long time for a system nobody here has used.
F
It is, and that is a fair trade-off to discuss. It might be possible to put an exit at the end of year one, though I would need to check that internally before I promise it. What I cannot do is move the number and keep the year.
Comprehension

About Ingrid

1. What is Ingrid comparing the price with, and why does that matter?
With what they spend today: a licence plus part of two people. It matters because it tells him the benchmark is internal, not a competitor, so the answer is about their cost and not about another vendor.
2. What number does Ingrid put on the table, and what does she say about it?
Fifty thousand in the first year. She says she has no room above it, which makes it her red line rather than an opening position.
3. In the last line he says it might be possible, though I would need to check. What has he promised?
Nothing. He has opened a door and attached a condition, so the conversation continues without the exit clause being agreed.
Listening 1 · The Pause

What He Does in the First Five Seconds

A negotiator on the moment after the objection. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say most people do in the first five seconds, and what does he do instead?
Most people start defending or start discounting. He asks what the price is being compared with, because until he knows that, he does not know what the objection means.
2. What does he say about the words too expensive?
That they are never a complete sentence. Too expensive compared with what, for whom, and over how long — three different objections that need three different answers.
3. What does he say about giving a concession without asking for anything?
That it teaches the other side that pushing works, so the next request arrives immediately, and the negotiation gets longer rather than shorter.
Listening 2 · From the Buyer

What a Fast Discount Tells Him

A buyer on the vendors who move too quickly. Sound first — no text.

1. What happened the last time a vendor dropped the price immediately?
He stopped believing the first price, and he says he did not respect the second one either, because there was no reason for either number.
2. What does he say he actually wants when he pushes back on price?
A reason. He says he is usually testing whether the vendor understands their own number, not trying to pay less.
3. What does he say about vendors who say no to him?
That the ones who hold firm and explain why are the ones he goes back to, because a supplier who cannot say no to him cannot say no to anybody else either.

The Page You Fill In Before the Call

Concession we can tradeWaive setup, in exchange for a three-year term
Red lineNo discount on the annual licence
Walk-awayOne year, discounted licence, no commitment
Six lines, and five of them are decisions made while nobody is watching. The sixth is the only one that gives the other five any weight: if there is no shape of deal you are prepared to refuse, then every line above is a wish. Notice that the concession is written next to what it buys. A concession with nothing beside it is not a concession, it is just a lower price.
1. Why does the note say five of the six lines only have weight because of the sixth?
Because without a shape of deal you are prepared to refuse, a red line is not a limit — it is a preference, and the other side finds that out quickly.
2. The concession line has two halves. What is the second half doing?
It names what the concession buys. Waiving the setup is not a gift here: it is exchanged for a three-year term.
3. Why is our leverage a fact about the customer rather than about the product?
Because leverage is what makes the deal urgent for them. The nine-day closing is a cost they are already paying; the product is only a way of stopping it.
