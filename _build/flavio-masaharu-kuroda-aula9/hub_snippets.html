Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An objection -- a reason the customer gives for not going ahead
"An objection is not a no. It is the reason they are giving you, and reasons can be answered."
To push back -- to disagree and say why, without ending the conversation
"She pushed back on the setup, not on the platform. That is useful information."
A concession -- something you give up in a negotiation in order to get something else
"Every concession buys something. A concession that buys nothing is just a lower price."
To hold firm -- to keep your position without changing it
"We held firm on the number and moved on the term. Both sides could live with that."
A trade-off -- accepting less of one thing in order to get more of another
"The trade-off is simple: a longer term for a lower price per year."
Leverage -- what gives one side power in a negotiation
"Our leverage is not the product. It is that their closing takes nine days."
To walk away -- to end the negotiation and accept that there is no deal
"You only have a position if you are able to walk away from it."
A red line -- a limit you will not cross, whatever happens
"Decide the red line before the call, not during it."
To reframe -- to describe the same thing from a different angle
"He reframed the setup as three months of the problem, and the number stopped being large."
A sticking point -- the one issue that is stopping the agreement
"There is one sticking point, and it is not the price. It is who signs."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Four words come back — it is too expensive — and what you say in the next thirty seconds either opens a door or gives the price away. An objection is not a no. It is the reason they are giving you, and reasons can be answered, but only once you know which reason it is. Too expensive compared with what, for whom, and over how long are three different sentences.

In English the distance between promising something and offering to talk about it is one small verb. "We can waive the setup" is done: you have just given it. "We could look at the term instead of the price" is a door, and it is still open tomorrow. Might and may do the same job. Would softens whatever comes after it, which is why "I would need to check the margin" buys you time without sounding evasive.

Two mechanical rules keep this clean. Never put to after a modal: we could to look does not exist. And never put two modals together: instead of I will can confirm, say I will be able to confirm. Also watch maybe, one word and an adverb, against may be, two words and a verb: it may be possible, not it maybe works.

The rest is discipline. Every concession should buy something, or it is just a lower price and it teaches the other side that pushing works. Decide the red line before the call, when nobody is watching, and know what you would walk away with, because a limit you are not prepared to defend is a preference. Then find the sticking point — there is almost always one — and offer a trade-off around it: the term, the scope, the start date. Anything except the number.

1. What is the difference between "We can waive the setup" and "We could look at the setup"?
A No real difference. Could is just more polite.
B The first one is done: you have given it. The second one opens a door and promises nothing.
C The first one is about the present and the second one about the past.
2. Why does the text say a concession that buys nothing is dangerous?
A Because it teaches the other side that pushing works, so the next request arrives immediately.
B Because it is illegal in most contracts.
C Because customers never notice concessions.
3. The text says a limit you are not prepared to defend is a preference. What does it mean in practice?
A That you should never set limits before a negotiation.
B That a red line only holds if there is a shape of deal you would genuinely refuse, and the other side finds that out quickly.
C That preferences and red lines are the same thing in English.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Could, Might, May and Would

GRAMMAR

Can gives it away. Could offers to talk about it.

JobFormExample
A commitmentcan / will + bare verbWe can waive it. I will send it Monday.
An open door, no promisecould / might / may + bare verbWe could look at the term.
Softening anythingwouldI would need to check the margin.
A preference between twowould rather + bare verb + thanI would rather change the scope than the number.
Never two modals togetheruse be able to for the secondNot I will can; say I will be able to.
Never to after a modalcould, might, would + bare verbNot we could to look
Two words, not onemay be (verb) vs maybe (adverb)It may be possible. Not It maybe works.
Attach a conditionif / provided thatWe could, if the term moves to three years.

Never: we could to look · I will can confirm · that maybe works · I would rather to change · it could to be possible

Stage 2: Put the Answer in Order

Order

Six moves after the objection, out of order. Put them in the order that keeps the price.

?"Thank you for saying it early. Can I ask what you are comparing it with?"
?"Then the sticking point is the setup, not the platform."
?"Let me break that number down before we talk about it."
?"We could look at the term instead of the price."
?"It might be possible to add an exit at the end of year one."
?"What I cannot do is move the number and keep the year."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The customer says it is too expensive. Your first sentence is:
A "How much do you need me to take off?"
B "Thank you for saying it early. Can I ask what you are comparing it with?"
C "Everybody says that at first."
You want to offer to discuss the term without committing to anything. You say:
A "We can change the term for you."
B "We could look at the term instead of the price."
C "We could to look at the term instead of the price."
You need to promise a future action without stacking two modals. You say:
A "I will can confirm on Monday."
B "I will be able to confirm on Monday."
C "I will could confirm on Monday."
They ask for fifteen per cent off the annual licence, which is your red line. You say:
A "What I cannot do is move the number and keep the year. I would rather change the shape."
B "Maybe, I will see what I can do."
C "That is impossible, sorry."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.
