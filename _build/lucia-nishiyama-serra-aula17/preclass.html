Lesson 17 -- Pre-class

Hedging, Nuance and Discourse Markers -- Saying Where the Edge Is

The words you add to a claim so the room knows what is solid and what is an estimate, and the markers that let you disagree without losing the room. Key words: roughly, arguably, to be fair, having said that, it depends on, broadly speaking, off the top of my head, to put it another way. Structure: lexical hedging and discourse markers -- the approximator next to the number, the stance frame at the front of the claim, the adverb next to the word it softens, and the marker between two sentences.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Notice that the hedge is spoken fast and low, and the stress belongs to what comes after it.

Roughly -- approximately, used before a number you have not checked exactly
"It was roughly two million over eighteen months."
Arguably -- you can make a strong case for it, although somebody could disagree
"That was arguably the hardest year of the program."
To be fair -- said before you add a point that helps the other side of the argument
"To be fair, the vendor warned us about the timeline."
Having said that -- said before a point that goes against what you have just said
"The model performed well. Having said that, we never tested it at that scale."
It depends on -- the honest answer changes with the situation, so you name the condition
"It depends on how much labeled data you already have."
Broadly speaking -- in general, without going into the exceptions
"Broadly speaking, the two teams work the same way."
Off the top of my head -- from memory, without checking, so the number may not be exact
"Off the top of my head, about forty people reported to me."
To put it another way -- to say the same idea again in different words so it is clearer
"We did not stop the project. To put it another way, we paused it until the audit closed."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

A hiring manager writes a short debrief after a panel. The candidate was arguably the strongest technical profile of the quarter. Broadly speaking, she runs programs the way his team does. Having said that, she has never worked at their volume. Her last program cost roughly two million over eighteen months, and she sent the exact figure the same afternoon. To be fair, one of the panelists pushed harder than usual and the candidate did not move. The level of the offer depends on what the safety team says next week. Every one of those marked words is doing the same job in a different place. A number that has not been checked takes an approximator right in front of it, which is why it is roughly two million and not two million exactly. A claim that belongs to one person takes a frame at the front, so I would say the second option is stronger tells the room that this is a judgment and not a shared fact. A single word that is too strong takes an adverb glued to it, which is why arguably sits next to hardest and softens only that word. And a sentence that turns against the one before it takes a marker, a comma, and then a full sentence: Having said that, we never tested it at that scale. None of these change the verb, and that is what separates them from a modal. Saying it might be two million doubts the figure itself, while saying it is roughly two million commits to it and only leaves the last digit open. The one rule that holds all of them together is simple. One hedge per claim, and each hedge owes the room something concrete afterwards: the exact figure you will send, or the condition your answer depends on. Stack four of them and the claim underneath disappears, which is the only real way to sound weak.

1. According to the text, what is the difference between "it might be two million" and "it is roughly two million"?
A There is no difference; both say the same thing in a more or less formal way.
B The first one doubts the figure itself, while the second one commits to it and only leaves the last digit open.
C The first one is correct English and the second one is not.
2. Why does the text say that "arguably" sits next to "hardest"?
A Because an adverb always has to come at the beginning of the sentence.
B Because it softens the whole claim, so its position does not matter.
C Because that kind of adverb softens the single word it is glued to, and nothing else.
3. What does the text say is the only real way to sound weak?
A Stacking several hedges in the same claim, until there is no claim left underneath.
B Admitting that a number has not been checked yet.
C Naming the condition that your answer depends on.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging and Discourse Markers

GRAMMAR

Four types, and where each one sits in the sentence.

TypeWhere it goesExample
Approximator
roughly, about, off the top of my head		Right before the number, or at the end after a comma. It softens the figure and nothing else."roughly two million"
"about forty people, off the top of my head"
Stance frame
I would say, in my experience, as far as I know		At the front of the claim, followed by a comma or by that. It marks the claim as your judgment."I would say the second option is stronger."
"In my experience, that only works with clean data."
Adverb hedge
arguably, broadly speaking, somewhat		Inside the clause, next to the single word it softens."arguably the hardest year"
"Broadly speaking, the two teams work the same way."
Discourse marker
to be fair, having said that, to put it another way		Between two sentences, always followed by a comma and then a full sentence. It softens nothing; it announces the direction."To be fair, the vendor warned us."
"Having said that, we never tested it at that scale."
The rule underneathA hedge is a word you add, so it never changes the verb — that is what separates it from a modal. One hedge per claim, and every hedge owes the room something concrete afterwards: the exact figure you will send, or the condition your answer depends on. And note the fixed forms: it is it depends on (never depends of) and to be fair (never for be fair).
