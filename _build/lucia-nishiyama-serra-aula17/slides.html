Saying Where the Edge Is

In an American room, the careful answer often sounds more senior than the confident one.

Chapter 1: Warm-Up

Same Question, Three Answers

“What did that program cost to run for a year?”

“Two million one hundred and forty thousand dollars.”

“I think maybe it could possibly be around two million, more or less, I am not sure.”

“Roughly two million, and that is the run rate, not the total. I can send you the exact figure today.”

One of them will be wrong and never admit it. One of them says nothing at all. Only the third one tells you exactly how much to trust it.

The Big Idea

A Hedge Is a Word You Add

What you sayWhat the room hears
“It might be two million.”I am not sure two million is the number at all.
“It is roughly two million.”Two million is the right number. I have not checked the last digit.
“It is two million one hundred and forty thousand.”This is exact, and I am staking everything else on it.
“I think maybe it could possibly be around two million.”Nothing. There is no claim left underneath.

The modal changes the verb. The hedge is a word you add next to the part you are not sure about. One hedge per claim — two of them cancel each other out.

Chapter 3: Where the Hedge Sits

What Exactly Are You Softening?

A number, a claim, one word, or the sentence before

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"It was roughly two million." — "About forty people, off the top of my head."

"I would say the second option is stronger." — "In my experience, that only works with clean data."

"That was arguably the hardest year." — "Broadly speaking, the two teams work the same way."

"The model worked well. Having said that, we never tested it at that scale." — "To be fair, the vendor warned us."

One of them softens only a number. One marks the whole claim as your judgment. One softens a single word. And one does not soften anything at all — it tells the room where the next sentence is going. Which is which?

TypeWhere it goesWhat it actually says
Approximator
roughly, about, somewhere around, off the top of my head		Right before the number, or at the end after a comma."roughly two million" — the figure is right, the last digit is not checked. Nothing else in the sentence is uncertain.
Stance frame
I would say, in my experience, as far as I know		At the front of the claim, followed by a comma or by that."I would say the second option is stronger." — this is my judgment, not something everyone already agrees on.
Adverb hedge
arguably, broadly speaking, somewhat, largely		Inside the clause, next to the word it softens."arguably the hardest year" softens hardest, not the year. Move it and you soften something else.
Discourse marker
to be fair, having said that, that said, to put it another way		Between two sentences, always followed by a comma and then a full sentence.It softens nothing. It announces the direction: concession, contrast, or the same idea said again.
The rule underneathA hedge is a word you add; it never changes the verb. That is what separates it from a modal: "it might be two million" doubts the figure itself, "it is roughly two million" commits to it. And one hedge per claim — stack two and the claim disappears.

Every hedge owes the room something concrete afterwards: name the exact figure you will send, or name the condition it depends on.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I think maybe it could possibly be around two million, more or less."
"Roughly two million. I can send you the exact figure today."
"Depends of how much data you have."
"It depends on how much data you have."
"For be fair, the vendor warned us about the timeline."
"To be fair, the vendor warned us about the timeline."

One hedge is nuance and four hedges are noise. Depend keeps its subject and takes on, never of. And the marker is fixed: it is to be fair, not for be fair.

Practice

Put the Hedge in Place

Say it first, then click to check

"The figure is not exact, so: 'It was ___roughly two million over eighteen months.'" (roughly + the number)
"The opinion is mine, so: '___I would say the second option is stronger.'" (front of the claim)
"Only the word hardest is soft, so: 'That was ___arguably the hardest year of the program.'" (next to the word)
"Now turn against your own point: 'The model performed well. ___Having said that, we never tested it at that scale.'" (marker + comma)
"Name the condition instead of guessing: '___It depends on how much labeled data you already have.'" (subject + preposition)
Detail

From Overclaim to Defensible

Turn the absolute sentence into one you could still defend six months later

"It cost two million one hundred and forty thousand dollars." → "It cost ___roughly two million, and I can send you the exact figure today."
"Yes, it will scale to your volume." → "___It depends on how much traffic you run today, but broadly speaking, yes."
"Your evaluation framework is incomplete." → "___To be fair, the coverage is strong. Having said that, I would not call it complete."
"That was the hardest year of my career." → "That was ___arguably the hardest year of my career."

I would say the first ninety days are for listening, not fixing. I would want to sit through three review cycles before I changed anything, which is roughly one quarter.

Listening 2

Why Careful Sounds Senior

A communication coach on hedging in American business English. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Why does the coach say a careful sentence can sound more senior?
Because marking where the edge of your knowledge is shows you have been close to that edge, while a person who never marks anything has either never been tested or is hoping nobody checks.
2. What is his first warning?
One hedge per claim. If you stack them, the sentence stops carrying anything and the room stops listening.
3. What does he say a hedge always has to be followed by?
Something concrete: commit to the exact figure, or name the condition. A hedge that leads nowhere is just noise.

What I Can Do Now

I can give a number I have not checked without losing authority: roughly, off the top of my head.
