Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Two Minutes to Convince -- Making and Defending a Case

A room, a decision, and somebody who is going to object. Key words: a claim, evidence, to make a case, a takeaway, an objection, to back up, a pitch, tone. Structure: the first conditional -- the sentence that turns an opinion into a consequence.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A claim -- something you say is true, before you have proved it
"That is a strong claim. What is behind it?"
Evidence -- the facts that show your claim is true, and that anybody can check
"I have four years of evidence and it is on one page."
To make a case -- to present the reasons for a decision, in order
"Give me two minutes and I will make the case."
A takeaway -- the one thing you want the room to remember tomorrow
"If there is one takeaway tonight, it is this number."
An objection -- a reason somebody gives against your position, out loud
"There is one objection in that room and I know whose it is."
To back up -- to support what you said with a fact, not with more words
"Every claim in this deck is backed up by one number."
A pitch -- a short, prepared presentation of one idea, made to get a decision
"The pitch is two minutes. The discussion is forty."
Tone -- the way you say a thing, which decides whether it sounds hard or fair
"The content was right. The tone lost the room."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Roberto has two minutes. He opens with the decision, not with the agenda, and then the claim: the premium segment is the only part of the category that is growing. Diane asks what backs it up. Four years of data on one page — that is the evidence, and anybody in the room can check it.

Then the objection, and she says it plainly: we are two years late. He does not argue. He turns it into a consequence: "If we wait another year, we will pay three times more to enter." Notice the shape of that sentence. After if comes the present — wait, not will wait — and the will lives only in the second half.

She asks one more: and if the shelf life test fails? "If the test fails, we will not launch in March." Same shape again. Then she asks for the takeaway in one sentence, and the room votes. The whole pitch was two minutes, and what decided it was not the amount of evidence. It was the tone in which he answered the one question he could not control.

1. "If we wait another year, we will pay three times more." Why is it wait and not will wait?
A Because the sentence is about the past.
B Because after if English uses the present, even when the meaning is the future. The will lives in the other half.
C Because wait is an irregular verb.
2. "We will lose the account if the price stays at that level." What changed compared with the first example?
A The meaning changed: this one is less certain.
B Only the order of the two halves. And because if is not first, there is no comma.
C The if half now takes will.
3. He answers the objection with a consequence instead of another argument. Why does that work?
A Because it agrees with the objection and makes it the reason to decide now, so there is nothing left to fight about.
B Because it proves the other person is wrong.
C Because it changes the subject.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The First Conditional

GRAMMAR

The sentence that turns an opinion into a consequence. Both halves are about the future, and only one of them looks like it.

UseFormExample
A real future consequenceIf + present simple, will + naked verbIf we wait another year, we will pay more.
The halves can swapwill + verb ... if + presentWe will lose the account if the price stays there.
The commaonly when if comes firstIf the test fails, we will not launch.
Negativewill not + verb · if ... does notWe will not launch. If the buyer does not answer...
QuestionWhat will happen if + present?What will happen if the price stays there?
The trapnever will after ifNot if we will wait. Not if the buyer will not answer.

Never: If we will wait another year · If the test fails, we do not launch · If the price stay at that level · What will happen if the buyer will not answer?

Stage 2: Put the Pitch in Order

Order

You have two minutes in front of a board. Put your pitch in order.

?"Let me start with what I want you to decide."
?"My claim is that this is the only part of the category that is growing."
?"Four years of data back it up, and it is all on one page."
?"The objection is that we are two years late, and it is a fair one."
?"If we wait another year, we will pay three times more."
?"If there is one takeaway tonight, it is this number."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

You want to show the cost of waiting. You say:
A "If we will wait another year, we will pay three times more."
B "If we wait another year, we will pay three times more."
C "If we wait another year, we pay three times more."
Somebody objects that the company is late. The strongest answer is:
A "We are not late. The market is late."
B "That is a fair point, and it is my argument. If we wait another year, we will pay three times more."
C "I do not agree with that at all."
You want to ask the room about a risk. You ask:
A "What will happen if the buyer does not answer?"
B "What will happen if the buyer will not answer?"
C "What happen if the buyer does not answer?"
You are opening a two-minute pitch. The best first sentence is:
A "Thank you all for your time, I will explain the agenda first."
B "Let me start with what I want you to decide."
C "There are seven points I would like to cover today."

Lesson 9 — Two Minutes to Convince

Talk
Simon Sinek -- "How great leaders inspire action" (TEDxPuget Sound)

Eighteen minutes, one idea, repeated until nobody can forget it. This is the talk you named yourself, and it is also the clearest example there is of the structure this lesson trains: one claim, evidence a room can check, and a takeaway said twice in the same words.

Tip: watch it once for pleasure and once with a notebook. Write down his first sentence and his last one, and notice how close they are.

Talk
Chris Anderson -- "TED's secret to great public speaking" (TED2016)

Six minutes from the man who has watched more of these talks than anybody alive, on the single thing every good one has: one idea, built in the listener’s head, step by step. Short, slow and unusually practical.

Tip: watch it before your next real presentation, not after. Then cut your own presentation until it has one idea in it.
