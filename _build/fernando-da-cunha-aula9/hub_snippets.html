Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Ahead of the Curve -- The Year That Has Not Happened

Somebody is going to ask where the group will be in twelve months, and the tense you choose tells them whether the date is fixed. Key words: a forecast, to phase something in, headroom, to break even, a milestone, to future-proof something, a window, churn, to bring something forward, capital expenditure, to be on track, to kick something off. Structure: future continuous and future perfect.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A forecast -- a statement of what you expect to happen, built on what you already know
"The forecast was built in October and the tariff moved in November."
To phase something in -- to introduce something gradually, in stages rather than all at once
"We are phasing the new system in, one plant at a time."
Headroom -- the room you still have to grow before you hit a limit
"At two thirds of capacity we have headroom. At ninety per cent we have none."
To break even -- to reach the point at which income covers costs exactly
"The transport company broke even in month fourteen, which was earlier than the plan."
A milestone -- a point in a plan that you can put a date on and check
"Every milestone in that plan has a name against it, not just a month."
To future-proof something -- to build something so that it still works when conditions change
"You cannot future-proof a plant. You can only leave room in it."
A window -- a limited period in which something is possible
"There is a window between January and March. After that the high season closes it."
Churn -- the rate at which customers leave and are replaced
"Churn is the number I watch. Growth with high churn is not growth."
To bring something forward -- to move a date earlier than it was planned
"If the volume holds we will bring the second line forward to August."
Capital expenditure -- money spent on things that last for years, rather than on running costs
"Capital expenditure is approved once a year, so the window matters."
To be on track -- expression: progressing as planned towards a target you have named
"We are on track for the December milestone, with one caveat."
To kick something off -- expression: to start something formally, at a moment everybody recognises
"We are kicking the pilot off on the ninth."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An analyst asks where the transport arm will be in twelve months, and there are four ways to answer that are all correct English and all mean different things. We are signing for the vehicles in January. That is the present continuous, and in this context it does not mean now — it means the date is in the diary. We are going to bring the second line forward if the volume holds. That is an intention with evidence behind it, and it is honest about not being fixed. I will send you the figures tonight is a decision made while speaking, which is what will is really for.

Then two shapes that need a moment to anchor them. This time next year we will be running two lines. The future continuous puts you inside a moment ahead and describes what is in progress there. By December we will have broken even. The future perfect puts a deadline in front of you and says the thing is finished before it. And a third, less common but useful: by March we will have been supplying them for a decade — how long something will have been going by a point ahead.

One trap catches almost every Portuguese speaker. After when, if, as soon as and by the time, English uses the present, not the future: when the volume holds, as soon as the capex is approved. Portuguese uses a future form there, so the instinct pushes the wrong way. And a habit worth more than any tense: a sentence about next year with no date in it is not a plan. Give the milestone a month, the window a quarter and the check a name, and the grammar has something real to carry.

1. Why is "We are signing for the vehicles in January" the present continuous and not "will sign"?
A Because the signing is happening right now.
B Because the date is already arranged and in the diary.
C Because "sign" cannot be used with will.
2. What does "This time next year we will be running two lines" describe?
A Something that will be in progress at a moment ahead.
B Something finished before next year.
C A decision made while speaking.
3. Which is correct after "when"?
A We will bring it forward when the volume will hold.
B We will bring it forward when the volume holds.
C We will bring it forward when the volume would hold.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Future Continuous, Future Perfect and the Rest

GRAMMAR

Four ways to say next year. Only one of them tells the listener the date is fixed.

JobFormExample
Already arranged, with a datepresent continuousWe are opening the line on the ninth.
Intention, evidence behind itgoing toWe are going to bring it forward.
A decision made as you speakwillI will send you the figures tonight.
In progress at a moment aheadwill be + verb-ingThis time next year we will be running two lines.
Finished before a moment aheadwill have + participleBy December we will have broken even.
How long by thenwill have been + verb-ingBy March we will have been supplying them for a decade.
The trapafter when, if and by the time, use the presentNot when the volume will hold. Say when the volume holds.

Never: when the volume will hold · by December we will broken even · this time next year we will running two lines · as soon as the capex will be approved · we will sign in January, it is already arranged.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The date for signing is already in the diary. You say:
A "We will sign for the vehicles in January."
B "We are signing for the vehicles in January."
C "We sign for the vehicles in January, probably."
You want to describe what will be in progress in twelve months. You say:
A "This time next year we will run two lines."
B "This time next year we will be running two lines."
C "This time next year we are running two lines."
You want to say something will be finished before December. You say:
A "By December we will have broken even."
B "By December we will broken even."
C "Until December we will break even."
An analyst asks how you will know early if the plan is wrong. The strongest answer is:
A "We will see how it goes and review at the end of the year."
B "There is a check at the end of March with a name against it. By then we will either be on track or we will have found out that we are not."
C "I am confident the forecast is right."
