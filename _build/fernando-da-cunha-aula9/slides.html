Lesson 9 · Twelve Months From Now

Ahead of the Curve

The transport company started as an internal need and became a business in two years. Somebody is going to ask you where it will be next year, and the tense you choose changes how certain you sound.

Chapter 1: First Words

From Reading the Room to Reading the Year

Last time the subject was what somebody was not saying in a meeting. Today it is what you are prepared to say about a year that has not happened, in front of people who will remember it.

Where will the transport company be in twelve months? Say it in one sentence, and then say how sure you actually are of that sentence.

The structure of the day is a set of four: we will open, we are going to open, we are opening in March and this time next year we will be running two lines. All four are the future. Only one of them tells the listener the date is already fixed.

One Question

What Will You Be Doing This Time Next Year?

Two sentences, out loud, right now.

One about something that is already arranged and has a date. One about something you expect but have not committed to. If both come out as will, the listener cannot tell them apart, and that is exactly the problem.

Chapter 3: The Code

Four Futures, Four Commitments

What each one tells the person listening

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"We are opening the second line on the ninth of March."

"We are going to bring it forward if the volume holds."

"This time next year we will be running two lines."

"By December we will have broken even."

All four are next year. Look at the blue time expressions: two of the sentences are anchored to a moment and two are not, and the verb changes shape depending on what the moment is doing.

Already arranged, with a datepresent continuousWe are opening the line on the ninth.
Intention, evidence behind itgoing toWe are going to bring it forward.

The trapafter when, if and by the time, use the presentNot when the volume will hold. Say when the volume holds.

In one line: the present continuous is a date, will be doing is a moment, and will have done is a deadline.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Year, at Full Speed

One conversation, three recordings, one timeline

Dialogue

The Meeting About Next Year

D
So, twelve months out. Where is the transport arm going to be?
F
It broke even in month fourteen, which was ahead of the plan. By December we will have doubled the fleet.
D
Doubled from what, and is that decided or expected?
F
Decided. We are signing for the vehicles in January, and we are phasing them in across the first quarter rather than all at once.
D
And the second line at plant two?
F
That one is expected rather than arranged. We are going to bring it forward to August if the volume holds, and there is a window for the capital expenditure in the first quarter or not at all.
D
What does the year look like if the volume does not hold?
F
Then this time next year we will be running one line with more headroom than I would like, and I would rather tell you that now than in June.
Comprehension

About Diane

1. What does Diane ask first, and how does she frame the timeframe?
She asks where the transport arm is going to be, and she frames it as twelve months out.
2. Which distinction does Diane insist on after his first answer?
Whether the doubling is decided or expected, and doubled from what.
3. What is Diane’s last question, and what is she testing with it?
What the year looks like if the volume does not hold. She is testing whether he has thought about the case where the plan fails.
Listening 1 · The Owner

The Plan That Met the Year

A manufacturer on what happened to a twelve-month plan. Sound first — no text.

1. What happened to the plan by February, and how does he treat that now?
About a third of it was already wrong. He has stopped treating that as a failure.
2. Which parts of the plan survived, and which did not?
The parts with a date and a name against them survived. Everything phrased as we will look at or we will consider did not.
3. What is the test he uses now?
If the sentence has no date, it is not a plan, it is a hope with good grammar.
Listening 2 · The Analyst

Why the Forecast Was Never Wrong

An analyst on the difference between a bad forecast and a bad plan. Sound first — no text.

1. What do people expect her to tell them, and why is that often wrong?
That the forecast was too optimistic. Quite often the number was reasonable when it was built.
2. What does she say actually goes wrong?
Nothing was set up to notice when the assumption underneath the forecast stopped being true.
3. What do the companies that handle it well do?
They write down two or three things that would tell them early, put a date on checking them, and name a person.
Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Automation That Took Three Times as Long

A business radio report on why factory automation slips. Sound first — no text.

1. According to the data, what arrives roughly on time and what does not?
The equipment arrives roughly when it was supposed to and the line runs roughly on time. The project then takes another year and a half.
2. What is the part nobody schedules?
The part where people learn to work differently around the new equipment.
3. What do firms that phase it in report, and why does it sound like a contradiction?
Longer plans and shorter overruns. By the time the second plant starts, everybody knows what went wrong at the first one.

Two lines on this page are arranged and two are expected, and the page marks the difference on purpose. The August line is conditional and says so. The March check exists because a forecast that is only tested in December is a report you will read too late. By the end of the first quarter we will either be on track or we will have found out that we are not, and both of those are useful in March.
1. Which lines on this page are arranged and which are expected, and how can you tell?
January, Q1, 9 March and By December are arranged, with fixed dates. August is expected: it carries the condition "if volume holds".
2. Why does the plan include a check on 31 March with a named owner?
Because a forecast tested only in December arrives too late to act on. The March check makes the assumption visible while there is still time.
3. Find the two future forms in the note and say what each one does.
"we will either be on track" describes a state in progress at a moment ahead; "we will have found out" describes something finished before that moment.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I use the present continuous for what is arranged and has a date.
I use going to for what I intend, with the evidence behind it.
I say this time next year we will be running, and by December we will have finished.
I never put will after when, if or by the time.
I know the words: a forecast, to phase in, headroom, to break even, a milestone, a window, capital expenditure.
