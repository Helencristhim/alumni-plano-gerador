Lesson 17 -- Pre-class

Advanced Modals -- Would Rather, Had Better, May as Well, and the Language of Executive Preference

How to say which option you want, and how much force the sentence carries when you say it. Key words: appetite, fallback, weigh up, on balance, lesser evil, err on the side of, hold off, sticking point, split the difference, non-starter, sleep on it, wiggle room. Structure: would rather, would just as soon, had better and may as well -- four preferences, four different amounts of authority.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words for the conversation where two people want the same thing and only one of them can have it.

Appetite -- how much risk, cost or change an organization is actually willing to take on
"Milan has the appetite for one large deal this year, not three."
Fallback -- the option you keep in reserve for the day the first one is not available
"The fallback is the twelve-month extension, and I would rather not need it."
To weigh up -- to hold two or three options against each other before you commit to one
"We weighed up all three before we put anything to the committee."
On balance -- after both sides have been considered, this is where I come out
"On balance I would rather hold off until the January renewals."
Lesser evil -- the option you choose because the others are worse, not because it is good
"Option two is the lesser evil, and I would say so in those words."
To err on the side of -- to choose the safer mistake on purpose, when you cannot be certain either way
"I would rather err on the side of caution than book the same capacity twice."
To hold off -- to delay an action deliberately until something becomes clearer
"We had better hold off until the treaty is signed."
Sticking point -- the one issue on which an agreement stops moving
"The sticking point is the reporting date, not the price."
To split the difference -- to settle halfway, when neither side will move the whole way
"We may as well split the difference and take one sheet to Milan."
Non-starter -- an option that cannot even be considered, so nobody spends time on it
"Moving the closing date is a non-starter for the Colombian team."
To sleep on it -- to leave a decision until the next day so that you see it more clearly
"I would rather sleep on it than agree to something at eight in the evening."
Wiggle room -- the space you actually have to move in a negotiation
"I have some wiggle room on the volume, none on the date."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Pay attention to how much force each preference carries, and to who it is addressed to.

Two regions wanted the same slot in the treaty and Milan could fund only one of them this quarter. Andreas opened the call the way peers open calls when neither of them has authority over the other: I would rather we agreed a position between us than let them choose for us. Not an instruction. A preference, with the alternative attached to it.

Denise had weighed up three ways of splitting the slot, and she brought the sheet rather than a recommendation. On balance, option two: Central Europe takes the slot now, and Brazil holds off on its layer until January. Andreas said what everyone thinks and nobody says — that it looked like the lesser evil for him and the expensive one for her, which made him wonder what she wanted in return.

She named it before he had to ask. The reporting date was her sticking point, with no wiggle room in it at all; on everything else they could split the difference. Moving his broker deadline was a non-starter, so they agreed to find the two days elsewhere. Then we had better put both dates on the sheet as one line, she said — safe between peers, and a sentence she would never have sent upwards to Milan in that shape. Underneath it went the fallback: if January could not be funded, she would just as soon take the twelve-month extension as go back and ask twice. Andreas said he would sleep on it.

1. "I would rather we agreed a position between us." Why is the verb in the past?
A Because the preference is about what another person does with him. The past form is distance and courtesy, not past time.
B Because the agreement was reached before the call started.
C Because would rather always takes a past verb, in every sentence.
2. Why would "we had better put both dates on the sheet" be a risky sentence to send to Milan?
A Because had better is informal, and Milan expects formal English.
B Because had better always carries an unspoken "or else". Between peers that reads as care; said upwards it reads as a warning.
C Because had better can only be used with the first person plural.
3. Why does she name the sticking point before Andreas asks about it?
A An unspoken limit gets tested all meeting. Stated in one line, it stops being a negotiation and becomes a constraint both sides plan around.
B To signal that she is not willing to compromise on anything at all.
C Because the reporting date is the only part of the deal she understands well.
4. "I would just as soon take the twelve-month extension as go back and ask twice." What is she doing?
A Refusing the January funding outright, before it has been offered.
B Saying that both outcomes are equally bad for her region.
C Declining a second request without refusing anything: a soft preference that keeps the fallback on the table and costs nobody face.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Preference and How Much Force They Carry

GRAMMAR

All four sentences below are correct English. What separates them is not grammar — it is rank.

FormForce, and who you can say it toExample
would rather + plain verbyour own preference. Clear, neutral, safe in every direction. Never to after itI would rather hold off until January.
would rather + person + past verba preference about what somebody else does. The past form is courtesy, not time. I would rather you don't is the classic slipI would rather you didn't announce it yet.
would sooneridentical to would rather, slightly more formal in writingI would sooner wait for the renewals.
would just as soonthe softest thing in English: a preference you will not fight for. How to decline without refusingI would just as soon have it in writing.
had better + plain verba recommendation with a consequence behind it — always an unspoken or else. Fine downwards and between peers; upwards it reads as a warningWe had better confirm before Friday.
had better notthe negative goes after better. Never had not better, never had better toWe had better not put this to the committee yet.
may as well / might as wellthe option that is left when nothing better remains. Acceptance, not enthusiasm — and the room hears thatWe may as well split the difference.
Why it matters here"We had better decide this week" and "I would rather we decided this week" are the same request. Sent to your peer, both work. Sent to the person who allocates your capacity, only one of them does — and nobody will ever tell you which.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

You are writing to the global head in Italy, who allocates your capacity. You want the decision taken this week. You write:
A "I would rather we decided this week, while the January dates are still open."
B "We had better decide this week, while the January dates are still open."
C "You had better decide this week, while the January dates are still open."
You do not want your counterpart to release the figures before sign-off. The correct sentence is:
A "I would rather you don't release the figures before sign-off."
B "I would rather you didn't release the figures before sign-off."
C "I would rather that you not to release the figures before sign-off."
Neither side will move on the volume and the meeting has ten minutes left. You say:
A "We had better split the difference." — and let him hear the consequence.
B "We may as well split the difference." — nothing better is left, and you both know it.
C "I would just as soon split the difference." — and hope he proposes something else.
Your counterpart asks you to accept a change of scope tonight, at eight in the evening. The most useful reply is:
A "I would rather sleep on it and come back to you first thing, with the two conditions written down."
B "It is very difficult for me to say anything at this hour."
C "Fine, we may as well agree now and fix the details later."
There is exactly one thing you cannot concede in this negotiation. The strongest moment to say it is:
A Early and unprompted, named as your sticking point, with the areas where you do have wiggle room said in the same breath.
B At the end, once you have seen how much the other side is willing to give.
C Never explicitly — simply refuse every proposal that touches it.
You have to recommend an option that nobody in the room will enjoy. In English that is:
A A non-starter — an option that cannot be considered.
B The lesser evil — and saying so is more credible than pretending to like it.
C A fallback — the option you keep in reserve for later.

They say I would rather, we may as well, and occasionally you had better — and the whole balance of the scene turns on which one they choose and who they are saying it to.
