Lesson 10 -- Pre-class

Relative Clauses -- Describing People, Projects and Systems with Precision

Packing three facts into one sentence, the way a senior person describes a team. Key words: bottleneck, to scale, workaround, counterpart, to onboard, turnover, to consolidate, to mentor. Structure: relative clauses with who, that, which, whose and where -- defining (no commas) versus non-defining (between commas), plus when the pronoun can be dropped.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Bottleneck -- the one point in a process that slows everything else down
"The bottleneck that slowed every release was in the review step."
To scale -- to grow a system or a team so it can handle much more work
"My counterpart in Dublin is copying the model to scale it."
Workaround -- a temporary fix used when the normal solution is not available
"The workaround we used for six months has finally been retired."
Counterpart -- the person who has the same role as you in another team or company
"My counterpart in Dublin runs the same platform for Europe."
To onboard -- to bring a new person into a team and get them ready to work
"She mentors every person we onboard."
Turnover -- the rate at which people leave a company and new people replace them
"The two teams whose turnover was highest are now the most stable."
To consolidate -- to combine several separate things into one stronger unit
"The group we consolidated in March owns the release pipeline now."
To mentor -- to guide a less experienced person over a long period
"The engineer who designed our first pipeline still mentors every new hire."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Lucia is preparing the team slide for a review. She could write three short sentences, but she writes one. "The engineer who designed our first pipeline still mentors every new hire," she types. Then: "The bottleneck that slowed every release was in the review step." Notice the pattern. Each clause sits right after the noun it describes, and there are no commas, because the clause tells the reader which engineer and which bottleneck. Places and possession work the same way: "The lab where the models are tested is in Zurich," and "The two teams whose turnover was highest are now the most stable." Then she adds a detail about someone everyone already knows, and there the commas appear: "My counterpart in Dublin, who runs the same platform, reports the same numbers." Last line, and the pronoun disappears completely: "The workaround we used for six months has finally been retired."

1. Why is there no comma in "The engineer who designed our first pipeline still mentors every new hire"?
A The clause tells the reader which engineer she means, so it is essential.
B Commas are never used with the pronoun "who".
C The sentence is too short to need a comma.
2. In "My counterpart in Dublin, who runs the same platform, reports the same numbers", why are there commas?
A Because the sentence has more than ten words.
B Because the reader already knows who she means, so the clause is extra information.
C Because "who" always takes commas in American English.
3. In "The workaround we used for six months has finally been retired", where is the relative pronoun?
A It is "we" -- that word replaces "that".
B It is dropped, because it is the object of the clause.
C It is missing by mistake -- the sentence is incorrect.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

How to attach a detail to a noun -- and what the commas change.

PronounUseExample
whoPeople.The engineer who designed our first pipeline still mentors new hires.
that / whichThings and systems.The bottleneck that slowed every release was in the review step.
whosePossession (his, her, their).The two teams whose turnover was highest are now the most stable.
wherePlaces.The lab where the models are tested is in Zurich.
, who / , whichExtra information about something already identified.My counterpart in Dublin, who runs the same platform, reports the same numbers.
no pronounDrop it when it is the object of the clause: "The workaround we used lasted six months." Never drop it when it is the subject. Never use that between commas, and never repeat the noun inside the clause ("the system that we replaced it").

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each real situation.

You want to say which team fixed the problem. The interviewer has never heard of any of your teams. You say:
A "The team that fixed the bottleneck owns the pipeline now."
B "The team, that fixed the bottleneck, owns the pipeline now."
C "The team what fixed the bottleneck owns the pipeline now."
Which sentence is correct?
A "The manager that his team was consolidated is now in Dublin."
B "The manager whose team was consolidated is now in Dublin."
C "The manager which team was consolidated is now in Dublin."
Your director is already known to everyone in the room, and you want to add one extra detail. You say:
A "My director that joined in 2019 approved the plan."
B "My director, who joined in 2019, approved the plan."
C "My director, that joined in 2019, approved the plan."
Which sentence sounds natural to a fluent speaker?
A "The system that we replaced it was ten years old."
B "The system we replaced was ten years old."
C "The system was ten years old that we replaced."
