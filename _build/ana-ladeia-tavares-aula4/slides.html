Today’s Goal

Three Judgements, One Shape

1. The Words

due diligence, negligence, a safeguard, an oversight, to flag, accountable, to escalate...

2. One Structure

criticism, deduction and the thing that was never necessary

3. Your Review

judge a decision out loud without judging the person who made it

All three sentences look identical: a modal, then have, then a participle. What changes is the modal, and the modal is the whole meaning. Must have accuses. Might have leaves a door open.

One Question

What Should She Have Done?

A trainee reads a draft agreement and notices that one clause contradicts another. She writes a single line about it in an email to four people, and nobody answers. She does not raise it again.

Say it out loud, in two sentences: what should she have done, and how sure are you that the other four read the email?

Chapter 4: The Code

One Word Changes Everything

Criticism, deduction, and the thing that was never necessary

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"She should have escalated it, and she did not."

"They must have seen the email. It went to four people."

"Somebody might have caught it, but nobody was looking."

"He needn’t have worried. The clause was never triggered."

The blue part is identical in all four: have plus a past participle. Only the red word changes, and it decides whether you are criticising, concluding, guessing, or letting somebody off.

JobFormExample
Criticism of what was not doneshould have + participleShe should have escalated it.
Criticism of what was doneshould not have + participleThey should not have signed off on it that day.
A confident conclusion about the pastmust have + participleThey must have seen the email.
A confident NEGATIVE conclusioncannot have / could not have + participleShe cannot have read it. It arrived after she left.
A possibility, not a conclusionmight have / may have / could have + participleSomebody might have caught it.
Something done that was unnecessaryneed not have + participleHe need not have worried.
The two trapsnever must not have for a conclusion; never should ofNot they must not have seen it. Say they cannot have seen it.

In one line: the shape never changes. Choose the modal, and you have chosen how hard you are hitting.

The Note, and Two Voices

The Note That Decides Who Answers

INTERNAL REVIEW NOTE

MatterTwo clauses in conflict, draft of 14 February
Flagged bytrainee, one line, email of 14 February
Recipientsfour · no reply from any of them

Not donenot escalated · not raised again · no second read
Unclearwhether the four recipients opened it
The firm has a safeguard for exactly this: any draft with a flagged inconsistency needs a second read before sign-off. The safeguard was not used, and nobody switched it off. The panel is being asked to decide whether this was an oversight, a lapse or negligence, and the three words carry very different consequences.
1. Which safeguard existed, and what does the note say happened to it?
Any draft with a flagged inconsistency needs a second read before sign-off. It was not used, and nobody switched it off either.
2. Say the line "Unclear: whether the four recipients opened it" back as a full sentence, twice: once as a conclusion and once as a possibility.
Conclusion: they must have seen it, because it went to four people. Possibility: one of them might have opened it and forgotten about it.
3. The panel has to choose between three words. Why does the note say the choice matters so much?
Because oversight, lapse and negligence carry very different consequences, even though all three describe the same missed clause.
Listening 1 · The Partner

Oversight, Lapse, Negligence

A senior partner on the three words a review panel has to choose between. Sound first — no text.

1. What test does he say separates an oversight from negligence?
What a reasonable person in the same job would have done. If a reasonable person would have caught it, the word stops being oversight.
2. What does he say about the trainee who flagged the problem?
That she did the hardest part, which is noticing. What she should have done was escalate it, and he adds that at twenty-four almost nobody does, because escalating feels like accusing four people at once.
3. What does he say the panel almost always gets wrong?
Looking for the person instead of the safeguard. He says the safeguard failed first, and that somebody must have decided at some point that the second read was optional.
Listening 2 · The Trainee

I Said It Once and Then I Stopped

A trainee, two years later, on the email she sent and did not follow up. Sound first — no text.

1. How does she describe the email she sent, and why did she write it that way?
One line, at the bottom of a longer message about something else. She wrote it small on purpose, so that if she was wrong it would not look like she was making an accusation.
2. What does she say she was actually afraid of, and what does she say she was not afraid of?
She was not afraid of being wrong about the clause. She was afraid of being the person who made four senior people stop and check something, and then turned out to be wrong.
3. What does she say she does differently now?
She says it twice. Once in writing and once out loud to one named person, and she asks that person to tell her if it is nothing.
