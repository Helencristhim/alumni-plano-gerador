What They Said

Reporting a customer conversation to somebody who was not in it. Key words: to mention, to claim, to deny, to imply, to insist, to point out, to reassure, to pass along, on record, off the record, to take somebody at their word, a misunderstanding. Structure: reported speech -- statements, questions and requests.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To mention -- to say something briefly, in passing, without making it the main point
"He mentioned a second supplier, and then moved straight on to the weather."
To claim -- to say that something is true when it has not been checked
"He claimed the payment had already left his bank. I have not seen it."
To deny -- to say that something is not true, when somebody has suggested that it is
"He denied having bought from anybody else this season."
To imply -- to suggest something without saying it, so that the listener works it out
"He never said he was short of cash. He implied it three times."
To insist -- to say something firmly and repeat it, even when somebody disagrees
"He insisted the delay was the weather, and he said it three times."
To point out -- to draw somebody’s attention to a fact they had not considered
"I pointed out that the invoice had been overdue since March."
To reassure -- to say something that makes another person stop worrying
"I reassured him that nothing had been handed to legal, which was true."
To pass along -- to repeat information to the next person who needs it
"He asked me to pass it along to the team, so I put it in the note."
On record -- said in a way that can be quoted and attributed to the person who said it
"He put it on record that the second harvest would cover the balance."
Off the record -- told to you on the condition that you never say who told you
"Off the record does not mean secret. It means you may know it and may not name him."
To take somebody at their word -- to accept what somebody says without checking it, as a deliberate choice
"After twenty years I take him at his word, and I still write it down."
A misunderstanding -- a situation where two people believed different things, and neither of them lied
"It turned out to be a misunderstanding about the date, which is the best possible outcome."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An analyst comes off a call and somebody who was not on it has to make a decision. The customer said, in his own words: I will pay after the second harvest. Reported an hour later, it becomes he said he would pay after the second harvest. Notice the verb. Will has become would. That happens to everything. I am waiting for the co-operative becomes he told me he was waiting. We can cover it in April becomes he said they could cover it. Every verb takes one step back, because the moment of speaking is now in the past.

Questions do something extra: they stop being questions. Can you extend the deadline? becomes he asked if we could extend the deadline — no question mark, no inversion, and if where the answer would be yes or no. With a question word, the word stays and the order goes back to normal: What is the problem? becomes he asked what the problem was, never what was the problem. And an instruction becomes an infinitive: Send the statement again turns into he asked me to send the statement again. One small trap for a Portuguese speaker: you tell somebody something, but you say something to somebody. He said me does not exist.

The grammar is only half of it. The other half is which verb you choose, because each one commits you to something different. If you say he mentioned it, you are saying it was brief and not the main point. If you say he claimed it, you are saying he said it and you have not checked. If you say he denied it, somebody had suggested it first. If you say he implied it, he never said the words at all, and that is your reading of him. To insist means he repeated it against resistance, and to point out means he brought up a fact the other person had missed. Keep those apart, keep what is on record apart from what was off the record, and above all keep his words apart from your judgement. You can take him at his word and still write down that it was, possibly, a misunderstanding. What you cannot do is put both in the same sentence and leave your manager to guess which half is yours.

1. What happens to the verb when you report what somebody said?
A It takes one step back: will becomes would, am becomes was, can becomes could.
B It stays exactly as the person said it, inside quotation marks.
C It always becomes the present perfect.
2. Why is "he asked what the problem was" correct and "he asked what was the problem" not?
A Because "problem" cannot come before "was".
B Because a reported question stops being a question, so the word order goes back to normal.
C Because "ask" is never followed by a question word.
3. What is the difference between saying he claimed it and saying he implied it?
A None. They are two ways of saying the same thing.
B "Claimed" is polite and "implied" is rude.
C "Claimed" means he said it and you have not checked. "Implied" means he never said the words, and it is your reading of him.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech

GRAMMAR

Everything takes one step back, and a reported question stops being a question.

am / is / arewas / were"I am waiting" → He told me he was waiting.

Stage 2: Put the Report in Order

Order

You are reporting a customer call to your director. Put it in a logical order.

?"He said he would pay after the second harvest."
?"He told me he was waiting for the co-operative to settle with him."
?"He denied buying from another supplier this season."
?"He claimed the last payment had already left his bank."
?"He asked if we could extend the deadline to May."
?"This next part is mine, not his: I think he is short of cash."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The customer said "I will pay after the second harvest". You report:
A "He said he will pay after the second harvest."
B "He said he would pay after the second harvest."
C "He said me that he pays after the second harvest."
The customer asked "What is the problem?". You report:
A "He asked what was the problem."
B "He asked what the problem was."
C "He asked me what is the problem?"
He told you the payment had left his bank, and you have not checked. You report:
A "The payment has left his bank."
B "He claimed the payment had already left his bank. I have not seen it."
C "He implied the payment has left his bank."
Your director asks what you think, not what he said. The best answer starts:
A "He was very nervous on the phone, honestly."
B "This next part is mine, not his: I think he is short of cash."
C "He said that he is short of cash, more or less."
