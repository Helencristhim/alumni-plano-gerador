Vocabulary

A milestone -- an important point in a project or a process that shows how far you have come
"Lesson twenty is a milestone: a good moment to look back before we move on."
A rubric -- a table of criteria and levels used to evaluate a piece of work or a skill
"With a rubric, a feeling like I'm better becomes a score you can compare."
A self-assessment -- an evaluation you make of your own work, skills or progress
"My self-assessment was harder than the teacher's. I gave myself a two for small talk."
A comfort zone -- the situations where you feel safe and relaxed, because they are familiar
"Presenting data is my comfort zone. Small talk at the bar is not, or not yet."
A blind spot -- a weakness you have but do not notice yourself
"The recording showed my blind spot: I rush every number."
A stretch goal -- an ambitious goal that is difficult but still possible to reach
"My stretch goal for the next block is a full panel answer with no notes."
Muscle memory -- the ability to do something automatically, without thinking, after a lot of practice
"After twenty repetitions, the opener is muscle memory. I don't plan it anymore."
To track progress -- to measure and record how you improve over time
"I track progress with one recording a month. The difference is easy to hear."
To plateau -- to stop improving for a while after a period of fast progress
"Most adult learners plateau at some point. It is normal, and it does not last."
To build on -- to use something you already have as the base for more progress
"The pitch is solid now. In the next block, we build on it for Las Vegas."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In lesson 6 you learned used to for old habits that stopped: Our bank used to send payments by fax. It is followed by the base form of the verb, and it only talks about the past.

Two other structures look almost the same, but they mean something different. Be used to means that something is normal and familiar for you now: I'm used to presenting data in English. Get used to describes the process of it becoming normal: I'm getting used to starting conversations with strangers. You can use it in any tense: At first I wasn't used to the accents, but I got used to them.

Here is the detail that most learners miss. In be used to and get used to, the word to is a preposition, not part of an infinitive. So it is followed by a noun or a verb with -ing: I'm used to presenting, never I'm used to present.

Together, the three structures tell the whole story of learning something difficult: what you used to do, what you are used to now, and what you are still getting used to. That is exactly the story of a milestone.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Used To, Be Used To, Get Used To

GRAMMAR

Then, now, and on the way.

MeaningFormExample
Old habit that stoppedused to + base verbI used to rush past every number.
Normal and familiar nowbe used to + verb-ing / nounI'm used to presenting data in English.
Becoming normalget used to + verb-ing / nounI'm getting used to starting conversations with strangers.
Negativenot be used to · didn't use toI wasn't used to small talk. · I didn't use to enjoy it.
QuestionAre you used to + -ing? · Did you use to + verb?Are you used to speaking on panels?
In the pastgot used to + -ing / nounAt first it was hard, but I got used to the accents.

Never: I'm used to present · I'm getting used to start · I used to reading · I used to small talk now
