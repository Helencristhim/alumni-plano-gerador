Checkpoint -- Twenty Lessons In

Twenty lessons in: one long text about two legal traditions, the structures of the first nineteen lessons working together, and a first look at test-style questions. Key words: to skim, to scan, to paraphrase, to infer, a premise, a counterargument, to corroborate, a distractor, to rule out, a topic sentence, verbatim, to converge. Structure: integrated review -- tenses, the passive, relative clauses, conditionals, clefts and participle clauses.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. All twelve belong to the moment you read a long text under pressure: how to read it, how to argue about it, and how a test tries to trick you.

To skim -- to read something quickly to get the general idea
"Skim the passage first, and then go to the questions."
To scan -- to look quickly through a text for one specific piece of information
"Scan the second paragraph for the date of the Civil Code."
To paraphrase -- to express the same idea using different words
"In the summary, paraphrase the writer; don't copy her sentences."
To infer -- to reach a conclusion from evidence that is not stated directly
"The text never says it, but we can infer that the writer prefers flexibility."
A premise -- an idea that an argument is based on
"Her whole argument rests on one premise: that judges should not make law."
A counterargument -- an argument against another argument
"A strong answer mentions the counterargument and then responds to it."
To corroborate -- to support a statement with more evidence
"Two other witnesses can corroborate her version of the meeting."
A distractor -- a wrong option in a test that is designed to look correct
"A good distractor uses the exact words of the passage."
To rule out -- to decide that something is not possible or not correct
"Rule out two options before you read the question again."
A topic sentence -- the sentence, usually the first one, that gives the main idea of a paragraph
"If you are short of time, read the topic sentence of each paragraph."
Verbatim -- using exactly the same words as the original
"Don't quote the contract verbatim; explain what it means."
To converge -- to come closer together and become more similar
"Over the decades, the two legal systems have started to converge."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Pedro has been studying English with a plan for twenty lessons now, and this week he was asked to read a long article in English before a meeting with an American firm. The article, which was written by a professor of comparative law, explained how common law and civil law think.

In the past, he would have read it like a contract: every word, twice. This time he skimmed it first. What helped him most was the topic sentence of each paragraph. Having found the main idea in five minutes, he went back and scanned the text for the details he needed.

At the meeting, an American partner asked him why Brazilian lawyers always start with the code. If he hadn't read the article, he wouldn't have had such a clear answer. It is often said that you only understand your own system when you explain it to a foreigner, and, to some extent, Pedro agrees.

None of these structures is new. Each one comes from a different lesson. What is new is that they all appear in the same short text, and Pedro can read them without stopping.

1. How did Pedro read the article this time?
A He read every word twice, the way he reads a contract, because the meeting was important.
B He skimmed it first and then scanned it for details.
C He asked a colleague to summarize it.
2. Which sentence is a cleft sentence?
A What helped him most was the topic sentence of each paragraph.
B The article, which was written by a professor of comparative law, explained the two traditions.
C He went back to the text.
3. If he hadn't read the article, he wouldn't have had such a clear answer. Did he read the article?
A No, he didn't, and so he had no answer at the meeting.
B Yes, he did.
C We can't know from the sentence, because the conditional only describes an imaginary situation.
4. What is the main point of the last paragraph?
A The structures are new.
B Pedro still needs to stop at every structure, because there are too many of them in one text.
C The structures are old; what is new is reading them together without stopping.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Inventory of Nineteen Lessons

GRAMMAR

There is no new grammar in this lesson. This table puts the main structures of the first nineteen lessons in one place, with an example from the reading.

StructureLessonWhat it doesExample
Present perfect (continuous)Lesson 1Something that started in the past and continues now.He has been studying for twenty lessons.
Passive voiceLesson 2The action matters more than who did it.He was asked to read an article.
Relative clausesLesson 3Extra information about a noun.The article, which was written by a professor, ...
Third and mixed conditionalsLessons 9 and 10An imaginary past and its result.If he hadn't read it, he wouldn't have had an answer.
Reporting verbs in the passiveLesson 12A general opinion, without a speaker.It is often said that...
HedgingLesson 13Making a claim less absolute.To some extent, Pedro agrees.
Participle clausesLesson 16Two actions in one sentence.Having found the main idea, he scanned for details.
Cleft sentencesLesson 17Putting the focus on one part.What helped him most was the topic sentence.

One habit for this week: when you read anything long in English, stop once per paragraph and name one structure from this table. After a few days, you will stop needing to name them.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are reading a long text under time pressure, or talking about it afterwards. Choose the best option.

1. You have three minutes for a long passage. What do you do first?
A Read every word carefully from the beginning, because the details are where the questions come from.
B Skim it and read the topic sentences.
C Go straight to the last paragraph.
2. A question asks for the year of the French Civil Code. What do you do?
A Read the whole passage again from the start.
B Guess, because years are hard to find.
C Scan the text for a number.
3. One option repeats a sentence from the text word for word, but answers a different question. It is probably:
A a distractor
B the correct answer, because it is in the text and the test always rewards the exact words of the passage
C a topic sentence
4. A colleague says common law and civil law are exactly the same today. You disagree politely:
A You're wrong.
B To some extent, they are converging, but they still start from different places.
C I think that perhaps maybe there could be some small differences, but I'm not really sure about it.
5. In a summary, how should you use the writer's sentences?
A Paraphrase them in your own words.
B Copy the best ones verbatim, so that you never change the meaning of what the writer said.
C Leave them out completely.
