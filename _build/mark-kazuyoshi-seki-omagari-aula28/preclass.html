Lesson 28 -- Pre-class

Some People Think So, Others Do Not -- Ellipsis and Substitution

Stop repeating the words the other person just used. English deletes them and leaves one short word behind: "I hope so", "So did I", "Neither do I", "the ones who voted no", "I would love to". Get the auxiliary wrong and the answer stops working. Key words: consensus, divisive, common ground, side with, back up, push back, sway, neutral, long-winded, redundant, trim, poll.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. All twelve describe what happens when a room disagrees.

Consensus -- an opinion that a whole group finally accepts, so the argument is over
"A result like that is not a consensus. It is a school divided in half."
Divisive -- causing people to disagree strongly instead of bringing them together
"She will not decide a divisive question on a fifty-one per cent vote."
Common ground -- the points that two opposite sides already agree on
"Everybody in the building wants more sleep. That is the only common ground we have."
Side with -- to support one person or group in a disagreement
"Several teachers sided with them, and so did the two drivers."
Back up -- to say publicly that somebody else is right
"Nobody in the hall backed him up, so he dropped the point."
Push back -- to argue against an idea instead of accepting it
"They did not disagree with a word of that. They pushed back on the consequence."
Sway -- to change what somebody thinks
"That single question swayed at least forty people in the room."
Neutral -- not supporting either side in an argument
"The head of the school has stayed neutral throughout."
Long-winded -- using far more words than the idea actually needs
"The first three speakers were long-winded, and the hall stopped listening."
Redundant -- repeating information the listener already has
"Every argument was redundant: it had already been made, twice, in the comments."
Trim -- to cut away the parts that are not needed
"She trimmed the whole case down to four sentences."
Poll -- a set of questions used to find out what a group thinks
"The poll closed on Wednesday with four hundred and twelve answers."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the report and answer the questions. Every bold piece is standing in for a longer phrase that was deleted.

The poll closed on Wednesday with four hundred and twelve answers. The question was one line long: should the school day start at nine instead of eight? Fifty-one per cent said yes and forty-three said no, which is not a consensus. It is a photograph of a school divided in half.

The ones who voted yes said the first lesson is wasted. The ones who voted no did not disagree with a word of that; they pushed back on the consequence instead. If the day starts an hour later it also ends an hour later. Several teachers sided with them, and so did the two drivers who came to the meeting.

The first three speakers were long-winded, and by the second one the hall had stopped listening, because every argument was redundant. Then a student from the ninth year trimmed the case down to four sentences and asked the yes camp one question: who pays for the extra hour of buses? Nobody in the room could answer, and neither could the office on Tuesday.

Will a second poll settle it? Most students think so. The head of the school, who has stayed neutral throughout, does not. Asked whether she would decide before March, she said not. She was invited to speak at the March meeting and answered that she would be glad to.

1. In "Several teachers sided with them, and so did the two drivers", what has been deleted?
A The verb phrase "side with them" — "did" is standing in for the whole thing.
B The subject "the two drivers", which is repeated at the end for emphasis.
C Nothing has been deleted; "so did" is a fixed expression with no meaning.
2. Why is it "neither could the office" and not "neither the office could"?
A Because "office" is a singular noun and needs the verb after it.
B Because after "so" and "neither" the auxiliary comes before the subject.
C Because the sentence is a question and questions invert the subject.
3. What is "so" replacing in "Most students think so"?
A The single word "yes", which cannot follow the verb "think".
B The whole clause "that a second poll will settle it".
C The name of the person who asked the question in the line before.
4. In "she would be glad to", why does the sentence stop after "to"?
A The verb after "to" was just said, so English keeps the "to" and deletes the rest.
B The writer ran out of space and the sentence is unfinished.
C "Glad" is an adjective that never takes a verb after it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Ellipsis and Substitution

GRAMMAR

English deletes what both people already have in their heads and leaves one short word in its place. The word you leave behind is not free: it has to match the sentence you are answering.

ToolWhat it replacesExample
so / nota whole clause, after think, hope, believe, guess, be afraid"Will it pass?" — "I think so." / "I am afraid not."
do / does / dida whole verb phrase already said"She trains harder than I do."
so + auxiliary + subjectagreeing with something positive"I get the early bus." — "So do I."
neither / nor + auxiliary + subjectagreeing with something negative"I did not vote." — "Neither did I."
one / onesa countable noun already named"The old buses are slow. The new ones are worse."
bare "to"the verb after to, when it was just said"Do you want to speak?" — "I would love to."
The three traps"I think yes" does not exist. The auxiliary has to match: I am tired takes so am I, not so do I. And after "to", stop — but keep the "to": "I would love", on its own, is not a sentence.

Stage 2: Put the Argument in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the five parts of a short debate argument in order.

?Ask the other side the one question they have not prepared for.
?Name the common ground, so the other side hears that you understood them.
?Say what would change your mind, so the room hears somebody arguing rather than repeating.
?Say which side you are on in one short sentence.
?Give the single strongest reason you have, and give only that one.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

Think of something your class or your family is genuinely divided about. In four sentences: say which side you are on, name the common ground, give your single strongest reason, and ask the other side the one question they cannot answer. Use at least one short answer with "so" or "neither" and at least one "the ones who". Do not say any group of words twice.
