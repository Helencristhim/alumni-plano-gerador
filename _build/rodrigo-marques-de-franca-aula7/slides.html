Lesson 7 · The Table

If You Move on Price

Every move at a negotiating table is a conditional sentence. One of them is an offer. The other is a question disguised as an offer, and today you get both.

Chapter 1: First Words

The Last Thing You Negotiated

Any negotiation, professional or not. What did you want, what did the other side want, and what was the one point that nearly stopped it?

Report the other side’s position without naming anybody: it is understood that, was described as, is thought to be.

Today’s Goal

The Offer, and the Question

1. The Words

a sticking point, leverage, a red line...

2. Two Structures

if you move, we sign · if you moved, we would sign

3. Your Table

explore, trade, hold firm, or walk

One of these two sentences commits you. The other one does not, and lets you find out what the other side wants without giving anything away. Choosing the wrong one is expensive in both directions.

One Question

How Do You Test an Idea Without Offering It?

You want to know whether the other side would accept a shorter notice period. You are not ready to offer anything. Say it out loud, right now.

Then the harder one: how do you say no to a term without ending the conversation?

Chapter 3: The Code

Which One Commits You?

The offer, and the question that looks like one

Grammar Discovery

Two Pairs, One Difference

"If you move on the cap, we will sign this week."

"If you moved on the cap, we would sign this week."

"If we do not settle the indemnity, my client will walk away."

"If the indemnity were capped, that would change the whole conversation."

In each pair the words are almost identical. Ask the only question that matters at a table: which of the two could the other side write down and hold you to? The if half and the then half both change, together.

JobFormExample
A real offerif + present, will + verbIf you move, we will sign.
A hypothesis you are testingif + past, would + verbIf you moved, we would sign.
A real warningif + present negative, willIf we do not settle it, they will walk away.
Softening the hypothesiswere, for all subjectsIf the cap were lower, we would accept.
Same idea, no ifunless · provided that · as long asProvided that the cap moves, we sign.
Even softermight / could instead of wouldwe might be able to move on the timetable
The trapnever will in the if halfNot if you will move, we sign.

In one line: present plus will is an offer they can hold you to. Past plus would is a question you can ask without giving anything away.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Table, at Full Speed

One sticking point, two recordings, one summary

Dialogue

The Cap, Third Call

S
We are at the same place we were on Tuesday. The cap is the only sticking point left.
R
Agreed, and let me ask you something before either of us puts anything on the table. If the cap were twelve months rather than unlimited, would the carve-outs still be an issue for you?
S
Honestly? Less of one. Fraud would have to stay outside it. That is not something I can move on.
R
Fraud outside the cap is not a problem. It never is. So here is where we are: if you accept twelve months with a carve-out for fraud, we will sign this week.
S
Twelve is low. I could take my client eighteen. If I go back with twelve he will ask me what I traded for it.
R
Then let us give you an answer to that. If you come down to twelve, we will concede the notice period and go from thirty days to ninety.
S
That is a real trade. I think we have an agreement in principle, and I still have to put it to him.
R
Understood. And to be clear about one thing, so that nobody is surprised on Friday: unlimited liability is a red line for us. Everything else is a conversation.
Comprehension

About Sarah

1. What is the one thing Sarah says she cannot move on, and how does she signal that?
Fraud staying outside the cap. She says it plainly: that is not something I can move on.
2. Why does Sarah say twelve months is difficult, even though she does not reject it?
Because it is low, she could take her client eighteen, and if she goes back with twelve he will ask her what she traded for it.
3. She ends by saying they have an agreement in principle. What is she NOT saying?
That it is agreed. In principle means the idea is settled and the details are not, and she adds that she still has to put it to her client.
Listening 1 · The Night Before

Three Columns on One Page

A negotiator on what she writes before anybody speaks. Sound first — no text.

1. What are her three columns, and which one does she say people never prepare?
What she must have, what she would like, and what she is willing to give away to get the first column. The third is the one nobody prepares.
2. What happens, according to her, if you arrive with nothing to trade?
Every request sounds like a demand, and the other side has no way to say yes without losing.
3. What does she say about a walk-away point that is not written down?
That it is not a walk-away point at all. It is a mood. She has crossed her own line twice in twenty years and regretted it both times.
Listening 2 · The Deal We Did Not Do

The Question That Ended It

Somebody explaining why they walked away three years ago. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say about the commercial terms, and what would have happened if the discussion had stayed there?
The terms were fine: a reasonable cap and a tight but workable timetable. He thinks they would have signed within a fortnight.
2. What actually killed the deal, and what happened every time they pushed back?
One clause and the way it was defended. Every time they pushed back on the indemnity, the other side explained why they were misunderstanding it. By the fourth call he stopped believing the misunderstanding was theirs.
3. What was the client's question, and what does he say about it now?
If they behave like this while they still want something from us, how will they behave in eighteen months when they do not. He had no answer, and neither of them has ever mentioned it with regret.

The Page That Goes to the Client

Next step: they take twelve months to their client on Friday. If it is accepted, we sign next week. If they come back at eighteen, we would need something in exchange, and the only thing left to trade is the timetable. Nothing above is binding until the mark-up is signed.
1. Two lines in the last paragraph use different conditionals. What is the difference between them?
If it is accepted, we sign is a real plan for a likely event. If they come back at eighteen, we would need something is a hypothesis about a move that has not happened, and it commits nobody.
2. What is the difference between the cap line and the carve-out line?
The carve-out is agreed. The cap is agreed in principle, which means the idea is settled and it still has to be put to the other client.
3. Why is the last sentence there at all?
Because nothing in the summary is binding until the mark-up is signed, and a client reading a tidy table can easily believe that it is.

The Table, Three Times

Role-Play 1 of 3 — Guided

Explore, Then Offer

Scenario: The cap is the only sticking point. Test one idea without committing to it, listen to the answer, then make a real offer with something traded in exchange. Finish by naming your red line so that nobody is surprised later.

Keyword chips:

if the cap were if you accept, we will we would concede a red line
Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Negotiating Without Leverage

Scenario: This time you need the deal and they do not. They have another offer on the table and no deadline. Find two things you can trade that cost you little, test both as hypotheses, and get one concession without pretending you have leverage you do not have.

Keywords:

would it help if to trade off a counterproposal
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Three Minutes on a Real Negotiation

Scenario: Take a negotiation you were actually in, with no names. Say what the sticking point was, what each side had to trade, what you would have accepted that you never offered, and where your red line was. Then say whether you would run it the same way today.

No keywords, no notes, three minutes.
