Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

If You Move on Price -- Negotiating With Conditionals

One sentence is an offer the other side can hold you to. The other is a question that costs you nothing. Key words: a sticking point, to concede, leverage, to push back, a deal-breaker, to hold firm, a counterproposal, to trade something off, to walk away, a red line, to split the difference, in principle. Structure: first and second conditionals.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A sticking point -- the one issue that is stopping an agreement from closing
"Everything else is agreed. The indemnity is the only sticking point."
To concede -- to give something up in order to move the negotiation forward
"We conceded the timetable and held firm on the cap."
Leverage -- the advantage that gives you the power to get what you want
"Their deadline is our leverage, and they know that we know."
To push back -- to resist a proposal without rejecting it outright
"Every time we pushed back on that clause, they explained it again."
A deal-breaker -- the term that would make you leave the table rather than sign
"Unlimited liability is a deal-breaker. Everything else is negotiable."
To hold firm -- to refuse to move from your position, however long the pressure lasts
"We held firm for three weeks and they moved on the last day."
A counterproposal -- an offer made in response to one you are not willing to accept
"Rather than say no, we sent a counterproposal the same afternoon."
To trade something off -- to accept less of one thing in exchange for more of another
"We traded the notice period off against a lower cap."
To walk away -- to end the negotiation rather than accept what is on the table
"We walked away three years ago and nobody has regretted it since."
A red line -- a condition you will not cross, whatever is offered
"Unlimited liability is a red line for us, and it always has been."
To split the difference -- to settle halfway between the two positions on the table
"They asked for eighteen months, we offered six, and we split the difference."
In principle -- agreed as an idea, with the details still to be settled
"We agree in principle. The number is another conversation."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Third call, and the cap is the only sticking point left. He does not open with an offer. He opens with a question: if the cap were twelve months rather than unlimited, would the carve-outs still be an issue? Nothing in that sentence commits him to anything, and the answer tells him what he needs to know — fraud has to stay outside the cap, and that is her red line.

Only now does he put something on the table: if you accept twelve months with a carve-out for fraud, we will sign this week. That one she could write down and hold him to. She pushes back — twelve is low, she could sell eighteen — and asks the question every negotiator asks: what did you trade for it? So he gives her an answer to take back: if you come down to twelve, we will concede the notice period, from thirty days to ninety.

That is a real trade, and they have an agreement in principle. Not an agreement: the idea is settled and the number still has to go to her client. Before they hang up he says the thing that saves everybody a bad Friday: unlimited liability is a deal-breaker, and if they cannot settle it, his client will walk away. Neither of them treats that as a threat. It is information, delivered early, by somebody who held firm on one point and had prepared what he was willing to give away on the other three. If he had arrived with nothing to trade, every request would have sounded like a demand.

1. "If the cap were twelve months, would the carve-outs still be an issue?" Why this form and not "if the cap is twelve months, will the carve-outs be an issue"?
A Because the past form is more polite, and politeness is the point.
B Because past plus would is a hypothesis he is testing. It commits him to nothing, and he is not ready to offer twelve months yet.
C Because the cap is in the past.
2. "If you accept twelve months, we will sign this week." What is different about this sentence?
A It is a real offer. Present plus will is something the other side can write down and hold him to.
B It means exactly the same as the previous one, only shorter.
C It is a warning about what will go wrong.
3. Why does the text say the agreement is only in principle?
A Because neither side actually wants the deal.
B Because the idea is settled and the number still has to go to her client, so nothing is binding yet.
C Because the notice period was conceded and concessions cannot be binding.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- First and Second Conditionals

GRAMMAR

One of these two sentences commits you. The other lets you find out what they want, for free.

JobFormExample
A real offerif + present, will + verbIf you move, we will sign.
A hypothesis you are testingif + past, would + verbIf you moved, we would sign.
A real warningif + present negative, willIf we do not settle it, they will walk away.
Softening the hypothesiswere, with every subjectIf the cap were lower, we would accept.
Same idea, no ifunless · provided that · as long asUnless the period comes down, we cannot sign.
Even softermight / could instead of wouldwe might be able to move on the timetable
Never in the if halfno will, no wouldNot if you will move, not if the cap would be.

Never: if you will move, we sign · if you moved, we will sign · if the cap would be lower · unless the period does not come down · if I would be you

Stage 2: Put the Negotiation in Order

Order

One sticking point, one call. Put your side of it in the order that works.

?"Agreed, the cap is the only sticking point left."
?"If the cap were twelve months, would the carve-outs still be an issue?"
?"Fraud outside the cap is not a problem. It never is."
?"So, if you accept twelve months, we will sign this week."
?"And if you come down to twelve, we will concede the notice period."
?"One last thing, so nobody is surprised: unlimited liability is a red line."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to test an idea without offering it. You say:
A "If you will lower the cap, we sign."
B "If the cap were twelve months, would the carve-outs still be an issue?"
C "Lower the cap to twelve months."
You are ready to make a real offer they can rely on. You say:
A "If you accept twelve months with a carve-out for fraud, we will sign this week."
B "If you accepted twelve months, we will sign this week."
C "If you would accept twelve months, we sign."
You need to name a limit without sounding aggressive. You say:
A "Accept it or we leave."
B "Unlimited liability is a red line for us. Everything else is a conversation."
C "We would maybe possibly consider not accepting that."
They ask whether the deal is done. It is not, quite. You say:
A "Yes, we are agreed."
B "We are agreed in principle, and nothing is binding until it is signed."
C "We split the difference, so it is a deal-breaker."
