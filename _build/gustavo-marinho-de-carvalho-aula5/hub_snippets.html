What You Must, What You Should -- The Rules Nobody Wrote Down

Every workplace runs on two sets of rules, and only one of them is written down. Key words: an expectation, to be expected to, a rule of thumb, to get away with, unwritten, to be frowned upon, to take the initiative, a heads-up. Structure: modals of obligation, advice and possibility.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An expectation -- what people believe is going to happen, or should happen
"Nobody set that rule. It is just an expectation, and everybody has it."
To be expected to -- to have other people believing that you will do something
"You are expected to reply the same day, and nobody will ever say so."
A rule of thumb -- a practical guide that is usually right, but not exact
"As a rule of thumb, if it needs three emails, it needs a call."
To get away with -- to do something wrong or unusual and have nothing happen
"You can get away with it once. The second time somebody says something."
Unwritten -- not written down anywhere, and everybody knows it
"That is an unwritten rule here, and it took me a year to learn it."
To be frowned upon -- to be considered wrong or rude by most people, without being forbidden
"Emailing at nine at night is not banned. It is just frowned upon."
To take the initiative -- to act first, without waiting to be asked
"Nobody asked her to call the supplier. She took the initiative."
A heads-up -- a warning that gives somebody time to prepare
"Give her a heads-up before the meeting, not during it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before you choose the word, ask yourself one question: where is the rule coming from? If it comes from outside — a contract, a client, the law — English uses have to or need to. We have to confirm every date in writing. If the obligation is one you put on yourself, English prefers must. I must call her before the end of the day. Nobody is forcing you. You decided.

And then there is the pair that costs a Brazilian speaker the most, because Portuguese covers both with the same expression. You do not have to reply tonight means there is no obligation: you may reply, and nothing happens if you do not. You must not reply tonight means it is forbidden. They are not two versions of the same idea. They are opposites, and if you say the second one to a client when you mean the first, you have just prohibited something you were trying to permit.

The rest is easier. Should and ought to are advice, and the other person can still decide otherwise. Might, may and could are possibility, not certainty, and they are the safest way to suggest something to somebody senior. One shape rule holds for all of them: after a modal, no to and no -s. I must send it, never I must to send it. She should give him a heads-up, never she shoulds.

1. According to the text, when does English prefer must over have to?
A When the obligation is one you put on yourself.
B When the obligation comes from a contract.
C When you are talking to a client rather than a colleague.
2. What is the difference between “you do not have to reply” and “you must not reply”?
A The second is simply a more formal version of the first.
B The first says there is no obligation. The second says it is forbidden. They are opposites.
C There is no real difference in spoken English.
3. What shape rule does the text give for all modals?
A They always take an -s in the third person.
B They always need to before the next verb.
C After a modal there is no to and no -s.

Stage 2: Put the Briefing in Order

Order

You are explaining how your side works to somebody new. Put the six lines in a logical order.

?"Let me tell you how things work on our side."
?"We have to confirm every date in writing. That one is not negotiable."
?"You should give us a heads-up when something is at risk."
?"You do not have to reply outside working hours. Nobody expects it."
?"And there is one unwritten rule that nobody will ever tell you."
?"What is it like on your side? I would rather ask than guess."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The client asks whether she needs to answer your email tonight. It can wait. You say:
A You must not reply tonight.
B You do not have to reply tonight, it can wait until the morning.
C You should not reply tonight, it is forbidden.
There is a rule in the contract about written confirmation. You say:
A We have to confirm every date in writing.
B We should to confirm every date in writing.
C We must to confirm every date in writing.
You want to suggest something to somebody senior, without pushing. You say:
A You have to check with them first.
B It might be worth checking with them first.
C You must checking with them first.
Your client mentions that something you did is “quietly frowned upon” there. The best response is:
A Apologise at length and promise it will never happen again.
B Ask what else you should know that nobody would ever write down.
C Explain that in your country it is completely normal, and leave it there.
