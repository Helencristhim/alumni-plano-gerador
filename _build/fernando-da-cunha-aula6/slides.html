The structure of the day is the one English uses to attach a description to a thing without starting a new sentence: the supplier that we contacted in March is a different piece of information from our freight forwarder, which we have used for six years — and the difference is a pair of commas.

One Question

Which Supplier, Exactly?

Two sentences, out loud, right now.

One that identifies which supplier you mean, out of several. One that adds a fact about a supplier there is only one of. In English those two sentences are punctuated differently, and using the wrong one changes what you have actually said.

Chapter 4: The Code

The Thing, and Which Thing You Mean

Two relative clauses, one pair of commas

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"The supplier that we contacted in March has since closed."

"Our freight forwarder, which we have used for six years, called us first."

"The plant whose adhesive we depend on is in one country."

"That is the risk nobody had accounted for."

Every sentence has a thing, and then a description attached to it. In one of them the description sits between commas, and in the others it does not. Try taking each description out and see which sentences still make sense.

JobFormExample

The testtake the clause outWithout it, sentence 1 is unclear. Sentence 2 still works.

Where the preposition goesnaturally at the endthe supplier we sourced from · the risk we had not accounted for.
The trapthat never follows a commaNot our forwarder, that we have used. Say , which we have used.

In one line: no commas tells them WHICH one; commas just add a fact about the only one there is.

The Log, and Two Voices

Real Document

The Log They Write While It Is Happening

DISRUPTION LOG — Q3
operations · one line per event, cause added when known
Day 1Container held up at port — customs clearance pending
Day 3Two further containers held — clearance now 9 days, no reason given
Day 8Root cause identified: adhesive shortage, upstream
OriginSingle plant, one country, down since week -3
Sources affected4 of our 5 qualified suppliers, all buying from it

Freight forwarderPerformed normally throughout
ActionStockpile 6 weeks on single-source items · qualify second source
The log records what was visible before it records what was true: for eight days the entry that mattered was the one about the port, which was never the cause. The adhesive, whose plant had been down for three weeks before anybody here noticed, reached us through four other companies. The freight forwarder, which we had been ready to blame on day three, had done nothing wrong.
1. Why does the log spend eight days on the port before naming the cause?
Because the port was the visible delay. The log records what was visible before it records what was true.
2. One sentence in the note uses commas around its description and one does not. What is the difference in meaning?
"The adhesive, whose plant had been down..." adds a fact about the one adhesive. A clause without commas would be saying which adhesive out of several.
3. What does the note say about the freight forwarder, and why is it there?
That it performed normally and had done nothing wrong, although they had been ready to blame it on day three. It is there because the log is meant to correct the first assumption, not preserve it.
Listening 1 · The Logistics Manager

The Week Nothing Moved

A logistics manager on the week the chain stopped, and where it actually stopped. Sound first — no text.

1. What happened on the Monday, and why did nobody panic?
A container was held up at the port, which happens, so it did not look unusual.
2. Where were they looking for the problem, and where was it actually?
They were looking at the port, because that was where they could see something going wrong. The problem was an adhesive from a plant in another country that had gone down three weeks earlier.
3. What is the lesson she says she would give anybody?
That the place where you can see the delay is almost never the place where it started.
Listening 2 · The Researcher

One Plant, Everybody

A supply chain researcher on why nobody sees a single point of failure coming. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does he say nobody sees the whole picture?
Because each buyer sees only their own suppliers, and each of those sees theirs. Nobody has the whole map.
2. What can happen when a company thinks it has five sources?
Four of the five may ultimately be buying from the same plant, so in practice there is one source, and they find out on the day it closes.
3. What is the human problem he describes, and what fix does he propose?
Attention goes where the money is, so the cheap component nobody watches is the one that stops the line. The fix is to take the bill of materials, find everything with one source, and ask what a month without it costs.
