Navigating the Unexpected -- When the Chain Breaks

A container is late, and eight days later you find out the delay started three weeks earlier in a country you had never thought about. Key words: a freight forwarder, customs clearance, a backlog, a shortage, to stockpile, redundancy, a defect, a specification, to be held up, a single point of failure, a knock-on effect, to ride something out. Structure: defining and non-defining relative clauses.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A freight forwarder -- the company that arranges the transport of your goods across borders
"Our freight forwarder handles everything from the port to the plant."
Customs clearance -- the official approval that lets goods enter or leave a country
"Customs clearance took nine days instead of two, and nobody told us why."
A backlog -- orders or work that have built up and are waiting to be dealt with
"By Friday we had a backlog of three hundred orders and one line running."
A shortage -- a situation in which there is not enough of something to meet demand
"There was a shortage of that adhesive across the whole market, not just for us."
To stockpile -- to build up a store of something before you need it
"After that year we started stockpiling six weeks of the critical material."
Redundancy -- spare capacity kept on purpose, so that one failure does not stop everything
"Redundancy costs money every month and saves the company once a decade."
A defect -- a fault in a product that makes it fall short of the agreed standard
"The defect rate on that batch was four times what the contract allowed."
A specification -- the exact written description a product has to meet
"They matched the price. What they did not match was the specification."
To be held up -- to be delayed by something outside your control
"The container was held up at the port for over a week."
A single point of failure -- the one part of a system whose failure stops everything after it
"One adhesive, one supplier, one country. That is a single point of failure."
A knock-on effect -- expression: a consequence that spreads from one problem into the next thing
"The knock-on effect reached the drugstore chains three weeks later."
To ride something out -- expression: to get through a difficult period without changing course
"We rode it out because we had the stock. The company next door did not."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A container is held up at a port and everybody looks at the port, because the port is where the delay is visible. Eight days later the operations team finds the real cause: an adhesive, made by a single plant in a country nobody in the company had ever had a reason to think about. Four of their five qualified suppliers were quietly buying from it. On paper they had five sources. In practice they had one, and the knock-on effect reached their clients three weeks later.

To tell that story you need to attach descriptions to things. English does it with a relative clause, and the punctuation carries the meaning. The supplier that we contacted in March has since closed. No commas: the clause tells you which supplier, out of several, and if you remove it the sentence stops making sense. Now compare: Our freight forwarder, which we have used for six years, called us first. Commas: there is only one forwarder, and the clause is extra. Remove it and nothing is lost.

Two details finish it. After a comma, English uses which or who, never that — this is the mistake a Portuguese speaker makes almost every time, because one word covers both jobs there. And the preposition goes at the end, where it sounds natural: the supplier we sourced from, the risk nobody had accounted for, the port the container was held up at. Use whose for possession: the plant whose adhesive we depend on. None of this is decoration. In a bad week, saying exactly which supplier you mean is most of the work.

1. Why does "The supplier that we contacted in March has since closed" have no commas?
A Because the sentence is short.
B Because the clause says which supplier out of several, and removing it breaks the sense.
C Because "that" never takes commas in any sentence.
2. What is wrong with "Our freight forwarder, that we have used for six years, called us"?
A After a comma English uses which or who, never that.
B The commas should not be there at all.
C "Forwarder" cannot take a relative clause.
3. Where does the preposition go in "the supplier we sourced ___"?
A Before the noun: from the supplier we sourced.
B At the end: the supplier we sourced from.
C It is left out entirely.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses -- Defining and Non-defining

GRAMMAR

Same words, different commas, different meaning. One tells you which one; the other just adds a fact.

JobFormExample
Says WHICH one you meanno commas · that / which / whoThe supplier that we contacted in March has closed.
Adds extra informationcommas · which / who (never that)Our freight forwarder, which we have used for six years, called.
The testtake the clause outWithout it, sentence 1 is unclear. Sentence 2 still works.
Possessionwhose + nounThe plant whose adhesive we depend on.
You can drop itwhen the clause has its own subjectthe risk (that) nobody had accounted for.
Where the preposition goesnaturally at the endthe supplier we sourced from · the risk we had not accounted for.
The trapthat never follows a commaNot our forwarder, that we have used. Say , which we have used.

Never: our forwarder, that we have used · the supplier, that we contacted in March, has closed · the plant which its adhesive we depend on · the risk for which nobody had accounted for · the port at which the container was held up at.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to identify which supplier you mean, out of several. You say:
A "The supplier, that we contacted in March, has closed."
B "The supplier that we contacted in March has closed."
C "The supplier what we contacted in March has closed."
You want to add a fact about your only freight forwarder. You say:
A "Our freight forwarder that we have used for six years called us."
B "Our freight forwarder, that we have used for six years, called us."
C "Our freight forwarder, which we have used for six years, called us."
A client asks whose fault the delay was. The strongest answer is:
A "It was the port, and there was nothing we could do."
B "The port was the visible delay. The cause was an adhesive whose plant had been down for three weeks."
C "There was a problem in the chain, as you know."
You want to say the component that everything depends on is cheap. You say:
A "The component the whole line depends on costs almost nothing."
B "The component which the whole line depends is almost nothing of cost."
C "The component, that the whole line depends on, costs almost nothing."
