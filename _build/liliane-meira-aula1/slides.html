Chapter 3: The Code

The Ten Years, and What Came Out of Them

Two forms of the same tense, doing two different jobs

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"I have been researching this question since 2015."

"The premise has changed twice."

"She has been sitting with the same question for ten years."

"I have not published anything yet."

Every red part starts the same way: have or has. But two of them carry an -ing and two do not, and the blue part tells you why.

JobFormExample
The activity, still runninghave / has been + verb-ingI have been researching it since 2015.
The result, already countablehave / has + past participleI have written three essays.
How longusually the -ing formHow long have you been working on it?
How manynever the -ing formThe premise has changed twice.
Negativehave / has not + participle (+ yet)I have not published anything yet.
State verbsknow, believe, own: no -ingI have known his work for years.
The trapnever the simple presentNot I research this since 2015.

In one line: -ing for the years you have spent, no -ing for the things those years produced.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Studio Visit, at Full Speed

One curator, three recordings, one wall label

Dialogue

A Studio Visit, Thursday Morning

D
So this is where it happens. I have to admit, I came expecting finished pieces, and there is nothing on the walls.
L
There is nothing on the walls. What you are looking at is the research. This is practice-based research, and the making and the thinking are one act.
D
Then help me. What is the premise?
L
That illness produces a kind of knowledge medicine has no room for. I have been testing that idea since 2015.
D
Ten years on one idea. What has changed in that time?
L
The premise has not changed. The strands have. I started with photographs, and for the last three years I have been working almost entirely with the field notes and the recorded voices.
D
And the outcome? I ask because a board will ask me.
L
The outcome is that somebody else can enter the question now. That is a tentative answer, and I would rather give you a tentative one than a tidy one.
Comprehension

About Daniel

1. What did Daniel expect to find in the studio, and what did he find instead?
He expected finished pieces, and there is nothing on the walls. What is there is the research itself.
2. Why does Daniel ask about the outcome, and who is the question really coming from?
Because a board will ask him. The question is coming from the institution behind him, not only from his own curiosity.
3. What does Daniel notice about the length of the project, and what does he want to know about it?
That she has spent ten years on one idea. He wants to know what has changed in that time.
Listening 1 · An Artist

When Will the Work Be Finished?

An artist at a symposium, answering the question she is asked most. Sound first — no text.

1. What has she stopped apologizing for, and why?
For her answer when people ask when the work will be finished. She has been working on the same question since 2015 and the question has not run out yet.
2. According to her, what is the hardest part of explaining practice-based research?
That it has no single object at the end of it. There is no canvas you can point at.
3. What did she answer when somebody asked about her outcome, and how did that person react?
That the outcome is that she understands the question better than ten years ago, and that other people can now enter it. The person looked disappointed.
Listening 2 · A Curator

The First Ten Minutes of a Studio Visit

A curator on a podcast, saying what he is actually listening for. Sound first — no text.

1. How quickly does he decide, and what is that decision NOT based on?
Usually in the first ten minutes. It has almost nothing to do with how finished the work looks.
2. What exactly is he listening for, and what would convince him?
Whether the artist can say what the work is asking, not what it is about. The premise in one sentence, and three things that test it in completely different ways, even if none is resolved.
3. What makes a visit go badly, and what is his advice at the end?
When the artist describes materials for forty minutes. His advice is to prepare two sentences, then stop talking and let him ask.
Listening 3 · An Editor

Who Is Producing Knowledge?

The audio edition of a magazine of ideas, introducing a new issue. Sound first — no text.

1. What complaint started this issue of the magazine?
That neither side believes the other one is producing knowledge: artists who work with researchers, and researchers who work with artists.
2. What do the scientists think the artwork is, and what do the artists answer?
The scientists treat it as illustration, arriving at the end to make the findings easier to look at. The artists say the research is not raw material for a picture: it is the practice itself, and it unfolds on its own terms.
3. What does the editor say about the format, and what does she ask of the reader?
The full text is on the site and every essay is read aloud, so you can listen while you walk. She asks the reader to take their time, because the essays are not short.
