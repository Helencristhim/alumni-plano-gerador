Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A ruling -- an official decision made by a court or a judge
"The ruling was published in English and in French."
To rule on -- to give an official decision about a case or a question
"The court will rule on the dispute next year."
Jurisdiction -- the official power of a court to hear and decide a particular case
"The court said it had no jurisdiction over the dispute."
A tribunal -- a court or a group of judges created for a special kind of case
"An arbitration tribunal decided the investment dispute."
To hear a case -- to listen to both sides in court before making a decision
"Fifteen judges hear every case at the International Court of Justice."
To file a complaint -- to make a formal accusation to an official body
"The family filed a complaint with the Inter-American Commission."
Admissible -- accepted by a court as valid enough to be considered
"The petition was not admissible, because the national courts had not decided yet."
To uphold -- to confirm that an earlier decision was correct
"The higher court upheld the first ruling."
To overturn -- to officially change a decision so that it is no longer valid
"The appeal court overturned the conviction."
An advisory opinion -- a court’s formal view on a legal question, given when an organization asks for it, not in a dispute
"The court gave an advisory opinion on the obligations of states."
A precedent -- an earlier decision that is used as an example for later cases
"The judgment created a precedent for future cases."
A member state -- a country that belongs to an international organization
"Every member state of the United Nations can use the court."
To consent to -- to officially agree to something
"A state is judged only if it has consented to the court’s jurisdiction."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Legal writing loves long noun phrases, and most of them are held together by relative clauses. “The court which hears disputes between states” is not the same as “the court which judges individuals”, and the clause is the only thing that tells the reader which tribunal the text means.

A defining clause identifies the noun, so it has no commas: “Students who confuse the courts make the same mistake.” Without it, the sentence would say that all students make the mistake. Use who for people, which for things, and that for either, but only in this kind of clause.

A non-defining clause only adds information about a noun we already know, so it goes between commas: “The Inter-American Court, which is based in San José, hears human rights cases.” There is only one Inter-American Court, so the clause is extra. After a comma, English never uses that.

Two more words complete the set. Whose shows possession, and it replaces the possessive completely: “a citizen whose rights were violated”, never “whose his rights”. Where introduces a place: “The Hague is the city where both courts sit.” Once you see these clauses, a ruling that looked impossible to read becomes a list of simple facts about who, which and where.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

No commas when the clause tells you which one; commas when it only adds information.

JobFormExample
People (defining)who / that + clauseJudges who sit on the court.
Things (defining)which / that + clauseThe court which hears disputes.
Extra information, which / , who + clause,The Court, which is in San José, …
Possessionwhose + nounA citizen whose rights were violated.
A placewhere + clauseThe city where both courts sit.
