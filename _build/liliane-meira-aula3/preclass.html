Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

I See It Differently -- Holding a Position and Disagreeing Well

Somebody you respect makes a claim you do not accept, in front of other people. Key words: a claim, to concede, a caveat, to push back, grounds, to overstate, a nuance, to take issue with something, compelling, to qualify a statement, a blind spot, common ground. Structure: concession and hedging.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A claim -- a statement somebody puts forward as true, which they then have to support
"The panel made three claims, and only two of them were supported."
To concede -- to accept that part of what the other person says is right
"I concede the first point entirely. The second one is where we differ."
A caveat -- a limit or a warning you attach to your own statement, so it is not read as absolute
"I will say yes, with one caveat: this only holds for long projects."
To push back -- to disagree openly, but without hostility
"I want to push back on that, gently."
Grounds -- the reasons on which an argument stands
"On what grounds do you call that consent?"
To overstate -- to say something is bigger or more certain than it really is
"I do not want to overstate the objection, but it matters."
A nuance -- a small difference in meaning that changes the whole point
"There is a nuance here that the summary lost."
To take issue with something -- to disagree with one specific part of what was said
"I take issue with the second claim, not with the whole position."
Compelling -- convincing in a way that is hard to argue against
"Two of the three claims are compelling. The third is not."
To qualify a statement -- to make a statement less absolute by adding a condition to it
"Let me qualify that: it is true of long projects, not of every project."
A blind spot -- something an argument fails to see about itself
"That is the blind spot in the whole position."
Common ground -- the part of a question that both sides already agree on
"Let us find the common ground first, and then name the difference."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A critic stands up after the panel and says he is not convinced. He is careful about it. Admittedly, he begins, the first two claims are compelling, and he does not want to overstate his objection. Although the intention behind the project is clearly good, he says, the ethics were settled at the beginning and never revisited.

Then he narrows it. He does not reject the position; he takes issue with one thing. Despite the careful wording of the catalogue, the same people are called collaborators in one document and subjects in another. That inconsistency, he says, is the blind spot of the whole argument, and it is where he would want an answer.

The artist does not defend everything. She concedes the inconsistency, and then she qualifies her original statement with one caveat: participation is not authorship, but it does change who the work belongs to. It could be argued that this is a small correction. While she accepts that reading, she asks the critic for his grounds, and by the end the two of them have found the common ground they did not expect, along with the one nuance they still do not share.

1. "Although the intention is clearly good, the ethics were never revisited." What does although do here?
A It cancels the first half: the intention was not good after all.
B It accepts the first half as true and still lets the objection stand.
C It gives the reason why the ethics were never revisited.
2. Why is it "Despite the careful wording" and not "Despite the wording is careful"?
A Because despite is followed by a noun phrase, never by a full clause with a verb.
B Because despite can only be used at the start of a sentence.
C Because the sentence is in the past.
3. "It could be argued that this is a small correction." Why not simply "This is a small correction"?
A Because the writer does not know what the word correction means.
B Because the passive is always more polite in English.
C Because it presents the reading as one possible position rather than as a settled fact.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Concession and Hedging

GRAMMAR

How to give ground in the first half of a sentence and take it back in the second.

MoveFormExample
Concede a whole clausealthough / even though / while + subject + verbAlthough the evidence is strong, I disagree.
Concede a noundespite / in spite of + noun or -ingDespite the strong evidence, I disagree.
Concede in one wordadmittedly / granted / trueGranted. The first point stands.
Hedge your own claimit could be argued / arguably / I would suggestIt could be argued that both are true.
Soften the rejectionI would not go that far / I am not sure I would sayInteresting, but I would not go that far.
Narrow the targettake issue with + the exact claimI take issue with the second claim.

Never: despite the evidence is strong · I am not agree · even I understand your point · take issue about something · in spite the cost.

Stage 2: Put the Disagreement in Order

Order

Six moves that make a disagreement land well. Put them in the order that keeps the other person listening.

?"If I understand you correctly, your point is that consent covers the whole project."
?"Admittedly, the first two claims are compelling."
?"I take issue with the third one, not with the whole position."
?"My grounds are ten years of recordings that say otherwise."
?"Let me qualify that, with one caveat about long projects."
?"So the common ground is larger than it looked, and the difference is one nuance."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that keeps the other person listening.

A critic says your project cannot be assessed. You accept part of it. You say:
A "Despite you are right, I disagree."
B "Admittedly it is harder to assess. Although I concede that, I would say it is assessed differently."
C "No, you are wrong about this."
You want to reject one claim out of three, without rejecting the person. You say:
A "I take issue with the third claim, not with the whole position."
B "I take issue about you."
C "I am not agree with nothing of this."
You want to soften a statement you made too strongly. You say:
A "Forget what I said before."
B "Let me qualify that, with one caveat: it holds for long projects, not for every project."
C "Despite I said that, it is not exactly like this."
Somebody asks you a question you genuinely cannot answer yet. You say:
A "That is not important for my research."
B "You are right, and that is the blind spot. I have no answer for it yet, and I would rather say so."
C "Maybe, I do not know, next question please."

Reflection
