Chapter 3: The Code

Giving Ground, and Taking It Back

although · while · admittedly · it could be argued

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Do First?

Although the evidence is strong, I would still question the conclusion."

"Admittedly, the first two claims are compelling. The third one is not."

"While I take your point about consent, I take issue with the timing."

"It could be argued that the two words mean the same thing, but I would not go that far."

Every one of them gives something away before it disagrees, and the blue word is what lets the sentence do both at once.

MoveFormExample
Concede a whole clausealthough / even though / while + subject + verbAlthough the evidence is strong, I disagree.
Concede a noundespite / in spite of + noun or -ing, never a clauseDespite the strong evidence, I disagree.
Concede in one wordadmittedly / granted / true, + full stopGranted. The first point stands.
Hedge your own claimit could be argued / arguably / I would suggestIt could be argued that both are true.
Soften the rejectionI would not go that far / I am not sure I would sayInteresting, but I would not go that far.
The trapdespite never takes a clauseNot despite the evidence is strong.

In one line: although takes a sentence, despite takes a thing, and both buy you the right to disagree.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Corridor, After the Panel

One critic, three recordings, one programme

Dialogue

Ten Minutes in the Corridor

P
I enjoyed the panel, but I am not convinced by the second half of what you said.
L
Tell me which part, and I will either defend it or drop it.
P
The claim that participation is the same as authorship. I find that hard to accept.
L
Admittedly, I stated it too broadly. Let me qualify it: participation is not authorship, but it changes who the work belongs to.
P
That is a different sentence, and a much better one. On what grounds, though?
L
Ten years of recordings in which people describe things nobody else could have described. While I concede that authorship is a legal term, the ethical question does not wait for the legal one.
P
Now that is compelling. I still take issue with one thing: you say collaborators in the catalogue and subjects in the archive.
L
You are right, and that is the blind spot. I have no answer for it yet, and I would rather say so than invent one.
Comprehension

About Peter

1. What exactly does Peter say he is not convinced by, and how does he narrow it down?
The second half of what she said, and then he narrows it to one claim: that participation is the same as authorship.
2. What does Peter ask for once she has restated her position?
The grounds. He accepts that the new sentence is better and then asks what it stands on.
3. Peter changes his position during the conversation. Where, and what does he still object to?
He says the answer about ten years of recordings is compelling. He still takes issue with the inconsistency between collaborators in the catalogue and subjects in the archive.
Listening 1 · An Objection

Something Uncomfortable on the Table

A critic putting an objection carefully, in public. Sound first — no text.

1. What claim is he questioning, and what does he concede about it?
The claim that work made with a community automatically carries their consent. He concedes that this is often true and says he does not want to overstate the objection.
2. What is his argument about consent and time?
Consent given at the start of a five year project is not consent to whatever the project became. The ethics are handled at the beginning and never revisited.
3. What is the one specific thing he takes issue with, and what does he call it?
That the participants are described as collaborators in the catalogue and as subjects in the archive, in the same week. He calls it the blind spot of the argument.
Listening 2 · The Ground Rules

Nobody Has to Win

A chair explaining how the session will run, and why. Sound first — no text.

1. What are they NOT looking for at the end of the session?
Consensus. Nobody should feel they have to win.
2. What is the one thing she asks people to do when they disagree?
To name the exact thing they disagree with, rather than the person or the whole position. If two of three claims are compelling, say so first, then take the third one apart.
3. What does she say it usually means when there is no common ground at all?
That the two people are answering different questions. She says it is worth saying out loud, calmly.
Listening 3 · A Habit

Say Their Point Better Than They Did

How somebody stopped hearing hard questions as attacks. Sound first — no text.

1. What is the one habit that changed things?
Before responding, saying back the strongest version of what the other person just said, not the weakest.
2. What two things happen when you do it?
The other person relaxes, because they have been heard, and you find out whether you actually understood the objection.
3. How long does it cost, and what has it saved the speaker from?
About fifteen seconds, and it has saved a great deal of unnecessary defensiveness, most of it the speaker’s own.
