Chapter 3: The Code

Three Sentences, One Word Apart

A habit that ended, a state you are in, and the process of arriving there

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I used to report to a CFO in New York."

"I am used to reporting in English every day."

"It took a year, but I got used to being challenged in public."

"I used to think a question like that was an attack."

Which of these are still true today, and what comes after each expression?

FormMeaningExample
used to + base verbA past habit or state that is overI used to report to New York.
be used to + -ingIt is normal for me now — a stateI am used to reporting in English.
get used to + -ingThe process of becoming accustomedI got used to being challenged.
The trapAfter be used to and get used to, the to is a preposition, so what follows is -ing or a noun. I am used to report is wrong. I am used to the pressure is right.
Negative and questionI did not use to think that — note there is no d. Are you used to working across time zones?
Why it matters todayThese three are how you talk about ADAPTATION, and adaptation is what a hostile question is really testing. "I used to find that difficult and I am used to it now" is a complete answer to half the questions in this lesson.

The -ing after be used to is the single most common advanced mistake in this family, and it survives for years because it never blocks understanding.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I am used to report in English every day."
"I am used to reporting in English every day."
"I did not used to think of it as an attack."
"I did not use to think of it as an attack."
"It took a year but I get used to be challenged in public."
"It took a year but I got used to being challenged in public."

After be used to and get used to, the next word ends in -ing. And the negative of the past habit is did not use to, with no d.

The Move

Concede, Evidence, Stop

1. Concede the true part

"Yes, the margin fell in those two years."

2. One piece of evidence

"What was happening underneath it was a fifth of the revenue leaving in February."

3. Stop

Say nothing else. The silence after a candid answer belongs to them.

Never blame the market, the parent company or the previous CFO. Every one of those may be true, and every one of them costs you the room.

Chapter 4: In the Room

She Has Read the Accounts

And she is going to ask twice

Dialogue

Yes, and Here Is What Was Underneath

K
Your group's margin fell in your last two years. How much of that is yours?
F
Yes, it fell, and I will own the first year of it. What was underneath was a fifth of the revenue leaving in February, and I did not move on the overhead fast enough.
K
The market was difficult for everybody that year, though.
F
It was, and I would rather not use that. Two of our competitors held their margin in the same market, so the market is context, not an explanation.
K
Let me ask it another way. Nine years in one group. Should I read that as loyalty, or as comfort?
F
That is a fair question, and to be candid I have asked it too. For six of those nine years the job changed underneath me. For the last three it did not, and that is the part you are pointing at.
K
Most people argue with me at this point.
F
I used to. I am used to hearing it as information now, and it took losing one process to get used to that.
Comprehension

About Karen

1. What does Karen offer him as an excuse, and what does he do with it?
She offers the difficult market. He declines to use it, because two competitors held their margin in the same market.
2. How does she reframe the question when the first answer does not close it?
She asks whether nine years in one group should be read as loyalty or as comfort.
3. What does she say about most people at that point in the conversation?
That most of them argue with her.
Listening

What a Hostile Question Is Testing

An interviewer on the first three seconds. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does she say she is not looking for the answer?
Because she already has it, usually from the accounts, which are public. She is watching the first three seconds.
2. What does she say candidates find hard to believe?
That agreeing with part of the question is the strongest thing they can do.
3. Which three explanations does she warn against, and why?
The market, the parent company and the previous CFO — because every one of them may be true, and every one costs you the room.
Listening 2

Loyalty, or Comfort?

The hardest question he was ever asked. Sound first — no text.

1. What was the question, and why did he say it was fair?
Whether nine years in one group meant loyalty or comfort — and it was fair because he had asked himself the same thing.
2. How did he split the nine years in his answer?
Six years in which the job changed underneath him, and three in which it did not — and he named the second part himself.
3. What happened to that application, and what does he say about it?
He did not get the role, and he has never regretted the answer.

The List

What They Wrote Down Before You Arrived

Panel Prep — Do Not Circulate
Areas to Test
Prepared 48h before
3 concerns

Concern 1 — tenure

Nine years in one group. Test whether the last three were growth or inertia. Ask directly.

Concern 2 — margin

Two years of decline in the published accounts. Offer him the market as an excuse and see whether he takes it.

Concern 3 — sector

Advertising, not events. Test whether he argues the sectors are identical or names what actually transfers.

Note to panel: ask each one twice. The second time is the one that tells us something.

1. In concern 2, what is the panel actually testing by offering the market as an excuse?
Whether the candidate takes it — that is, whether he deflects instead of owning the part that is his.
2. In concern 3, what would the wrong answer look like?
Arguing that the two sectors are identical, instead of naming precisely what transfers.
3. Why does the note tell the panel to ask each question twice?
Because the second time is the one that tells them something — the first answer is usually prepared.
