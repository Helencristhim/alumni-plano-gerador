Verbs That Choose Their Company -- -ing or to

To be keen to -- to want to do something very much

A pet peeve -- a small thing that annoys you every time it happens
"My pet peeve is people replying all to a forty-person email."
To make a point of -- to do something on purpose, because you think it matters
"I make a point of reading something in English every night."
To be into -- to like an activity or a subject a lot
"I am really into true crime documentaries at the moment."
To be worth -- to be useful or good enough to spend time on
"The podcast is worth listening to, even at double speed."
Can't help -- to be unable to stop yourself from doing something
"I can't help checking my email at dinner."
To have a go at -- to try something, usually for the first time
"I had a go at running a meeting in Spanish last week."
A guilty pleasure -- something you enjoy but feel a little embarrassed about
"Reality shows are my guilty pleasure, and I do not apologize for it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In Portuguese you never have to choose. Eu gosto de treinar, eu decidi treinar, eu parei de treinar — the second verb looks the same every time. English does not work like that. The verb in front decides the shape of the verb after it, and the speaker has no say in the matter. I enjoy training is correct; I enjoy to train is not. We decided to move the close is correct; we decided moving is not.

There is a rule of thumb that gets most cases right. Verbs that talk about the activity itself — enjoy, avoid, keep, finish, mind, consider, suggest — take -ing. Verbs that point forward, to something that comes after the decision — decide, plan, agree, hope, expect, manage, refuse — take to. It is not a law, but it is a better guide than a list.

Three verbs take both forms, and the meaning changes. I stopped checking email at night means I no longer check it. I stopped to check my email means I paused what I was doing in order to check it. Remember sending it? is about a memory; remember to send it is a task that is still waiting. And try works the same way: try calling him is an experiment, I tried to call him is an effort.

Two traps deserve their own warning. Look forward to ends in to, so the base verb seems natural, but that to is a preposition: I look forward to meeting you. And suggest never takes to at all: I suggest moving the call, or I suggest we move it. Those two appear in almost every email that goes to a head office, which is why getting them right is worth more than memorizing the rest.

1. What does the text say decides between -ing and to?
A The verb that comes in front.
B Whether the sentence is about the past or about the future of the company.
C How formal the email or the conversation is.
2. What is the difference between stopped checking and stopped to check?
A There is no difference; both forms are correct and mean exactly the same thing.
B The first means you no longer do it; the second means you paused in order to do it.
C The first is British English and the second is American English.
3. Why does the text say I look forward to meet you is wrong?
A Because look forward is always followed by a noun, never by a verb of any kind.
B Because the to in look forward to is a preposition, so the next verb takes -ing.
C Because meet is an irregular verb.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- -ing or to

GRAMMAR

The verb in front chooses. You only have to listen to it.

Verb in frontWhat comes afterExample
enjoy, avoid, keep, finish, mind, consider, suggest+ -ingI enjoy training people.
decide, plan, agree, hope, expect, manage, refuse+ to + base verbWe decided to move the close.
look forward to, be worth, can't help, can't stand+ -ingI look forward to meeting you.
stop-ing = no longer · to = in order toI stopped checking email at night.
remember-ing = a memory · to = a taskRemember to send the pack.
try-ing = an experiment · to = an effortTry calling him after lunch.

Never: I enjoy to train · we decided sending · I look forward to meet you · I suggest to move the call
