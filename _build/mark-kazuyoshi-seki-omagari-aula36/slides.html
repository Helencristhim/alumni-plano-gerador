Five Minutes on a Train

The person next to you on a long train ride is also a tourist. What is the first thing you would say, and how would you keep the conversation going?

Four Ways to Check the Same Thing

Same Question, Four Tones

"Have you been to Osaka?"

Correct, and cold. It sounds like a form you have to fill in.

"You have been to Osaka, right?"

Understood everywhere, and used for everything. It is the English version of "né?".

"You've been to Osaka, haven't you?"

You think you know the answer, and you invite the other person to say more.

"You haven't been to Osaka, have you?"

You think the answer is no, and you are ready to tell them about it.

A direct question gets an answer. A question tag gets a conversation.

Chapter 3: The Tag

The Tiny Question at the End

How English checks, invites and keeps a conversation going

Grammar Discovery

Four Sentences, Four Tags

You've been to Osaka, haven't you?

You didn't take the fast train, did you?

I'm on the right platform, aren't I?

Let's get some ramen, shall we?

How is each orange question built from the first half of the sentence? Why is one of them negative and another one positive?

RuleHow it worksExample
positive sentence → negative tagsame auxiliary, turned negative"You've been there, haven't you?"
negative sentence → positive tagsame auxiliary, turned positive"You didn't go, did you?"
no auxiliary → do / does / didpresent simple and past simple"She likes sushi, doesn't she?"
I am → aren't I?the one irregular tag"I'm late, aren't I?"
Let's → shall we? / imperative → will you?invitations and requests"Pass me the map, will you?"
never / nobody / hardly → positive tagthe sentence is already negative"Nobody told you, did they?"
voice down / voice upVoice going down: you are sure and you expect agreement. Voice going up: it is a real question, and you do not know the answer.

Build the Tag

Say it first, then click to check

"You've been to Kyoto, ___haven't you?" (positive sentence, auxiliary have)
"She didn't miss the train, ___did she?" (negative sentence)
"I'm on the right platform, ___aren't I?" (the irregular one)
"Let's try the ramen, ___shall we?" (an invitation)
"Nobody told you about the rule, ___did they?" (nobody is already negative)
"Tokyo is huge, ___isn't it?" (positive sentence, verb be)
The Tool

Three Places It Goes Wrong

Trap 1 · "isn't it?" is not the English "né?"

The tag copies the auxiliary of the sentence: "You like sushi, don't you?", "They've left, haven't they?" "Isn't it?" only works after "it is". When you are not sure, "right?" is safe, but it is not a tag.

Trap 2 · negative words need a positive tag

Never, nobody, nothing and hardly already make the sentence negative, so the tag is positive: "You've never been there, have you?" Never "haven't you?".

Trap 3 · the voice changes the meaning

The same words can be two different things. "You're from Osaka, aren't you?" with the voice going down means "I'm sure, tell me more". With the voice going up, it means "I really don't know".

Get the tag right and people answer with a story. Get it wrong and they answer with a correction.

Comprehension

About Nina

1. How did Nina meet the guy in the photo?
By chance: she bumped into him on the train.
2. Why did they break the ice so quickly?
Because he was so chatty.
3. What did he do for Nina and her friends, and what is her advice to Mark?
He showed them around the market for an hour. She tells Mark to finish his sentences with a little question.
Listening

The Tiny Question at the End

Your teacher on question tags and the three traps. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Why does she say a question tag is better than a direct question when you meet somebody?
Because a direct question sounds like a form, and a tag sounds like a conversation.
2. How do you build a tag?
Copy the auxiliary from the sentence and turn it around: a positive sentence gets a negative tag, and a negative sentence gets a positive one.
3. What mistake does she say many Brazilians make?
They use "isn't it?" for every sentence, but it only works after "it is".
4. What does the voice going down mean, and what does the voice going up mean?
Down: you are sure. Up: it is a real question.

Fix the Tag

1. "You like Japanese food, isn't it?"

2. "You've been to Osaka, didn't you?"

Your reply: one message with a tag.
1. Kenji writes "Nobody leaves hungry, do they?" Why is the tag positive?
Because "nobody" already makes the sentence negative, so the tag is positive.
2. Lucas writes "It isn't far from here, is it?" What does Lucas think the answer is?
He thinks it is not far. A negative sentence with a positive tag expects the answer "no, it isn't".
