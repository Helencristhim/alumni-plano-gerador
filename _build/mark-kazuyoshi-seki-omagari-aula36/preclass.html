Lesson 36 -- Pre-class

You Have Been There, Have You Not? -- Question Tags

A direct question gets an answer; a question tag gets a conversation. This lesson gives you the tiny questions English puts at the end of a sentence -- haven't you, did you, aren't I, shall we, will you -- and the voice that goes with them, and you use them to talk to the people you meet on a trip. Key words: small talk, break the ice, awkward, chatty, hit it off, bump into, catch up, fellow traveler, tag along, show around, open up, keep the ball rolling.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. These are the words people use when they talk about meeting new people on a trip.

Small talk -- polite, easy conversation about things like the weather or a trip
"We made small talk while we waited for the train."
Break the ice -- to say or do something that makes people relax when they first meet
"A question about the food is the easiest way to break the ice."
Awkward -- uncomfortable, when nobody knows what to say or do
"There was an awkward silence after my joke."
Chatty -- liking to talk a lot, in a friendly way
"The man at the ramen counter was really chatty."
Hit it off -- to like each other right away
"We hit it off in five minutes and talked the whole way to Osaka."
Bump into -- to meet somebody by chance, without planning it
"I bumped into a guy from my school at the station in Tokyo."
Catch up -- to talk to somebody about what has happened since you last met
"Let's catch up after your trip, and you can show me the photos."
Fellow traveler -- another person who is traveling, like you
"The hostel kitchen is the best place to meet fellow travelers."
Tag along -- to go somewhere with people, following their plan
"They were going to the market, so I tagged along."
Show around -- to take a visitor to see the interesting parts of a place
"He showed us around the market for an hour."
Open up -- to start talking more freely about yourself
"After a few minutes, she opened up and told us her whole story."
Keep the ball rolling -- to keep a conversation or an activity going so it does not stop
"Ask a question at the end, and you keep the ball rolling."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the travel page and answer the questions. Notice how each tag in bold is built from the sentence before it.

Ask travelers about the best part of a trip, and many will mention a person: somebody they bumped into on a train, or a fellow traveler who showed them around. The hard part is the first minute of small talk.

Our writer noticed that the travelers who hit it off with people fastest said what they thought and added a tiny question. "You're from Brazil, aren't you?" "You've been to Sao Paulo, haven't you?"

A negative sentence gets a positive tag: "You didn't like the hostel, did you?" And words like nobody need a positive tag too: "Nobody told you about the market, did they?"

Invitations have their own tags. "Let's get some ramen, shall we?" "Show me around tomorrow, will you?" And remember: when your voice goes down at the end, you are sure; when it goes up, it is a real question.

1. "You're from Brazil, aren't you?" Why is the tag negative?
A Because the sentence is positive.
B Because the person is not from Brazil.
C Because it is a rude question.
2. "Nobody told you about the market, did they?" Why is the tag positive?
A Because "nobody" already makes the sentence negative.
B Because the market is open.
C Because "they" is plural.
3. Which tag goes with "Let's get some ramen"?
A "don't we?"
B "shall we?"
C "isn't it?"
4. When your voice goes down at the end of a tag, what does it mean?
A You really do not know the answer.
B You are sure, and you expect the other person to agree.
C You are angry.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Tiny Question at the End

GRAMMAR

A question tag copies the auxiliary of the sentence and turns it around. It invites the other person to agree and to keep talking.

RuleHow it worksExample
positive sentence -> negative tagsame auxiliary"You've been there, haven't you?"
negative sentence -> positive tagsame auxiliary"You didn't go, did you?"
no auxiliary -> do / does / didpresent and past simple"She likes sushi, doesn't she?"
I am -> aren't I? / Let's -> shall we?the special ones"I'm late, aren't I?"
imperative -> will you?a friendly request"Pass me the map, will you?"
never / nobody / nothing -> positive tagalready negative"Nobody told you, did they?"
The voice and one trapVoice down = you are sure. Voice up = a real question. And "isn't it?" only works after "it is" -- it is not the English "né?".
