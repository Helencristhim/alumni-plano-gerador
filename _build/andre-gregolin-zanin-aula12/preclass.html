Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A sequence -- a series of things that happen in a fixed order
"Warm-up, drills, game: the sequence never changed."
To overlap -- to happen partly at the same time, or to cover part of the same space
"Never let two difficult subjects overlap."
To precede -- to happen before something else
"Two hard years preceded the best one."
To coincide with -- to happen at the same time as something else, often by chance
"The move to São Paulo coincided with the end of the season."
To trigger -- to be the small thing that makes a bigger thing start
"One short walk between blocks triggered the biggest change of her year."
Simultaneously -- at exactly the same time
"She stopped trying to study and answer messages simultaneously."
Whereas -- introduces a contrast between two different facts
"Whereas volleyball had a clear sequence, studying had no shape at all."
The moment -- exactly when something happens, used to join two events
"The moment the coach read the list, she knew."
As long as -- on the condition that, and only while it stays true
"I will keep going as long as I am improving."
To fall into place -- to finally start working, after a period of confusion
"Once she had a routine, everything fell into place."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A story told in short sentences sounds like a list: I played basketball. I moved to São Paulo. I started cursinho. The facts are there, but the listener does not know how they connect. An adverbial clause fixes that. It is a small clause, with its own subject and verb, that answers one question about the main clause: when, why, in spite of what, or what for. Once I moved to São Paulo, everything became more structured is one sentence, and it already tells you the order.

For time, English has a whole family: when, as soon as, once, until, by the time. One rule is strict. When the clause talks about the future, the verb inside it stays in the present: By the time the exam is over, I will have decided — never by the time the exam will be over. For a reason, since and as sound a little more formal than because. For contrast inside one sentence, although and whereas.

For purpose, use so that before a full clause, or in order to before a verb: I study in blocks so that every subject has an end. The form for to does not exist in English, although it is one of the most common mistakes students make. Once these clauses are automatic, a list of facts becomes a timeline, and the listener can follow it without effort.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Adverbial Clauses: When, Why, Despite, What For

GRAMMAR

A small clause inside the sentence answers one question about the main clause.

QuestionSubordinatorsExample
When?when · as soon as · once · by the time · until · whileAs soon as I get my results, I will call you.
Why?because · since · as · given thatSince I have already applied, there is nothing left to do.
In spite of what?although · even though · whereas · whileAlthough I miss the game, I do not regret it.
What for?so that + clause · in order to + verbI study in blocks so that every subject has an end.
The future rulepresent inside the time clauseBy the time the exam is over, I will have decided.

Never: when I will finish · until you will call · by the time I will get there · for to have
