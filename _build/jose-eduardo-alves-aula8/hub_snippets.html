Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To interrupt -- to stop something that is already happening, in the middle
"The call interrupted her while she was reading the delivery clause."
A delay -- time lost, when something happens later than it should have
"The delay started at eleven forty-seven at night."
To confirm -- to say officially that something is true, so that nobody has to ask again
"A deadlock is the moment when neither side can confirm anything."
To finalize -- to close the last details, so that a document is ready to be signed
"Her team was finalizing a sugar contract with a buyer in Alexandria."
A counter-offer -- the answer you send when you do not accept the price and you propose another one
"It was the third counter-offer of the week, and everybody wanted to sleep."
Due diligence -- the checking you do on the other company before you sign anything
"We were finishing due diligence when the regulator asked for one more document."
A briefing -- the short meeting where somebody explains what is going on before you act
"She uses that sentence in every briefing since that night."
Regulatory approval -- the permission a public authority has to give before the deal can go ahead
"Everybody was waiting for regulatory approval, and nobody was calling the regulator."
A deadlock -- the point where neither side will move, and nothing can happen until one of them does
"A deadlock is never about the money. It is about what nobody can confirm."
To back out -- to leave a deal before it is signed, when you were already inside it
"Six weeks later the buyer backed out. Nobody argued."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A story needs two kinds of past, because two kinds of thing happen in it. Something was already running — long, unfinished, still going. And something else happened once and finished, cutting across it. English marks the first one with was or were plus the verb in -ing, and the second one with the plain past. "She was reading the delivery clause when the phone rang."

Two little words join them, and they are not interchangeable. When goes with the SHORT action: "She was reading the clause when the phone rang." While goes with the LONG one: "While she was reading the clause, the phone rang." Both sentences mean exactly the same thing; what changes is which half of the story each word introduces.

There is a third use, and for a lawyer it is the most valuable one: two long actions at the same moment, in different places. Then both verbs take the -ing shape. "While the lawyers were arguing about the cause, the shipbroker was asking the port for the berth log." One sentence, two rooms, one clock. That is how an incident is described, and no list of events can do it.

Now the trap, and it is expensive. If you put BOTH verbs in the plain past — "I read the contract when he called" — the sentence is correct English, but it says something else: first he called, then you read it. The order of the facts is inverted, and nobody notices, because nothing sounds wrong. One last detail: verbs that describe a state, not an action, refuse the -ing here too. Nobody knew what caused the delay; nobody was knowing it.

1. Which action takes was or were plus -ing?
A The more important one.
B The one that was already running when the other one happened.
C The one that happened second.
2. What is the difference between when and while here?
A When introduces the short action; while introduces the long one.
B While is for formal writing and when is for speaking.
C There is no difference at all.
3. Why is "I read the contract when he called" a dangerous sentence in a report?
A Because read is an irregular verb.
B Because it is too informal for a client.
C Because it is correct English that says the opposite: he called first, and you read it afterwards.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Long One and the Short One

GRAMMAR

When goes with the short action. While goes with the long one.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A client asks what you were doing when the delay started. You say:
A "I read the delivery clause when the broker called me."
B "I was reading the delivery clause when the broker called me."
C "I was read the delivery clause when the broker was calling me."
You want to put two things in two countries at the same moment. You say:
A "While the buyer was sleeping, the trader was watching the news."
B "While the buyer slept, the trader watched the news."
C "When the buyer was sleeping, the trader watched the news at the same moment."
You want to ask your colleague about the moment it stopped. You say:
A "What you were doing when the regulator called?"
B "What were you doing when the regulator called?"
C "What did you doing when the regulator was calling?"
You want to say that nobody had the information yet. You say:
A "Nobody was knowing what caused the delay."
B "Nobody knew what caused the delay, so nobody could confirm anything."
C "Nobody were know the cause of the delay."
