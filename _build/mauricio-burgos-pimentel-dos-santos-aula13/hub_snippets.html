The Question You Fear

Eight words for tough questions (tough question, fair point, push back, admit, figure, off the top of my head, get back to, for instance) and the grammar that lets you say what you can and cannot answer: can, could, be able to. Around 30 minutes, in one sitting.

Stage 1.1: Words for Tough Questions

Vocabulary

Tap Listen, then say each word out loud. Eight words, three minutes.

Tough question — a question that is difficult to answer
"Every session ends with one tough question."
Fair point — a good argument, and you accept it
"Fair point. We could do that faster."
Push back — to disagree and ask for a reason
"A classmate pushed back on my numbers."
Admit — to say that something is true, even if it is not good for you
"I have to admit that we were late."
Figure — a number, often in business
"I cannot give you the exact figure today."
Off the top of my head — without checking, from memory
"Off the top of my head, it is under two percent."
Get back to — to answer someone later
"I will get back to you on Thursday."
For instance — here is one example
"For instance, we were able to cut the waiting time in half."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the text. Then answer the questions.

At the end of my presentation, a woman from Toronto pushed back. It was a tough question: how many clients did we lose last year? I couldn’t remember the exact figure. In August I would apologize and stop. This time I said, "Fair point. I can’t give you the exact number now, but off the top of my head, it is about thirty." Then I admitted the real problem: five years ago we couldn’t answer a client in one day. Now we can. For instance, last quarter we were able to keep our three biggest clients. I promised to get back to her the next day, and I did. Later she told me it was the most honest answer of the week.
What could he not remember?
A The exact number of clients they lost.
B The name of the woman from Toronto who asked the question.
Why is it "we were able to keep" and not "we could keep"?
A Because could is only used in questions and never in the past.
B It is one specific success in the past.
What did he do the next day?
A He got back to her.
B He asked his team to prepare a new presentation for the class.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Say what you can — and what you can’t

Can = now. Could = past, in general. Was / were able to = one specific success. All take the bare verb.

can — now

I can tell you the trend.
I can’t share that.

could — the past

In 2010 we couldn’t do it online.
At 24 I could speak English every day.

able to — one success

Last year we were able to keep every client.
I was able to answer her.

could you — polite

Could you say that again?
Could you give me an example?

× "I can’t to give you" — bare verb: I can’t give
× "we able to keep" — with be: we were able to keep

For a tough question: "I can’t ..., but I can ..."

Stage 4: In the room

Quiz
Which sentence is correct?
A "Last year we able to keep every client in the portfolio."
B "Last year we were able to keep every client."
Someone asks for a number you do not remember. What is the best answer?
A "I can’t give you the exact figure, but it is about thirty."
B "Exactly twenty-seven." (You do not know. You invent it.)
You did not understand the question. What do you say?
A "Sorry, sorry, my English is bad, I don’t understand anything you say."
B "Could you say that again?"

Stage 5: The question you fear

Reflection
Think of the question you are most afraid of hearing in the program. Record one minute in English. Say the question first. Then answer it in three steps: what you can’t say, what you can say (with one example: "for instance, we were able to ..."), and when you will get back to the person.
