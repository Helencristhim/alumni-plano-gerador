Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is one structure in English that lets you give advice without giving an order, disagree without hardening a table, and ask a hard question without anybody losing face. It is the second conditional, and it works by a small trick: it uses a past form to talk about the present. If I were in your position, I would look at the regulatory angle first. Nothing in that sentence is in the past. The past form is simply the distance, and the distance is the politeness.

Compare the two shapes. The first conditional is for things that may really happen: if plus the present simple, then will. If the panel finishes early, I will come and find you. That is a real plan for tonight. The second conditional is for things you are only imagining: if plus the past simple, then would. If we expanded into that market, we would need a local partner. Nobody is expanding into anything; you are thinking out loud, and everybody at the table knows it.

Three details decide whether it sounds natural. First, the verb be in the if half is were for everybody, including I and he: if I were you, if he were in charge. You will hear native speakers say if I was you in casual conversation, but in a professional room the expected form is were. Second, would never appears in the if half. Not if I would be you — that mistake comes straight from Portuguese and it is the most audible one. And third, the past form is not about the past at all: if we expanded is about now, and about a decision nobody has taken.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Three minutes at a dinner table with a stranger. Open the conversation without talking about the event, find one piece of common ground, give one piece of advice with "if I were you", disagree softly with one thing they say, and then leave the conversation in three parts so that the follow-up writes itself.
