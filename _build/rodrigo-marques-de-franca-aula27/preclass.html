Lesson 27 -- Pre-class

Objection, Politely -- Interrupting and Taking Back the Floor

How to come into a conversation without offending anyone, how to keep the floor when someone cuts in on you, and how to pick up where you left off. Key words: an objection, to sustain, to overrule, the chair, a point of order, to badger, to cut in, to get a word in edgewise, to ramble, to go off on a tangent, to pick up where you left off, assertive. Structure: could / may + I + just + verb to ask for the floor (could I just come in here?), if I could just finish and let me just finish to keep it, and as I was saying and to get back to my point to take it back.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An objection -- a formal complaint in a hearing that a question or a statement from the other side is not allowed
"Objection, Your Honor. The question is not relevant."
To sustain -- to agree, as a judge, that an objection is correct
"Objection sustained. The witness doesn't have to answer."
To overrule -- to reject an objection or a decision, using a higher authority
"The judge overruled the objection, and the witness went on."
The chair -- the person who leads a meeting and decides who speaks
"Please speak through the chair, not over each other."
A point of order -- a question about whether a meeting is following its own rules
"A point of order, Madam Chair: we agreed on five minutes each."
To badger -- to ask someone the same questions again and again, in an aggressive way
"Objection. Counsel is badgering the witness."
To cut in -- to start speaking while another person is still talking
"Sorry to cut in, but the deadline is Friday, not Monday."
To get a word in edgewise -- to manage to say something when another person is talking too much
"He talked for an hour, and I couldn't get a word in edgewise."
To ramble -- to talk for a long time without a clear point
"Sorry, I'm rambling. Let me get to the point."
To go off on a tangent -- to start talking about something different from the main topic
"We were discussing the budget, and then he went off on a tangent about golf."
To pick up where you left off -- to continue from the exact point where you stopped
"After the break, let's pick up where we left off."
Assertive -- confident and direct about what you think or want, without being rude
"You can be assertive and still be polite."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At ten o'clock on Thursday, Claire Hoffman, counsel for an American distributor, sat down with Rodrigo in his office in São Paulo. They had one hour to settle a dispute about a distribution agreement. Claire started to ramble about the history of the relationship, the trade fairs in Chicago and the excellent volumes of 2019.

After three minutes, Rodrigo cut in: "Sorry to interrupt, Claire, but could I just suggest an agenda?" Later, when he tried to come in again, Claire held the floor: "If I could just finish this point, Rodrigo." He let her finish. She explained that her client had six months of stock in a warehouse in New Jersey.

Then Rodrigo took the floor back without raising his voice. "As I was saying, the notice was valid. But to get back to your point, we may be able to buy back part of that stock." Nobody went off on a tangent again, and after a short break they picked up where they left off.

1. "Could I just suggest an agenda?" Why does Rodrigo use could and just?
A To show that he is angry with Claire and wants her to stop talking about the past immediately, before the hour is over.
B To ask for the floor politely, so that the interruption sounds small.
C To talk about the past.
2. What was in the warehouse in New Jersey?
A Six months of stock from Claire's client.
B The original copy of the distribution agreement that the two companies had signed in Chicago in 2019.
C A new agenda.
3. Rodrigo wants to go back to what he said before Claire cut in. Which phrase is correct?
A As I was say,
B As I was saying,
C Like I said you before, and I want to repeat it now very clearly for everybody at the table,

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Interrupting and Taking Back the Floor

GRAMMAR

Ask with could or may, soften with just, and come back with as I was saying. After could, may and let, the verb has no "to".

MovePatternExample
Ask for the floorCould / May + I + just + verb?Could I just come in here?
Apologize firstSorry to + verb, but ... / Sorry for + -ingSorry to cut in, but the date is wrong.
Keep the floorIf I could just + verb ... / Let me just + verbLet me just finish this point.
Give it backGo ahead. / Please, you first.Sorry, go ahead.
Take it backAs I was saying, / To get back to my point, / Where was I?As I was saying, the notice was valid.

Never: Sorry for interrupt · Let me to finish · Could I just to add · As I was say

Stage 2: Put the Meeting in Order

Order

Someone rambles, you come in, they keep the floor, you give it back, and then you take it back. Put the conversation in that order.

?"Let me start with some history. Back in 2019, the volumes were excellent..."
?"Sorry to cut in, but could I just suggest an agenda?"
?"If I could just finish this point, the real issue is the stock."
?"Of course. Sorry, go ahead."
?"As I was saying, the notice was valid."
?"Let's take ten minutes and then pick up where we left off."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose what a real speaker would say.

In a video call, a colleague has talked for ten minutes and you need to correct a number. What do you say?
A "Excuse me, I will interrupt you now because I need to correct one number that you said a few minutes ago."
B "Sorry to cut in, but could I just correct one number?"
C "Stop. The number is wrong."
You are explaining your advice and the client starts talking. You want to finish first. You say:
A "Let me to finish, please."
B "If I could just finish this point, I'll hand it straight back to you."
C "Sorry for interrupt you, but I was talking first and I would like to finish my whole explanation now."
Your phone rang during a meeting. You hang up and want to continue. You say:
A "Sorry about that. As I was saying, the deadline is in March."
B "Sorry about that. As I was say, the deadline is in March, and I think we should discuss it now."
C "Sorry about that. Could I just to continue?"
At dinner, your friend goes off on a tangent about his new car. You want to go back to planning the trip. You say:
A "Your car is not important."
B "Sorry for interrupt, but we were talking about something much more important than your car before."
C "Sorry, can we just get back to the trip?"
