Chapter 3: The Code

Could You Tell Me What It Is?

The polite question that never sounds lost

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

Could you tell me what the deadline is?

Do you know when they signed it?

I was wondering whether the penalty applies here.

Do you mean the cap, or the penalty?

After the opener, what happened to the word order — and where did do and did go?

FormRuleExample
Direct questionVerb before subject. Fine between colleagues, sharp with a client.What is the deadline?
Opener + question word + subject + verbAfter Could you tell me / Do you know / I was wondering, the order goes back to normal.Could you tell me what the deadline is?
(not: what is the deadline)
do / does / didThey disappear. The main verb carries the tense.Do you know when they signed it?
(not: when did they sign)
Yes / no questionUse if or whether instead of a question word.I was wondering whether the penalty applies.
The narrowing checkDo you mean X, or Y? closes the space. Sorry? makes the other person repeat the same words; an alternative makes them choose.

The opener carries the politeness. The rest of the sentence is a statement.

The Habit

Three Moves, Always in This Order

When the conversation goes off-script, there are only three things to do.

1. Buy time

"Bear with me." · "Give me a second."
The pause is allowed. The unannounced pause is not.

2. Narrow

"Could you tell me what that means here?" · "Do you mean the cap, or the penalty?"
Do not ask for a repeat. Ask for a choice.

3. Park it

"Let me double-check." · "I will get back to you today."
Postponing with a date is professional. Guessing is not.

You are not buying time to think of the word — you are buying time to think.

Thinking before you speak was never the problem. The missing part was the sentence that tells the other person you are thinking.

Chapter 4: The Call That Derails

One Question From Nowhere

Buy time, narrow it, park it

Dialogue

The Chilean Master

D
Before we finish — quick one. The Chilean master agreement. Where are we on the MFN clause there?
A
Bear with me for a second — that one is not in front of me. Give me a second to open it.
D
Of course. It only came up this morning, with the regional team, so it is new to you.
A
Thank you. Just to confirm — could you tell me what MFN stands for in this contract? I want to be sure we mean the same clause.
D
Most favoured nation. If we give a better price to another distributor, they get the same price.
A
Understood. Off the top of my head, that clause is in the Chilean draft and the wording is wider than ours. But I am not going to give you a number from memory. Let me double-check it and I will get back to you before the end of the day.
D
That is the right answer. I would rather wait four hours than fix it in four weeks.
Comprehension

About David

1. What does David ask about at the end of the call?
The MFN clause in the Chilean master agreement.
2. Why does he say the question is new to Ana?
Because it only came up that morning, with the regional team.
3. How does he react when she says she will check and come back?
He calls it the right answer — he would rather wait four hours than fix the problem in four weeks.
Listening

What He Actually Notices

An executive with teams in eight countries. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he say he does not notice on a call?
When somebody is searching for a word. Everybody does it, including him.
2. What does silence with no explanation make him do?
Guess — is she thinking, does she disagree, did the connection drop? And his guesses are usually wrong.
3. Which phrase does he respect most, and why?
"Bear with me." It turns the silence into a pause he can understand, so he waits.
4. What is the one thing he does not forgive?
A confident guess — a number invented to fill the gap.
