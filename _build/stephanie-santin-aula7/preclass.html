Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

Must Not Is Not the Same as Do Not Have To

Giving instructions, advice and options to a patient in English. Key words: obligation, restriction, recommendation, urgent, procedure, to clarify, permission, second opinion. Structure: modal verbs (should, must, have to, must not, might, may, could).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words a patient hears from you, not the ones you read in a paper.

Obligation -- something you have to do, whether you want to or not
"Reporting this case is a legal obligation."
Restriction -- a rule that limits what you can do
"There is one restriction: no food before the procedure."
Recommendation -- what a specialist advises you to do
"My recommendation is to see a cardiologist first."
Urgent -- needing attention right now, and not later
"This is not urgent, but do not leave it for next year."
Procedure -- a medical operation, or a fixed series of steps
"The procedure takes about forty minutes."
To clarify -- to make something easier to understand
"Let me clarify one point before you sign."
Permission -- official agreement that you can do something
"You need permission from the family."
Second opinion -- the assessment of a different doctor about the same case
"You might want to get a second opinion."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Dr. Lima has to explain the same instructions twenty times a day, and she says the words matter more than people think. Must not and do not have to sound similar to a patient, and they are opposites: the first is a prohibition, the second is a choice. Should is advice, and the patient can say no. Must and have to are both obligation, and the difference is who decided: must usually comes from the doctor in the room, have to from a rule outside it. And might is possibility, not a promise. She says one sentence explains why she is so careful: a patient who confuses must not with do not have to eats breakfast before surgery.
What is the difference between "must not" and "do not have to"?
A They mean almost the same thing
B "Must not" is a prohibition; "do not have to" is a choice
C "Must not" is simply stronger advice
According to the text, what separates "must" from "have to"?
A Who decided: must comes from the doctor in the room, have to from a rule outside it
B "Must" is used for the past
C "Have to" is more polite

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Verbs

GRAMMAR

A modal never changes, and the verb after it stays in the base form: she must be, never she must to be, never she musts.

adviceshould, ought toYou should see a specialist first.
obligationmust (from inside) / have to (from outside)I must call him. / I have to submit the report by Friday.
prohibition against no obligationmust not against do not have toYou must not eat. / You do not have to answer.
possibility and deductionmight, may, could / mustYou might want a second opinion. / She must be in pain.

Careful: must not and do not have to are opposites, not synonyms. One forbids, the other frees. With a patient in front of you, that difference is the whole instruction.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A "You do not have to eat before the procedure."
B "You must not eat after ten tonight. Water is fine until midnight."
C "You should not to eat before the procedure."
The patient has not moved and is very quiet. You are almost sure she is in pain. What do you tell the nurse?
A "She must be in pain."
B "She must to be in pain."
C "She should be in pain."
The decision can wait, and it is the patient's choice. What do you say?
A "You must not decide today."
B "You do not have to decide today."
