The Thing Is — Emphasis in Real Conversation

Defending a place nobody else defends, and the small words that make a claim land. Key expressions: to grow on you, overrated, to live up to the hype, a hidden gem, sprawling, bustling, run-down, to gentrify, faded grandeur, walkable, a melting pot, to be steeped in, a backwater, to be teeming with, soulless, to capture the essence of, to put somewhere on the map, to be worth the detour. Structure: emphatic structures — do + base form, the very fact that, it is precisely because, every bit as.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

To grow on you -- to become likeable slowly, after a bad first impression
"It is not a city that charms you. It grows on you, and then it keeps you."
Overrated -- praised more than it deserves
"The cathedral is overrated and the street behind it is not."
To live up to the hype -- to be as good as people said it would be
"Very few places live up to the hype, and that one does."
A hidden gem -- somewhere excellent that almost nobody has heard of
"Every city has a hidden gem and every guidebook prints the same one."
Sprawling -- spread out over a large area, without much plan
"It is sprawling in a way that makes walking pointless."
Bustling -- full of noisy, energetic activity
"The market is bustling at six in the morning and dead by ten."
Run-down -- in poor condition because nobody has maintained it
"The quarter is run-down, and it is precisely because of that that the rents are low."
To gentrify -- to change a poor area by bringing in wealthier residents
"It was gentrified in about six years and lost the thing people came for."
Faded grandeur -- beauty left over from a much richer past
"Faded grandeur, in the honest sense: the buildings remember money that has gone."
Walkable -- designed so that you can reach things on foot
"A walkable city is not a small city. It is a city that decided."
A melting pot -- a place where people of many origins mix and change each other
"It is a genuine melting pot, which is rarer than the phrase suggests."
To be steeped in -- to be deeply filled with a history or a tradition
"The whole district is steeped in a history nobody there talks about."
A backwater -- a place cut off from what is happening elsewhere
"It was a backwater for two centuries and that is why it survived."
To be teeming with -- to be extremely crowded with something
"In August the old town is teeming with people and worth avoiding."
Soulless -- efficient, clean and completely without character
"The new quarter is soulless, and I say that having wanted to like it."
To capture the essence of -- to express the most characteristic quality of something
"One photograph captured the essence of the place better than the guidebook."
To put somewhere on the map -- to make a place known to people who had ignored it
"One film put it on the map, and the residents are divided about that."
To be worth the detour -- good enough to justify going out of your way for it
"It is two hours off the route and it is worth the detour."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

I do understand why people dislike it. They arrive in August, when the old town is teeming with visitors; they spend both days inside a square that is overrated and photographed from one angle; and they leave convinced that the place is soulless. The centre is the worst part of that city, as it is of most cities, and it is the only part almost anybody sees.

The very fact that the place photographs badly is what has protected it. It was a backwater for two centuries, which is why the eighteenth-century streets are still there; it never lived up to the hype, because there was none; and no film ever put it on the map. Whole districts are steeped in a history that nobody who lives there mentions, and the buildings carry a faded grandeur that would be a museum anywhere else.

It is precisely because the northern quarter is run-down that anybody under thirty can still afford to live in it. That is not romanticism about poverty; it is arithmetic. Gentrify it, as three comparable districts have been gentrified, and within a decade it will be clean, safe, expensive and every bit as dull as the developments people fly past on the way in.

What I would tell a visitor is short. The market at seven, while it is bustling and before it becomes a photograph; ten minutes at the cathedral and no more; then a walkable hour in a residential street forty minutes out, where nothing whatever is happening. It is nothing like as impressive as the guidebook version, and it is the only version that captures the essence of the place. It grows on you. Two days is not enough, and it is worth the detour anyway.

1. "I do understand why people dislike it." What is do doing here?
A Making the sentence a question.
B Adding emphasis, and conceding a point before the objection comes.
C Marking the past tense.
2. "The very fact that the place photographs badly…" What does the very add?
A It points at exactly this fact and no other, and makes it the argument.
B It means the fact is recent.
C It means the fact is doubtful.
3. "It is precisely because the quarter is run-down that anybody under thirty can afford it." What is this structure doing?
A Isolating the cause and ruling out the alternatives.
B Describing when it happened.
C Conceding the opposite point.
4. "It is nothing like as impressive as the guidebook version." What does this mean?
A It is slightly less impressive.
B It is far less impressive.
C It is just as impressive.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Emphasis — Putting Weight on a Claim

GRAMMAR

Written English emphasises by moving words around. Spoken English does it with a small set of markers and with stress. Neither adds information, and both change how much of what you said somebody remembers.

FormWhat it doesExample
do / does / did
+ BASE form		The emphatic auxiliary. It answers a doubt nobody has spoken aloud. Affirmative only, never with another auxiliary, and the verb after it does not carry the tense."I do think so." (not do thought)
"She did warn us."
the very + nounExactly this one and no other. The very fact that is the most useful member of the family: it turns a detail into the whole argument."The very fact that nobody goes there is the point."
"That is the very street."
It is precisely /
exactly / only because …
that …		Isolates ONE cause and excludes the rest. Especially useful just before a counter-intuitive claim, because it warns the listener to pay attention."It is precisely because it is run-down that it survives."
every bit as · far and away
by some margin · nothing like as		Comparative intensifiers. Note the trap: nothing like as good means MUCH WORSE, not similar. Portuguese speakers read it the wrong way round at first."Far and away the best square."
"Nothing like as bad as they say."
FrontingMoving the complement to the front of the sentence. Emphatic and slightly literary; in speech it is a strong move and should be used once, not four times."Beautiful, it is not."
"That I do not accept."

A word of calibration, which matters more at your level than the forms themselves. Emphasis with a stranger reads as insistence; with somebody you know it reads as conviction. The C1 skill is not producing these structures — it is choosing how many to use, and with whom.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are defending a place other people dismiss. Choose the version that lands.

You want to emphasise that you really do agree:
A "I do agreed with you."
B "I do agree with you."
C "I am do agreeing with you."
You want to isolate the cause before a counter-intuitive claim:
A "It is precisely because of it is run-down that the rents are low."
B "It is precisely because it is run-down that the rents are low."
C "It is precisely why it is run-down that the rents are low."
You want to turn one detail into the whole argument:
A "The very fact of that nobody photographs it is the point."
B "The very fact that nobody photographs it is the point."
C "The fact very that nobody photographs it is the point."
You want to say the place is FAR less difficult than its reputation:
A "It is nothing like as difficult as they say."
B "It is nothing like difficult as they say."
C "It is not nothing as difficult as they say."
Somebody calls the city soulless after two days in the centre:
A "You are completely wrong."
B "I do understand why — and the very fact that you only saw the centre is the answer."
C "It is precisely because of the centre."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Choose a city, a neighbourhood or a country that other people dismiss and you would defend. In about ninety seconds, make the case: concede what is genuinely wrong with it, then put weight on two or three claims using today's structures, and finish with the single hour you would give a visitor who had only one. Say it once flat and once with emphasis if you have time — the difference is the lesson.
