Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

What the Critics Said -- Reading a Review and Disagreeing With It

Two reviews of the same production, three weeks apart, and nothing on stage had changed. Key words: understated, gripping, atmospheric, uneven, overrated, to be panned, a lead role, a slow burn, to fall flat, a standing ovation, a revival, to be set in. Structure: passive voice and impersonal report structures.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Understated -- deliberately quiet and restrained, never showing off
"The performance is so understated that on the first night the critics missed it."
Gripping -- holding your attention completely, from the first minute to the last
"The last twenty minutes are gripping. The first thirty are not."
Atmospheric -- creating a strong mood through the setting, the light and the sound
"The staging is genuinely atmospheric. The light does half the work."
Uneven -- good in some parts and weak in others, without a steady level
"The second act is a little uneven, and the first is faultless."
Overrated -- praised more than it actually deserves
"It is thought to be a masterpiece, and I found it overrated and far too long."
To be panned -- to be criticized very severely by the reviewers
"It was panned on the opening night and sold out three weeks later."
A lead role -- the main part in a film or a play
"She has the lead role, and she is on stage for two and a half hours."
A slow burn -- something that starts quietly and builds slowly to its effect
"It is a proper slow burn, so do not judge it in the first half hour."
To fall flat -- to fail to produce the effect it was clearly aiming for
"One scene does fall flat, and everybody in the room notices."
A standing ovation -- the audience standing up to applaud at the end
"There was a standing ovation, which in that theatre means very little."
A revival -- a new production of a play that was written and staged long ago
"It is the fourth revival in twenty years, and the first one that works."
To be set in -- to take place in a particular time or place
"It is set in a single room, over one night, and it never leaves."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

It is often said that critics agree about the great productions and only argue about the mediocre ones. The current revival is a useful test, because it has been reviewed twice by the same newspaper and the two verdicts barely touch. The first review, written after the opening night, called the staging atmospheric and the lead role understated to the point of invisibility. The second, three weeks later, described the same performance as the most gripping thing on a London stage this year.

Nothing on stage had been changed. What had changed was the audience. A production that is built as a slow burn is punished by an opening night, where the room is looking for an effect in the first ten minutes. Three weeks later the theatre is full of people who paid and who arrive prepared to give it time. It is now widely believed in the industry that opening-night reviews measure the room as much as the work, and several papers have quietly moved their reviewers to the second week.

None of which means reviews are worthless. A review that says a production is uneven, and then says exactly which twenty minutes fall flat, is worth more than any number of stars. A play can be panned and still be worth your evening; a play that is thought to be a masterpiece can be, on the night you go, overrated and far too long. The production is set in a single room over one night, it ends with a standing ovation most evenings, and none of that tells you whether you will like it. The useful question is never whether the critic liked it. It is what the critic was looking at.

1. "It is now widely believed in the industry that opening-night reviews measure the room." Who believes this?
A The writer of the article, and only the writer.
B Nobody is named. The structure reports an opinion held generally, without the writer having to adopt it or attribute it to anybody.
C The newspaper that published the two reviews.
2. "Nothing on stage had been changed." Why the passive rather than "nobody had changed anything on stage"?
A Because who might have changed it is irrelevant. The passive puts the stage at the front, which is what the sentence is about.
B Because the passive is always more formal, and formal is always better.
C Because the writer does not know who was responsible.
3. "A play that is thought to be a masterpiece can be overrated." What is this structure doing?
A Stating that the play is definitely a masterpiece.
B Stating that the play is definitely not a masterpiece.
C Reporting the general opinion and then disagreeing with it in the same sentence. The writer never has to say who thinks so.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Passive Voice and Impersonal Reports

GRAMMAR

How to put an opinion on the table without standing behind it -- something you already do daily in Portuguese.

JobFormExample
The passivebe + past participleThe play was reviewed twice.
Naming who, if it mattersby + agent, at the end...twice by the same newspaper.
Present perfect passivehas / have been + participleIt has been reviewed twice.
Reporting what people thinkit is said / believed / thought thatIt is often said that critics agree.
The shorter versionsubject + is thought / said to beIt is thought to be a masterpiece.
Describingbe described / regarded asIt is widely regarded as her best.
Why you would use itreport an opinion without adopting itIt is thought to be brilliant. I disagree.

Never: the staging described as atmospheric · has been review twice · panned from every critic · Is often said that... · it is thought to being a masterpiece

Stage 2: Put the Recommendation in Order

Order

A friend read one bad review and decided not to go. Put your answer in a logical order.

?"It was panned on the opening night, and I would ignore that."
?"It is a slow burn, and an opening-night audience will not wait."
?"The staging is atmospheric and the lead performance is understated."
?"One scene does fall flat, I will admit that."
?"But the last twenty minutes are the most gripping thing I have seen this year."
?"So go, but go in the second week. Shall I book?"

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to report the general opinion without saying you share it. You say:
A "Is often said that the second act is too long."
B "It is often said that the second act is too long."
C "The second act is too long, everybody knows."
The critics destroyed it. You report that. You say:
A "It was panned from every critic in London."
B "It was panned by every critic in London, and it sold out anyway."
C "Every critic in London panned by it."
You liked it and you want to be precise about why. You say:
A "The performance is understated, and that is exactly why it works."
B "It was very good, five stars."
C "It is set in a standing ovation."
You are recommending it with a condition attached. You say:
A "Do not go, it was panned."
B "Go, but go in the second week. It is a slow burn and it needs an audience that will wait."
C "It is overrated, so you will love it."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Review something you actually saw: a film, a play or a concert. In one minute: what it is about and where it is set, what the general opinion of it is, where you agree and where you do not, and a recommendation with a condition attached. Use at least two impersonal structures.

Lesson 6 — What the Critics Said

Podcast
Fresh Air (NPR) -- "In 1981, this Sondheim musical flopped. Now 'Merrily We Roll Along' is a hit"

The lesson, but real. The same show was panned on Broadway in 1981 and won seven Tony nominations forty years later, with the director and the lead actor explaining what changed. Terry Gross asks the question the whole lesson was built on: was it the production, or was it us?

Tip: listen for every impersonal structure they use to report what the critics said in 1981. Write down two, then use them about something that was badly received when it came out and is admired now.

Hayley Levitt -- "Who decides what art means?" (TED-Ed, 5 min)

Five minutes on the question underneath this lesson: how much the artist's intention should weigh against what the viewer sees. Narrated slowly and written tightly, so it is also a small masterclass in saying a complicated thing in short sentences.

Tip: at the end, answer the title question out loud in three sentences, using one impersonal structure. Then answer it the opposite way, also in three sentences.
