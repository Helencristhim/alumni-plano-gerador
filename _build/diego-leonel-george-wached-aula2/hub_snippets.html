Reading the Abstract -- How a Paper Is Built

Two hundred words in a fixed order, written almost entirely in one voice. Key words: a trial, to enroll, a cohort, baseline, an outcome, to assess, adherence, significant, to conclude, a limitation, to report, to be associated with. Structure: the passive voice, present and past simple.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A trial -- a scientific study that tests whether a treatment works
"The trial compared the new cream with a placebo."
To enroll -- to sign a patient up to take part in a study
"Two hundred and forty adults were enrolled between March and July."
A cohort -- a group of people studied together over a period of time
"The cohort was followed for twelve months."
Baseline -- the measurement taken before the treatment starts
"Severity was recorded at baseline and at week twelve."
An outcome -- the result that a study sets out to measure
"The main outcome was the change in severity at week twelve."
To assess -- to measure something carefully in order to judge it
"Severity was assessed by two dermatologists."
Adherence -- how closely a patient follows the treatment as prescribed
"Adherence was low in the group that applied the cream twice a day."
Significant -- large enough that it is unlikely to be the result of chance
"The difference was significant, although it was small."
To conclude -- to state, at the end, what the results mean
"The authors conclude that once-daily treatment is enough."
A limitation -- a weakness the authors admit about their own study
"The main limitation was the short follow-up period."
To report -- to state a finding in writing, in a paper
"Nine patients reported burning in the first week."
To be associated with -- to appear together with something, without proving it caused it
"Poor adherence was associated with a worse outcome, but the paper does not claim it caused it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An abstract always tells the same story in the same order, and almost always in the same voice. First the question. Then the method: two hundred and forty adults were enrolled at four centers, and half of them were treated once a day. Notice that nobody is named. We are not told who signed the patients up, because it does not matter.

Then the numbers. Severity was assessed at baseline, at week six and at week twelve, and it was assessed by two dermatologists who did not know which schedule each patient had been given. Here the doer does matter, so the sentence names it, at the end, after the word by. Adherence was measured by weighing the tubes. Nine patients reported burning in the first week — and this one is active, because the patients themselves did the reporting.

Then the result. The difference between the two groups was small and it was not significant. Better adherence was associated with a better outcome. And then the last paragraph, where the authors conclude what it all means and admit one limitation: twelve weeks is short. In a well-written paper, the strong claim never appears in the passive to hide who is making it. It appears in the active, with the authors’ name on it.

1. "Two hundred and forty adults were enrolled at four centers." Why does the sentence not say who enrolled them?
A Because the authors want to hide a mistake.
B Because the doer does not matter here, and the passive lets the sentence start with the people who were studied.
C Because English never names the doer in the past.
2. "Severity was assessed by two dermatologists." Why is by used here and not in the sentence about enrolment?
A Because who did the assessing is part of the evidence: two independent dermatologists who did not know the schedule.
B Because by is always used with people and never with places.
C Because the verb assess cannot be used without by.
3. "Nine patients reported burning in the first week." Why is this one active?
A Because the number nine is small.
B Because report has no passive form in English.
C Because the patients themselves did the reporting, and the sentence is about them doing it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Passive Voice

GRAMMAR

One decision: does the reader need to know who did it? If not, put the receiver first and build the verb with be.

JobFormExample
Finished study, doer irrelevantwas / were + past participleAdults were enrolled at four centers.
General instruction, nowis / are + past participleThe cream is applied once a day.
The doer is evidence+ by + the doer, at the endSeverity was assessed by two dermatologists.
Negativewas / were not + participleNine patients were not withdrawn.
Questionwas / were + subject + participleWas adherence measured?
Irregular participles to knowgive → given, take → taken, show → shown, find → foundThe result was shown in figure two.
Stay activewhen the doer is the pointNine patients reported burning. The authors conclude...

Never: two hundred adults enrolled by the study team · severity was assess at week twelve · reviewed for a second dermatologist · the results was reported · it was concluded by us that (say we conclude that).

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A colleague asks how many people took part in the study. You answer:
A "Two hundred and forty adults enrolled by four centers."
B "Two hundred and forty adults were enrolled at four centers."
C "It was enrolled two hundred and forty adults."
You want to say that the two dermatologists who judged the skin are part of the evidence. You say:
A "Severity was assessed for two dermatologists."
B "Severity was assessed by two dermatologists who did not know the schedule."
C "Two dermatologists was assessed the severity."
The paper shows that two things appeared together. It does not show cause. You say:
A "Better adherence was associated with a better outcome, but the study does not prove it caused it."
B "Better adherence made the outcome better."
C "The outcome was better because of the adherence, obviously."
A colleague says the difference was significant, so it must be large. The accurate reply is:
A "Yes, significant means large in a paper."
B "Significant only means it is unlikely to be chance. A significant difference can still be too small to matter to a patient."
C "Significant means the authors are confident about the treatment."
