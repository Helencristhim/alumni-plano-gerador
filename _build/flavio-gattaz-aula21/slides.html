Chapter 3: The Code

How English Asks a Question

When you need do, where the preposition goes, and how to follow up

Grammar Discovery

Where Did the Do Go?

"Who decides which team each student joins?"

"Who do the learning teams report to?"

"What is the program best known for?"

"Isn’t the first year the hardest?"

In the first question there is no do. In the second there is. What is the difference between who in the two questions? Where did the prepositions to and for go? And in the last question, what answer does the speaker expect?

ToolFormExample
Object questionquestion word + do / does / did + subject + verbWho do the teams report to?
Subject questionwho / what is the subject: verb directly, no doWho decides the teams? What happens in week one?
Preposition at the endthe preposition stays after the verbWhat is the program known for? Who will I work with?
Negative questionto check something you think is true; expects yesIsn’t the first year the hardest?
Short follow-upsWhat makes you say that? / How so? / Such as? / How did that work out?What makes you say that?
One at a timeask one question, listen, then follow up — never two in oneWhat do they find hardest? → How so?

In one line: if who or what is the subject, no do; the preposition goes to the end; and one question at a time, then follow up.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Interview, Turned Around

One conversation, two recordings, one score sheet

Dialogue

Any Questions for Me?

M
We have about five minutes left. What questions do you have for me?
F
Thank you. What do the students who do well in the first year have in common?
M
Mostly, they ask for help early. The ones who struggle are usually the ones who try to do everything alone.
F
What makes you say that? Is it something you see in the learning teams?
M
Exactly. A student who never asks a question in week two is usually in trouble by week ten.
F
That is very insightful. Who decides which team each student joins?
M
The program office does. We never put two people from the same industry in one team, so you would probably be the only engineer.
F
Then let me dig deeper. How have engineers done in teams like that, and what did they find hardest?
Comprehension

About Megan

1. What does Megan say the students who do well have in common?
They ask for help early; the ones who struggle try to do everything alone.
2. What warning sign does she mention?
A student who never asks a question in week two is usually in trouble by week ten.
3. How does the program office build the learning teams?
It never puts two people from the same industry in one team, so Flavio would probably be the only engineer.
Listening 1 · An Interviewer

Not a Formality

An admissions interviewer on the last question of the interview. Sound first — no text.

1. Why is her last question not a formality?
Because it is the last part of the evaluation.
2. What are the two weakest answers?
Saying no, and asking a generic question that anybody could answer from the website, like how long the program is.
3. What do the best candidates do, and what does it tell her?
They listen to her answer and ask a follow-up question. It tells her they will be curious in the classroom too.
Listening 2 · An Alumnus

The Question Nobody Had Asked

An alumnus on the question he asked at the end of his interview. Sound first — no text.

1. What question did he ask, and why did it matter to him?
What students who run family companies do to keep things going while they are away. He would be away one week in every six.
2. How did the interviewer react, and what did she tell him?
She smiled, said nobody had asked her that before, and told him about two alumni who set up a weekly call with their deputies.
3. What did she ask him, and what happened afterwards?
Who his deputy would be. He had no answer, and by the time the program started he had prepared somebody for the job.
