Lesson 21 -- Pre-class

The Question You Ask Them -- One Question, Then a Follow-up

Every interview ends with the same question: do you have any questions for me? The answer is scored. A specific question and one follow-up show that you did your homework. Key words: a follow-up question, to probe, to dig deeper, open-ended, a leading question, insightful, generic, to do your homework, candid, to gauge, a takeaway, to put somebody on the spot. Structure: direct question forms -- subject and object questions, questions ending in a preposition, negative questions, and short follow-ups.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A follow-up question -- a question that you ask after an answer, to learn more about it
"Her answer was short, so I asked a follow-up question."
To probe -- to ask careful questions to find out something that is not obvious
"A good interviewer probes until she finds the real answer."
To dig deeper -- to ask more questions or look more carefully to understand something better
"When she said the first year was hard, I decided to dig deeper."
Open-ended -- describes a question that cannot be answered with yes or no
"Ask an open-ended question: what, how or why, not is or do."
A leading question -- a question that pushes the other person toward the answer you want
"Isn’t this the best program in the world? That is a leading question."
Insightful -- showing a clear and deep understanding of something
"Her answer about the learning teams was very insightful."
Generic -- not specific; the same for everybody and every situation
"How long is the program? That is a generic question."
To do your homework -- to prepare carefully by finding information before something important
"Your question shows the panel that you did your homework."
Candid -- honest and direct, even when the truth is not pleasant
"I asked for a candid answer, and she gave me one."
To gauge -- to judge or measure something that is difficult to measure
"Your questions help them gauge how curious you are."
A takeaway -- the most important idea you remember after a meeting or a conversation
"My main takeaway was that the learning team matters more than the classes."
To put somebody on the spot -- to ask somebody a difficult question that he or she must answer immediately
"I did not want to put her on the spot, so I asked it gently."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At the end of the interview, the panel will ask if you have any questions. A generic question gets a low score; a specific one, with a follow-up, gets the top score. And a specific question only works if its grammar is clean. The first rule is about do. When who or what is the subject of the question, there is no do: Who decides the learning teams? When it is the object, you need it: Who do the teams report to? Brazilian speakers often say "Who does decide", which sounds heavy and unnatural.

The second rule is about prepositions. In a question, English leaves them at the end: What is the program known for? Who would I work with? A negative question checks something you already believe: Isn’t the first year the hardest? The listener hears that you expect a yes.

The third rule is not grammatical. Ask one question at a time, listen to the whole answer, and then follow up with a short question that comes from what you heard: What makes you say that? How so? Such as? A follow-up is the clearest evidence of curiosity, and curiosity is what the panel is trying to gauge.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Questions and Follow-ups

GRAMMAR

Subject question: no do. Object question: do. Preposition at the end. One question at a time, then follow up.

ToolFormExample
Subject questionwho / what + verb, no doWho decides the teams?
Object questionquestion word + do + subject + verbWho do the teams report to?
Preposition at the endthe preposition stays after the verbWhat is the program known for?
Negative questionchecks something you believe; expects yesIsn’t the first year the hardest?
Follow-upWhat makes you say that? / How so? / Such as?What makes you say that?

Never: Who does decide the teams? · What the program is known for? · For what is the program known? · Why you say that? · two questions in one breath

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

These are the last five minutes of an interview. One question, one answer, one follow-up.

?"We have a few minutes left. Do you have any questions for me?"
?"What do the students who do well in the first year have in common?"
?"Mostly, they ask for help early."
?"What makes you say that?"
?"A student who never asks a question in week two is usually in trouble by week ten."
?"That is very insightful. Who decides which team each student joins?"
