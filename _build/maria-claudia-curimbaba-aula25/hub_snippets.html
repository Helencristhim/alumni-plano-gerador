Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Quote -- a written price for a specific order, before anything is signed
"Their quote came in eight percent above last year's."
Markup -- the amount added to what something cost, so the sale makes a profit
"The markup on that line is thinner than it looks."
Ballpark -- an approximate figure, given so that both sides can start talking
"Give me a ballpark and we can work from there."
Undercut -- to offer a lower price than another supplier, to take the order
"A mill in Asia undercut us by twelve percent."
Sticking point -- the single item two sides cannot agree on, while the rest is settled
"Payment was the only sticking point left."
Deadlock -- a point where no side gives way and nothing moves at all
"Two hours in, we were in complete deadlock."
Split the difference -- to settle exactly halfway between the two offers on the table
"Let's split the difference and close it today."
Walk away -- to end a negotiation without agreeing, and mean it
"Know the number where you walk away before the first call."
Firm up -- to make something provisional final and certain
"Can we firm up the delivery dates this week?"
Shave off -- to reduce a price or a time by a small amount
"We shaved two days off the delivery date."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The second quote from Pittsburgh arrives eight percent above the first one, and Maria Claudia does not open with a number of her own. She asks for the reasoning instead. Ray, the supplier's commercial director, gives it: the February price was valid until the end of March, and the purchase order landed on the fourteenth of April. "If your order had landed in March, you would have paid the February number" -- the cause is a date, so nobody has to defend themselves. He also says that a mill in Asia had undercut him twice that quarter, and that the markup on the line is thinner than it looks. Maria Claudia accepts April and isolates what she cannot accept: the unit price is workable, and payment in advance is the only sticking point left. Ray points out that he already shaved two days off the delivery date at no charge. Twenty minutes later they are in deadlock: he cannot carry ninety days and she cannot pay on day zero. So she names it out loud and puts a way out on the table -- "let us split the difference: half in advance, half in thirty days" -- and adds that she would have walked away last week if the margin on her side had been any thinner. Ray takes the split, and they firm up the dates the same afternoon. Note the two forms: after if it is had plus the past participle, and the result is would have plus the past participle -- never would inside the if part.

1. In "If your order had landed in March, you would have paid the February number" -- did the order land in March?
A Yes, and that is why the price held.
B No -- it landed in April, so the February price no longer applied.
C It is still not clear when it will land.
2. Which form follows "if" in this structure?
A "would have" plus the past participle ("if we would have confirmed").
B "had" plus the past participle ("if we had confirmed").
C The simple past ("if we confirmed") in every case.
3. Why does Maria Claudia say that payment is the only sticking point?
A Because she wants to reopen the unit price as well.
B To keep everything already agreed closed, so only one item is still open.
C Because she has decided to walk away from the deal.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Third Conditional

GRAMMAR

This is the conditional for a past that is closed: the thing did not happen, and it is too late for it to happen. The condition is IF + HAD + past participle. The result is WOULD HAVE + past participle. Either half can come first; when the result comes first, there is no comma.

PartFormExample
conditionif + had + past participle"If we had confirmed the specs..."
resultwould have + past participle"...we would have held the old price."
negativehadn't / wouldn't have"We wouldn't have moved at all."
questionWould + subject + have + participle"Would you have accepted the ballpark?"
passive resultwould have been + participle"The move would have been shared."

Key point: compare the three conditionals you now have. "If we lower the price, we will win it" -- still open. "If we lowered the price, we would win it" -- imaginary, today. "If we had lowered the price, we would have won it" -- over, finished, too late. Two traps: "if we would have confirmed" → "if we had confirmed"; and "we would have walk away" → "we would have walked away". In fast speech, "we'd have held" is would, not had.

Stage 2: The Negotiation in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the steps of a multi-round negotiation in the correct order.

?You separate the sticking point from everything already agreed, so one open item does not reopen the deal.
?You firm up the dates and the payment in writing the same day, while both sides still remember what they agreed.
?Before the call, you write down the number where you walk away, with the reason next to it.
?When neither side moves, you name the deadlock out loud and offer to split the difference.
?You open by asking for the reasoning behind the quote, not by naming a figure of your own.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best sentence for each situation.

You want to explain the price increase without blaming the buyer. Which is best?
A "You should have sent the order earlier."
B "If the order had landed in March, we would have paid the February number."
C "If the order would have landed in March, we would have paid less."
Everything is agreed except payment, and you do not want the rest reopened. Which is best?
A "The unit price and the volume are settled. Payment is the only sticking point left."
B "Let's go back to the beginning and look at all the numbers again."
C "Nothing here works for us, honestly."

A "I will wait until you give me a better number."
