Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

The Meeting With the Americans

A fast call with the American side: interrupting, asking for clarification and confirming what was decided. Key expressions: to chime in, to circle back, to be on the same page, to run by, a ballpark figure, to push back, a blocker, to take it offline, a hard stop, to weigh in, to be swamped, to loop in, a workaround, to be up to speed. Structure: indirect and embedded questions — no inversion, no do/does/did, and if or whether for yes/no.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to meetings, and most of them are almost never used anywhere else.

To chime in -- to add something to a conversation that is already going on
"Please chime in if I miss anything on your side."
To circle back -- to return to a point later in the same conversation
"Let us circle back to the budget once the scope is agreed."
To be on the same page -- to have the same understanding of something
"I want to be sure we are on the same page before we move on."
To run by -- to tell somebody an idea in order to get their reaction
"There is one thing I would like to run by you before the call."
A ballpark figure -- an approximate number, given quickly
"Give me a ballpark figure now and we can refine it next week."
To push back -- to disagree or resist, without refusing outright
"I would push back on that timeline before we commit to it."
A blocker -- the one thing that is stopping the work from moving
"The only blocker left is the signature on page nine."
To take it offline -- to move a discussion out of the meeting
"Let us take it offline, since it only affects two of us."
A hard stop -- a fixed time at which you have to leave
"I have a hard stop at four, so let us start with the decisions."
To weigh in -- to give your opinion on something being decided
"Could you weigh in on the indemnity before we close this?"
To be swamped -- to have far more work than you can handle
"I am swamped this week, but I can look at it on Friday."
To loop in -- to include somebody in a conversation from now on
"I will loop in the finance team before the next call."
A workaround -- a way of getting past a problem without actually solving it
"We found a workaround, but it will not survive a year."
To be up to speed -- to have all the current information about something
"Give me two minutes to get up to speed on the numbers."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways to ask the same question in English, and the polite one is not simply longer. Where are we on the indemnity? is direct and perfectly correct. Could you tell me where we are on the indemnity? is the version that opens a conversation instead of demanding an answer — and the moment you add that opening, something has to move inside the sentence.

What moves is the word order. Once the question has already been asked by Could you tell me, the second half goes back to being a normal statement: subject, then verb. where we are, not where are we. The auxiliary goes too: Do you know what he wants?, never what does he want. The tense is not lost — it moves onto the main verb, which is doing the work all along.

For a yes/no question there is nothing to move, so English adds a word instead: if or whether. I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed. Not that, which is the commonest mistake here, because Portuguese uses que in both places and nothing warns you. Whether is safer in writing and obligatory before or not; if is what everybody says out loud.

The reason any of this matters is speed. In a meeting you have about two seconds to take the turn, and Sorry, can I jump in? and Could you tell me... are prefabricated: you start speaking before the sentence is finished in your head, and the grammar buys you the rest. That is the real function of these openers. They are not manners. They are time.

1. What happens to the word order inside an indirect question?

C The verb comes first.
2. What happens to do, does and did?
A They stay, to keep the sentence a question.

3. Which word does the text say to use for a yes/no question?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect and Embedded Questions

GRAMMAR

Portuguese keeps the same word order in both the direct and the indirect version, so nothing in your first language warns you that anything has to move. That is why this error survives in otherwise excellent English — and why an American ear catches it immediately.

FormWhat it doesExample
no inversionInside the second half, the word order goes back to normal: subject, then verb. This is the whole rule, and it is the one that gets forgotten under pressure."Could you tell me where we are?"
not "where are we"
no do / does / didThe auxiliary belongs to the direct question and does not survive the move. What is left is the plain verb, carrying the tense itself."Do you know what he wants?"
not "what does he want"
if · whetherA yes/no question needs one of these to attach. Whether is the safer one in writing and before or not; if is everywhere in speech."I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed."
who · what as subjectWhen the question word IS the subject, nothing changes at all — there was never an inversion to undo. This is why these two feel easy and the others do not."Do you know who signs this?"
the openersFour that cover almost every meeting: Could you tell me..., Do you know..., I was wondering..., Can I ask.... Learn them as one block, not as grammar."I was wondering whether we could circle back to that."

One habit for this week: before your next call, write down the three questions you know you will need and put Could you tell me or I was wondering whether in front of each one. Say them out loud twice. On the call they will come out without you assembling them.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are on a call with the American side. Choose the version that works.

You want to ask where a point stands, politely:
A "Could you tell me where are we on the indemnity?"
B "Could you tell me where we are on the indemnity?"
C "Could you tell me where do we are on the indemnity?"
You want to ask what the other side wants:
A "Do you know what does he want from us?"
B "Do you know what he wants from us?"
C "Do you know what he want from us?"
You want to ask a yes/no question indirectly:
A "I was wondering that the deadline is fixed."
B "I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed."
C "I was wondering is the deadline fixed."
The call is moving on and the item matters to you:
A Say nothing and send an email afterwards.
B "Sorry, can I jump in? This one is a blocker on our side."
C "Excuse me, you are speaking too fast for me."
You are asked for a number you do not have:
A "I do not know."
B "I would rather not guess on the call. Can I run a figure by the board and come back to you?"
C "Maybe around one million, more or less, I am not sure."
