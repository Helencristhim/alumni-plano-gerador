Lesson 14 -- Pre-class

When Numbers Tell a Story -- By, To and From

The words for a number that moves, and the small words that say how much. Key words: to climb, to plunge, to peak, to bottom out, to fluctuate, to edge up, steadily, sharply, a spike, a dip. Structure: describing trends with verb + adverb, adjective + noun, and by, to, from and at.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To climb -- to go up slowly over a period of time
"Our tax bill has climbed every year since 2021."
To plunge -- to fall suddenly and by a lot
"Sales plunged by forty percent in April."
To peak -- to reach the highest point before going down
"Visitors peaked at two million in July."
To bottom out -- to reach the lowest point and stop falling
"Prices bottomed out in March and started to recover."
To fluctuate -- to go up and down many times
"The exchange rate fluctuated all year."
To edge up -- to rise by a very small amount
"Inflation edged up to 4.1 percent last month."
Steadily -- at a regular, even speed, without sudden changes
"The number of users grew steadily for five years."
Sharply -- suddenly and by a large amount
"Costs rose sharply after the new rule."
A spike -- a short, sudden rise that quickly goes down again
"There was a spike in calls on the first day of the month."
A dip -- a small fall that does not last long
"After a dip in May, the numbers recovered."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

When you present a number, increase and decrease tell people the direction, and nothing more. A good trend word also tells them the speed and the size. Costs can climb slowly or plunge in a week; a rate can edge up a little or peak and come back down.

Every movement has two shapes. With a verb, use an adverb: costs rose sharply, sales grew steadily. With a noun, use an adjective: a sharp rise, steady growth. The noun shape is useful for the title of a slide: A sharp rise in Q3.

The small words carry the numbers. By gives the size of the change: payments rose by 1.5 million. To gives the end point: they rose to 5.6 million. From gives the start, and at gives the top: visitors peaked at two million. Never say increased in 10 percent; say increased by 10 percent.

Time still matters. A finished period takes the past simple: in 2020 trips plunged. A period that continues until now takes the present perfect: since 2022 the number has edged up every year.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- By, To and From

GRAMMAR

A verb takes an adverb, a noun takes an adjective; by is how much, to is where it ended.
