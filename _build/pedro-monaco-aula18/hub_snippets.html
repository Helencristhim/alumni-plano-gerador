Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to a business trip: the plan, the paperwork, and the rules at the border.

An itinerary -- a detailed plan of a journey, with dates, times and places
"I'll send you my itinerary as soon as the flights are confirmed."
A layover -- a stop between two flights on the way to your destination
"We have a four-hour layover in Lisbon, so I'm going to answer emails at the airport."
A border officer -- the official who checks your passport and asks why you are visiting
"The border officer will probably ask how long you are staying."
A letter of invitation -- a letter from the company you are visiting that explains why you are coming
"The client is sending us a letter of invitation on Monday."
Proof of funds -- documents that show you have enough money for your trip
"Bring proof of funds, even if nobody asks for it."
A travel authorization -- official permission to travel to a country, requested online before the trip
"Brazilians now need a travel authorization before they fly to the UK."
To be valid for -- to be legally accepted for a certain period of time
"The authorization is valid for two years, or until your passport expires."
To expire -- to stop being legally valid on a certain date
"My passport is going to expire in March, so I'm renewing it this month."
The fine print -- the small, detailed part of a document where the important conditions often are
"Read the fine print before you agree to do any work during the visit."
To be denied entry -- to be refused permission to enter a country at the border
"Travelers who cannot explain their trip may be denied entry."
To overstay -- to stay in a country longer than you are allowed to
"If you overstay, it will be much harder to visit the country next time."
A permitted activity -- something a visitor is officially allowed to do during the visit
"Attending meetings is a permitted activity; working for a UK company is not."
To declare -- to tell customs officials about goods or money you are carrying
"If you carry ten thousand pounds or more in cash, you have to declare it."
Jet lag -- the tiredness you feel after a long flight across time zones
"By Wednesday, the jet lag will have gone, and I'll be ready for the negotiation."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Pedro has a trip to London next month, and his emails show four different ways to talk about the future. To a colleague, he writes: I'm going to ask the client for a letter of invitation. He decided that last week; it is his plan. To the client, he writes: I'm meeting your legal team on Tuesday at ten. That one is an arrangement: the date is in both calendars.

When his assistant asks for the hotel details, he answers without thinking: Sure, I'll send them tonight. That is will: a decision made at the moment of speaking, an offer, a promise. And when the partner asks if the contract will be ready, he says: By Thursday, we will have agreed on every clause. That is the future perfect: the work will be finished before Thursday arrives.

Two more details. Timetables use the present simple: the flight leaves at 11:40. And after when, before and as soon as, English uses the present even for the future: As soon as I land, I'll call you — never as soon as I will land.

None of these forms is more correct than the others. Each one tells the listener something different about the plan: whether it is an idea, a decision, an arrangement or a finished result.

1. Why does Pedro write I'm meeting your legal team on Tuesday and not I will meet?
A Because will is too informal to use in an email to a client in London.
B Because the meeting is an arrangement already fixed with the client.
C Because the meeting is a decision he is making while he writes.
2. Which sentence is a decision made at the moment of speaking?
A I'm going to ask the client for a letter of invitation.
B By Thursday, we will have agreed on every clause.
C Sure, I'll send them tonight.
3. What does the future perfect show?
A That an action will be finished before a point in the future.
B That a plan was decided some time ago and has not changed.
C That an action happens regularly, every week, according to a timetable or a schedule.
4. According to the text, which sentence is correct?
A As soon as I will land, I'll call you.
B As soon as I land, I'll call you.
C As soon as I'm going to land, I call you.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Future Forms

GRAMMAR

Your first language usually solves the future with one or two forms. English uses four, and the form tells the listener how fixed the plan is.

PatternWhen to use itExample
willA decision made now, an offer, a promise, or a prediction based on opinion.I'll send you the itinerary tonight. / I think it will be fine.
be going toA plan decided before now, or a prediction from evidence you can see.I'm going to ask for a letter of invitation. / Look at that queue: we're going to be late.
present continuousAn arrangement fixed with other people, usually with a time or a place.I'm meeting the client on Tuesday at ten.
present simpleTimetables and official schedules.The flight leaves at 11:40.
will have + past participleSomething that will be finished before a point in the future, often with by.By Thursday, we will have agreed on every clause.
when / before / as soon as + presentA time clause about the future: present, never will.When I land, I'll call you.

One habit for this week: every time you write about a plan in English, stop for one second and ask yourself: is it an idea, a decision, or an arrangement? The answer chooses the form for you.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are planning a business trip, or standing at the border. Choose the version a professional would actually use.

1. The border officer asks why you are visiting:
A I will attend some meetings, maybe, and then I will see.
B I attend meetings with a client in the City and I fly back.
C I'm attending meetings with a client, and I'm flying back on Friday.
2. Your assistant asks for the hotel address, and you offer to send it:
A Sure, I'll send it to you in five minutes.
B Sure, I'm sending it to you in five minutes, as we arranged with the hotel last week.
C Sure, I send it to you in five minutes.
3. The partner asks about the contract. You want to say it will be finished before Thursday:
A By Thursday, we will agree on every clause already.
B By Thursday, we are agreeing on every clause.
C By Thursday, we will have agreed on every clause.
4. You tell a colleague when you will call the client:
A When I will get to the hotel, I will call the client.
B When I get to the hotel, I'll call the client.
C When I am going to get to the hotel, I call the client.
5. The client asks you to stay two more weeks and work with her team. You answer politely:
A I'd love to help, but that would be work, not a meeting. I'm going to propose a plan we can do online.
B Of course, no problem.
C I can't, because the rules say I will be denied entry automatically if I stay for one more day.
