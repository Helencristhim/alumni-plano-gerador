The Supplier Call -- Asking Without Sounding Rude

The words a supply call cannot happen without: lead time, purchase order, batch, shipment, to delay, to confirm, quote, to push back. Structure: direct and indirect questions, and the word order that changes inside them. Mission: get three pieces of information from a supplier without sounding rude.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Lead time -- the number of days between an order and the delivery
"The lead time is forty days, so nothing arrives before March."
Purchase order -- the official document that says what a company buys and when
"The purchase order still shows the old date."
Batch -- a group of products made or delivered together at the same time
"The first batch leaves the factory on the eighteenth."
Shipment -- the goods that travel together in one delivery
"The shipment arrives two weeks after the store opening."
To delay -- to make something happen later than planned
"A busy port delays every container by three days."
To confirm -- to say officially that something is certain
"Please confirm the new date in writing."
Quote -- a written price for an order or a job
"The quote does not include freight to Santos."
To push back -- to ask politely for a later date, or to say no without closing the door
"If the fabric is late, we push back the delivery to April."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A first call with a supplier is a list of questions, and the list is always the same. Could you tell me when the first batch leaves? Do you know if the quote includes freight? Would you mind telling me who signs the change? Three questions, three openers, and the same information you would get with a direct question.

The difference is what happens after the opener. A direct question turns the sentence around: When does it ship? An indirect question does not. Once you have said Could you tell me, the rest goes back to normal order: when it ships, and the does disappears. For a yes-or-no question you need if or whether: Do you know whether the shipment is on time? The opener is doing all the work, so the rest of the sentence can relax.

1. Why does the text say Could you tell me when it ships and not when does it ship?
A Because ship is an irregular verb after could.
B Because the question is already in the opener. After it, the sentence goes back to normal order and the auxiliary disappears.
C Because the indirect question is always in the past.
2. When do you need if or whether inside the question?
A When the answer is yes or no, and there is no question word like when or who.
B Every time you start with Could you tell me.
C Only when you are writing, never when you are speaking.
3. What is the effect of the opener on the person listening?
A It makes the question longer, so it is less clear.
B It gives them a second to prepare, and it asks permission before it asks for information.
C It shows that you are not sure of your own question.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Direct and Indirect Questions

GRAMMAR

One question, two doors. The direct door is fast and fine with people you know. The indirect door is what you use on a first call.

FormUse it forExample
Could you tell me...The most common opener at work. Works with when, where, who, how much and with if.Could you tell me when the batch ships?
Do you know if / whether...Yes-or-no questions. Whether is slightly more formal.Do you know if the quote is final?
I was wondering...The softest opener. It is a statement, so it ends with a full stop.I was wondering whether you could confirm the date.
Would you mind telling me...Very polite, useful when you are asking for something the person may not want to give.Would you mind telling me who signs this?
The word orderAfter the opener: subject, then verb. No do, no does, no did. Could you tell me when it arrives, never when does it arrive.
Who as subjectWhen the question word is already the subject, nothing moves at all: Who signs this? becomes ...who signs this.

A habit that fixes almost everything: say the opener, take a breath, and then say the rest as if it were a statement. If it still sounds like a question inside, the word order is wrong.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are on a call with a supplier. Choose the best thing to say.

It is your first call with this supplier and you need the ship date. You say:
A Could you tell me when does the first batch ship?
B Could you tell me when the first batch ships?
C Tell me the ship date of the first batch.
You want to know whether freight is in the price, and you do not want to sound suspicious. You say:
A Do you know if the quote includes freight?
B Do you know does the quote include freight?
C The quote includes freight or not?
The supplier gives you a date three weeks too late. The most useful answer is:
A That is impossible. We need it earlier.
B That date does not work for our opening. Could you tell me what is possible?
C I was wondering that you can deliver earlier.
You did not understand the number the supplier just said. You say:
A Repeat, please. I did not understand nothing.
B Sorry, I did not catch that. Could you say it again?
C What? Say again the number.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Think about one real order you are following this week. Record about ninety seconds. Start by saying what the order is and where it comes from. Then ask four questions about it out loud, each one with a different opener: Could you tell me, Do you know if, I was wondering whether, and Would you mind telling me. Finish by saying what you would do if the supplier pushed the date back by two weeks. Do not stop to correct yourself.
