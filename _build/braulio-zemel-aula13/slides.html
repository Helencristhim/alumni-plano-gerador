Chapter 1: The Door

Where Does the Evening Go Wrong?

Think of a dinner in English where you ended up quiet. At which exact moment did you stop being in the conversation? The door, the table, or after the main course?

Where You Start

Three Moments, Not Three Hours

The door

Forty seconds while the coats come off. One prepared question.

The lull

After the main course. Whoever fills it hosts the next ten minutes.

The exit

One specific sentence. It is why people invite you again.

Nobody loses a table in the middle. You lose it at the door, with nothing prepared, or in the silence after the main course, when everybody waits for somebody else. Both are fixable, and neither is about your level of English.

Chapter 3: The Code

It Is, Isn't It?

The two smallest structures in English, and the two most social

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"You lived in San Francisco, didn't you?"

"That is not what the article said, is it?"

"I spent a month there in 2012." — "Did you?"

"She has never been to Brazil." — "Hasn't she?"

None of these four is really a question. Nobody is asking for information. So what are they doing, and what decides the little words at the end?

FormWhat it doesExample
Positive sentence, negative tagInvites the other person to agree and to keep talking. It is not a real question."You lived there, didn't you?"
Negative sentence, positive tagSame job, from the other side. Common when you are challenging something gently."That is not what it said, is it?"
Which verb goes in the tagWhatever auxiliary the sentence used. No auxiliary in the sentence means do, does or did."She has been, hasn't she?"
"You went, didn't you?"
Echo questionTwo words that say I am listening, tell me more. Repeat the auxiliary, change nothing else."I spent a month there." — "Did you?"
Why they matterThey cost two words and they keep you inside a conversation you are struggling to follow."Have you?" · "Is it?" · "Didn't he?"

Portuguese does all of this with ne? and a change of tone. English makes you choose the verb, which is the only difficult part — and the echo question skips even that.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"You lived in San Francisco, no?"
"You lived in San Francisco, didn't you?"
"She is Brazilian, is she?"
"She is Brazilian, isn't she?"
"They have been to Japan, don't they?"
"They have been to Japan, haven't they?"

Two rules and nothing else. The tag goes the opposite way to the sentence — positive sentence, negative tag. And the tag repeats the same auxiliary the sentence used, or takes do, does, did if there was not one. The Portuguese ne? has no equivalent in English, and no? at the end marks a foreigner instantly. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Chapter 4: The Whole Evening

Before Anybody Arrives

The map of the table, from somebody who has hosted it before

Dialogue

Whoever Fills It Hosts the Table

H
Two things before they get here. Paul talks. He is not rude, he just fills every silence, so you will have to interject.
B
And he will not mind, will he?
H
He will not even notice. Ann is the opposite. Four sentences all evening, and every one of them is worth hearing.
B
What do I do when the table goes quiet? That is the part I always lose.
H
Have one question ready. Every dinner has a lull after the main course, and whoever fills it hosts the table for the next ten minutes.
B
Does it? I had always assumed that silence was somebody else's job.
H
It is nobody's job, which is exactly why it is yours if you want it. And one more thing. How you leave matters more than anything you say in the middle.
B
So one specific sentence at the door, and they invite me again.
Comprehension

About Vivian

1. What does she say about Paul?
That he talks and fills every silence -- he is not rude, but you will have to interject, and he will not even notice.
2. How does she describe Ann?
The opposite -- four sentences all evening, and every one of them worth hearing.
3. What does she say happens to whoever fills the lull?
They host the table for the next ten minutes.
4. What is the last thing she tells him, and why does it matter?
That how you leave matters more than anything you say in the middle.
Listening

A Map of Who Is Coming

The hostess, ten minutes before the doorbell. Sound first -- no text.

1. What does she say about Paul, and what does she advise?
That he fills every silence and is not rude -- so if you want to say something you will have to interject, and he will not mind.
2. What should you do about Ann, and when?
Ask her something if there is a lull -- she says four things all evening and every one is worth hearing.
3. What is the warning about her brother, and what is the correct answer?
He will ask what you used to earn about twenty minutes in -- and the correct answer is a joke and a change of subject.
Listening 2

Three Moments That Decide the Evening

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

1. How long is the first moment, and what is worth more than good intentions?
About forty seconds while the coats come off -- and one prepared question is worth more than an hour of good intentions.
2. When does the lull arrive, and what should you have ready?
Usually after the main course -- and you should have a question ready, not a story.
3. What does leaving well take, exactly?
One sentence: name a specific thing somebody said, and say you want to hear the rest of it next time.

The Silence After the Main Course

Scenario: The plates have gone and nobody is talking. Fill the lull with a question for the whole table, then draw out the quietest person there.

Keywords:

Can I ask the table something? You have been very quiet What do you make of it? to draw somebody out
Free Practice

Door to Door

Scenario: The whole evening, compressed. Arrive and open with one question. At the table, hold the floor once for ninety seconds. Fill the lull. Deflect one question about your own money. Then leave with one specific sentence.

No keywords. The evening is yours.

The Habit

Two Questions, In Your Pocket

Before the next class, write two questions and keep them. One for the door, specific and not about work. One for the lull, for the whole table. Say them out loud twice so that they arrive without thinking.

Nobody has ever regretted arriving at a dinner with a question ready.
