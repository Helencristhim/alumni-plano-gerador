Lesson 13 -- Pre-class

The Dinner Table Debrief -- Three Hours, From the Door to the Door

The whole evening in three moments -- the door, the lull and the exit -- and the two smallest structures in English that keep you inside a conversation. Key expressions: to interject, gracefully, to deflect, rapport, animated, tactful, articulate, to hold the floor, to draw somebody out, to steer, a lull, to bow out. Structure: question tags and echo questions.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. None of these is about a subject -- they are all about what people are doing while they talk. These twelve stay here in your material.

To interject -- to come into a conversation while somebody else is still speaking
"He fills every silence, so you are going to have to interject, and he will not mind."
Gracefully -- in a smooth way, so that nobody feels the effort
"He changed the subject gracefully and nobody at the table noticed."
To deflect -- to send a question somewhere else instead of answering it
"She deflected the question with a joke and asked him one back."
Rapport -- the easy comfort between two people who are talking well
"They had rapport within four minutes, and neither of them worked at it."
Animated -- lively, with the hands moving and the voice going up
"The whole table got animated the moment somebody said the word election."
Tactful -- careful not to hurt somebody, without lying to them
"It was a tactful answer, and it was also completely true."
Articulate -- able to say a complicated thing clearly, out loud
"He is more articulate in his second language than most people are in their first."
To hold the floor -- to keep talking while everybody else listens, on purpose
"She held the floor for ten minutes and nobody looked at their phone."
To draw somebody out -- to make a quiet person talk, by asking the right question
"Ask her about the northeast. It is the one thing that draws her out."
To steer -- to move a conversation gently towards a different subject
"He steered the table away from the election before anybody got hurt."
A lull -- the uncomfortable silence that arrives after the main course
"Every dinner has one lull, and whoever fills it hosts the next ten minutes."
To bow out -- to leave a conversation politely, before you have to
"He bowed out at eleven and everybody was sorry to see him go."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nobody loses a dinner in the middle. The middle takes care of itself. What decides the evening is three short moments, and two of them are silences.

The first is the door. Forty seconds while the coats come off, and one prepared question is worth more than an hour of good intentions. The second is the lull, which arrives after the main course at every dinner ever held. Everybody feels it and almost nobody fills it, so whoever does becomes the host of the table for ten minutes. The third is the exit, and leaving well takes exactly one sentence: name something specific that somebody said, and say you want the rest of it next time.

In between, the work is smaller than it looks. If one person is holding the floor, you interject — which is not rude in English and is often expected. If somebody is quiet, you draw them out with a question about the one thing they care about. If the subject is getting dangerous, you steer it somewhere else gracefully. And if the question comes to you and you do not want it, you deflect it with a joke and hand it back.

The smallest tools are the two at the end of a sentence. You lived in San Francisco, didn't you? is not a question; it is an invitation to talk. I spent a month there. — Did you? is not a question either; it is two words that say I am listening, keep going. They cost almost nothing and they keep you inside a conversation you are still working to follow, which is exactly what a second language needs.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Tags and Echoes

GRAMMAR

The two smallest structures in English, and the two most social. Neither of them is really a question, and both of them keep you inside a conversation for the price of two words.

FormWhat it doesExample
Positive sentence, negative tagInvites the other person to agree and to keep talking."You lived there, didn't you?"
Negative sentence, positive tagThe same job from the other side, and useful when you are challenging something gently."That is not what it said, is it?"
Which verb goes in the tagWhatever auxiliary the sentence used. If there was none, use do, does or did."She has been, hasn't she?"
"You went, didn't you?"
Echo questionTwo words that mean I am listening, tell me more. Repeat the auxiliary and change nothing else."I spent a month there." — "Did you?"
The trapsThe Portuguese ne? has no equivalent, and no? at the end marks a foreigner instantly. The tag must also go the OPPOSITE way to the sentence.NOT "You lived there, no?"
NOT "She is Brazilian, is she?"

A practical note. The echo question is the easier of the two and it does most of the work, because it skips the hard part entirely: you only repeat the auxiliary you have just heard. When a conversation is moving faster than you are, two words keep your seat in it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a dinner with your wife's foreign friends. Choose the best thing to say.

You are at the door with somebody you have never met. The best opening is:
A "Hello. How are you? Nice weather, isn't it?"
B "Vivian told me you have just come back from Lisbon. What surprised you most?"
C "Hello. Sorry, my English is not very good."
Somebody says: "We spent three weeks in Portugal." You want to show you are listening:
A "Did you? Whereabouts?"
B "Do you? Whereabouts?"
C "Yes, yes." (and you nod)
The table has gone quiet after the main course. The best move is:
A Wait -- somebody else will start something.
B "Can I ask the table something?" and then one question.
C Start a long story about your career.
One person has been silent all evening. To draw them out you would say:
A "You have been very quiet about all this. What do you make of it?"
B "Why are you not talking?"
C Nothing -- if they wanted to talk they would.
It is eleven o'clock and you are leaving. The sentence that gets you invited again is:
A "Thank you very much, it was very nice, good night everybody."
B "That thing you said about the north -- I am going to be thinking about it all week."
C "Sorry to leave early. I have to get up tomorrow."
