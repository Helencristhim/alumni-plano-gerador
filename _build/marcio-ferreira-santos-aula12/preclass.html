The Art of the Executive Question

Asking, probing and redirecting in a case room. Key words: to probe, premise, assertion, qualifier, implication, nuance, to elaborate, to redirect. Structure: indirect questions and tag questions -- the question hidden inside a statement.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To probe -- to ask careful questions to get past the first answer
"I would like to probe the premise behind exhibit four."
Premise -- the idea an argument is built on, before any evidence
"If we accept the premise that volume drives price, this case falls apart."
Assertion -- a statement given as fact, with nothing to support it
"Prices always fall is an assertion, not an argument."
Qualifier -- a word like usually or roughly that limits how strong a claim is
"One qualifier turns an assertion into something the room can accept."
Implication -- something a text suggests without saying it directly
"The implication is that the author never tested the second market."
Nuance -- a small difference in meaning that changes the whole point
"The nuance is that prices only fall when the competitor enters at scale."
To elaborate -- to say more about something you have already said
"Could you elaborate on how you arrived at that range?"
To redirect -- to move a conversation back to the point that matters
"Let me redirect us to the question the committee actually asked."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Halfway through the session, one participant finally spoke. He did not say the author was wrong. He said: "Could I ask whether that figure is gross or net?" The room went quiet, because everybody had read the same exhibit and nobody had wanted to look slow. Then he went further: "I would be curious to understand how you arrived at that range." That is not an attack, it is a request to elaborate. His third question was the one that changed the discussion: "Do you know what the premise of the model is?" The premise was that volume drives price, and nobody had tested it. The implication was uncomfortable: half the case rested on an assertion. He closed with a tag: "The exhibit only shows the aggregate, doesn't it?" One qualifier would have saved the author. And the whole nuance, the professor said later, came from a participant who never raised his voice once. He simply knew how to probe a premise and how to redirect a room.

1. Why is it "Could I ask whether that figure is gross or net?" and never "Could I ask is that figure gross or net?"
A After the opener the clause is a statement, so there is no inversion and we need if or whether.
B Because "whether" is more formal than "if", and formal is always better.
C Because the question is about the past.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "Do you know what is the premise of the model?"
B "Do you know what the premise of the model is?"
C "Do you know what does the premise of the model?"
3. The sentence is "The exhibit only shows the aggregate". Which tag fits?
A ", isn't it?"
B ", doesn't it?"
C ", does it?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions and Tag Questions

GRAMMAR

One structure, and it is the one that makes a hard question sound polite instead of aggressive.

StructureRuleExample
Opener + wh-wordAfter the opener the clause is a statement: subject, then verb.Could I ask how you arrived at that figure?
No auxiliaryDrop do, does and did. The main verb carries the tense.what the margin is — never "what is the margin"
Yes / no insideUse if or whether when there is no wh-word.I was wondering whether the figure is gross or net.
Tag questionA positive sentence takes a negative tag, and the reverse. The tag repeats the auxiliary and the pronoun.That is interesting, isn't it? / You did not test it, did you?
IntonationYour voice falls at the end of an indirect question, because the sentence is a statement inside. On a tag, falling asks for agreement and rising asks a real question.
