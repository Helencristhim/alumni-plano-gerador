"The danger of a single story" -- Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, TED

Eighteen minutes from a Nigerian novelist on what happens when a country is known abroad through one story only. Connection to Lesson 7: this is the stereotype problem from the other side, told by somebody who has spent her life answering the same question you get asked at dinner. The page carries the full English transcript beside the video.

Tip: watch once with English subtitles and once without. Then write down the single story foreigners tell about Brazil, and one sentence that complicates it -- with a relative clause attached.

"How fake money saved Brazil" -- Planet Money, NPR

Twenty minutes of American English on the Real Plan, told to an audience that knows nothing about Brazil. Connection to Lesson 7: you lived through this story, so the content costs you nothing and all your attention goes to the language. Listen for how the reporters handle inflation, a deficit and a reform for listeners who have never heard the words in this context.

Tip: you already know the ending, so use the episode as pure listening practice. Then retell one minute of it out loud in English, one fact per sentence, with a relative clause attached to each.

"How to use relative clauses" -- British Council, Mini English Lesson

A short, direct lesson on the structure you built today: which, who, whose and where, and the difference the commas make. Connection to Lesson 7: this is the second half of your two-part answer, explained by a teacher at a speed you can slow down.

Tip: pause after each example and say one true sentence about Brazil in the same shape. Then take the clause out and check whether the sentence survives -- that test is the whole rule.
