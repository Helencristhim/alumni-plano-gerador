Key expressions: inequality, infrastructure, corruption, a reform, a deficit, a coalition, a commodity, an emerging market, resilience, a stereotype, red tape, to romanticize. Structure: defining and non-defining relative clauses -- which, who, whose, where, and what the commas change.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

Inequality -- the gap between what the richest and the poorest have
"Inequality here is older than the republic, and everybody knows it."
Infrastructure -- the roads, ports and systems a country runs on
"In the northeast the infrastructure gets thinner and the welcome does not."
Corruption -- the dishonest use of public power for private gain
"Corruption is not our invention, and it is not our monopoly either."
A reform -- a change made to improve a system that already exists
"The reform everybody is waiting for has been promised for twenty years."
A deficit -- the gap when a government spends more than it takes in
"Every government here inherits a deficit and promises to close it."
A coalition -- a group of parties that govern together because none can govern alone
"No president here governs without a coalition, and the coalition costs something."
A commodity -- a raw material a country sells abroad, such as soy or iron ore
"When the price of one commodity falls, three states feel it the same month."
An emerging market -- a growing economy that is not yet counted among the rich ones
"We have been an emerging market for forty years. At some point the word stops working."
Resilience -- the ability to take a hit and keep going
"What foreigners call resilience, we call Tuesday."
A stereotype -- a fixed picture of a place that survives the facts
"That is a stereotype, and like most stereotypes it started somewhere."
Red tape -- the paperwork and rules that make a simple thing slow
"Opening a company here means three months of red tape, and everybody plans for it."
To romanticize -- to describe something as more beautiful than it is
"Visitors romanticize the northeast, and the people who live there do not."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The question always arrives in the same six words, and there are only two bad ways to answer it. The first is to defend the country, which turns dinner into an argument. The second is to apologize for it, which turns dinner into an embarrassment. Both of them end the conversation, and neither of them is about English.

The third way has two halves. Give one fact, then attach a qualification to it. Brazil is the largest economy in Latin America, which sounds better than it feels from the inside. There is a public health system that covers two hundred million people, though the wait for an appointment can run to six months. Sao Paulo, which nobody visits for the beaches, is where most of the money is. The city where he grew up is not on any tourist map at all.

Notice that some of those clauses have commas around them and some do not. The ones with commas are extra: take them out and the sentence still stands. The ones without commas say which one: take them out and the sentence falls over. It is a country whose exports are still mostly commodities, and the same sentence without whose would not survive.

And the vocabulary follows the same logic. Inequality, corruption, red tape, a deficit, a coalition: none of these words is a confession. They are simply how a country is described by somebody who lives in it. Visitors romanticize the place or they write it off as an emerging market that never emerged, and what they miss is everything in between, which is where the country actually lives.

1. "Sao Paulo, which nobody visits for the beaches, is where most of the money is." Why are there commas?
A Because the clause says which Sao Paulo is meant.
B Because the clause is extra information -- the sentence still works without it.
C Because the sentence is long, and long sentences take commas.
2. "The city where he grew up is not on any tourist map." Why is there no comma?
A Because the clause identifies which city -- remove it and the sentence loses its point.
B Because where never takes a comma in English.
C Because the clause is extra information.
3. According to the text, why are both of the obvious answers bad?
A Because they are grammatically difficult in English.
B Because both of them end the conversation -- one as an argument, one as an embarrassment.
C Because foreigners never believe either of them.
4. What does to romanticize mean in this text?
A To describe a place as more beautiful than it is.
B To criticize a place without knowing it.
C To explain a place patiently to a foreigner.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses, and What the Commas Change

GRAMMAR

A relative clause is the second half of a two-part answer. It is what lets you say a fact and qualify it in the same breath, instead of defending it or apologizing for it.

TypeWhat it doesExample
Defining
no commas		Says WHICH one. Remove it and the sentence loses its point. Takes that, which, who, where."The city where I grew up is not on any tourist map."
Non-defining
two commas		Adds extra information about something already identified. Remove it and the sentence still stands."Sao Paulo, which has twelve million people, is not the Brazil people imagine."
whoseFor possession, and it works with things as well as with people."It is a country whose exports are mostly raw materials."
where and whenFor places and times. They replace in which and on which, which sound stiff in speech."Bahia, where we have a house now, is a different country."
The trapsAfter a comma, never that. Once the clause has where, the preposition is already inside it. And the commas carry meaning: without them, my wife who studied at Grady implies there is another wife.NOT "Brazil, that has..."
NOT "The city where I grew up in..."
NOT "It is a country which its exports..."

One test decides every case, and it takes two seconds. Take the clause out of the sentence. If what is left still means what you wanted, the clause needed commas. If what is left collapses, it never did.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a dinner with foreign friends and everything is in English. Choose the best thing to say.

Somebody asks what Brazil is like. The answer that keeps the conversation alive is:
A "It is a wonderful country and people say bad things because they do not know it."
B "It is big enough that the question has four answers. Which part do you want?"
C "It is complicated. Sorry, my English is not good enough for this."
You want to add extra information about Sao Paulo, which everybody at the table already knows:
A "Sao Paulo, which has twelve million people, is where most of the money is."
B "Sao Paulo, that has twelve million people, is where most of the money is."
C "Sao Paulo which has twelve million people is where most of the money is."
You want to say that the exports of your country are mostly raw materials:
A "It is a country which its exports are mostly raw materials."
B "It is a country whose exports are mostly raw materials."
C "It is a country that the exports are mostly raw materials."
Somebody repeats a fixed picture of Brazil that no fact ever changes. That is:
A a coalition
B a stereotype
C a deficit
A visitor describes the northeast as if it were a painting. The word for that is:
A to romanticize it
B to scrutinize it
C to reform it
