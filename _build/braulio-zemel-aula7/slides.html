Where You Start

Two Reflexes, and a Third Option

Defend it

The other person stops asking, because now it is an argument

Apologize for it

The other person stops asking, because now it is embarrassing

Complicate it

One fact, one qualification, and then they ask you the next question

Nobody at that table wants a defense of Brazil and nobody wants a complaint about it. They want the version an insider gives, with the second half attached. The second half is a relative clause, and that is the whole trick.

Chapter 3: The Code

The Clause That Carries the Second Half

which, who, whose, where -- and what the commas are for

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Sao Paulo, which has twelve million people, is not the Brazil people imagine."

"The city where I grew up is not on any tourist map."

"Brazil is the country that exports more soy than anybody else."

"My wife, who studied at Grady, speaks like an American."

Two of these have commas and two do not. Take the coloured part out of each sentence. Which ones still make sense on their own?

TypeWhat it doesExample
Defining
no commas		Says WHICH one. Take it out and the sentence loses its point. Use that, which, who, where."The city where I grew up is not on any tourist map."
Non-defining
two commas		Adds extra information about something already identified. Take it out and the sentence still stands."Sao Paulo, which has twelve million people, is not the Brazil people imagine."
whoseFor possession, and it works with things as well as people."It is a country whose exports are mostly raw materials."
where and whenFor places and times. They replace in which and on which, which sound stiff in speech."Bahia, where we have a house now, is a different country."
The trapsAfter a comma, never that. And once the clause has where, the preposition is already inside it.NOT "Brazil, that has..."
NOT "The city where I grew up in..."

One test decides everything: take the clause out. If the sentence survives, it needed the commas. If it collapses, it never did.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Brazil, that has the largest economy in Latin America, is often misunderstood."
"Brazil, which has the largest economy in Latin America, is often misunderstood."
"The city where I grew up in is not on any tourist map."
"The city where I grew up is not on any tourist map."
"My wife who studied at Grady speaks like an American."
"My wife, who studied at Grady, speaks like an American."

The third pair is not a grammar mistake at all, and that is why nobody corrects it. Without the commas the sentence says you have several wives and you are telling the listener which one you mean. The commas are not decoration in English. They carry meaning.

Chapter 4: Dinner in Sao Paulo

Dinner in Sao Paulo

Your wife's friends, your city, and a question you have been asked a hundred times

Dialogue

Eleven Years, and One Question

N
I have been studying this country for eleven years, and there is still one question I cannot answer at home.
B
Which question is that?
N
What Brazil is really like. Every time, and I have never found an answer that survives the dinner.
B
It is big enough that the question has four answers. Which part do you want?
N
That is the first time anybody has asked me that back. Start with yours.
B
The city where I grew up is not on any tourist map, and most of what visitors see is either a beach or a crisis.
N
The beach or the crisis. My students arrive with one of those two and leave with both.
B
Then send them to Sao Paulo, which nobody visits for the beaches, and to a state whose economy depends on one commodity. They will stop romanticizing it in a week.
Comprehension

About Nora

1. How long has Nora been studying Brazil?
Eleven years.
2. Which question does she say she cannot answer at home?
What Brazil is really like -- she has never found an answer that survives the dinner.
3. What surprises her about Braulio's reply?
That it is the first time anybody has asked her the question back.
4. What does she say about her students?
That they arrive with either the beach or the crisis, and leave with both.
Listening

A Reporter's Last Dispatch

A correspondent closing three weeks in Brazil. Sound first -- no text.

1. Which number surprised her, and why?
Around seventy percent of the electricity comes from renewable sources -- a figure most European countries would like to have and few visitors ever hear about.
2. What two pictures of Brazil do people describe to her before she flies?
Either a beach or a crisis.
3. What is the one thing she says she is taking home?
That nobody she met simplified their own country for her -- they complicated it, patiently, until she understood.
Listening 2

One Fact, One Qualification

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

1. What are the two losing moves he describes?
Defending your country, or apologizing for it.
2. What is the third option, in three steps?
Give one fact, then one qualification, then stop talking.
3. What does he say the second half of every one of those sentences is?
A relative clause -- that is all it is.

The Habit

One Headline, Out Loud

Before the next class, take one Brazilian headline from the paper you already read every morning and explain it out loud in English, to nobody, in four sentences. Each sentence: one fact, one relative clause.

You already have the opinion. What you are practicing is the second half of the sentence.
