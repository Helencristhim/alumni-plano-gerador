Chapter 3: Two People Who Almost Did It

Two People Who Almost Did It

One of them bought the land. The other one is still describing it.

Dialogue

Marco Bought the Field

M
So the field is still for sale. If I were you, I would go and offer them something this week.
A
I have been weighing it up for two years, Marco.
M
Two years. And in two years, has the price ever gone down?
A
No. Saying it will is wishful thinking and I know it. But if I bought it, I would have nothing left for the roof.
M
Then buy half. Or is that settling for something you would resent?
A
Honestly, yes. If I had the whole field, I would picture the fence and the two horses. Half a field is just grass.
M
That is not far-fetched, you know. Two horses on that land would be completely feasible. I did the same thing in Umbria and I was terrified for a year.
A
And if it had gone wrong? What would you have done?
Comprehension

About Marco

1. What does Marco advise at the very start, and how does he phrase it?
He tells her to go and offer them something this week, and he phrases it as advice: "If I were you, I would go and offer them something."
2. What is the argument Marco uses about the two years?
That in two years the price has never gone down, so waiting for it to drop is not a plan.
3. What did Marco do himself, and how did he feel about it?
He bought land in Umbria and did the same thing she is considering. He says he was terrified for a whole year.
Listening

I Would Have Said It Was Impossible

An Italian on the year she stopped describing the plan and started it. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What did she have ready for six years, and how detailed was it?
A complete answer to a question nobody had asked: the piece of land, the number of olive trees, even the colour of the shutters. She could describe it in more detail than her actual flat.
2. What was the different question her friend asked?
What would have to be true for her to do it THIS year, not someday.
3. What did she discover when she finally did the arithmetic?
That the thing she had been calling impossible was expensive and difficult and completely possible. It still took two years, but the six years before counted for nothing.
Chapter 4: The Code

A Past Tense With Nothing to Do With Time

if + past · would · and the were that survives in every person

Grammar Discovery

None of These Is True

"If I had ten more hectares, I would keep two horses."

"If I were you, I would offer them something this week."

"I would move to the coast tomorrow if it were not for the dogs."

"If I won that field at auction, I might sell the far end of it."

Every one of these describes something that is not the case. Look at the verb after if: it is in the past. Now ask the only question that matters — is any of this about the past?

FormWhat it doesExample
if + past simple, would + verbAn unreal or imaginary present or future.If I had more land, I would keep horses.
the past is not pastIt marks DISTANCE from reality, not distance in time.If I lived there... (I do not live there)
were, in every personStandard in this structure, including I and she.If I were you · if it were not for the dogs
would / could / mightThe result half can soften exactly as it did last week.I might sell the far end.
never would after ifThe same rule as the first conditional, one tense down.never: if I would have more land
first vs secondFirst: it may really happen. Second: it is not the case.If it rains... vs If I won the lottery...
If I were to...A more remote, slightly formal way of proposing a hypothesis.If I were to sell, where would I go?

In one line: the past tense here means not real, and it never once means yesterday.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If I would have more land, I would keep horses."
"If I had more land, I would keep horses."
"If I was you, I would not sell it."
"If I were you, I would not sell it."
"If I have more time, I would restore the whole house."
"If I had more time, I would restore the whole house."
"If I lived closer, I will visit every week."
"If I lived closer, I would visit every week."

The first is would in the condition, which is forbidden in every conditional there is. The second is the one native speakers argue about, and in this structure were is the safe answer in every person. The third and fourth are the same crime from opposite sides: they mix a real condition with an unreal result, or the reverse. Both halves have to agree on whether this is happening or not.

Listening 2

The Question Nobody Asks Back

An Englishman on the difference between a dream and a decision. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What is his uncomfortable theory about people who say what they would do?
That they are almost never talking about the future. They are describing a decision they have already made and do not want to admit to.
2. What is the honest version of most of those sentences, according to him?
Not "I would move to the coast if I could", but "I could move to the coast and I have decided not to, and here are eleven reasons that sound like obstacles."
3. What did his wife ask him, and what happened in the end?
Whether he actually wanted the boat or wanted the conversation about the boat. He never bought it, but he stopped talking about it, which he says was the more honest outcome.

1. Say what you would do first if the field were yours tomorrow.
"If the field were mine, the first thing I would do is fence the open side." Past tense in the condition, would in the result, and none of it is true yet.
2. Use the well and the shared entrance to say why this is not far-fetched.
"If the water did not work, it would be a different conversation. As it is, I would not have to dig a well or build an entrance." Notice the second sentence is real, so it drops out of the conditional entirely.
3. Now give yourself advice, out loud, in the form Marco used.
"If I were you, I would offer them something below the asking price and see what happens." If I were you is the single most useful second conditional in the language.
