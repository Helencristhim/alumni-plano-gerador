Lesson 13 -- Pre-class

If I Lived in the Middle of Nowhere -- Dreams, Hypotheses and What Is Not True

One-euro houses in Italy, a cottage with no signal and the tense English keeps for things that are not true. Key words: far-fetched, feasible, remote, self-sufficient, tempting, hypothetically, to picture something, to weigh something up, to be cut off, off the grid, a long shot, wishful thinking. Structure: second conditional for unreal and hypothetical situations.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Far-fetched -- so unlikely that it is difficult to take seriously
"Moving to a village in Italy is not far-fetched. People do it every year."
Feasible -- possible to do in practice, with the time and money you really have
"Restoring one small room is feasible. Restoring the whole house is not."
Remote -- far away from towns, roads and other people
"The village is so remote that the bus comes twice a week."
Self-sufficient -- able to produce what you need without help from outside
"If we grew our own vegetables, we would be almost self-sufficient."
Tempting -- attractive enough to make you want it, even when it is not a good idea
"A house for one euro is very tempting, until you read the small print."
Hypothetically -- used to introduce a situation that is imagined, not real
"Hypothetically, if you had a year off, what would you do with it?"
To picture something -- to form a clear image of something in your mind
"I can picture the house: blue shutters, a fig tree and no neighbors."
To weigh something up -- to think carefully about the good and bad sides before you decide
"She weighed it up for months before she said no."
To be cut off -- to be separated from other people, roads or communication
"In winter the road floods and the whole village is cut off."
Off the grid -- living without public electricity, water or internet
"If I lived off the grid, I would miss the internet more than the electricity."
A long shot -- something with a very small chance of working, but worth trying
"Getting one of those houses is a long shot, but I sent an email anyway."
Wishful thinking -- believing something because you want it, not because it is likely
"Thinking the renovation will cost nothing is wishful thinking."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every year, small villages in Italy sell old houses for one euro. It sounds far-fetched, but it is real. The catch is that you must restore the house in three years. Ana has been weighing it up. If she bought one, she would replace the roof first, and if she had more time, she would restore the whole house herself.

Her friend Marco lives in one of those villages. It is very remote: in January the road closes and the village is cut off. "If I were you, I would visit in winter first," he says. He grows his own vegetables and is almost self-sufficient.

Ana can picture the house very clearly, but she knows that thinking it will be easy is wishful thinking. If the village were not so far from a hospital, she would apply tomorrow. For now, it is a long shot — and a very tempting one.

1. "If she bought one, she would replace the roof first." Why bought and not buys?
A Because she has not bought one: the past tense shows the situation is not real.
B Because she bought the house last year.
C Because bought is more polite than buys.
2. "If I were you, I would visit in winter first." What is Marco doing?
A Telling a story about his past.
B Giving Ana advice.
C Making a promise.
3. Why does Ana not apply for the house tomorrow?
A Because the house is too expensive.
B Because Marco told her not to.
C Because the village is far from a hospital.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Second Conditional

GRAMMAR

A past tense that has nothing to do with the past. It shows that something is not real.

FormWhat it doesExample
if + past simple, would + verbAn unreal present or futureIf I lived there, I would miss the noise.
the past is not pastIt shows distance from reality, not timeIf I had a year off... (I do not)
were, for every personThe standard form, even with I and sheIf I were you...
would / could / mightThe result can be softenedI might travel around Europe.
question formwould + subject + verbWhat would you do if you won?
first vs secondFirst can happen; second is not trueIf it rains... vs If I won...

Never: If I would live in Italy · if I have more time, I would restore it · if I lived closer, I will visit. The first puts would after if; the other two mix a real half with an unreal half.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

A friend asks where you would live if you could choose. You answer:
A "If I could choose, I would live in a small village in Italy."
B "If I would choose, I would live in a small village in Italy."
C "If I can choose, I would live in a small village in Italy."
You want to give a friend advice about a house. The most natural version is:
A "If I would be you, I would ask a builder."
B "If I were you, I would ask a builder first."
C "If I am you, I would ask a builder."
A British friend wants to buy a cottage with no signal. A soft, polite way to give advice is:
A "You are wrong. Do not do it."
B "If I were you, I might try it for two weeks first."
C "If I will be you, I try it."
Somebody asks about your plans for Saturday if the weather is good. You answer:
A "If it were sunny, I would go to the lake."
B "If it is sunny, I will go to the lake."
C "If it was sunny, I will go to the lake."
