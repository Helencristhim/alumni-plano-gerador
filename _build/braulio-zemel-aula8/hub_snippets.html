The Art of Disagreement -- Why They Agree With You Before They Disagree

The concession move, and the register that challenges a claim without attacking the person who made it. Key expressions: a counterargument, to concede, to push back, diplomatic, nuanced, to oversimplify, provocative, to moderate, a premise, to qualify, a straw man, to double down. Structure: the subjunctive after suggest, recommend, insist and it is essential that.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

A counterargument -- a reply built to answer the argument on the other side
"He had the figures. What he did not have was a counterargument."
To concede -- to admit out loud that one point the other side made is right
"Concede one true thing first. It costs you nothing and it buys you the room."
To push back -- to challenge something directly, without being rude about it
"I would push back on that, and I think the numbers are on my side."
Diplomatic -- careful with words, so that a hard thing still lands softly
"That was the diplomatic version. The other version was in his face."
Nuanced -- aware that a question has more than two sides
"It was a nuanced position, which is why nobody quoted it afterwards."
To oversimplify -- to make something sound simpler than it really is
"You are oversimplifying, and I say that as somebody who agrees with you."
Provocative -- designed to get a reaction out of the room
"It was a provocative opening, and it worked. Everybody wanted to answer it."
To moderate -- to keep a discussion fair and moving, without taking a side
"Somebody has to moderate, or the loudest person wins by default."
A premise -- the assumption an argument starts from, before any evidence
"I have no problem with the conclusion. I have a problem with the premise."
To qualify -- to add a limit to what you have just said
"Let me qualify that, because I said it more strongly than I meant it."
A straw man -- a weak version of the other side's argument, invented so it can be knocked down
"Nobody said that. You are arguing with a straw man."
To double down -- to commit harder to a position instead of softening it
"He was asked to explain, and instead he doubled down."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He was right, and he lost the room. Those two things can happen in the same four minutes, and in English they happen more often than anybody admits. He had the figures and nobody argued with them, because by the time he produced them nobody was listening. He had corrected the other speaker in his first sentence.

The missing move has a shape. You concede one small true thing before you push back on the rest. The cost is nothing, because the point you gave away was never your point. The gain is the attention of the room, which goes to whoever has just proved that they were listening. Give away nothing and you sound like a man with an answer prepared before the question. Give away everything and you have agreed, and there is nothing left to say.

Two traps sit on either side of it. Concede something nobody claimed and you have built a straw man, which is visible from every seat at the table. And when somebody answers an argument you never made and then doubles down on it, the useful response is not to raise your voice. It is to say what you actually said, and to qualify it if you said it too strongly.

Then there is the register, which is where English does something Portuguese does not. I suggest that the figure be checked is a harder challenge than shouting that the figure is wrong, because the sentence has no person in it. It is essential that this be understood: the form is not diplomatic decoration. It is precision. I would ask that the vote be taken at the end, said quietly, moves a room that an accusation would only have divided.

1. "I suggest that the figure be checked." Why is the verb in the base form?
A Because the sentence is in the passive voice.
B Because suggest that takes the subjunctive, which is always the base form.
C Because the figure is plural.
2. According to the text, why is that sentence a harder challenge than shouting?
A Because it has no person in it -- only the claim is left standing.
B Because it is longer, and long sentences sound more serious.
C Because it is more polite, and politeness always wins.
3. Why did nobody argue with his figures?
A Because the figures were wrong.
B Because nobody was still listening -- he had corrected the speaker in his first sentence.
C Because the moderator stopped him at four minutes.
4. What is a straw man in this text?
A Conceding or attacking something nobody actually claimed.
B A speaker who has no figures to support the argument.
C A position with more than two sides to it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Subjunctive, or Taking the Person Out

GRAMMAR

After certain verbs, English drops back to the base form of the verb and stays there. It sounds formal, and it is the standard register of the press you read. What it does in a conversation is remove the person from the sentence.

TriggerWhat happens to the verbExample
suggest / recommend / propose thatBase form, always. No s in the third person, however singular the subject is."They recommended that he withdraw the claim."
insist / demand / ask thatBase form as well, and it carries more force than a modal would."I would ask that the vote be taken at the end."
It is essential / vital / important thatSame form, with an impersonal opening that removes you from the sentence entirely."It is essential that this be understood."
The negativenot goes straight in front of the verb. No does, no did."I suggest that he not answer that question."
The trapsNo s, no does, and suggest never takes a person after it in English.NOT "I suggest that he withdraws..."
NOT "I suggest that he does not answer..."
NOT "Can I suggest you a different way..."

This is standard American English and it is everywhere in the papers you already read every morning. British speakers often use should instead, and both are correct. What matters at a table is the effect: you are not telling somebody they are wrong, you are putting a claim in front of the room and asking that it be checked.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at a debate evening and afterwards at dinner, and everything is in English. Choose the best thing to say.

The speaker is half right. You want the room to keep listening to you:
A "That is completely wrong, and I have the figures here."
B "That is a fair point about the cost, but it is not the whole story."
C Say nothing and check the figures at home.
You want the room to check a number without accusing anybody:
A "I suggest that the figure be checked."
B "I suggest that the figure is checked."
C "I suggest you to check the figure."
You think somebody should stay quiet on one question tonight. You would say:
A "I suggest that she does not answer that question."
B "I suggest that she not answer that question."
C "I suggest her to not answer that question."
Somebody attacks an argument you never made. The word for that is:
A a premise
B a straw man
C a counterargument
You said something too strongly and you want to add a limit to it:
A "Let me qualify that."
B "Let me double down on that."
C "Let me oversimplify that."
