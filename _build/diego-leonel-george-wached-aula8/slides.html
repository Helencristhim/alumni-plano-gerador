Chapter 4: The Code

Always True, or Tomorrow

Two conditionals, and the difference is who you are talking about

Grammar Discovery

Four Sentences, One Word of Difference

"If ultraviolet light reaches the skin, it damages the cells."

"If you swim, sunscreen wears off faster."

"If you stay on the beach all afternoon, you will burn."

"If we catch it early, one operation will usually be enough."

All four begin with if, and in all four the red verb after if is a plain present. Look at the second half: two of them add will, and two do not. What changes about who the sentence is talking about?

JobFormExample
A general truth, alwaysif + present, presentIf light reaches the skin, it damages the cells.
A real future consequenceif + present, will + base verbIf you stay out, you will burn.
NEVER will after ifthe condition stays presentNot if you will stay out.
Advice inside the consequenceif + present, should / canIf it changes, you should come in.
Order can be reversedno comma when if comes secondYou will burn if you stay out.
when, not iffor something certain to happenWhen it wears off, reapply.
unless= if notYou will burn unless you reapply.

In one line: present in both halves for what is always true; present then will for what will happen to somebody. Never will after if.

Listening 1 · On the Radio

Five Minutes on Public Radio

A dermatologist answering listeners on a morning programme. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she say is the single most common mistake people make with sunscreen?
Applying it once, in the morning, and treating it as done. She says it wears off after about two hours and much faster in water.
2. What does she say about a tan?
That a tan is not protection, it is the visible sign that damage has already happened.
3. What does she tell listeners to do about a mole, and in what order?
Photograph it, monitor it over three months, and come in if it changes -- rather than waiting to be certain.
Listening 2 · A Campaign Designer

Why Fear Does Not Work

Somebody who designs public health campaigns, on what changes behaviour. Sound first — no text.

1. What happens, according to him, when a campaign leads with fear?
People look away and do nothing, because fear without a clear action produces avoidance rather than behaviour change.
2. What are the two halves every effective message has?
A consequence that is real and specific, and one action small enough that somebody can do it today.
3. What does he say about the word preventable?
That it is the most useful word in the field, because it tells the audience the ending is still open.

Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Sixty Seconds, For People Who Did Not Ask

Scenario: You have sixty seconds in front of a room of people who came for something else. Give them the whole prevention message: what happens in general, what will happen to them specifically, one action they can take today, and when to come in. No fear, no jargon.
