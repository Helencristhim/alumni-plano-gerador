Sun, Skin and the Public Message -- Prevention in Plain Conditionals

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Exposure -- how much of something the body is in contact with, and for how long
"Most sun exposure happens before the age of twenty."
To screen -- to test people who have no symptoms, in order to find disease early
"We screen patients with more than fifty moles once a year."
A risk factor -- something that makes a disease more likely, without causing it
"Fair skin and childhood sunburn are the two strongest risk factors."
Broad-spectrum -- protecting against both kinds of ultraviolet light, not only one
"Look for the words broad-spectrum on the bottle."
To wear off -- to stop working gradually, over a period of time
"Sunscreen wears off after two hours, and faster in water."
Shade -- an area protected from direct sunlight
"Stay in the shade between ten and four."
Peak hours -- the part of the day when the sun is strongest
"Peak hours are between ten in the morning and four in the afternoon."
To monitor -- to watch something over time to see whether it changes
"Photograph the mole and monitor it every three months."
Preventable -- able to be stopped before it ever happens
"Most skin cancer is preventable, and that is the whole message."
Awareness -- knowing that something exists and that it matters
"The campaign is about awareness, not about fear."
Compliance -- following a rule or a piece of advice in practice
"Compliance with sunscreen advice falls sharply after the first week."
To catch it early -- to find a disease while it can still be treated easily
"If we catch it early, a single operation is usually enough."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Public health English has almost no vocabulary a doctor does not already know. What it has is one sentence shape, repeated until it becomes invisible: if this, then that. And there are two versions of it, which say very different things.

The first is about everybody, always. If ultraviolet light reaches the skin, it damages the cells. If you swim, sunscreen wears off faster. Present in both halves, no will anywhere, because nothing here is about the future -- it is about how the world works. This is what carries every general fact in a leaflet.

The second is about one person, tomorrow. If you stay on the beach all afternoon, you will burn. If we catch it early, one operation will usually be enough. Present in the condition, will in the consequence. And the rule that has no exceptions: never put will after if. The condition stays in the present, always.

The rest of the leaflet is vocabulary you already read every week: exposure, a risk factor that makes something more likely without causing it, a broad-spectrum sunscreen, the shade during peak hours, a mole to monitor, and the word that carries the whole campaign -- preventable, which tells the reader that the ending has not been written yet.

1. "If ultraviolet light reaches the skin, it damages the cells." Why is there no will?
A Because the sentence is about a general truth, not about a future event for one person.
B Because damage is a noun.
C Because leaflets never use the future.
2. Why is "If you will stay on the beach all afternoon, you will burn" wrong?
A Because the sentence needs a comma.
B Because the condition after if always stays in the present, even when the meaning is future.
C Because burn is an irregular verb.
3. What does the text say the word preventable does?
A It softens the warning so people are less frightened.
B It is a technical term for a disease with a known cause.
C It tells the reader that the ending has not been written yet.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Zero and First Conditional

GRAMMAR

Present in both halves for what is always true. Present then will for what will happen to somebody. Never will after if.

JobFormExample
A general truth, alwaysif + present, presentIf light reaches the skin, it damages the cells.
A real future consequenceif + present, will + base verbIf you stay out, you will burn.
NEVER will after ifthe condition stays presentNot if you will stay out.
Advice inside the consequenceif + present, should / canIf it changes, you should come in.
Order can be reversedno comma when if comes secondYou will burn if you stay out.
when, not iffor something certain to happenWhen it wears off, reapply.
unless= if not, so never add another notYou will burn unless you reapply.

Never: if you will stay out you will burn · if light will reach the skin · if you go out tomorrow without sunscreen, you burn · you will burn unless you do not reapply · if it will change, come in

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You are stating a general fact about ultraviolet light. You say:
A If light will reach the skin, it will damage the cells.
B If ultraviolet light reaches the skin, it damages the cells.
C If ultraviolet light reached the skin, it would damage the cells.
You are warning one person about tomorrow afternoon. You say:
A If you stay out all afternoon without sunscreen, you will burn.
B If you will stay out all afternoon without sunscreen, you burn.
C If you stay out all afternoon without sunscreen, you burn.
A patient says her tan protects her. The accurate reply is:
A A light tan is fine, a dark one is not.
B A tan is not protection. It is damage you can see.
C A tan protects you, but only for a few weeks.
You want to say that reapplying is the only thing standing between her and a burn. You say:
A You will burn unless you do not reapply.
B You will not burn if you will reapply.
C You will burn unless you reapply every two hours.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

You have sixty seconds in front of a room of people who came for something else. Give them the whole prevention message: what happens in general, what will happen to them specifically, one action small enough to do today, and when to come in. No fear, no jargon, at least five conditionals.
