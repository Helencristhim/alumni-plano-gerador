Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. Four words tell the story of a discovery; four are the data behind it.

Observation -- something you see or notice when you watch carefully
"The first observation came from a nurse, not from a doctor."
Unexpected -- surprising, because you did not think it would happen
"We noticed something unexpected in the morning group."
Pattern -- something that happens again and again in the same way
"We checked the data three times, and the pattern is still there."
Shift -- a change in the direction of something
"This could be a real shift in how we treat nerve pain."
Cohort -- a group of people that a study follows over time
"We followed a cohort of eighty patients for two years."
Adherence -- how well patients follow their treatment
"Treatment adherence went up when we called the patients."
Inflammatory marker -- a substance in the blood that shows inflammation
"One inflammatory marker was very high in the patients with more pain."
So far -- until now; the story is not finished
"So far, we have tested three inflammatory markers."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Dr. Ayse Demir works in a pain clinic in Izmir. At a congress lunch, she tells her story. "Two years ago, we started a study with sixty patients. In March, a nurse noticed something unexpected: the patients who took the drug in the morning had less pain. Since then, we have checked the data three times, and the pattern is still there. We haven't published anything yet, but we have changed our protocol. So far, it is the most interesting finding I have ever had."
Dr. Demir says "In March, a nurse noticed", not "has noticed". Why?
A Because "in March" is a finished time
B Because the nurse is not a doctor, so we always use a simpler form of the verb for her
C Because it is a question
"We haven't published anything yet" means...
A they published a paper a long time ago, and now they are writing a second paper about it
B they will never publish
C until now, there is no paper

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Have Found or Found?

GRAMMAR

The present perfect (have / has + past participle) connects the past to now. A research story often moves from the past simple (what happened) to the present perfect (where we are today).

past simplefinished time: last year, in March, two years agoLast year, we tested two markers.
present perfectresults up to now: so far, already, everSo far, we have tested three markers.
negativehaven't / hasn't + participle (+ yet)We haven't published anything yet.
questionhave / has + subject + participleHave you published anything yet?
with sincefrom a point in the past until nowSince we started the calls, adherence has gone up.

If you say WHEN (last year, in 2024), use the past simple. If you say "so far" or "yet", use the present perfect.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz
At lunch, a researcher asks: "So, what have you found so far?" What is the best start?
A "We have found many things in many years, and all of them are in my presentation, which you can find online."
B "So what happened was this. We were measuring pain when we noticed something unexpected."
C "Eighty."
Which sentence is correct?
A "So far, we have tested eighty patients."
B "So far, we test eighty patients in our clinic, and we will test more of them next year too."
C "So far, we tested eighty patients yesterday."
Somebody asks: "Have you published it yet?" Your paper is not ready. What do you say?
A "No, I didn't publish it yesterday, and I didn't publish it last year, and I don't know when."
B "Yes, already."
C "Not yet, but we're writing it now."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Ninety seconds. Tell the story of one real finding from your research in five moves: "So what happened was this...", what you were doing, what you noticed, what you have found so far, and an invitation with if. No script -- just speak.
