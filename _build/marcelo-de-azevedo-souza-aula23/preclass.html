Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To make out -- to hear or understand something, but only with difficulty
"I could not make out a single name."
To get the gist -- to understand the general meaning, not every word
"I did not get every word, but I got the gist."
To tune in -- to start paying attention to what someone is saying
"Tune in again when the story starts."
To zone out -- to stop paying attention without noticing it
"I zoned out after the third island."
To rattle off -- to say a long list very quickly, without stopping
"He rattled off the names like a shopping list."
To slur your words -- to speak so the words run together and are not clear
"After midnight, everybody slurs their words."
Under your breath -- very quietly, almost to yourself
"She said something under her breath."
A filler word -- a small word with no meaning, like um or you know
"You know is the most common filler word."
Background noise -- the sounds behind the voice you want to hear
"The background noise in the pub was terrible."
To fill in the gaps -- to guess the missing parts from what you understood
"Your brain fills in the gaps for you."
A broad accent -- a strong regional accent, easy to recognize
"The guide had a broad Irish accent."
To drown out -- to be so loud that another sound cannot be heard
"The engine drowned out half of his talk."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Marcelo is on a boat tour in Galway. The guide has a broad accent and rattles off the names of the islands, and the engine drowns out half of them. "I could not make out a single name," Marcelo says after the tour. "Neither could I," says Nora, "and I grew up here." He admits he zoned out after the third island. "So did half the boat," she laughs. "Did you get the gist of the shipwreck story?" "I think so." Notice the short answers. So did agrees with a positive sentence, Neither could I agrees with a negative one, and the auxiliary copies the first sentence. I think so answers a question without repeating it.

1. Why does Nora say "Neither could I" and not "So could I"?
A Because she is agreeing with a negative sentence: Marcelo could NOT make out the names.
B Because "neither" is more polite.
C Because she wants to disagree with Marcelo and tell him that she understood every single name on the tour.
2. Why "So did half the boat" and not "So do half the boat"?
A Because "did" is always more natural in Irish English, in every kind of sentence and in every tense.
B Because the first sentence is in the past: "I zoned out".
C Because "boat" is singular.
3. What does "I think so" replace?
A I think I got the gist of the shipwreck story.
B I think about the boat.
C I think that the guide should speak more slowly next time, so that everybody on the boat can follow him.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Short Answers in Speech

GRAMMAR

Natives say less than you expect, and the listener fills in the gaps.

FormYou use it forExample
So + auxiliary + subjectagreeing with a positive sentence"I love this road." "So do I."
Neither + auxiliary + subjectagreeing with a negative sentence"I could not hear." "Neither could I."
think / hope / be afraid + so / notanswering without repeating"Will it rain?" "I hope not."
dropped wordsrelaxed, informal speech"Coffee?" "Sounds good."

Key point: the auxiliary copies the first sentence: "I went" → "So did I", "I have never been" → "Neither have I". Say "I hope not", never "I hope no".
