Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read it inside a real sentence. These are the words a critic uses when nice is not precise enough.

Suffused -- gently filled or spread through, usually with light, color or feeling
"The corner of the room is suffused with a cool, even light."
Rendered -- represented or shown in a work of art, especially with a particular skill
"The bread is rendered in tiny raised dots of paint."
Imbue -- to fill something deeply with a quality, feeling or idea
"Vermeer imbues a working woman with quiet dignity."
Juxtapose -- to place two things side by side so that their differences stand out
"He juxtaposes the rough crust with the smooth glaze of the jug."
Subvert -- to undermine an expectation or convention, often quietly and on purpose
"Vermeer subverts the comic convention without announcing it."
Austere -- severely plain, with no decoration and no comfort
"The wall behind her is austere, bare plaster and a single nail."
Evocative -- bringing strong images, memories or feelings to mind
"The effect is quietly evocative."
Luminous -- full of light, or seeming to glow from within
"The blue of the apron is almost luminous."
Arresting -- so striking that it stops you and holds your attention
"His refusal to decorate the wall is what makes the picture arresting."
Restrained -- controlled and understated, never showy or sentimental
"His handling of the scene is restrained rather than sentimental."
Meticulous -- done with extreme care and attention to every small detail
"The stream of milk is painted with meticulous care."
Palpable -- so strong or real that it can almost be touched
"The contrast makes each material more palpable."
Vantage point -- the position from which you see something, or the way you see it
"The vantage point matters: we look at her from slightly below."
Ekphrasis -- a vivid written or spoken description of a work of visual art
"Ekphrasis becomes more than an exercise."
Tableau -- a scene in which figures are arranged as if in a picture or on a stage
"Critics have sometimes called the painting a tableau."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Every art history student eventually discovers that description is not the easy part. The assumption that describing a painting simply means listing its contents is understandable, but it rarely survives a first assignment. A list tells a reader what is there. A description, in the sense that critics use the word, tells a reader why it matters that those things are there and not others.

Consider the difference between two sentences about the same picture. The first says that a woman is pouring milk in a kitchen and that the wall behind her is empty. The second says that the emptiness of the wall concentrates our attention on the woman's task. Both are accurate. Only the second makes a claim, and it does so largely through a single grammatical move: an adjective, empty, has become a noun, emptiness, and that noun can now act as the subject of a sentence and cause something to happen.

This move, usually called nominalization, is one of the defining features of academic and critical prose. It allows a writer to compress an entire observation into a phrase and then to evaluate it. Vermeer's refusal to decorate the wall, the modesty of the subject, her concentration, his handling of the light: each of these phrases packs a clause into a noun and leaves the rest of the sentence free to argue.

Used well, nominalization produces the density that readers of college essays and critical reviews expect. Used carelessly, it produces fog. A sentence such as "the utilization of illumination facilitates the enhancement of textural perception" contains no error that a spell checker would catch, yet it communicates almost nothing. The reader's frustration is justified, because the writer has hidden every actor and every action behind abstract nouns.

The skill, then, is judgment rather than habit. A useful test is to ask whether the noun lets you say something you could not say as easily with a verb. If the rediscovery of Vermeer in the 1860s is the subject of your paragraph, the noun phrase earns its place. If you write "the performance of an examination of the painting" when you simply mean looking at the painting, it does not.

Description, in the end, is where interpretation begins. The precision of your nouns is the first evidence a reader has that you have actually looked.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Nominalization

GRAMMAR

Critical and academic prose turns actions and qualities into nouns. The noun becomes the subject of the sentence, and the rest of the sentence is free to evaluate it. Density is the tool, not the goal.

FormWhat it doesExample
verb → nounturns an action into a thing the sentence can evaluaterefuse → His refusal to decorate is deliberate.
adjective → nounturns a quality into a subject that can cause somethingempty → The emptiness of the wall focuses the eye.
the + noun + of + nounpacks who and what into one phrasethe handling of the light
possessive + nounkeeps the agent inside the noun phraseVermeer's restraint
The C2 trapstacking nouns until nobody acts. If a verb says it more clearly, use the verb.

A test before you keep a noun: can you say something with it that you could not say as easily with a verb? The rediscovery of Vermeer passes. The performance of an examination of the painting does not.

Stage 2: Build an Ekphrasis in Order

Order

Put the moves of a careful description in the order a critic would make them.

?Zoom in on one material and say exactly how it is rendered.
?Name the work, the artist and the scale before anything else.
?End with the choice behind the image and why that choice matters.
?Describe where the light comes from and what it touches.
