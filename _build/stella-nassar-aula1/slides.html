HOW WE TALK

HOW A CRITIC WRITES

"The emptiness of the wall leaves the eye nowhere to go but to the woman's task, and to the cool light that falls across it."

Both sentences are true. Only one of them makes a claim about the painting, and only one of them could open a college essay.

Chapter 4: The Style Code

The Verb Hidden Inside the Noun

Why critics write refusal, modesty and concentration

Discover the Style

Where Did the Verbs Go?

"Stay a little longer, and the modesty of the subject begins to feel deliberate."

"His refusal to do so is what makes the picture arresting."

"The woman's concentration is total."

"His handling of the scene is restrained rather than sentimental."

Each colored phrase could have been a whole clause with a verb. What does turning it into a noun allow the rest of the sentence to do?

FormWhat it doesExample
verb → nounturns an action into a thing the sentence can evaluaterefuse → His refusal to decorate is deliberate.
adjective → nounturns a quality into a subject that can cause somethingempty → The emptiness of the wall focuses the eye.
the + noun + of + nounpacks who and what into a single phrasethe handling of the light
possessive + nounkeeps the agent inside the noun phraseVermeer's restraint
The C2 trapstacking nouns until nobody acts. "The utilization of illumination" is dense and says nothing. If a verb says it more clearly, use the verb.

Chapter 5: Voices in the Gallery

The Same Painting, Out Loud

A conversation, an audio guide and a first lecture

Dialogue

Ten Minutes in Front of a Small Painting

J
You have been standing here for almost ten minutes. Most people give this one about thirty seconds.
S
I know. I came for the big canvases in the next room, but this one is strangely arresting.
J
Strangely how? Tell me what is holding you here.
S
The light, mostly. The whole corner is suffused with it, but it does not feel dramatic. It feels like an ordinary Tuesday morning.
J
That is a good way of putting it. I usually tell groups to find the one thing in the picture that moves.
S
The milk. It is the only thing in motion, and she treats it as if spilling a single drop would be a disaster.
J
When I was your age, I thought this was the most boring picture in the whole museum. It took me years, and a very patient professor, to see it.
S
What changed?
J
She made us write two pages about it without using the word beautiful. I was furious with her for a week. Then I started actually looking.
S
That sounds exactly like the kind of prompt I am going to find on every college application.
Comprehension

About Julian

1. According to Julian, how long do most visitors spend in front of this painting?
About thirty seconds, while Stella has been there for almost ten minutes.
2. How did Julian feel about the painting when he was younger, and what helped him change his mind?
He thought it was the most boring picture in the museum. It took him years and a very patient professor.
3. What did Julian's professor ask the class to do, and how did he react at first?
She made them write two pages without using the word beautiful. He was furious with her for a week, then started actually looking.
Listening 1

Stop Fourteen: The Audio Guide

A museum audio guide at the painting. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. Where does the light enter, and what does it fall on?
Through the window on the left. It settles on the woman's face, on her arms and on the bread on the table.
2. What does the guide say about the tiny dots on the bread?
They are not an accident. Vermeer placed them one by one so that the surface would seem to glitter.
3. Why does the guide mention the pigment of the apron and the empty wall?
The expensive blue shows how seriously Vermeer took a humble scene, and the empty wall was a choice that does more work than decoration could.
Listening 2

Day One of Art History

A professor opens a first-year course. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. What does the professor say students expect to be hard, and what is actually hard?
They expect memorizing names and dates to be hard. The real difficulty is describing what they see precisely enough for someone who has never seen the work to imagine it.
2. What exactly is the first assignment, and when is it due?
Choose one painting in the university museum, look at it for at least twenty minutes, and write six hundred words. It is due next Thursday.
3. What are the two rules, and why does she forbid looking things up?
No beautiful, amazing or interesting, and no research before writing. She wants their own eyes, not the internet.

Note for applicants: our readers are looking for precision, not praise. Words such as beautiful or amazing tell us how you felt. They do not show us the work.

Not to explain why the work is famous. They have to show what they saw.

Four hundred: two hundred and fifty for the first and one hundred and fifty for the second.
