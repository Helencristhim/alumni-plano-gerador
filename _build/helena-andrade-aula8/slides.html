Chapter 4: The Code

Finished Before, or In Progress At

will have done / will be doing

Grammar Discovery

Two Futures, One Deadline

"By the time a ship reaches the pilot station, her crew will have completed the pre-arrival round."

"The deck team will be standing by fore and aft from thirty minutes before the pilot boards."

"The arrangements will have been verified by name and not by signature."

"By the end of the quarter the company will have replaced every emergency signal card in the fleet."

Two of these are finished before a moment that has not arrived yet, and one is still going on at it. Sort them -- and then find the two little words the finished ones cannot do without.

FormWhat it doesExample
will have + past participlefuture perfect: complete BEFORE a stated point in the futureBy the time she arrives, the crew will have completed the round.
will be + -ingfuture continuous: in progress AT that point, not finishedThe deck team will be standing by fore and aft.
will have been + past participlefuture perfect passive: already done by then, by somebody the text does not namethe arrangements will have been verified
by / by the time / by the end ofthe deadline the future perfect needs. Without it the tense has nothing to be earlier thanby the end of the quarter the company will have replaced...
used to / be used to / get used toa past habit now over / being accustomed to something / becoming accustomed to itShe used to serve on tankers. She is used to working night watches.
The reading trapfuture perfect says FINISHED BEFORE; simple future says AT or AFTER. An option that turns will have completed the round into will complete the round on arrival moves the whole operation to the wrong side of the deadline, and every other word stays the same.

An operational order is written in the future perfect because it describes a state the ship has to be IN when the moment comes, not a task she starts then. Read the deadline first, then decide which side of it the verb sits on.

Chapter 6: Say It Back

Say It Back

Listening 1

Four Minutes in a Machinery Space

The chief engineer on the February fire. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he insist on putting on the record first?
That the fixed system did exactly what it was designed to do: four minutes from detection to containment, compartment sealed, no distress call, nobody hurt.
2. What do the numbers not show?
That two people arrived at the wrong muster station, and that the forward locker could not be opened because it had been painted shut at the last dry dock.
3. Why does he say those two things are anecdotes and not findings?
Because the order to abandon ship was never given. If it had been, they would have mattered.
Listening 2

What the Instruction Will Actually Change

The fleet safety manager explains the new order. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she say the instruction does not do?
It does not make anybody safer on its own. Nothing written down does.
2. What is the one word that changes, and what does it mean in practice?
Verification is by name and not by signature: the round is completed person by person, and the master reports it that way, out loud and on the record.
3. What does she give as the honest measure of success?
Not the paperwork, but whether the next two people who go to the wrong station are doing it in a drill.
