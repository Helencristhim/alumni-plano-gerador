Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An elevator pitch -- a very short presentation of who you are and what you offer, short enough for an elevator ride
"My elevator pitch is thirty seconds. The full pitch is three minutes."
A narrative arc -- the shape of a story, from the beginning through the tension to the end
"A good pitch has a narrative arc: context, problem, angle and invitation."
A hook -- the first sentence or question that catches the listener's attention
"Start with a hook: one question the listener cannot ignore."
A call to action -- a clear request at the end that tells the listener what to do next
"The pitch was good, but it had no call to action, so nothing happened after it."
A positioning statement -- one sentence that says what you do, for whom, and why you are different
"Our positioning statement is simple: we connect Brazilian exporters to the world, at domestic speed."
A credibility marker -- a short fact about you or your work that makes the listener trust what you say
"Twenty years in trade finance is a credibility marker. Say it early, and say it once."
A conversation anchor -- a memorable idea in your pitch that the other person can come back to later
"Domestic speed across the border became our conversation anchor. Everybody asked about it at dinner."
To lead with a question -- to begin a pitch or a conversation with a question instead of a statement
"I lead with a question: how long does your last cross-border payment take?"
To open a door -- to create an opportunity for a future conversation or business
"The pitch does not close a deal. It opens a door."
To leave them wanting more -- to stop before you say everything, so the other person asks for a second conversation
"Three minutes is enough. Leave them wanting more, and they will ask for the meeting."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Listen to a strong speaker at any conference and you will hear one structure again and again. They do not say We need a partner. They say What we need is a partner. The information is the same, but the second sentence makes the listener wait for the important part, and so the important part lands.

This is called a cleft sentence, because it splits one idea into two parts. The first part starts with what and prepares the spotlight: What we're really trying to solve for, What makes us different, What I'd love to explore. Then comes is, and then the information you want people to remember: ...is speed at the border.

When the important part is a full idea, add that: What makes us different is that we see the gap from both sides. A close cousin works for reasons: The reason I bring this up is that the border is still slower than the country. Notice that careful speakers say the reason ... is that, not the reason ... is because.

Use it once or twice in a pitch, not in every sentence. One spotlight on the problem and one on the invitation are enough: That's actually what I'd love to explore with someone like you.

1. According to the text, why do strong speakers use this structure?
A Because it adds more information to the sentence.
B Because it makes the listener wait for the important part, so it lands.
C Because it is shorter than a normal sentence.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A What we need a partner in the United States.
B What we need is of a partner in the United States.
C What we need is a partner in the United States.
3. How often should you use it in a three-minute pitch?
A Once or twice, on the problem and on the invitation.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences for Emphasis

GRAMMAR

What + subject + verb + is ... the important part lands at the end.

UseFormExample
Spotlight on a thingWhat + subject + verb + is + nounWhat we need is a partner in the United States.
Spotlight on an ideaWhat + verb + is that + sentenceWhat makes us different is that we see both sides.
Spotlight on a reasonThe reason + sentence + is that + sentenceThe reason I bring this up is that the border is slow.
NegativeWhat + subject + verb + is not ...What we need is not another report. It's a pilot.
Question (checking)So what you're saying is ...?So what you're saying is that the pilot comes first?
PastWhat + subject + past verb + was ...What surprised me was the speed of the pilot.

Never: What we need a partner · What we need is of a partner · What makes us different is is that · The reason is because

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a strong speaker would give.

You have three minutes with an executive. How do you open?
A Let me tell you the whole history of Bradesco since it was founded.
B How long does your last payment to Brazil take?
C Sorry, my English is not very good, but I will try.
You want to put the spotlight on the problem. You say:
A What we're really trying to solve for is speed at the border.
B What we're really trying to solve for speed at the border.
C We are trying really to solve for the speed of the border.
Your pitch is finished. What is the best last sentence?
A OK, that is all. Thank you very much.
B That's actually what I'd love to explore with someone like you. Do you have thirty minutes tomorrow?
C Please sign a contract with us before the end of the conference.
Somebody interrupts after your context and asks for the gap. You say:
A Wait, let me finish the introduction first.
B Good question. In one sentence: inside Brazil a payment takes seconds, and across the border it can take two days.
C I will explain that at the end of the presentation.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Record your full pitch in about three minutes, in four parts: open with a hook, give the context in two sentences with one credibility marker, show the problem with one number, explain your angle, and finish with an invitation. Use at least one cleft sentence.

Talk
Nancy Duarte — “The secret structure of great talks” (TED)

A presentation designer shows the shape that great speeches share: they move between what is and what could be, and they end with a clear call to action. It is the narrative arc of your pitch, drawn on a screen.

Tip: draw the shape she describes on paper. Then mark where your context, problem, angle and invitation go.

Podcast
Think Fast, Talk Smart (Stanford GSB) — “Lean Messaging: How Simple Messages Really Stick”

A marketer and entrepreneur explains how to build a short message that people remember and repeat, starting from what the listener needs and not from what you want to say. Useful for your positioning statement.

Tip: after listening, rewrite your positioning statement in fifteen words or fewer.

Listen on Stanford GSB ↗
Video
Simon Sinek — “Start with why: how great leaders inspire action” (TEDxPugetSound, YouTube)

One of the most watched talks about persuasion. He argues that people respond to why you do something before what you do, which is a strong test for the context part of your pitch.

Tip: write one sentence that says why your area exists, not what it does. Try it as the second sentence of your pitch.
