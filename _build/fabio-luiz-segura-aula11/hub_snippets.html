Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A shortlist -- the few names you keep after you cut all the others
"Forty conversations, and a shortlist of three. The other thirty-seven were interesting, not useful."
To narrow down -- to reduce a long list to a small one, using a clear criterion
"We narrowed it down with one question: which of these solves a gap we have already measured?"
A business case -- the document that argues why the money should be spent, with numbers in it
"Without a business case you have an opinion. With one you have a request."
An internal sponsor -- the senior person who defends your project in the rooms you are not in
"No internal sponsor, no project. It dies in the first meeting you do not attend."
A stakeholder -- anybody inside the bank who can move your project forward or stop it
"List the stakeholders before you write a single line. One of them will surprise you."
A decision-maker -- the person whose signature actually releases the money
"Everybody in the room had an opinion. Only one of them was the decision-maker."
A budget cycle -- the fixed period in which money is allocated for the year ahead
"Miss the budget cycle and an excellent idea waits eleven months for its turn."
A deliverable -- the concrete thing you promised to produce, by a date
"Two deliverables before December: the corridor numbers and the vendor comparison."
Momentum -- the energy a project keeps while people still remember why it mattered
"Momentum is the only thing a conference really gives you, and it lasts about three weeks."
To follow through -- to do the thing you said you would do, after the enthusiasm has gone
"Anybody can agree in the room. Following through in November is the rare part."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A conference gives you exactly one thing, and it is not a partnership. It is momentum, and it lasts about three weeks. What you do inside those three weeks decides whether forty conversations become two real projects or forty business cards in a drawer. And almost every sentence you need in those weeks is built from two verbs in a row.

Look at what happens between them. We decided to shortlist three of the forty. I recommend starting with one corridor. They agreed to send a reference client this week. We postponed making a decision until the numbers exist. Two of the second verbs end in -ing and two begin with to, and no rule about meaning explains which is which. The first verb decides, and that is the whole mechanism: decide, agree, promise, refuse, manage and arrange take to; recommend, suggest, consider, avoid and postpone take -ing.

There is a third pattern, and it is the easiest of the three. When a person comes between the two verbs, the second one always takes to: she asked me to write the business case, they expect us to answer before Friday, he advised me to talk to the sponsor first. And there is one trap that is not a verb pattern at all. In look forward to, interested in and before, that little word is a preposition, and a preposition is always followed by -ing. So the closing line of your email is I am looking forward to hearing from you, never to hear.

Why this matters more here than anywhere else: a debrief is almost nothing but these verbs. What was decided, what you recommend, what the other side agreed to do, what you postponed, what you need somebody to approve. Get the pattern wrong and the sentence still communicates, but it marks you as a guest in the language. Get it right and the whole memo sounds like it was written by somebody who does this every week — which, from November, you will be.

1. According to the text, what decides whether the second verb takes -ing or to?
A The meaning of the sentence.
B The first verb, and nothing else.
C Whether the sentence is about the past or the future.
2. What does the text say happens when a person comes between the two verbs?
A The second verb always takes to: she asked me to write it.
B The second verb always takes -ing: she asked me writing it.
C The pattern depends on the first verb, as usual.
3. Why is "I am looking forward to hearing from you" correct?
A Because look forward is one of the verbs that take -ing.
B Because that to is a preposition, and a preposition is always followed by -ing.
C Because the sentence is in the present continuous.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Gerund and Infinitive After Verbs

GRAMMAR

The first verb decides. Learn the verb, not a rule about meaning.

First verbWhat follows itExample
decide · agree · plan · offer · promise · refuse · manage · expect · hope · fail · arrangeto + verbWe decided to shortlist three vendors.
recommend · suggest · consider · avoid · postpone · involve · risk · keep · finish · mindverb + -ingI recommend starting with one corridor.
ask · want · advise · expect · allow — with a PERSONperson + to + verbShe asked me to write the business case.
after a preposition (to, in, about, for, of, before)always -ingLooking forward to hearing from you · interested in running a pilot.
The meaning changesstop · rememberWe stopped funding it = we ended it · We stopped to fund it = we paused in order to fund it.
The trapsuggest, recommend and avoid NEVER take toNot I suggest to start. Say I suggest starting.
The other trapthe to of look forward to is a prepositionNot I look forward to receive. Say to receiving.

Never: I suggest to start · We decided postponing it · I look forward to receive your reply · She asked me writing the summary · I would avoid to run three pilots

Lesson 11 — After Sibos

Talk
Adam Grant — “The surprising habits of original thinkers” (TED, 15 min)

Grant studies the people inside organisations who actually get a new idea adopted, and the habits are not the ones you would expect: they doubt the idea rather than themselves, they postpone finishing on purpose, and they pitch many proposals to land a few.

Tip: write down every verb he uses for acting on an idea and mark whether it takes to or -ing — he decided to leave, he kept working on it, he postponed finishing. Five of them go straight into your own debrief.

Podcast
Insights Podcast — “Innovating Global Payments: The Power of Tokens and Interoperability” with Nick Kerigan, Swift (31 min)

Swift's Head of Innovation explains how tokenisation and interoperability move from pilot projects into real infrastructure, and what it takes to get an ecosystem of banks to commit to anything. Two British speakers, so it is also accent practice with no American voice to lean on.

Tip: listen once with no notes and write the three things he says Swift agreed to do or is working on. Then listen again and copy the exact phrase he uses each time he commits to a deliverable or a date.
