Chapter 3: The Code

Two Sentences in One

How to start a sentence with having, working or asked

Grammar Discovery

Where Did the Subject Go?

"Having run the company for twelve years, I know where it is weak."

"Working with my uncle, I learned to disagree without breaking anything."

"Asked about my weaknesses, I always start with delegation."

"Not having a business degree, I learned finance on the job."

The colored part has no subject. Who is the subject, then? In the first sentence, which action happened first? In the third, did the speaker ask, or was he asked? And where does not go?

ToolMeaningExample
Having + past participlethis happened first, and it explains the main clauseHaving run the company, I know its weak points.
Verb + -ingat the same time, or the reasonWorking with my uncle, I learned to disagree.
Past participlepassive meaning: something was done to the subjectAsked about my weaknesses, I start with delegation.
Not + participlenot goes before the participleNot having a business degree, I learned finance on the job.
Same subjectthe subject of the main clause is the person who does the first actionHaving finished the bid, I called the client. (not: the client called)

In one line: drop the subject and the conjunction, keep the participle, and make sure the next word is the person who did it.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

The Mock, From the Outside

One conversation, two recordings, one feedback form

Dialogue

Minute Twenty-Five

R
We are halfway through, so let me throw you a curveball. What would you do if we said no?
F
Having thought about it a lot, I would apply again next year, with a stronger plan for the company.
R
Good, that was concise. Could you elaborate on the stronger plan?
F
Yes. Working with my head of planning this year, I would hand over more of the daily decisions.
R
And if your uncle disagreed with that? He sounds like a strong personality.
F
He is. Asked to choose between the plan and the relationship, I would protect both, and I would talk to him first.
R
You did not get sidetracked there. Can you recap your answer in one sentence?
F
Let me recap: I would apply again, delegate more, and bring my uncle into the plan before I start.
Comprehension

About Rebecca

1. What curveball does Rebecca throw, and when?
Halfway through the interview she asks what he would do if the school said no.
2. What does she say about his first answer, and what does she ask next?
She says it was concise, and she asks him to elaborate on the stronger plan.
3. What does she ask him to do at the end?
To recap his answer in one sentence.
Listening 1 · An Admissions Coach

What Only a Full Mock Shows

A coach on why she runs the whole interview without stopping. Sound first — no text.

1. Why does she never stop a mock interview in the middle?
Because practicing one question at a time does not show what happens after twenty minutes.
2. What happens to most candidates around minute twenty?
Their answers get longer, filler words come back, and they start to get sidetracked.
3. What does she ask candidates to do at the end of the dry run, and why?
To listen to the recording the next day and write down five changes, because the day after you hear what you said, not what you meant.
Listening 2 · An Alumnus

Three Dry Runs

An engineer on what he changed after rehearsing his interview. Sound first — no text.

1. What surprised him when he heard his first recording?
His answer about his career took four minutes, and he used the word basically nineteen times.
2. What three changes did he make?
He cut every first answer to ninety seconds, replaced filler words with a short pause, and ended each long answer with a one-sentence recap.
3. What did the interviewer write about him, according to his friend?
That he came across as calm and concise, and that he thought on his feet.
