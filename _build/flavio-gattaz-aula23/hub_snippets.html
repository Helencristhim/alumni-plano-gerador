Lesson 23 -- Pre-class

The Full Mock Interview -- Two Sentences in One

The dress rehearsal: the whole interview, in order, with nobody stopping the clock. Long answers get shorter when two sentences become one. Key words: a dry run, to pace yourself, concise, to elaborate on something, a curveball, to think on your feet, to get sidetracked, delivery, to recap, a filler word, to come across as something, a lasting impression. Structure: participle clauses -- having + past participle, -ing clauses, past participle clauses, and not + participle, always with the same subject.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A dry run -- a complete practice of an event before the real one
"Today is a dry run: the full interview, with nobody stopping the clock."
To pace yourself -- to use your energy and time carefully, so you still have some at the end
"The interview is forty minutes long, so pace yourself in the first answers."
Concise -- short and clear, with no unnecessary words
"A concise answer gives the panel more time to ask about what matters."
To elaborate on something -- to give more details about something you have already said
"Could you elaborate on the hospital project? It sounds interesting."
A curveball -- a question or a problem that you did not expect
"Near the end she threw me a curveball: what would you do if we said no?"
To think on your feet -- to think and answer quickly, with no time to prepare
"Running a construction site teaches you to think on your feet."
To get sidetracked -- to start talking about something different from your main point, without planning to
"I got sidetracked by the story about my uncle and never answered the question."
Delivery -- the way you speak: your speed, your voice, your pauses
"Your content is strong; now we work on your delivery."
To recap -- to repeat the main points of something in a short way
"Let me recap: three projects, two partners, one decision."
A filler word -- a sound or word like um, uh or like, that you say while you think
"A short pause sounds more confident than a filler word."
To come across as something -- to seem to other people to be a certain kind of person
"I want to come across as calm, not rehearsed."
A lasting impression -- the feeling or memory that people keep about you after they meet you
"Your last answer is often the one that leaves a lasting impression."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In a full mock interview, the problem is rarely what you say. It is how long it takes you to say it. Around minute twenty, most candidates start to use two sentences where one would do: "I ran the company for twelve years. Because of that, I know where it is weak." Senior speakers compress that into one: Having run the company for twelve years, I know where it is weak.

The structure has no subject at the start. Having + past participle shows that something happened first: Having lost a bid in 2019, I learned to price risk. A verb with -ing shows the same time or the reason: Working with my uncle, I learned to disagree. A past participle alone has a passive meaning: Trained as an engineer, I think in numbers. And not goes first: Not having a business degree, I learned finance on the job.

There is one trap. The next word after the comma must be the person who did the first action. "Having finished the bid, the client called me" says that the client finished the bid. Keep the subject the same, and a long answer becomes concise without losing anything.

1. According to the text, when do most candidates start to use two sentences where one would do?
A In the first answer, when they are still nervous about the panel.
B Around minute twenty.
C Only when they answer a curveball.
2. What does "Having + past participle" show?
A That two actions happen at exactly the same moment, with two different subjects.
B That the sentence has a passive meaning.
C That one action happened first.
3. Why is "Having finished the bid, the client called me" a problem?
A Because it says the client finished the bid.
B Because the verb finish cannot be used after having in English.
C Because it is too long.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Participle Clauses

GRAMMAR

Drop the subject and the conjunction, keep the participle, and keep the same subject after the comma.

ToolMeaningExample
Having + past participlethis happened firstHaving run the company, I know its weak points.
Verb + -ingsame time, or reasonWorking with my uncle, I learned to disagree.
Past participlepassive meaningTrained as an engineer, I think in numbers.
Negativenot goes before the participleNot having a business degree, I learned on the job.
Question formthe panel may use it tooHaving run a company, what do you expect to learn?

Never: Having finished the bid, the client called me · Having ran the company · Having not a degree · Working with my uncle, the company grew

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

Minute twenty-five of a mock interview. A curveball, a concise answer, and a recap.

?"Let me recap: I would apply again, delegate more, and bring my uncle into the plan before I start."
?"You did not get sidetracked there. Can you recap your answer in one sentence?"
?"We are halfway through, so let me throw you a curveball. What would you do if we said no?"
?"Good, that was concise. Could you elaborate on the stronger plan?"
?"Yes. Working with my head of planning this year, I would hand over more of the daily decisions."
?"Having thought about it a lot, I would apply again next year, with a stronger plan for the company."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The interviewer throws you a curveball you did not prepare for. You start with:
A "Um, well, you know, basically, I think that, um, it depends on a lot of things."
B "I did not prepare that one."
C "That is a good question. Let me think about it for a second."
You want one concise sentence about your engineering background. You say:
A "Training as an engineer, the numbers are how my company thinks."
B "Trained as an engineer, I think in numbers."
C "Having trained as an engineer, so I think in numbers."
Your answer about the hospital bid took four minutes. At the end, you say:
A "Let me recap: one bid, two partners, one decision."
B "And that is more or less everything, I think, about that project and the people in it."
C "Sorry, that was long."
Which sentence keeps the same subject after the comma?
A "Having finished the bid, the client called me."
B "Having finished the bid, the company was very happy with the result."
C "Having finished the bid, I called the client."

Reflection
