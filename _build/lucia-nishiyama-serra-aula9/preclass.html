Lesson 09 -- Pre-class

Reported Speech for High-Stakes Situations -- Quoting Leaders and Decisions

Carrying a leadership decision to your team without distorting it. Key words: to brief, to reiterate, consensus, alignment, takeaway, to push back, minutes, to convey. Structure: reported speech -- say and tell, the one-step-back shift of the verb, and reported questions with statement word order and if or whether.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To brief -- to give someone the key information they need, quickly and clearly
"I briefed the board on the incident before the news reached them."
To reiterate -- to say something again so that it is completely clear
"The director reiterated that safety comes before speed."
Consensus -- general agreement among a group of people
"We reached consensus after two hours of debate."
Alignment -- a state in which people agree on the same goal and direction
"The meeting ended in full alignment on the roadmap."
Takeaway -- the main point that people remember and act on after a meeting
"The key takeaway was that the launch would be delayed."
To push back -- to disagree with or resist a request, politely but firmly
"I pushed back on the deadline and explained why."
Minutes -- the written record of what was said and decided in a meeting
"The minutes said that the budget had been approved."
To convey -- to communicate an idea or a feeling clearly to someone
"I had to convey the risk without alarming the team."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

When Lucia leaves a leadership meeting, six teams are waiting to hear what happened. In the room, the head of engineering said, "The release is delayed by a quarter." Outside the room, Lucia reports it: "He told me that the release was delayed by a quarter." The meeting is over, so the verb moves one step back in time: is becomes was, will becomes would, and tested becomes had tested. Two verbs carry the report. Tell always needs a person after it -- "he told me" -- while say never does: "he said that". Questions change shape too. In the room, Legal asked, "Did you test the model?" Reported, it becomes a statement, with no inversion and no did: "They asked whether we had tested the model." When Lucia briefs her team, she reiterates what was decided, names who said it, and ends with one takeaway. That precision is why the minutes match her version, and why the organization stays in alignment.

1. Why does the text say "he told me" and not "he said me"?
A "Tell" takes a person after it; "say" does not.
B "Said me" is correct, but it sounds informal.
C "Tell" is used only for bad news.
2. In the room the engineer said "The release is delayed." Why does Lucia report "was delayed"?
A Because the release is no longer delayed.
B Because the meeting is over, so the verb moves one step back in time.
C Because "is delayed" cannot be used with a person.
3. What happens to a question when it is reported?
A It keeps the question word order and the auxiliary "did".
B It takes statement word order, with no inversion and no "did".
C It must always start with "what".
