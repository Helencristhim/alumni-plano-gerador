Lesson 11 -- Pre-class

Having People Over -- Inviting, Receiving, Offering

This time you are the host: the invitation, the door and the table. Key words: to have people over, a host, a potluck, to RSVP, a dietary restriction, to make yourself at home, to help yourself, a refill, seconds, to drop by, a housewarming, leftovers. Structure: would you like and shall, for offers and invitations.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To have people over -- to invite people to your home, usually for a meal or a drink
"We are having people over on Saturday — just the neighbors."
A host -- the person who invites guests to their home and takes care of them
"A good host notices an empty glass before anybody asks."
A potluck -- a meal where every guest brings one dish to share
"It is a potluck, so bring the dish you cook best."
To RSVP -- to tell the host if you are coming or not
"Please RSVP by Wednesday, so I know how much food to make."
A dietary restriction -- a food a person cannot or does not eat
"Does anybody have a dietary restriction I should know about?"
To make yourself at home -- to relax in another person’s house as if it were yours
"Come in, give me your coat, and make yourself at home."
To help yourself -- to take the food you want without waiting to be served
"There is more rice on the stove, so please help yourself."
A refill -- a second serving of a drink in the same glass
"Your glass is empty. Can I get you a refill?"
Seconds -- a second plate of food after the first one
"The lasagna was so good that everybody had seconds."
To drop by -- to visit somebody for a short time, without a fixed plan
"Feel free to drop by on Friday and leave the chairs."
A housewarming -- a party soon after you move into a new home
"We never had a housewarming, so this dinner is our housewarming."
Leftovers -- food nobody ate, kept for later
"Please take some leftovers home — there is far too much."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Gisele and Glauber finally have furniture, so they are having people over for a housewarming. On the sidewalk, Gisele asks her neighbor Thomas: "Would you like to come over for dinner on Saturday?" He says, "We'd love to. Would you like me to bring something?" It is not a potluck, so she answers, "No, thanks. Just bring yourselves."

On Saturday the bell rings. "Come in, please. Shall I take your coat? Make yourself at home." Then she offers: "Would you like a glass of wine, or something without alcohol?" Thomas does not eat meat, but that is not a problem: he told her about his dietary restriction when he RSVPed. When the lasagna is ready, Gisele says, "Shall we sit down?"

Later, Thomas wants seconds, and Gisele says, "Please, help yourself." At the end of the night there is far too much food, so she asks, "Would you like me to pack some leftovers for Claire?" She never asks "Do you like some wine?" — because that is a question about taste, not an offer.

1. Why does Gisele say “Would you like a glass of wine?” and not “Do you like wine?”
A Because “Do you like” is only for food, and wine is a drink that needs would.
B Because she is offering something; “Do you like” only asks about taste.
C Because the dinner is formal.
2. “Shall I take your coat?” Who is going to take the coat?
A Gisele, the person who is asking the question.
B Thomas, because he is the guest and shall is for guests.
C Both of them, together.
3. Thomas asks “Would you like me to bring something?” What does he want to know?
A If Gisele likes the things he usually cooks at home.
B If Gisele is going to bring something to his apartment.
C If Gisele wants him to bring something.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Offers and Invitations with Would You Like and Shall

GRAMMAR

Would you like is an offer. Do you like is a question about taste.

UseFormExample
Offer a thingWould you like + noun?Would you like some wine?
InviteWould you like + to + verb?Would you like to come over on Saturday?
Offer to do it yourselfShall I + base verb?Shall I take your coat?
Ask if they want you toWould you like + me + to + verb?Would you like me to bring something?
Suggest togetherShall we + base verb?Shall we sit down?
Accept / refuseYes, please · That would be lovely · No, thanks · I’d love to, but...No, thanks — I’m fine for now.

Never: Do you like some wine? (for an offer) · Would you like that I take your coat · Shall I to open the window · Would you like coming on Saturday
