Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A trade flow -- the goods and money moving between two countries over a period
"The trade flow between Brazil and Asia has grown every year since 2019."
Transaction volume -- how many payments an institution processed in a given period
"Trade flow is the country. Transaction volume is my bank. Do not mix them in a panel."
Basis points -- one hundredth of one per cent, used when small differences cost money
"Twenty basis points on that volume is a real number, not a rounding error."
A cross-border corridor -- a named pair of countries that money regularly moves between
"We will start with two cross-border corridors, not with the whole map."
A baseline -- the number you measure everything else against
"Forty per cent from what? Without a baseline the figure is decoration."
Market penetration -- how much of the possible market already uses the service
"Market penetration is the number that limits your own story, which is why nobody volunteers it."
A benchmark -- somebody else's number, used as the standard you compare yourself to
"Against the regional benchmark we are ahead. Against the global one we are not."
Projected growth -- the increase somebody expects, which has not happened yet
"Projected growth is a forecast. Say which assumption inside it is the fragile one."
GDP per capita -- the size of an economy divided by the number of people in it
"Two countries with the same GDP are not the same market if the GDP per capita is different."
Year on year -- compared with the same period twelve months earlier
"Volume is up eleven per cent year on year, on a corridor that grew nine."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of saying the same event, and choosing between them is not a matter of style. Our team identified the gap in 2022 puts a person first. The gap was identified by our team in 2022 puts the fact first and the person second. Both are correct; they do different jobs. The second one is the passive voice, and its form is simple: the verb be, in whatever tense you need, plus the past participle.

Use it when the fact is what matters and the author does not. The pilot was approved last quarter. Nobody in the room needs the name of the committee. Payments are settled in two days on that corridor. That is how the world is, not something a person did. And forecasts live almost entirely here: trade flow is projected to grow again this year has no author at all, which is honest, because a projection belongs to a model rather than to a person.

Now the part most people get wrong after they discover the structure. The passive is for data and for institutional facts. It is not for ownership. The model was built by me and the numbers are owned by my team is grammatically perfect and it sounds like somebody hiding. Say I built the model and my team owns the numbers. A presentation entirely in the passive sounds like a report nobody wrote, and the moment you need to be accountable, you switch back to the active and stay there.

One mechanical warning. The passive needs the participle, not the past. It is never the gap was identify and never the pilot approved last quarter when what you mean is was approved. And a habit worth building alongside it: a figure said out loud gets a baseline in the same breath. Not volume grew forty per cent, but volume grew forty per cent year on year, from a two-year base, on a corridor that grew nine.

Stage 2: Put the Data Answer in Order

Order

An analyst has just asked about your growth figure. Put your answer in a logical order.

?"Transaction volume is up forty per cent year on year."
?"That is from a two-year base, so the absolute number is still small."
?"The corridors themselves grew about nine per cent, so we did take share."
?"The gap was identified by our team in 2022 and the pilot was approved last quarter."
?"Market penetration is twelve per cent, so there is still room."
?"What benchmark do you use for a market at that stage?"

Quiz

Talk
Hans Rosling — “The best stats you've ever seen” (TED)

Twenty minutes of somebody saying numbers out loud better than anybody else has managed. Watch what he does after each figure: he never leaves one standing on its own, and every single one arrives next to a comparison.

Tip: count the seconds he leaves after his biggest number. That pause is the technique from this lesson, done by a master.

Talk
David McCandless — “The beauty of data visualization” (TED)

Eighteen minutes on the same argument from the other side: a number means nothing until you put it next to another number. His military spending example is the clearest demonstration of a baseline you will ever see.

Tip: after the military spending section, take one figure from your own page and describe it twice out loud — once alone, once against a comparison. Notice how different they sound.
