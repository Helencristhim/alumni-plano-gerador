Chapter 3: The Code

What You Can Leave Out

The grammar of saying less

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"I've never been to Lisbon." "Neither have I."

"She said she'd call, and she did."

"Is the table booked? If not, I'll call them."

"Not that bottle — the one we had in Porto."

Every orange part stands for words that are not there. What is missing in each one — and how does the listener know?

FormWhat it doesExample
so / neither / nor
+ auxiliary + subject		Agreement without repeating the sentence. The auxiliary copies the tense of the original verb; with no auxiliary, use do / does / did. The word order is inverted."I love it." "So do I."
"I haven't." "Neither have I."
the auxiliary aloneVerb-phrase ellipsis: keep the auxiliary, drop everything the listener already has. The same works with to: I'd love to."She said she'd call, and she did."
"Are you coming?" "I'd love to."
think / hope / expect / afraid
+ so / not		A whole clause replaced by so (positive) or not (negative). I think yes is a translation and does not exist; I don't think so is more natural than I think not."Will it rain?" "I hope not."
"I'm afraid so."
if so / if notA whole condition in two words, referring back to the question just asked."Is it booked? If not, I'll call."
one / ones · do soOne and ones replace a countable noun (never an uncountable one). Do so replaces a whole verb phrase, and belongs to the formal register."The one we had in Porto."
"He may resign, but he has not done so."

Every one of these works because the listener already has the missing words. Ellipsis is not laziness: it is the speaker trusting the listener to remember.

Chapter 4: Around the Table

Around the Table

A dinner where complete sentences are against the rules

Dialogue

The Rule for Tonight

J
New rule for tonight: nobody finishes a sentence they don't need to finish. Fluent speakers leave things out, and so should we.
H
Fair enough. Although I suspect the rule exists because I tend to hold the floor.
J
You do. Last time I couldn't get a word in edgewise. More wine?
H
Please. Not the red one, though — the one we had in Porto, if there's any left.
J
There is. Did you watch the debate last night?
H
I did. Did you?
J
I meant to, but didn't. Was it as long-winded as the last one?
H
Longer. If you want the succinct version: everybody agreed on the problem, and nobody on the solution.
Comprehension

About Julia

1. What rule does Julia propose for the evening?
Nobody finishes a sentence they don't need to finish — they leave things out, like fluent speakers.
2. What happened to her at the last dinner?
She couldn't get a word in edgewise, because Henrique held the floor.
3. Did Julia watch the debate?
No. She meant to, but didn't.
4. What does she want to know about it?
Whether it was as long-winded as the last one.
Listening 1

A Quiet Act of Trust

A linguist on what real conversations leave out. Sound first — no text.

1. What does she notice first when real conversations are written down?
How few complete sentences there are.
2. How does a full answer like “Yes, I would like some coffee” sound?
Grammatically perfect, but like somebody filling in a form.
3. What rule does she give her students?
Say only the part that is new — the listener already has the rest.
Listening 2

When to Spell It Out

A doctor who trains young doctors answering back. Sound first — no text.

1. Where does he argue against ellipsis?
At hospital handovers, when one team passes its patients to the next.
2. What goes wrong with an answer like “He did”?
The listener has to fill in the gaps — which patient, which dose — and may fill them in wrongly.
3. What is his rule?
Leave things out at dinner; spell it out when somebody is going to act on what you said.
