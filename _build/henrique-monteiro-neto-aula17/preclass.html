Lesson 17 -- Pre-class

So Do I, and So Should You — What Fluent Speakers Leave Out

A dinner table, a strict rule, and everything a fluent speaker leaves out on purpose. Key expressions: to chime in, to pick up on, a tangent, to cut somebody off, to hold the floor, banter, to trail off, to get a word in edgewise, to steer the conversation, to be on the same wavelength, to go without saying, long-winded, terse, succinct, to spell it out, implicit, to fill in the gaps, a throwaway line. Structure: ellipsis and substitution — so do I, neither have they, I hope so, if not, one and ones, do so.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

To chime in -- to add your remark to a conversation that is already under way
"Feel free to chime in whenever you disagree with me."
To pick up on -- to return to something somebody said and develop it
"Can I pick up on what you said about the hospital?"
A tangent -- a remark that drifts away from the main subject
"Sorry, that was a tangent. Where were we?"
To cut somebody off -- to interrupt somebody before they have finished
"She cut him off halfway through the story."
To hold the floor -- to keep speaking while everybody else waits their turn
"He held the floor for twenty minutes and nobody minded."
Banter -- quick, friendly teasing between people who know each other well
"The banter at that table is half the reason I go."
To trail off -- to let a sentence fade away without finishing it
"She trailed off, and everybody understood the rest."
To get a word in edgewise -- to manage to speak when somebody else is dominating
"With my uncle at the table, you never get a word in edgewise."
To steer the conversation -- to guide a conversation gradually toward a chosen subject
"She steered the conversation away from politics, very gently."
To be on the same wavelength -- to understand each other easily, almost without explaining
"We are on the same wavelength, so half a sentence is enough."
To go without saying -- to be so obvious that it does not need to be said
"It goes without saying that the first round is on me."
Long-winded -- using far more words than the point needs
"His toasts are famously long-winded."
Terse -- so brief that it sounds cold or unfriendly
"Her reply was terse: two words and a full stop."
Succinct -- brief and clear, with nothing wasted
"Give me the succinct version; dessert is coming."
To spell it out -- to explain every step, because the listener has not understood
"Do I really need to spell it out for you?"
Implicit -- understood without being stated directly
"The invitation was implicit, and everybody came."
To fill in the gaps -- to supply the parts that were not said
"A good listener knows how to fill in the gaps."
A throwaway line -- a remark made casually and not meant to be taken seriously
"It was a throwaway line, and it became the story of the night."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Watch two old friends at dinner and count the complete sentences. There are almost none. "Coffee?" "Please." "Have you seen the film?" "I have." "So have I." Nobody spells it out, because nobody needs to: the missing words are already on the table, and each listener fills in the gaps without noticing. People who are on the same wavelength can run a whole evening on half-sentences, and the banter moves faster than any script.

Learners tend to do the opposite. Asked "Would you like to come?", they answer "Yes, I would like to come," which is correct and sounds strangely formal. A fluent speaker says "I'd love to." Asked "Will it rain?", the learner says "I think yes"; the fluent speaker says "I hope not" or "I'm afraid so." Asked which glass is hers, she points: "The one by the window." And "Is the table booked? If not, I'll call."

Leaving words out is not laziness. Every time you repeat what both of you already know, you suggest that your listener might have forgotten it. That is why a long-winded answer can sound cold, and why a succinct one sounds warm — though not a terse one, which cuts the other person short.

There is a limit, of course. Where somebody will act on what you say — a hospital handover, an instruction, a contract — the shared context cannot be assumed. Doctors are trained to say the full sentence, with names and numbers, even when it sounds stiff, and many institutions that promise to keep things brief have never actually done so. At dinner, leave things out. At work, think twice before you do.

1. "Have you seen the film?" "I have." What has been left out?
A "Seen the film" — the listener already has it, so only the auxiliary is said.
B Nothing important; the answer is simply incomplete and slightly rude.
C The word "yes", which a careful speaker should always add.
2. According to the text, how does "Yes, I would like to come" sound?
A Rude, because it is far too short for a polite invitation.
B Correct, but strangely formal.
C Natural, and exactly what a fluent speaker would say at dinner.
3. Why does a long-winded answer sometimes sound cold?
A Because it repeats what both people already know, as if the listener had forgotten.
B Because it takes too long and the food gets cold on the table.
C Because it uses formal vocabulary that friends would never choose.
4. Where does the text say ellipsis is a bad idea?
A Between old friends who know each other well enough to be honest.
B Where somebody will act on what you say, like a hospital handover.
C In any situation where the speaker is a native speaker of English.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Ellipsis and Substitution

GRAMMAR

Fluent English leaves out whatever the listener already has, and replaces the rest with a few small words. These are the five patterns that do most of that work — and three of them are built differently from their Portuguese equivalents.

FormWhat it doesExample
so / neither / nor
+ auxiliary + subject		Agreement without repeating the sentence. The auxiliary copies the tense of the original verb; with no auxiliary, use do / does / did. The word order is inverted."I love it." "So do I."
"I haven't." "Neither have I."
the auxiliary aloneVerb-phrase ellipsis: keep the auxiliary, drop everything the listener already has. The same works with to: I'd love to."She said she'd call, and she did."
"Are you coming?" "I'd love to."
think / hope / expect / afraid
+ so / not		A whole clause replaced by so (positive) or not (negative). I think yes is a translation and does not exist; I don't think so is more natural than I think not."Will it rain?" "I hope not."
"I'm afraid so."
if so / if notA whole condition in two words, referring back to the question just asked."Is it booked? If not, I'll call."
one / ones · do soOne and ones replace a countable noun (never an uncountable one). Do so replaces a whole verb phrase, and belongs to the formal register."The one we had in Porto."
"He may resign, but he has not done so."

The habit worth building: before you answer, ask yourself which part of your sentence is NEW. Say that part, plus the auxiliary that carries the tense, and let the listener supply the rest. The exception is any situation in which somebody will act on what you say — there, spell it out.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at dinner with friends, and later writing an email. Choose the version a fluent speaker would use.

Your friend says "I've never been to Lisbon." You haven't either:
A "Neither I have, and I would like to go there one day."
B "Neither have I."
C "So haven't I, but my brother has been there twice."
Somebody asks "Do you think the restaurant is still open?" You believe it is:
A "I think yes, because they normally close very late."
B "I think so."
C "I think it, although I am not completely sure."
You are offered a lift home and you want to accept warmly:
A "I'd love to."
B "Yes, I would like to accept the lift home that you are offering."
C "I'd love."
There are two bottles. You want the one from Porto:
A "The Porto, the bottle that we had in Porto last year."
B "The one we had in Porto."
C "The one wine we had in Porto."
In a formal email, you want to avoid repeating "extend the deadline":
A "The committee may extend the deadline, but it has not done so."
B "The committee may extend the deadline, but it has not done it so far this year."
C "The committee may extend the deadline, but it has not so."
