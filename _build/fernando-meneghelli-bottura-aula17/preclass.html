Lesson 17 -- Pre-class

The Culture Question -- Fit, Values and How You Lead

Answering the people questions with the same precision as the numbers: a real moment instead of an adjective, what you would rather do, and a condition that does not sound rigid. Key words: cultural fit, core values, to micromanage, collaborative, to lead by example, an open-door policy, a sounding board, approachable, to be on top of something, down-to-earth, a team player, to walk the talk. Structure: would rather, had better and may as well.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These twelve are for the interview that has no numbers in it: how you lead, what you value, and what your team would say.

Cultural fit -- how well your way of working matches the company's
“The numbers were checked in round one; round two was about cultural fit.”
Core values -- the few principles a company says it will not compromise
“Their core values were on the wall; I asked for an example of one in practice.”
To micromanage -- to control every small detail of other people's work
“In my first years I micromanaged the close, and the team stopped thinking.”
Collaborative -- working with others to decide, rather than alone
“Everybody says they are collaborative; give one example where you changed your mind.”
To lead by example -- to do yourself what you expect from your team
“If I want the close on day five, I lead by example and send my part on day four.”
An open-door policy -- anybody can come and talk to you without an appointment
“An open-door policy only works if people actually walk in.”
A sounding board -- a person you test your ideas on before you decide
“My controller was my sounding board for every big decision.”
Approachable -- easy to talk to, even when you are senior
“My last team would say I was approachable, and a little demanding.”
To be on top of something -- to know the detail of something well, without doing all of it
“I am on top of the cash position every Monday, and the team runs it.”
Down-to-earth -- practical and simple in manner, without pretensions
“The chief executive was down-to-earth, and the interview felt like a conversation.”
A team player -- somebody who works well with others and puts the group first
“Do not say you are a team player; tell them what your team did without you.”
To walk the talk -- to act on what you say you believe
“Values are easy to say; the question is whether the CFO walks the talk.”

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The chief people officer already had my numbers, so she asked about me. How would my last team describe me? I said approachable and on top of the detail, and then I stopped: I would rather she heard it from them, so I offered two names.

She asked whether finance leaders micromanage. Ten years ago my team would have said yes. I checked every line of the close myself. These days I would rather give them the problem than the answer, and my controller was my sounding board for every big call.

When she said decisions take time in a collaborative culture, I did not argue. I said we had better agree early on which decisions are mine. And at the end, since we were on culture, I may as well ask it, I said: why is the role open?

1. What does he offer after describing himself in two words?
A A longer list of adjectives, with an example for each one of them.
B Two names from his last team.
C His performance reviews.
2. What did he use to do ten years ago?
A Check every line of the close himself.
B Give his team the problem, and let them find the answer by themselves.
C Ask his controller to decide.
3. How does he react when she says decisions take time?
A He says that slow decisions are a problem for him and for any finance leader.
B He suggests agreeing early on which decisions are his.
C He says nothing.
4. What does he ask at the end?
A What the salary range is.
B How many people are on the finance team and who they report to.
C Why the role is open.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Prefer, Warn, Move On

GRAMMAR

Three short structures. All of them take the base verb, with no to. Would rather is a preference, had better is a polite warning about now or the near future, and may as well means there is no good reason not to.

FormMeaningExample
would rather + base verb (+ than)Your preferenceI would rather give my team the problem than the answer.
would rather + person + past simpleWhat you prefer somebody else to doI would rather you heard it from my last team.
had better + base verbA polite warning, about nowWe had better agree early on which decisions are mine.
may as well + base verbNo good reason not toI may as well ask it now.
Negativenot after rather and betterI would rather not guess. We had better not wait until the board meets.
The trapHad better looks like the past, but it is about now: we had better agree today. And none of the three takes to: never would rather to or had better to.

In a culture interview, would rather shows a preference without saying the other way is wrong. That is exactly what an interviewer wants to hear from a senior leader.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen, then record yourself. In natural speech would rather becomes I'd rather and had better becomes we'd better: listen for the short form, and keep the stress on rather and better.

These days I would rather give my team the problem than the answer.
I would rather you heard it from my last team, so I am happy to give you two names.
I would rather be corrected in March than surprised in December.
We had better agree early on which decisions are mine and which are shared.
My controller was my sounding board for every big decision.
Since we are on culture, I may as well ask it now: why is the role open?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

The interviewer has your numbers already. Choose the answer that works in a culture interview.

"How would you describe your leadership style?" You start with:
A Three adjectives: collaborative, hands-on and strategic, and a short definition of each.
B One real moment with a name and a date.
C Your results from last year.
"Some finance leaders micromanage. Would your team say that about you?" You say:
A What you used to do, and what you would rather do now.
B "Never. I have always trusted my people completely, from my very first day as a manager."
C "Only when it is necessary."
The interviewer says decisions in the company are slow. The best response is:
A "That will be difficult for me."
B "That is fine, I can wait for any decision as long as it takes, I am very patient."
C "We had better agree early on which decisions are mine."
You want the interviewer to trust what you say about your team. You:
A Offer two names she can call.
B Repeat that your team always liked working with you and would say so to anyone.
C Talk about "my people" in general.
The interview is ending and you have a hard question about the role. You say:
A Nothing; it is better to ask the recruiter later by email, when the process is over.
B "I may as well ask it now: why is the role open?"
C "Why did the last CFO fail?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is correcting this one, and there is no score.

Record a two-minute answer to "How would you describe your leadership style?" Do not start with an adjective. Start with one real moment: a person on your team, a date, and what you did the next morning. Then say how you have changed, using "I would rather", and finish with one condition using "we had better". Listen once and count the adjectives you used.
