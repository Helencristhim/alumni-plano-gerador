Chapter 3: The Code

Prefer, Warn, Move On

would rather · had better · may as well

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

“These days I would rather give my team the problem than the answer.”

“I would rather you heard it from my last team than from me.”

“We had better agree early on which decisions are mine.”

“Since we are on culture, I may as well ask it now: why is the role open?”

What comes after each expression in color? Which one is a preference, which is a warning?

FormWhat it doesExample
would rather + base verb (+ than...)Your preferenceI would rather give my team the problem than the answer.
would rather + person + past simpleWhat you prefer another person to doI would rather you heard it from my last team.
had better + base verbA polite warning about now or the near futureWe had better agree early on which decisions are mine.
may as well + base verbThere is no good reason not to do itI may as well ask it now.
In an interviewWould rather shows a preference without saying the other way is wrong. Had better sets a condition without sounding like a demand. Neither takes to after it.

The Move

The Moment, Not the Adjective

1. One real moment

“In my second year, my controller told me the forecast was too optimistic.”

2. What you did next

“The next morning I asked her to present her version to the board herself.”

3. What it says about you

“I would rather be corrected in March than surprised in December.”

The adjective comes last, if it comes at all. The moment already said it.

Chapter 4: The People Interview

No Numbers on the Table

A conversation with the chief people officer, and two voices on what they listen for

Dialogue

How Would Your Team Describe You?

L
We have seen your numbers. Now I would like to hear about you. How would your last team describe you?
F
Approachable, I think, and on top of the detail. But I would rather you heard it from them, so I am happy to give you two names.
L
Some finance leaders micromanage. Would they say that about you?
F
They would have, ten years ago. I checked every line of the close myself. These days I would rather give my team the problem than the answer.
L
And when somebody on your team disagrees with you?
F
I want to hear it before the decision, not after. My controller was my sounding board for every big call, and she disagreed with me often.
L
Our culture is very collaborative. Decisions here can take time.
F
Then we had better agree early on which decisions are mine and which are shared. That saves time for everybody.
L
Fair enough. Is there anything you would like to ask me?
F
Since we are on culture, I may as well ask it now: why is the role open?
Comprehension

About the Conversation

1. What does he offer Laura instead of describing himself at length?
Two names from his last team, so she can hear it from them.
2. How has his way of leading changed in ten years?
He used to check every line of the close himself; now he gives his team the problem, not the answer.
3. What does he suggest when Laura says decisions take time?
That they agree early on which decisions are his and which are shared.
Listening

One Real Moment

A chief people officer on the culture question. Sound first — no text.

1. What is she not looking for?
The right adjectives, because everybody says they are collaborative.
2. What does she listen for instead?
One real moment: somebody on the team disagreed, and what he did the next morning.
3. Which candidates does she remember?
The ones who say what they would rather do, and why, without pretending there is only one way to lead.
Listening 2

On a Bad Tuesday

A former finance director, now a coach. Sound first — no text.

1. What mistake did he make for fourteen years?
He prepared least for the culture question.
2. What is left to decide in a final round?
Whether people want to work with you on a bad Tuesday.
3. What should you tell, and how?
One habit that shows how you lead, with a name and a date, and say so if you have changed.
