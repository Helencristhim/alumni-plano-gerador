Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

Turning Passive Watching Into Active Language -- The Weight Goes First

Not once in eight episodes does the show explain what the case is about, and not once does it matter. Key words: a premise, to hinge on, compelling, contrived, to foreshadow, an arc, understated, gratuitous, to resonate, a portrayal, to hold up, by any measure. Structure: inversion for emphasis.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A premise -- the idea a story or an argument starts from, before anything else is built on it
"The premise is that a junior lawyer finds a file nobody was supposed to see."
To hinge on -- to depend completely on one single thing, so that everything falls if it fails
"The whole season hinges on a document that turns up in the fourth episode."
Compelling -- so convincing, or so interesting, that you cannot look away from it
"The case is thin and the performance is compelling, and only one of those two things matters here."
Contrived -- arranged so obviously by the writer that it stops being believable
"The reconciliation in the last ten minutes is contrived, and everybody watching knows it."
To foreshadow -- to give an early sign of something that only happens much later
"The line about the missing signature foreshadows the whole ending, and nobody notices it twice."
An arc -- the shape of a change across a whole story, from where somebody starts to where they end
"Her arc is the only honest thing in it: she begins certain and ends useful."
Understated -- deliberately quiet, and stronger precisely because it does not raise its voice
"The closing speech is understated, which is why it lands."
Gratuitous -- put in without any reason, especially something shocking that the story did not need
"The violence in the third episode is gratuitous, and it costs the show the argument it was making."
To resonate -- to stay with somebody, because it matches something they already know or have lived
"The scene resonates with anybody who has ever been the youngest person in a meeting."
A portrayal -- the way a person, a job or a group is shown in a book, a film or a series
"It is the most accurate portrayal of a mediation I have seen, and it lasts four minutes."
To hold up -- to still work when you look at it again years later, with everything you have learned since
"Almost none of them hold up. That one does, and it is thirty years old."
By any measure -- however you judge it, whichever standard you choose to use
"By any measure it is the best thing she has written, and it is also the least watched."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Not once in eight episodes does the show explain what the case is actually about, and not once does it matter. The premise is thin: a trainee finds a file nobody meant her to see, and the whole season hinges on one line in the fourth episode. What holds it together is her arc — she begins certain and ends useful — and a closing speech so understated that half the audience misses it the first time.

Look at how that first sentence is built. A negative expression, not once, has been moved to the front, and the moment it goes there the subject and the auxiliary swap places, exactly as they would in a question: does the show explain. Where there is no auxiliary in the plain version, English supplies one: Rarely does a courtroom scene hold up thirty years later. Only later did I realise what the fourth episode was doing. Not only is the portrayal wrong, it is wrong in a useful way.

This is not decoration. It is what a written argument does instead of raising its voice, and it belongs in a closing speech as much as in a review. Use it once in a paragraph; twice is shouting. Two warnings for a Portuguese speaker. The first: do not move the expression to the front and then forget to swap — not Never I have seen, but Never have I seen. The second: no sooner takes than, never when. And one more thing worth saying about this show: the violence in the third episode is gratuitous, the ending is contrived, and by any measure it still resonates more than anything else on that channel.

1. "Not once does the show explain what the case is about." Why is it "does the show" and not "the show does"?
A Because the sentence is a question.
B Because a negative expression was moved to the front, and that forces the subject and the auxiliary to swap.
C Because "explain" cannot follow a subject directly.
2. Where does the "did" in "Only later did I realise" come from?
A English supplies it, exactly as it would in a question, because the plain sentence has no auxiliary.
B It is a spelling variant of "realised".
C It is optional and can be left out in writing.
3. According to the text, when should you use this structure?
A As often as possible, because it sounds educated.
B Only in casual conversation, never in writing.
C In written argument and in a closing speech, once in a paragraph. Twice is shouting.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Inversion for Emphasis

GRAMMAR

Put the negative first, then build the rest as if you were asking a question.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to say, with emphasis, that the show never explains the case. You say:
A "Not once the show explains what the case is about."
B "Not once does the show explain what the case is about."
C "Not once is the show explaining what the case is about."
You want to say you understood something only afterwards. You say:
A "Only later did I realise what the fourth episode was doing."
B "Only later I realised what the fourth episode was doing."
C "Only later have I realise what the fourth episode was doing."
You are writing a closing speech and want to add weight without raising your voice. You write:
A "At no point they explained the delay to my client."
B "At no point did they explain the delay to my client."
C "At no point was they explaining the delay to my client."
You want to say that two things are wrong, and that the second is worse. You say:
A "Not only the ending is contrived, also the violence is gratuitous."
B "Not only is the ending contrived, the violence in the third episode is gratuitous."
C "Not only that the ending is contrived, but the violence is gratuitous."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Review something you have actually watched, in two minutes. Say what the premise is, what the whole thing hinges on, what is understated and what is contrived, whether it holds up, and one thing about it that resonates with you and would not with somebody else. Put the emphasis at the front at least three times. If a sentence comes out wrong, finish it anyway.
