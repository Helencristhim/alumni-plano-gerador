Vanity Fair -- "Lawyer Fact Checks Court Scenes, from 'Better Call Saul' to 'The Good Wife'"

A practising lawyer watching famous courtroom scenes and saying, calmly and specifically, what could never happen and why it works anyway. It is exactly the argument of today's reading, delivered by somebody with the standing to make it.

Tip: every time he says a scene is impossible, pause and turn his point into one sentence with the emphasis at the front. You should end up with five, and at least three should use a different trigger.

Podcast
Pop Culture Happy Hour -- "'Better Call Saul' ends with redemption, your honor"

Four critics arguing, quickly and with real disagreement, about whether a legal drama earned its ending. This is what today's vocabulary sounds like when native speakers use it at speed: premise, arc, contrived, understated, holds up. Transcript on the page.

Tip: listen once for the argument, not the words. Then read the transcript and mark every sentence where a critic gives a reason instead of a verdict. Those are the sentences worth copying.

TED -- Andrew Stanton, "The clues to a great story"

The writer of several Pixar films on what makes an audience lean in, and on the single promise a story makes in its first minute. Useful far beyond film: the same rules decide whether a room stays with an opening statement.

Tip: watch it once, then apply one of his rules to the case you argued in class. Say out loud what promise your opening statement makes, and whether you keep it.
