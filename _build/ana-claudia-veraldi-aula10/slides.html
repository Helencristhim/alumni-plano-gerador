Chapter 1: First Words

You Spent Eleven Years Doing the Same Thing

Last time you found out that nobody actually says going to, and that the first two minutes of a call go over everybody’s head. You listened to a man giving an update at full speed and you caught the headline.

What did you do at exactly a quarter past seven, every weekday, for eleven years?

Portuguese does this with the imperfect and nobody has to think about it. English built two separate structures for the same job, and put a rule between them that almost nobody teaches you.

The Real Question

What Do You Not Miss?

Everybody asks what you miss about the city. It is the easy question, and the answer is usually a restaurant.

The harder one: what did you do every single day for years that you have not thought about once since you left?

She Left Oslo Four Years Ago

I
Ana, people keep asking me what I miss about Oslo, and honestly I have to invent something. What I really remember is what I used to dread.
A
Which was?
I
Sunday evening. Every Sunday I would start checking the calendar at about six and the whole evening was ruined. Did you have that?
A
For eleven years. And I never noticed, because I was a creature of habit and it had become second nature.
I
That is the part nobody warns you about. You fall into a routine and then it disappears. There used to be a bakery under my flat and I could not tell you the man’s name.
A
I was a regular in the same place for nine years and I never asked either. I took it completely for granted.
I
And now? Do you ever look back on it and want any of it?
A
No. I missed out on eleven years of evenings in that car. It took me two hours every night to wind down from a journey I do not remember making.
Comprehension

About Ingrid

1. What does Ingrid say happens when people ask her what she misses about Oslo?
She has to invent something. What she actually remembers is not what she misses but what she used to dread.
2. What was Ingrid’s Sunday evening like, and when did it start?
Every Sunday she would start checking the calendar at about six, and the whole evening was ruined from then on.
3. What example does Ingrid give of something disappearing inside a routine?
There used to be a bakery under her flat and she could not tell you the man’s name, after years of going there.
Listening

The 07:14 Every Single Day

A Londoner on eleven years of the same train. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What exactly was his routine, and how long did it last?
He got the seven fourteen train — not the twelve, not the twenty — and stood at the same point on the platform where the doors opened. Every morning, for eleven years.
2. Which parts of those eleven years can he remember, and why those?
The first week, because he was watching everything, and the last day, because he knew it was the last. Everything in between has gone.
3. How does his wife describe what happened, and what does he say about it?
She says he did not waste those years, he just was not there for them — and he thinks that is the more accurate way of putting it.
Chapter 4: The Code

Two Forms, One Rule Between Them

used to · would — and the one place would cannot go

Grammar Discovery

Three Are Habits. One Is Not.

"I used to leave the flat at ten past seven, every single morning."

"Every Monday I would sit in the same traffic, listening to the same radio."

"There used to be a bakery on the corner. It closed years ago."

"I did not use to notice the noise at all. I notice it now."

Three of these four describe something repeated. One describes a situation that was simply true. Find it — and then try to say that one with would. It will not go.

FormWhat it doesExample
used to + verbA habit or a state that was true then and is not now.I used to live in Sao Paulo.
would + verbA repeated action, inside a memory or a story.Every Monday I would take the seven fourteen.
STATES take used to onlybe, have, know, like, live: would is impossible here.There used to be a bakery. (never: there would be)
negativedid not use to — the d moves to did.I did not use to notice it.
questiondid you use to...?Did you use to walk to work?
one finished eventA single event takes the past simple. Never used to.I moved in March. (never: I used to move)
not the same as be used tobe / get used to + -ing is a different structure entirely.I am used to the quiet now — that is next week's lesson.

In one line: used to opens the door; would keeps the memory going once you are inside.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I use to live in Sao Paulo."
"I used to live in Sao Paulo."
"I did not used to notice the noise."
"I did not use to notice the noise."
"There would be a bakery on the corner."
"There used to be a bakery on the corner."
"I used to move to the interior in March."
"I moved to the interior in March."

The first two are the same d, in the wrong place: it belongs to used in the affirmative and to did in the negative, never to both. The third breaks the state rule — be never takes would. The fourth is the commonest of all: one finished event is past simple, no matter how important it was.

"I ___did not use to notice the noise at all." (not / notice — watch where the d goes)

Listening 2

Breaking It Was Harder Than Living It

A Norwegian on the first six months after leaving. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What was tiring about the first months, and what was not?
Not the boxes and not the paperwork. It was that every small thing had to be decided again: where to buy bread, what time to leave, which way to walk.
2. What did he think at about week three, and was he right?
That he had made a terrible mistake. He had not — he had broken about two hundred habits at once.
3. What is his one-sentence explanation, and what happened after six months?
A habit is expensive to break and free to keep. After about six months the new life became invisible too, which he says is either wonderful or terrifying depending on the day.

The Old Weekday

07:10leave the flat, same lift, same doorman, no conversation
07:40same avenue, same radio station, no memory of any of it

Sunday 18:00open the calendar. Evening over.
1. Say the 05:50 line as a full sentence, twice: once with used to and once with would.
"I used to snooze the same alarm twice every morning." / "Every morning I would snooze it twice." Both work, because snoozing is a repeated action.
2. Now say the 08:40 line with would, and then say what was really wrong with that.
"Every morning I would buy a coffee from the same man." That is fine. But "I would never ask his name" is odd — a negative habit is much more natural with used to: "I never used to ask his name."
3. The last line says the evening was over. Say it with used to, and explain why would would be strange.
"Sunday evenings used to be ruined by six o’clock." Would is impossible with be: it is a state, not an action, and states only take used to.

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I use used to for habits and for states that are over.
I use would only for repeated actions, never with be, have or know.
I say did not use to, without the d, in the negative.
I use the past simple for one finished event, however important it was.
I know the words: a creature of habit, second nature, on autopilot, back then, to dread.
