If I Have Time This Weekend -- Plans With Conditions Attached

The Saturday that depends on the sky and the delivery, and the first conditional that carries it. Key words: to put something off, a downpour, to bother, weather permitting, to squeeze something in, to run errands, provided that, to call something off, daunting, a window, to hold off, manageable. Structure: first conditional for real future possibilities.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To put something off -- to move something to a later date, usually because you do not want to do it
"I have put the shutters off for four weekends in a row."
A downpour -- a sudden fall of very heavy rain that does not last long
"There was a downpour at eleven and the whole plan collapsed."
To bother -- to make the effort to do something — most often used in the negative
"If it is raining at eight I will not even bother getting the ladder out."
Weather permitting -- only if the weather allows it — said at the end of a plan
"We are painting the veranda on Sunday, weather permitting."
To squeeze something in -- to find time for something in a schedule that is already full
"If the delivery comes early I might squeeze the market in as well."
To run errands -- to do the small necessary jobs outside the house: post, bank, shop
"Out here, running errands means one trip or none."
Provided that -- only if this one condition is true — more explicit than plain if
"I will finish the kitchen this weekend, provided that the tiles arrive."
To call something off -- to cancel something that had already been arranged
"If the forecast is that bad on Friday I will call the whole thing off."
Daunting -- making you slightly afraid to start, because it is large or difficult
"A whole wall of sanding is daunting until you divide it into three days."
A window -- a short period of time in which something is possible
"There is a dry window on Saturday morning and nothing after that."
To hold off -- to not start yet — used about rain, and about a decision
"If the rain holds off until noon I can get the first coat on."
Manageable -- small enough or simple enough that you can actually deal with it
"Two hours of sanding is manageable. Eight hours is a fantasy."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ana has put the veranda off for four weekends in a row, and this time she has a plan with the condition written next to it. If the rain holds off until noon, she will get the first coat on. If it does not, she will not even bother getting the ladder out, because wet wood takes nothing.

The tiles are supposed to arrive between eight and twelve. As long as they arrive by ten, she can finish the kitchen floor the same day. Provided that the delivery is done, she will squeeze the market in as well, because out here running errands means one trip or none. She will not go at all unless the morning is clear.

The forecast says rain from one o’clock, and the forecast is rarely wrong about that. So when the rain starts, everything outdoors stops, and the shutters finally happen: a daunting job indoors that has waited since April. At six, whatever the weather, the dogs go out. That is the only line on the whole list with no condition attached at all.

1. "If the rain holds off until noon, she will get the first coat on." Why holds and not will hold?
A Because the verb after if stays in the present, however future the meaning is.
B Because holds off is an expression that has no future form.
C Because the sentence is about a habit rather than a plan.
2. "She will not go at all unless the morning is clear." What does unless mean here?
A Even if the morning is clear.
B If the morning is not clear.
C Until the morning is clear.
3. Why "when the rain starts" and not "if the rain starts"?
A Because when is more polite than if in a written plan.
B Because the forecast makes the rain certain; only the exact time is open.
C Because if cannot be used with the weather.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The First Conditional

GRAMMAR

One sentence, two halves. Only one of them is allowed to talk about the future.

FormWhat it doesExample
if + present, will + verbA real possibility in the futureIf it rains, I will work inside.
never will after ifThe condition stays in the presentnever: if it will rain
unlessMeans if not; only one negativeI will not start unless it is dry.
as long as / provided thatA more explicit, slightly formal conditionProvided that the tiles arrive...
other resultsmight, could, can, or an imperativeIf it rains, I might just read.
if vs whenif may not happen; when certainly willWhen the rain stops...
present perfect conditionFor after this is finishedIf I have finished by four...

Never: If it will rain · unless it does not rain · if it rains I would work inside · when it rains on Saturday (when the rain is not certain). The first puts the future in the wrong half, the second doubles a negative that unless already contains, the third belongs to an imaginary Saturday, and the fourth promises weather nobody promised.

Stage 2: Put the Conversation in Order

Order

Dave wants to borrow the ladder and Ana has a Saturday with conditions. Put the exchange in a logical order.

?"So are you doing the veranda on Saturday or not? You have put it off three times now."
?"If the rain holds off until noon, I will get the first coat on."
?"And if it does not?"
?"Then I will not even bother getting the ladder out. Wet wood takes nothing."
?"Fair enough. I never call anything off. I just start and see what happens."
?"That works until you are halfway up a ladder in a downpour."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

A neighbour asks whether you are painting on Saturday. You answer:
A "If it will be dry, I will paint the veranda."
B "If it is dry, I will paint the veranda."
C "If it is dry, I would paint the veranda."
You want to say you will only start when the wood is completely dry. You say:
A "I will not start unless the wood is dry."
B "I will not start unless the wood is not dry."
C "I will not start if the wood will be dry."
The forecast says the rain will certainly stop at four. You say:
A "If the rain stops at four, I will go out."
B "When the rain stops at four, I will go out."
C "Unless the rain stops at four, I will go out."
You want to make the market trip depend on one explicit condition. The most natural version is:
A "I will go to the market, provided that the delivery is done."
B "I will go to the market, provided that the delivery will be done."
C "I would go to the market, provided that the delivery is done."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Describe the Saturday you have coming up. For every job, say what it depends on and what you will do instead if that condition is not met. Finish with the one thing you will do whatever happens. Use if at least three times, unless at least once, and when at least once for something certain.
