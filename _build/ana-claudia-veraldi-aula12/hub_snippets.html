Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A forecast -- a statement about what the weather is probably going to do
"If the forecast is good on Friday, we put out forty tables."
A downpour -- a short period of very heavy rain
"A downpour started at twenty past seven."
To call off -- to cancel something that was already arranged
"The fair is never called off, whatever the sky does."
To postpone -- to move an event to a later date
"If a friend calls, everything else gets postponed."
A backup plan -- a second plan you keep ready in case the first one fails
"You cannot count on the weather, so you count on the backup plan."
To fall through -- to fail before it happens, after it was already arranged
"If Sunday falls through, I will ask somebody else."
To squeeze in -- to find time for something in a day that is already full
"If I finish early, I will squeeze in the painting."
On short notice -- with very little warning before something happens
"If a friend calls on short notice, everything else waits."
To count on -- to trust that something will happen or that somebody will help
"You cannot count on the weather out here."
To run out of -- to use all of something so that none is left
"If I run out of time, I will postpone it."
Chances are -- it is very likely that something will happen
"Chances are the first entrance will be flooded again."
To be up to you -- to be your decision to make, and nobody can make it for you
"It is up to you. Either way I will be fine with it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Every second Saturday there is a fair in the square, and Ana has a table. On Thursday night she checks the forecast and the whole weekend reorganizes itself around it. If it rains before eight, everybody will move into the old school gym; if the rain starts later, the sellers will cover their tables and carry on. The fair is never called off and it is never postponed, because if they cancel one Saturday, half the visitors will not come back for three months. Her real problem is not the sky, though: it is the varnish. If she does not get two dry days, the chairs will not be ready, and she might take only the small pieces. Her backup plan is Tiago, who always has too much space. Unless something falls through at the last minute, she will be in the square by nine, with coffee. And if she runs out of time on Friday, she will squeeze in the last coat of varnish at six in the morning, in the dark, the way she always does. Chances are it will rain anyway. It is up to her, and she goes every month.

1. Which half of "If it rains before eight, everybody will move into the gym" talks about the future?
A The half that starts with "if".
B The half with "will move".
C Both halves, equally.
2. According to the text, what is Ana’s real problem this month?
A The fair might be called off if the weather is bad.
B The varnish needs two dry days, and she may not get them.
C She has no table, so she has to share with Tiago.
3. Why does the text say the fair is never postponed?
A Because if they cancel one Saturday, half the visitors will not come back for three months.
B Because the gym is always free on Saturday mornings.
C Because it never rains before eight in that town.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The First Conditional

GRAMMAR

Two halves, one sentence -- and only one of them is allowed to carry the future.

FormWhat it meansExample
If + present simple, will + verbA real possibility in the future.If it rains, we will move inside.
The trapwill is banned in the half that starts with if.If it will rain → If it rains
Either orderThe comma appears only when the if half comes first.We will move inside if it rains.
Less certainwill becomes might or may.If the forecast changes, I might stay home.
unlessIt already means if not. Never add a second negative.Unless it rains, we will set up outside.
as long asA condition you insist on.I will go as long as the road is fine.
when vs ifwhen = you are sure it happens. if = you are not.When I get there / If I get there
In one lineThe future lives in one half only, and it is never the half with if.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each real conversation.

A neighbor asks what you will do about Saturday. The sky is not decided yet. You say:
A "If it will rain, I will stay at home."
B "If it rains, I will stay at home."
C "If it rained, I will stay at home."
You want to say that the fair goes ahead in every case except rain. You say:
A "Unless it rains, the fair will go ahead."
B "Unless it does not rain, the fair will go ahead."
C "Unless it will rain, the fair will go ahead."
You are almost sure you will get there, but not completely. You say:
A "When the road is dry, I might get there by nine."
B "If the road is dry, I might get there by nine."
C "If the road will be dry, I get there by nine."
You want to put the result first, with no comma. You say:
A "I will call you tonight, if the forecast will change."
B "I will call you tonight if the forecast changes."
C "I call you tonight if the forecast will change."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

Plan your next Saturday out loud and put a condition on every step. What will you do if the sky is clear? What will you do if it is not? What will you postpone if you run out of time, and what is your backup plan if the whole day falls through? Take your time and use "if", "unless" and "as long as".
