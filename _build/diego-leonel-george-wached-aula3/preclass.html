Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Where Does It Itch? -- Taking the History

When it started, where it started, what makes it worse, what the patient already tried. Key words: onset, a patch, scaly, a trigger, to scratch, swollen, a blister, persistent, to prescribe, a side effect, to examine, to rule out. Structure: the past simple in questions and negatives.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Onset -- the moment when a problem first begins
"The onset was three weeks ago, after a long flight."
A patch -- a small area of skin that looks different from the skin around it
"There is one patch on each elbow and a smaller one on the knee."
Scaly -- covered with small dry pieces of skin that come off
"The patches are scaly and they are silver at the edge."
A trigger -- something that makes an episode of the problem start or get worse
"Stress is her main trigger, and cold weather is the second one."
To scratch -- to rub the skin hard with your nails
"She scratched it in her sleep and it started bleeding."
Swollen -- bigger than normal because of fluid under the skin
"The finger was swollen and she could not take her ring off."
A blister -- a small bubble of fluid under the top layer of skin
"Two small blisters appeared on the palm before the rash spread."
Persistent -- continuing for a long time without stopping
"The itching is persistent: it does not stop, day or night."
To prescribe -- to write an order for a medicine that a patient must take
"I am going to prescribe a stronger cream for two weeks."
A side effect -- an unwanted effect that a medicine also causes
"The most common side effect is burning in the first few days."
To examine -- to look at a patient’s body carefully in order to find out what is wrong
"Let me examine the whole arm, not only the patch you showed me."
To rule out -- to check something in order to show that it is NOT the cause
"I want one blood test, only to rule out an infection."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The doctor never begins with how long have you had it. He begins with a question that asks for a date: when did it start? And patients remember. This one said the onset was six weeks ago, in the week she changed jobs. Two patches appeared on her elbows first, and a third one came later, on the knee.

Then the second question, the one patients never answer without being asked: did anything change in the week before it started? She changed jobs in March and she did not sleep for about a month. Stress, she says, is her trigger. The patches are dry and scaly, and when she scratches them small pieces come off. Last week the skin around one of them was swollen.

And then the question residents forget: did you use anything on it? She used a cream her sister gave her. It did not help and it burned, which may be a side effect or may be the reason it looks worse today. Before the doctor prescribes anything, he wants to examine the whole body surface, and he wants one test, only to rule out an infection.

1. "When did it start?" Why not "When it started?"
A Because start is an irregular verb.
B Because an English past question needs the auxiliary did, and the main verb goes back to its base form.
C Because when is a question word and question words never take -ed.
2. "She did not sleep for about a month." Why not "She did not slept"?
A Because did already carries the past. The past moves once, never twice.
B Because sleep has no past form in English.
C Because a month is a long period and long periods take the base form.
3. "A third one came later." Why is it came and not comed?
A Because it is a question and questions change the verb.
B Because the subject is singular.
C Because come is irregular: it has its own past form, and -ed is never added to it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past Simple in Questions and Negatives

GRAMMAR

One rule to remember: the past sits on the verb in a statement, and on did in a question or a negative -- never on both.

JobFormExample
Statement, regular verbverb + edIt started three weeks ago.
Statement, irregular verbits own past formThe cream made it worse.
Questiondid + subject + base verbWhen did it start?
Yes or no questiondid + subject + base verbDid you use anything on it?
Negativedid not + base verbIt did not help.
Time words that take the pastago, last week, yesterday, in March, when I wasTwo blisters appeared last Friday.
Irregular verbs for this lessonbegin → began · come → came · get → got · give → gave · make → made · take → took · become → became · sleep → sleptShe gave me a cream and it became worse.

Never: when it started? · did you changed anything? · the cream not helped · it becomed worse · did it started last week?
