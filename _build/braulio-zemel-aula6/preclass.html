Lesson 06 -- Pre-class

Reading the World -- The Magazine You Are Not Supposed to Finish

How a serious weekly is built, and how to talk about one article instead of a hundred pages. Key expressions: editorial, a correspondent, a briefing, an assertion, to refute, to scrutinize, an inference, an implication, a subscription, to skim, a caveat, to gloss over. Structure: cleft sentences -- it was X that, what X was, all I want is X.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

Editorial -- the opinion of the publication itself, printed with no name at the bottom
"The editorial took a position I did not expect from them."
A correspondent -- a journalist who lives in one country and reports from there
"Their correspondent in Ankara filed the piece on Sunday night."
A briefing -- a longer piece that gives you the background on one story
"I read the briefing first, because I knew nothing about the country."
An assertion -- a statement made confidently, with no proof attached to it
"That is an assertion, not a finding. Where are the numbers?"
To refute -- to prove that a claim is wrong, using evidence
"They do not just disagree with him. They refute him, line by line."
To scrutinize -- to examine something very closely, looking for what is wrong with it
"You cannot scrutinize every line and still reach the end."
An inference -- a conclusion the reader draws, from what the text does not say directly
"The number is there. The inference is yours to make."
An implication -- what a statement suggests without ever saying it
"Nobody wrote that the minister lied. The implication was loud enough."
A subscription -- paid access to a publication, month after month
"I took out a subscription in March and read four issues."
To skim -- to read fast, taking only the shape of the text
"Skim the whole thing first. Then choose one piece and read it properly."
A caveat -- a warning that quietly limits what has just been said
"The forecast is good, with one caveat, and the caveat is the whole story."
To gloss over -- to pass over something difficult quickly, to avoid dealing with it
"He glossed over the cost in half a line and moved on."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He took out a subscription in March and read four issues. Then the magazines began to arrive faster than he could open them, and by June there was a pile on the table that he walked past every morning. What stopped him was not the English. It was the pile.

Nobody had ever explained the shape of the thing to him. The editorial at the front is the opinion of the publication itself, which is why it carries no name at the bottom. The briefing behind it gives the background on one story and assumes the reader knows nothing. After that come the pages written by a correspondent who lives in the country concerned, and it is there that the reporting actually sits.

The difficulty was never the vocabulary. A sentence makes an assertion in eight words and leaves the implication to the reader. Something inconvenient is glossed over inside a subordinate clause, with a caveat attached so quietly that the retreat is almost invisible. To scrutinize every line is the wrong instinct, because the end never arrives. What works is to skim first for shape, read one piece properly, and draw the inference yourself.

And there is a habit worth stealing from the writing. The editors do not simply disagree with the people they cover. They refute them, which means they take the claim, name it, and put the evidence against it. It is that move that makes the writing sound authoritative, far more than any word in it. All he needed was one good article a week, and permission not to finish the rest.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Cleft Sentences, or Splitting the Sentence in Two

GRAMMAR

A cleft sentence takes a normal sentence and splits it, so that one piece of it stands alone at the front. It is longer on purpose. Length here is emphasis, not complication.

FormWhat it doesExample
It + be + X + that / whoPuts ONE piece of information under a spotlight and pushes the rest into the shade."It was the tone that surprised me."
What + clause + be + XBuilds up to the point and delays it, so the listener leans in before the noun arrives."What surprised me was the tone."
All + clause + be + XSays the same thing and adds and that is all. It makes a request sound modest."All I want is one good article a week."
The reason ... is that ...Announces that an explanation is coming, before giving it. Buys you two seconds."The reason I stopped is that I thought I had to finish it."
The trapsThe link word is that for things and who or that for people, never what. A what sentence has one be in the middle, not two. And it is never dropped.NOT "It was the tone what surprised me."
NOT "What surprised me it was the tone."
NOT "Is the data that tells the story."

One practical note. In a conversation, the what form is the more useful of the two, because it starts before you have decided how the sentence ends. What surprised me was... buys you a second and a half of thinking time, and it sounds deliberate rather than hesitant.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are at the reading group and afterwards at dinner, and everything is in English. Choose the best thing to say.

Somebody asks what you thought of the article. You want to point at one thing and only one:
A "It was the tone what surprised me."
B "It was the tone that surprised me."
C "Was the tone that surprised me."
You need a second to think before you name the thing that struck you. The most natural start is:
A "What struck me was..."
B "What struck me it was..."
C "The what struck me was..."

A "Only I want one good article a week."
