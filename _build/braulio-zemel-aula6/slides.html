Chapter 3: The Code

It Is This That Matters

The structure that moves one idea to the front of the sentence

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"It is not the vocabulary that stops me. It is the speed."

"It was the tone of the editorial that surprised me."

"What slows me down is everything the sentence does not say."

"All I want is one good article a week, not a hundred pages."

Every one of these could be said in five words. Something has been moved to the front instead. What has been moved, and what does the speaker gain?

FormWhat it doesExample
It + be + X + that
It was the tone that...		Puts ONE piece of information under a spotlight, and pushes everything else into the shade."It was the tone that surprised me."
What + clause + be + X
What surprised me was...		Builds up to the point and delays it, so the listener leans in. The room goes quiet before the noun arrives."What surprised me was the tone."
All + clause + be + XSays the same thing and adds: and that is all. It makes the request sound modest."All I want is one good article a week."
The reason ... is that ...Announces that an explanation is coming, before giving it."The reason I stopped is that I thought I had to finish it."
that or who?Things take that. People take who or that. Nothing here ever takes what."It is the data that tells the story."
"It was the correspondent who filed it."

These are called cleft sentences, because the sentence is split in two. English does with word order what Portuguese often does with the voice.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"It was the tone what surprised me."
"It was the tone that surprised me."
"What surprised me it was the tone of the editorial."
"What surprised me was the tone of the editorial."
"Is the data that tells the real story."
"It is the data that tells the real story."

Three rules and no exceptions. The link word is that or who, never what. A what sentence carries one verb be in the middle, not two. And it is never dropped, however obvious it sounds to a Portuguese ear. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Listening

A Note from the Editor

The audio edition, opening the week. Sound first -- no text.

1. Who filed the briefing this week, and what had to move because of it?
The correspondent in Ankara. The briefing runs longer than usual, so the finance pages moved back by four.
2. Why does the editor expect some readers to dislike the editorial?
Because they ran a version of the same argument in March, and the letters came in for a month.
3. What was the correction, and whose mistake was it?
The vote was on the twenty-first, not the twelfth. The error was the magazine's, not the correspondent's.
Listening 2

Twenty Minutes a Week

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

1. Where does she say you should start, and why?
At the end -- the last paragraph is where the argument lands, so the middle stops being a fog.
2. When you skim, what exactly are you looking for?
Shape, not meaning: the names, the numbers, and the two or three words that come back three times.
3. What should you mark while you read, and what is it for?
One sentence you disagree with, even slightly -- it is what you will be able to say out loud at a table on Friday.
