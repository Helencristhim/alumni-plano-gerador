Chapter 3: The Code

What You Can Count, and What You Cannot

A split that Portuguese does not make, and English never forgets

Grammar Discovery

In Two of These You Could Say a Number.

"Too many meetings and not enough hours."

"How much time does that leave you?"

"I have a few good people on my team."

"He has a lot more patience than he used to."

You can put a number in front of meetings, hours and people. You cannot put one in front of time or patience. That single fact decides which blue word goes in front — many or much, a few or a little. Only enough and a lot of work on both sides.

JobCountable (you can say a number)Uncountable (you cannot)
Asking the amountHow many meetings?How much time?
A large amountmany tasks · a lot of tasksmuch time · a lot of time
A small amounta few peoplea little patience
More than you wanttoo many meetingstoo much work
The right amount — works for bothenough hours · enough time · not enough of either
Where enough goesbefore a noun: enough time · after an adjective: fast enough
The uncountable list worth learning by heartadvice · information · work · time · money · patience · energy · space · traffic · news
The trapPortuguese counts some of these. English does not. Never an advice, never two informations. Say some advice, a piece of advice.

In one line: if you could say a number, use many. If you could not, use much. And enough never has to choose.

Chapter 4: Real Speed

A Coffee, and an Honest Answer

P

C
That sounds like a lot more than enough. Give me some advice: how did you decide?
Comprehension

About Paulo

1. What does Paulo say about his new job?
It is quieter. Less money, more space in his head, and a lot more energy at seven.
2. What does he say about money and about the time he goes home?
He has enough for what he needs, and he goes home at six.
3. What does he ask about her team, and how does he answer her question about his job?
He asks whether she gets any help. And he answers her question with amounts — less money, more space, more energy — not with adjectives.
Listening 1 · Twenty-Three

Nobody Has Ever Asked Who Else Is In It

A woman who counted her own week. Sound first — no text.

1. How many meetings was she in that week, and what does she say about the short ones?
Twenty-three. She says a nine-minute meeting is worse than an hour, because nine minutes takes the whole hour anyway.
2. What did she do on the Friday?
She cancelled everything that did not have a decision in it, and she put two hours with no name on them in the calendar every morning.
3. What does she tell people those two hours are, and what happened after that?
She says they are a meeting. And nobody has ever asked her who else is in it.
Listening 2 · Twenty Per Cent Less

Enough Is a Real Amount

A man on the question nobody asked him. Sound first — no text.

1. What did everybody ask him, and what was his answer?
How much less? About twenty per cent.
2. What does he say he has more of now?
More time, more energy at seven in the evening, and a lot more patience with his daughter.
3. What does he say about money, and about the word enough?
He says money matters a great deal and there is a number below which none of it works. But enough is a real amount — it is just very hard to see from where he was standing.
Listening 3 · Count Something Else

Not How Many Hours. How Many You Chose.

A short talk on when energy actually runs out. Sound first — no text.

1. According to the speaker, when does energy actually run out?
Not at the end of the day. At the point where you stop choosing what to spend it on.
2. What does she suggest counting for a week?
Not how many hours you worked, but how many of them you chose.
3. What does she say if the answer is most of them and you are still exhausted?
Then it really is the amount of work, and that is a completely different conversation with a completely different person.

One Week, In Numbers

Some of these lines answer how many. Some of them answer how much. Say each line out loud and put the right question in front of it.
1. Which lines answer how many, and which answer how much?
How many: meetings, people on the team, lunch breaks, free evenings. How much: time in meetings, uninterrupted work — because those are hours, and hours of what, not a number of things.
2. Compare the time in meetings with the uninterrupted work. What does the card show?
Fourteen hours in meetings against three hours of uninterrupted work — almost five times more time spent talking than working alone.
3. Say one sentence with too many and one with not enough about this week.
There were too many meetings, and there was not enough uninterrupted time. She also did not take enough lunch breaks — only two out of five.
