Lesson 07 -- Pre-class

How Much, How Many -- Counting What You Have, and What You Do Not

English splits the world into things you can count and things you cannot, and Portuguese does not make the same split. Key words: energy, patience, advice, space, a task, to run out of something, enough, in a hurry, to spend time, a budget. Structure: quantifiers — much and many, a few and a little, too much and too many, and the one word that works on both sides: enough.

"I have too many tasks and not enough hours."

"I do the real work after seven, when everybody runs out of questions."

"I have enough for what I need, and I go home at six."
In a hurry -- needing to move fast, because there is not enough time
"Do not decide when you are in a hurry. That is when you say yes to everything."
To spend time -- to use your hours on one thing instead of another
"How much time do you spend in meetings every week?"
A budget -- the money you have for something, decided before you spend any of it
"We have a small budget and too many things on the list."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English divides every noun into two piles, and it never forgets which pile a word is in. In the first pile are the things you can put a number in front of: three tasks, eleven meetings, nine people. In the second pile are the things you cannot: you can have a lot of time, but never three times; a lot of patience, but never two patiences. The test is that simple, and it is the only one you need: could I say a number here?

Everything else follows from that. To ask the amount, the first pile takes how many and the second takes how much: "How many meetings did you have?" against "How much time does that leave you?" For a large amount you use many or much; for a small one, a few for the first pile and a little for the second: "I have a few good people on my team" and "give me a little more space". When there is more than you want, too many tasks and too much work. And a lot of is the lazy friend that works on both sides, which is why everybody overuses it.

One word crosses the line completely: enough. Enough hours, enough time, not enough of either. It goes before a noun (enough time) but after an adjective (fast enough), and it never takes an of in front of the noun. Now the trap, and it is a real one, because Portuguese counts some of these words and English does not: advice, information, work, money, news. Never an advice, never two informations. Say some advice, or a piece of advice. Learn that short list by heart and you will sound right for the rest of your life. Then, as always, hand the question back: "And you? How much free time do you have this week?"

1. What is the only test the text says you need?

A Always at the end of the sentence.
B Before a noun and after an adjective — enough time, fast enough.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Countable, Uncountable, and Enough

GRAMMAR

If you could say a number, use many. If you could not, use much. And enough never has to choose.
