Disagreeing Without Damage

Saying no to your boss and to the client, in the same meeting. Key expressions: to play devil's advocate, a reservation, to take issue with, to see where somebody is coming from, to be at odds with, blunt, tactful, to sugarcoat, to save face, common ground, to raise a concern, to agree to disagree, to overrule, a sore point. Structure: hedging and modality -- tend to, would suggest, might well, it would seem that, and the softeners.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. All fourteen belong to the moment in a meeting when you do not agree — or when somebody does not agree with you.

To play devil's advocate -- to argue the opposite side on purpose, to test an idea
"Let me play devil's advocate for a minute: what if the partner is right?"
A reservation -- a doubt that stops you agreeing completely
"I have one reservation about the timeline, and it is not a small one."
To take issue with -- to disagree with one specific point
"I do not take issue with the decision, only with the date."
To see where somebody is coming from -- to understand why somebody thinks what they think
"It helps to see where somebody is coming from before you tell them they are wrong."
To be at odds with -- to disagree with, or not match, something else
"The draft is at odds with what we promised the board in March."
Blunt -- saying exactly what you think, without softening it
"His answer was blunt, and it was also right."
Tactful -- careful not to upset people when you say something difficult
"There is a tactful way to tell a partner that his idea will not work."
To sugarcoat -- to make bad news sound better than it really is
"I would rather not sugarcoat it: the deadline is gone."
To save face -- to avoid looking foolish or losing respect in public
"We gave him a way to change his mind and save face in front of his team."
Common ground -- the things both sides already agree on
"We started from common ground: nobody wanted the case to reach a court."
To raise a concern -- to mention a worry formally, so that it is on the record
"I want to raise a concern before we sign, and I will put it in writing the same day."
To agree to disagree -- to accept that neither side is going to change its view
"In the end we decided to agree to disagree and let the director decide."
To overrule -- to change somebody else's decision because you have more authority
"Only the director can overrule a decision like this, and she usually explains why."
A sore point -- a subject that makes somebody upset or defensive
"Last year's delay is still a sore point with the client, so we do not mention it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A Brazilian lawyer in an American meeting is rarely rude. The more common problem is the opposite: disagreeing out loud feels like a risk, so the disagreement stays silent in the room and arrives later, in a long email, after the decision has been made. English has a set of small tools that make it possible to disagree in the room without damaging anybody.

The first is tend to. Clients read a cap as distrust is a claim that one exception destroys. Clients tend to read a cap as distrust is a pattern, and it is much harder to attack. The second is would with a verb of thinking: I would suggest, I would argue, and the most polite of all, I would have thought twelve months was the minimum — which means, quite clearly, that you think the other person is wrong.

The third is might well, may well or could well. It admits that the other side could be right, and it usually comes just before a but: that might well be true, but the last two partners did not respect it. The concession is what earns you the disagreement. The fourth puts distance between you and the conclusion: it would seem that the partner has already signed. And the last group shrinks the size of the disagreement without removing it: slightly, somewhat, arguably, not entirely, not quite.

There is one limit, and it matters more than the tools. Hedge the opinion, never the fact. If the deadline is Friday, the deadline is Friday. One hedge in a sentence sounds diplomatic; three sound like an apology.

1. Why does the writer say a Brazilian lawyer in an American meeting is more likely to be silent than rude?

2. What does "clients tend to read a cap as distrust" do that "clients read a cap as distrust" does not?

B It puts the sentence in the past.
C It makes the sentence more certain.
3. In "that might well be true, but...", what is might well doing?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging and Modality

GRAMMAR

These tools do not change WHAT you disagree with. They change how hard it lands, and whether the other person can change their mind without losing face.

ToolWhat it doesExample
tend to / tends toTurns a claim about everybody into a PATTERN: true most of the time, not always. Nobody can prove you wrong with one exception."Clients tend to read a cap as distrust."
would + suggest / argue / say / have thoughtPresents your view as a VIEW, not a verdict. I would have thought is the politest way in English to say I think you are wrong."I would suggest we keep it."
"I would have thought twelve months was the minimum."
might well / may well / could wellConcedes that the other side could be right — usually just before a but. The concession is what earns you the but."That might well be true, but..."
seem / appear / it would seem thatPuts distance between you and the conclusion: the facts are saying it, not you."It would seem that the last two partners did not."
softeners: slightly, somewhat, arguably, not entirely, not quiteShrink the size of the disagreement without removing it."I am not entirely convinced."
"Six months arguably works."

Forms to watch: tend to is a normal verb (clients tend, he tends, never clients are tend). After would, might and may the verb is bare (it would seem, that might well be, never would seems or might well to be). One habit for this week: in every email where you disagree, use one tool per sentence at most, and check that every date and number is left exactly as it is.

Order

?In the meeting, you show that you see where they are coming from before you say anything else.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are in the meeting. Choose the version that disagrees clearly without damaging anybody.

Your boss has already agreed with the client, and you think it is a mistake. You start with:
A "You are both wrong about this."
B "I can see where you are coming from, but I do have one reservation."
C "OK. I will send you an email later."
You want to say that clients usually, but not always, see a cap as distrust:
A "Clients are tend to see a cap as distrust."
B "Clients always see a cap as distrust."
C "Clients tend to see a cap as distrust."
The client makes a fair point and you still disagree:
A "That might well be true, but the last two partners did not respect it."
B "That might well to be true, but no."
C "Maybe, perhaps, possibly you are right, sorry."
The deadline is Friday and you want to be clear about it:
A "It would seem that the deadline is possibly Friday."
B "The deadline is Friday, and I would suggest we move the call to Thursday."
C "I would have thought the deadline might be Friday, maybe."
Nobody is going to change their mind, and the meeting has to end:
A "Fine. Do what you want."
B "I think we may have to agree to disagree and let Marcos decide."
C "I agree with you completely."
