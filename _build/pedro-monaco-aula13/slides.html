Chapter 3: The Code

The Small Words That Soften a No

Tend to, would suggest, might well — and where they stop

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Clients tend to read a cap as a sign of distrust."

"I would suggest that we keep the clause for twelve months."

"That might well be true, but it would seem that the last two partners did not respect it."

"I'm not entirely convinced that six months is enough."

Every one of these sentences disagrees with somebody. None of them says "you are wrong". Which words carry the disagreement — and which words make it land softly?

ToolWhat it doesExample
tend to / tends toTurns a claim about everybody into a PATTERN: true most of the time, not always. Nobody can prove you wrong with one exception."Clients tend to read a cap as distrust."
would + suggest / argue / say / have thoughtPresents your view as a VIEW, not a verdict. I would have thought is the politest way in English to say I think you are wrong."I would suggest we keep it."
"I would have thought twelve months was the minimum."
might well / may well / could wellConcedes that the other side could be right — usually just before a but. The concession is what earns you the but."That might well be true, but..."
seem / appear / it would seem thatPuts distance between you and the conclusion: the facts are saying it, not you."It would seem that the last two partners did not."
softeners: slightly, somewhat, arguably, not entirely, not quiteShrink the size of the disagreement without removing it."I am not entirely convinced."
"Six months arguably works."

Hedge the opinion, never the fact. "The deadline is Friday" stays exactly as it is. "I would suggest we move it" is where the softening goes.

Chapter 4: The Meeting

One Clause, Two People to Disagree With

Dana Whitfield, the partner's general counsel, and a decision your boss has already made

Dialogue

The Clause That Can Go

D
Thanks for joining, Pedro. Marcos and I agreed yesterday that the exclusivity clause can go. It tends to slow these deals down, and frankly it seems unnecessary in the first year.
P
I can see where you are coming from, and I would agree that speed matters. I do have one reservation, though, and I would rather raise it now than after signature.
D
Go ahead. I should say that Marcos has already signed off on it.
P
Understood, and I do not take issue with the decision itself. What I would suggest is that without exclusivity, our content might well end up on a competitor's platform within a month.
D
That seems a little unlikely. Our partners tend to respect these arrangements without a clause.
P
They may well do. But it would seem that the last two partners in your market did not, and both disputes ended in court. Could we find some common ground — exclusivity for twelve months only?
D
I'll be blunt. Twelve months is too long for us. Six, perhaps.
P
Six would arguably work if we kept the right to review it at the end. If not, I think we may have to agree to disagree and let Marcos make the final call.
Comprehension

About Dana

1. What does Dana say about the exclusivity clause, and why?
That it can go: it tends to slow these deals down and seems unnecessary in the first year.
2. What does she say Marcos has already done?
He has already signed off on dropping the clause.
3. How does she react to the risk Pedro describes?
She says it seems a little unlikely, because their partners tend to respect these arrangements without a clause.
4. What does she offer instead of twelve months?
Six months, perhaps — she says twelve is too long for them.
Listening 1

Direct About the Problem, Careful About the Person

A general counsel in New York on how disagreement is heard in American meetings. Sound first — no text.

1. According to her, what are American meetings direct about, and what are they careful about?
Direct about the problem and careful about the person.
2. What does she say the small phrases like I would suggest actually do?
They leave the other person a way to change their mind without losing face in front of their own team.
3. What mistake does she see most often from very capable foreign lawyers?
They say nothing in the room and disagree afterwards in a long email, when the decision has already been made.
Listening 2

One Hedge Is Diplomacy, Three Is an Apology

A negotiation coach on the lawyers who soften everything. Sound first — no text.

1. What does he say happens to many lawyers after a few weeks of learning to hedge?
They overdo it, and by the end of the sentence nobody knows what they actually think.
2. What is his rule?
Hedge the opinion, never the fact.
3. What does he say the other side assumes when you apologize for disagreeing?
That you are about to give ground.
