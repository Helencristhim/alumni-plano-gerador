Where the World Reaches You

Where your picture of the world actually comes from, and how to say what you know apart from what you are guessing. Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He is well-informed in the way that takes thirty years and no shortcuts. Four outlets every morning, two of which he chose precisely because they argue against him, and a habit of reading the ones he distrusts first. He would tell you it is not virtue. It is that he has been caught out too often by hearsay that arrived looking like a fact.

Last month a small regional paper broke a story about three public contracts. By Thursday the national coverage was everywhere; by Friday a minister had gone. He read all of it and took almost none of it at face value. The documents must have been sitting there since March, he said, so somebody can't have been reading them. And the officials involved may well have known for months.

What interests him is not the scandal but the spin. Every side has a vested interest, and the same three numbers can be arranged into two opposite stories. He is hard-pressed to remember a single week when that was not true. So he follows the story closely for a fortnight, and only then does he weigh in on it — usually at a dinner, usually when somebody makes a sweeping statement he cannot let stand.

He is honest about the rest. Africa is a blind spot, and he knows it. Once or twice a year he is swayed by an argument he sat down intending to demolish. And there are decisions that, in hindsight, he should have seen coming and did not. He takes his own certainty with a grain of salt too. It is the only way to keep abreast of a world that keeps rearranging itself.

1. Why does the text say the documents "must have been sitting there since March"?
A Because the speaker read them in March and knows it.
B Because he is drawing a confident conclusion from evidence, not stating knowledge.
C Because it is an obligation that somebody failed to meet.
2. "Somebody can't have been reading them." What does can't have do here?
A It expresses the negative of a confident conclusion.
B It expresses a prohibition in the past.
C It says the action was physically impossible.
3. "They may well have known for months." How sure is the speaker?
A Certain — he has proof.
B Not at all — he is inventing it.
C He thinks it is likely, and he is careful not to claim proof.
4. Why does the text say he takes his own certainty "with a grain of salt"?
A Because he doubts his own conclusions on purpose, the way he doubts everyone else's.
B Because he has stopped reading the news.
C Because he never has an opinion about anything.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Perfects — the Scale of Certainty About the Past

GRAMMAR

Five ways to talk about something that already happened when you were not there to see it. The auxiliary is what tells your listener how sure you are, so choosing it is not decoration.

FormWhat it doesExample
must have
+ past participle		A confident conclusion drawn from evidence. You did not see it; you are inferring it, and you are prepared to say so out loud."The leak must have come from inside."
may / might / could haveA real possibility and nothing stronger. May well have pushes it towards likely without ever claiming proof."They may well have known for months."
can't / couldn't haveThe negative of must have. This is the one Portuguese speakers get wrong, because mustn't have feels symmetrical — and does not exist in this meaning."She can't have expected that."
should / ought to haveRetrospective judgement. The right thing was not done, and hindsight makes that visible."We should have seen it coming."
would have
+ past participle		The unreal past: what was going to be true under a condition that never came about."A week earlier, it would have changed the vote."

One structure underneath all five: modal + have + past participle. The verb after have never changes form, however far back the event is. And in speech the have reduces to 've — must've come, should've seen — which is why it disappears from the ear long before it disappears from the grammar.

The Long Argument — How History Gets Rewritten

Structure: inversion after negative and restrictive adverbials — not only, rarely, no sooner, little did, at no point.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

A watershed moment -- a point after which nothing returns to the way it was
"Nineteen eighty-nine was a watershed moment by any measure."
To set the stage for -- to create the conditions that made something later possible
"Three decades of stagnation set the stage for what followed."
A precedent -- an earlier case that later ones are measured against

"His prediction was borne out within eighteen months."
To trace back to -- to follow a cause backwards to where it began

Long-standing -- having existed for a long time and not easily undone

To gloss over -- to mention something quickly in order to avoid its difficulty
"The official version glossed over the two years before the vote."
A revisionist account -- a retelling that challenges the version everybody accepted
"A revisionist account appeared in 2004 and it changed the syllabus."
To be at odds with -- to conflict with something, so that both cannot be true
"The documents are at odds with almost everything taught in schools."
To lay the groundwork -- to do the preparation that makes something possible much later
"Those arguments laid the groundwork a full decade in advance."
An upheaval -- a large and sudden change that unsettles everything around it
"You would be looking at the greatest upheaval of the century."
To be overstated -- to be presented as larger or more decisive than it really was
"The role of any single leader is almost always overstated."
A footnote -- a detail that history records without dwelling on it
"The meeting was a footnote until the archives were opened."
To hold up to scrutiny -- to survive close and hostile examination
"The old explanation simply does not hold up to scrutiny."
Received wisdom -- what everybody believes largely because everybody believes it
"By 1995 it was received wisdom, and nobody was attacking it."
To cast doubt on -- to make people question something they had accepted
"One letter cast doubt on the entire sequence of events."
A product of its time -- explainable, and partly excusable, by the era it belonged to

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

For fifteen years the account was received wisdom. It appeared in every textbook in more or less the same words, and it had the shape that comfortable explanations always have: a beginning, a decision and a consequence, with nothing awkward in between. Not only was it taught that way, but it was also examined that way, which is a far more efficient way of making a story permanent.

The archive undid it slowly. Researchers traced the central decision back to a memorandum written ten years earlier, in which two departments were already arguing about the same question the textbooks presented as sudden. The long-standing nature of that dispute had been glossed over completely. No sooner had the first paper appeared than a second one cast doubt on the dates as well.

The revisionist account that followed was at odds with the syllabus, and it held up to scrutiny rather better. Rarely does a new reading survive its first decade so intact. What it showed was that a quiet decade of argument had laid the groundwork, and that what looked like a single event had in fact come to a head over years. Little did the original authors imagine how completely the sequence would be rearranged.

None of this makes the year a footnote. It remains a watershed moment, and the upheaval that followed set a precedent that is still cited. What was overstated was the neatness — the idea that something set the stage and the rest simply followed. The prediction that the account would itself be revised has already been borne out twice, and the version we teach today will look, in its turn, like a product of its time.

1. "Not only was it taught that way, but it was also examined that way." Why is the verb before the subject?
A Because it is a question.
B Because a negative adverbial has been moved to the front, and English then uses question word order.
C Because the sentence is in the passive.
2. "No sooner had the first paper appeared than a second one cast doubt on the dates." What does this tell us?
A The second thing happened almost immediately after the first.
B The two things happened at the same moment.
C The second thing happened much later.
3. "Rarely does a new reading survive its first decade so intact." What is the writer doing here?
A Saying it happens frequently.
B Emphasising how unusual it is, by fronting the adverb.
C Asking whether it happens.
4. What does the text mean by saying the tidiness was overstated?
A That the events never happened.
B That the account was invented.
C That the story was real but presented as far neater than it was.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Inversion — When the Adverbial Comes First

GRAMMAR

Move a negative or restrictive adverbial to the front of the sentence and English obliges you to change the word order after it. This is the structure that makes an argument sound weighed rather than merely stated.

FormWhat it doesExample
Not only ... but alsoOne claim fronted, a second promised behind it. The clause after not only takes question word order; also normally appears in the second half."Not only did it survive, but it also held up better."
Rarely · Seldom · Never
Nowhere · Little		A restrictive or negative adverb at the front. Borrow the auxiliary a question would use: does, did, has, was."Rarely does a consensus collapse at once."
"Never have I seen it settled."
No sooner ... than
Hardly / Scarcely ... when		Two events, the second on top of the first. Keep the pairs straight — no sooner takes than, hardly and scarcely take when. The first verb is usually past perfect."No sooner had it opened than the arguments began."
Little did + subjectRetrospective irony: nobody knew then, and the speaker knows now. Almost always with know, realize, imagine, suspect."Little did anyone realize what it meant."
At no point · Not until
Under no circumstances		The most formal of the group, and the most useful in a disagreement, because it leaves no room to negotiate the claim."At no point was the decision put to a vote."

Two traps. First, the inversion belongs to the clause with the adverbial, not to the whole sentence: in not only did it survive, but it also held up, only the first half is inverted. Second, if the verb has no auxiliary of its own, you borrow do, does or did — exactly as you would to make a question.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. These eighteen stay in your material — they do not disappear after the class.

To concede a point -- to accept that part of the other argument is right
"I will concede the point about timing, but not the conclusion."
To take issue with -- to disagree with one specific thing that was said
"I take issue with the second half of that sentence, not the first."
To push back on -- to resist a claim firmly without attacking the person making it
"Somebody has to push back on that number before it becomes a fact."
Common ground -- the part of the question both sides already agree on
"We had more common ground than either of us expected."
To talk past each other -- to argue for a long time without ever addressing the same question
"For twenty minutes we simply talked past each other."
A straw man -- a weakened version of the other argument, invented so it can be defeated
"That is a straw man, and I think you know it."
To play devil's advocate -- to argue a position you do not hold, in order to test it
"Let me play devil's advocate for a moment."
To be beside the point -- to be true and irrelevant to the question at the same time
"That may well be true, and it is beside the point."
To qualify a statement -- to add the conditions that make a claim accurate
"I would qualify that: in most countries, and not since 2010."
To dig in -- to become more fixed in a position the more it is attacked
"Push him twice and he digs in for the rest of the evening."
To give ground -- to move towards the other position, a little at a time
"She gave ground on the figures and none at all on the principle."
A fair point -- an objection you accept, even though you still disagree overall
"That is a fair point, and I had not considered it."
To split hairs -- to argue over a distinction too small to change anything
"We are splitting hairs now, and the room has stopped listening."
To backtrack -- to move away from something you said earlier
"He backtracked so smoothly that half the table missed it."
To hold your own -- to argue competently against somebody stronger than you
"She held her own against three economists and a minister."
To agree to disagree -- to stop the argument without either side conceding

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

She spent thirty years disagreeing for a living, and she says the skill has almost nothing to do with being right. The first move is always the same: find the common ground out loud, before anybody has to defend anything. The second is to concede a point early and clearly. Most people do the opposite, and by the third exchange both sides have dug in so far that the argument is about the argument.

The next move is precision. Rather than rejecting a whole position, she would take issue with one sentence in it and say which one. It is essential that the objection be nameable, she says — a quarrel cannot be solved, but a named problem can. And where the claim is nearly right, she would qualify it rather than deny it: not that is false, but in most countries, and not since 2010.

She is severe about two failures. The first is the straw man: answering a weakened version of what was said, which feels like winning and persuades nobody. The second is talking past each other — two people arguing honestly about two different questions for an hour. I would rather we agreed on the question first, she says, even if it takes twenty minutes we do not have. And if she were advising a younger negotiator, that is where she would start.

What she never does is confuse persistence with strength. Sometimes the honest end is to agree to disagree, name the sticking point, and go to dinner. Suppose we were to stay another two hours, she asks — what exactly would change? When the answer is nothing, the conversation is over, and the relationship is not. That, she says, is the whole trade.

1. "It is essential that the objection be nameable." Why be and not is?
A Because it is a passive.
B Because expressions like it is essential that take the subjunctive — the bare form of the verb.
C Because the sentence is in the past.
2. "I would rather we agreed on the question first." Is she talking about the past?
A Yes, about a meeting that already happened.
B No — the past form marks the preference as unreal and softens it.
C No, it is simply a mistake for agree.
3. What does the text say is wrong with the straw man?
A It feels like winning and persuades nobody.
B It is too polite to be effective.
C It concedes far too much ground.
4. "Suppose we were to stay another two hours." What is she doing with this structure?
A Making a firm proposal.
B Describing what happened last time.
C Opening a hypothesis she is not committing to.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Unreal Past and the Subjunctive

GRAMMAR

English steps a verb backwards in time to step the speaker back from the claim. Nothing here is about the past — it is about distance, and distance is what makes a hard sentence bearable.

FormWhat it doesExample
would rather / would sooner
+ subject + past		A preference about what somebody else does. With the same subject you use the infinitive (I would rather stay); with a different subject, the past form."I would rather you did not quote that."
"I would rather stay." (same subject)
essential / vital / imperative
insist / suggest / demand + that		The present subjunctive: the bare verb, no -s in the third person and no to. The negative is not + bare verb."It is essential that he be present."
"I suggest that she not speak first."
Suppose / Imagine / What if
+ past · were to + verb		Opens a hypothesis you have not accepted. Were to is the most distant and therefore the most diplomatic of the three."Suppose we were to accept that premise."
If I were you
as if / as though + past		Were for all persons. Was is heard in speech, but were is what a careful speaker uses, and it is the form examiners and diplomats expect."If I were you, I would concede that."
"He talks as though he had read it."
It is (high) time + past
I wish + past / past perfect		The past form says the thing is not true yet, and turns a complaint into a shared observation."It is high time somebody said so."
"I wish he had qualified that."

One warning worth having. I wish he would qualify that is a complaint about somebody else's behaviour; I wish he qualified that is a wish about a fact. English keeps the two apart, and the difference is audible to a native speaker even when the room is loud.

A "I would rather you do not quote that figure."
B "I would rather you did not quote that figure."
C "I would rather that you would not quote that figure."
You want to insist, formally, that everyone be present:
A "It is essential that everyone is present."
B "It is essential that everyone be present."
C "It is essential that everyone will be present."
You want to test an argument you do not accept:
A "Suppose we were to accept that premise for a moment."
B "Suppose we will accept that premise for a moment."
C "Suppose we accept that premise for a moment, and it is true."
Somebody says everybody they know agrees with them. You want to push back without insulting them:
A "That is meaningless and you know it."
B "If I were you, I would treat that as a warning rather than as evidence."
C "You are splitting hairs and digging in."
The argument is going nowhere and you want to end it well:
A "Fine. You win."
B "Let us agree to disagree — the sticking point is the definition, and neither of us will move on it tonight."
C "I would rather we talked past each other."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Think of one opinion you hold strongly about world politics. In about ninety seconds, argue the OPPOSITE case as convincingly as you can — announce that you are playing devil's advocate, then make the strongest version of the argument you do not believe. Use at least two of today's structures. The test is simple: would somebody listening be unable to tell which side you are on?
