Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Say It and Back It Up -- Holding One Position for Two Minutes

Anybody can say what they think in five seconds. The hard part is holding the same idea for two minutes and making it stand up. Key words: a point of view, evidence, a drawback, a trade-off, commitment, to back something up, to make a case for something, in the long run, to have an impact on something, a counter-argument. Structure: clauses of reason and result.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A student is asked whether a season with a club team abroad is worth the hours it takes. He does not start with it depends. He starts with a point of view: five months on a team teach you more than a year of classes. Then he gives the reason, and the word that opens a reason is because. He improved fast because he spoke English at training, on the bus and at home. Since nobody on the team spoke his language, there was no easy way out. Notice that since does the same job as because, only it comes first, before the claim.

The second half of an argument does the opposite job. It does not explain why; it says what followed. Training took four evenings a week, so he studied after ten at night. The whole season ran in English, which is why he stopped translating in his head. In a written paragraph the same idea is often carried by as a result. All three point forward, to the consequence, while because and since point backward, to the cause.

Two traps catch almost every Portuguese speaker here. The first is because of, which takes a noun and never a full sentence: because of the championship, he missed two weeks of school. The second is the phrase the reason is because, which sounds right and is not. Say the reason is that. Get those two right and the rest is repetition: a claim, a reason, a piece of evidence, one honest drawback, and an answer to the counter-argument before anybody has to ask for it.

1. "He improved fast because he spoke English at training." What does the word "because" open here?
A The consequence of the first idea.
B The cause, which is the reason behind the claim.
C An example that backs the claim up.
2. According to the text, what is the difference between "since" and "because"?
A They do the same job, but "since" usually comes first, before the claim.
B "Since" opens a consequence and "because" opens a cause.
C "Since" is only used for time and never for reason.
3. The text warns against "the reason is because". What should you say instead?
A The reason is because of.
B The reason is so.
C The reason is that.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody reads an application file in about seven minutes, and in those seven minutes two kinds of sentence do two different jobs. The first kind closes a box. He studied in Canada for five months. The team qualified for the final round last November. He received two recommendations before he came home. Every one of those sentences carries a time that is over, and English answers a closed time with the past simple. Note the trap: last November, in 2025 and two months ago all close the box, so none of them can take the present perfect.

The second kind of sentence points at today, and that is where the present perfect lives. He has played competitive soccer since he was nine, and he still plays. He has not submitted the language certificate yet, so the file is still open. His listening has changed in a way he calls significant, and it is still changing. Nothing here is finished, so the verb takes have or has plus the past participle.

There is a third use, and it is the one that opens interviews: experience with no date at all. I have lived abroad. Have you ever studied in another country? Nobody is asking when; they are asking whether it is in you. One warning for a Portuguese speaker, and it is the most common mistake of all: never use the present tense with for or since. Not I am back in Brazil since June, but I have been back in Brazil since June. Get that one right and half the file writes itself.

In an argument, the difference between "it is" and "it might be" is not politeness. It is how much you are willing to defend. Key words: reliable, a shortcut, bias, to rely on something, overrated, to replace, hands-on, to keep up with something, in practice, to have a point. Structure: modal verbs of opinion and probability.

Reliable -- working the same way every time, so people can plan around it

"Copying the answer is a shortcut. The problem is the day the answer is wrong."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two people can look at the same fact and be equally certain about opposite things, which is why English puts the certainty in one small word and leaves the rest of the sentence alone. The tool might replace part of the job. That is a possibility and nothing more, and may and could do exactly the same work. Move one step up and you get probability: junior developers are likely to use it every day, or they will probably use it every day. Nothing has been proved, but you are now standing behind the claim.

The top of the dial is not about the future at all. It is about a conclusion you are drawing right now from something you can see. The course spends six weeks on the fundamentals, so that must be deliberate. He has written software for ten years, so he cannot be a beginner. Must and cannot are a pair: one says you are sure it is true, the other says you are sure it is not. And here is the trap that catches every Portuguese speaker: the opposite of must be is cannot be, never must not be.

The second trap is smaller and just as common. Can is not a way of saying maybe about one specific event. The interview can happen next week is wrong; the sentence you want is the interview might happen next week. The third trap is stacking two openers: pick in my opinion or I think, never both. Get those three right, add in practice when the theory and the reality disagree, and concede with you have a point when the other person is partly right. That last one costs you nothing and buys you the rest of the conversation.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An application year is made of sentences that begin with if, and there are two kinds. The first kind is a plan. If I get the offer in March, I will accept it the same week. If I miss the deadline, there will not be a second chance this year. Both of those could really happen, so the verb after if is the present simple and the other half takes will. Notice that the present simple is doing the work of the future here, which is exactly what confuses a Portuguese speaker.

The second kind is a thought experiment. If the tuition were free, I would go tomorrow. If I had another year, I would apply to six places instead of two. Nothing there is a plan: the tuition is not free and there is no extra year. So the verb after if steps back into the past simple, and the other half takes would. With the verb to be, careful English uses were for every person: if I were you, never if I was you.

One rule fixes almost every mistake at this level, and it has no exceptions worth learning yet: never put will or would immediately after if. Not if I will get the offer, and not if I would have the money. The other half of the sentence carries those words; the if half never does. And do not mix the two kinds: if I have another year, I would apply is half a plan and half a daydream, and it leaves the listener unsure which one you meant.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- First and Second Conditionals

GRAMMAR

Two minutes made of short sentences sounds like a form being filled in. The same two minutes with the halves joined sounds like somebody thinking, and the information does not change at all. Compare these. I want a university. It has a strong football programme. Now: I want a university that has a strong football programme. The second half tells you which university, so it cannot be removed and it takes no commas. That is the essential kind, and who, which and that all work in it: who for people, which for things, that for either.

The other kind is extra. My supervisor, who is Canadian, had never met a Brazilian student. There is only one supervisor here, so nothing needs identifying; the clause simply adds detail and it sits between two commas. Remove it and the sentence survives. In this kind, that is not allowed: after a comma it has to be who or which. And a comma plus which can also comment on the whole idea before it: the campus is the size of a town, which nobody tells you.

Two more words finish the set. Where joins a place, with no preposition afterwards: the city where I lived, never where I lived in. And whose shows possession: a student whose brother plays for the same club, never a student his brother plays. Three traps, then, and they are always the same three: which used for a person, that used after a comma, and a preposition left hanging at the end of a where clause. Fix those and your sentences double in length without getting harder to follow.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody says something to you today, and tomorrow you carry it into an argument. Two things happen on the way. The first is a choice of verb. Say never takes a person after it and tell always does: she said that the campus was huge, or she told me that the campus was huge, but never she said me. And beyond those two there is a whole set of verbs that carry meaning of their own: she admitted that, he claimed that, he insisted that, she pointed out that. Choosing between them is not decoration; it tells the listener what kind of thing was said.

The second thing is that the verb takes one step backwards in time. The first month is the hardest becomes he told me the first month was the hardest. I will call you becomes she said she would call me. It was worse than I expected becomes she admitted it had been worse than she expected. When the thing is still true today, keeping the present is also fine: he said the campus is huge, and it still is.

Questions do something extra, and it is the part almost everybody gets wrong. A reported question is not a question any more, so it loses its inversion. Where do you live? becomes he asked me where I lived, never where did I live. With no question word, use if or whether: she asked if I had the certificate. And one thing that is not grammar at all: when you carry somebody's words, do not improve them. Changing a quote so that it fits your argument better is the fastest way to lose an argument you could have won.
