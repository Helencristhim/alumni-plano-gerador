Tell Me About Yourself

The two-minute answer that opens every interview. Key words: background, track record, to lead, headcount, to report to, scope, to roll out, turnaround. Structure: present perfect with for, since, ever, never, just, already and yet.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words that describe a career instead of a job.

"My background is in organizational pedagogy, not in law."

"We rolled out the mental health program in eleven countries."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect

GRAMMAR

Use the present perfect when the past is still connected to now: have / has + past participle. The moment is not the message — the result today is.

career experienceever / neverHave you ever led a global team? I have never worked for an American company.
recent newsjust / already / yetWe have just rolled out the policy. I have not met the regional team yet.
how longfor / sinceI have worked in HR for twenty years. I have led the area since 2018.
finished pastpast simple, with a momentI left the company last month.

Careful: the moment kills the present perfect. If you say yesterday, last month or in 2019, English goes to the past simple: "I left the company last month", never "I have left the company last month".

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Reading the Numbers

An HR dashboard in English: rates, gaps and the sentence that compares them. Key words: turnover, absenteeism, benchmark, rate, gap, steady, to break down, overtime. Structure: comparatives and superlatives.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These eight words are on every HR dashboard in English.

Turnover -- the percentage of people who leave a company and have to be replaced
"Turnover is at fourteen point two percent this quarter."
Absenteeism -- days lost when people are away from work and should be there
"Absenteeism has been steady for four quarters."
Benchmark -- a standard number you compare your own result with
"The market benchmark for our sector is eleven point five."
Rate -- a number, usually a percentage, that shows how often something happens
"The rate went up by one point six points."
Gap -- the difference between two numbers or two situations
"The gap between operations and the offices is enormous."
Steady -- staying at the same level, with no big change
"Absenteeism is steady at three point eight percent."
To break down -- to divide a total number into its smaller parts
"If you break the number down by area, the story changes."
Overtime -- the hours somebody works beyond the normal working day
"We paid one point four million dollars in overtime."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

A comparative puts two things side by side. A superlative puts one thing against all the others, and it always needs the.

short word-er than / the -esthigh → higher than → the highest. wide → wider than → the widest.
long wordmore ... than / the mostexpensive → more expensive than → the most expensive.
irregulargood / badgood → better → the best. bad → worse → the worst.
equalas ... asAbsenteeism is as steady as it was four quarters ago.
make it biggermuch / far + comparativeOperations is much worse than the offices. The gap is far wider.

Two traps in a meeting: never say "more higher" — one form is enough. And the decimal point is said as point, never as comma: fourteen point two percent, one point four million.

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

If We Get This Right

Selling a plan that does not exist yet. Key words: onboarding, stakeholder, root cause, buy-in, to pilot, to scale, initiative, succession plan. Structure: first conditional, with unless, as long as, provided that and in case.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words a proposal is written with.

Onboarding -- the first weeks of a new employee inside the company
"Half of the people who leave in the first year leave because of onboarding."
Stakeholder -- anybody whose work is affected by a decision you make
"Finance is the stakeholder I have to convince first."
Root cause -- the real reason behind a problem, not the visible symptom
"Overtime is the symptom. The root cause is how we plan the shifts."
Buy-in -- the agreement of the people who have to support a decision
"Without buy-in from the site managers, nothing will happen."
To pilot -- to test a program in one small area before you launch it everywhere
"We are going to pilot it in one site, starting in March."
To scale -- to make something work for a much bigger number of people
"If the pilot works, we will scale it to the whole region."
Initiative -- a program you start in order to change something
"The initiative has one owner and one number to move."
Succession plan -- the list of people ready to take a key role if it becomes empty
"If we lose that manager tomorrow, the succession plan is one name long."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- First Conditional

GRAMMAR

The first conditional talks about a real, likely future. Two halves: if + present simple for the condition, and will + verb for the consequence.

basic formif + present, willIf the pilot works, we will scale it to the region.
unless= if notWe will not get approval unless finance supports it.
as long as / provided that= only on this conditionAs long as the managers release the time, this will work.
in case= as a precaution, beforeI will prepare a second version in case they ask for one.
ordereither half firstWe will stop if it does not work. / If it does not work, we will stop.

The trap: never put will after if. It is "if the pilot works", never "if the pilot will work". And unless already carries the negative, so "unless we do not get buy-in" says the opposite of what you mean.

Must, Should, May

Psychosocial risk and the English a policy is written in. Key words: workload, psychosocial risk, harassment, to comply with, guidelines, whistleblower, grievance, duty of care. Structure: modals of obligation and recommendation.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words a lawyer reads before anybody else does.

Workload -- the amount of work one person is expected to do
"Managers must review the workload of the team every quarter."
Psychosocial risk -- something in the work itself that can damage mental health
"Every manager must complete the psychosocial risk training."
Harassment -- behavior that humiliates or intimidates somebody at work
"A complaint of harassment cannot be investigated by the same area."
To comply with -- to do what a law or a rule requires
"We have to comply with the new rule before the end of the year."
Guidelines -- the written instructions that say how something has to be done
"The guidelines say you should document the conversation."
Whistleblower -- somebody who reports wrongdoing inside their own company
"A whistleblower may use the anonymous channel at any time."
Grievance -- a formal complaint an employee makes about the workplace
"A written grievance has to be answered within five working days."
Duty of care -- the legal responsibility a company has for the health of its people
"If it is part of the duty of care, the verb cannot be should."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

The wellbeing policy has four lines that matter. Every manager must complete the psychosocial risk training each year. A written grievance has to be answered within five working days, in writing. Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Obligation and Recommendation

GRAMMAR

All of these are followed by the base verb: must complete, never must to complete. What changes is the STRENGTH.

obligationmust / have toEvery manager must complete the training. A grievance has to be answered in five days.
recommendationshouldYou should document the conversation.
permissionmay / canEmployees may use the anonymous channel.
prohibitionmust not / cannotYou must not investigate a complaint about your own team.
no obligationdo not have toYou do not have to use the template — it is optional.

The trap that costs money: must not and do not have to are opposites, not variations. "You must not report" forbids it. "You do not have to report" makes it optional. In a policy, choosing the wrong one changes what the company promised.

The Question You Did Not Prepare

Asking back instead of freezing. Key words: to clarify, to probe, assumption, to follow up, blind spot, candid, to hedge, takeaway. Structure: question formation and indirect questions.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. These are the words for the conversation about the conversation.

To clarify -- to make something clearer when it was not understood
"Let me clarify what I meant by early attrition."
To probe -- to ask more questions to get to what is underneath
"He is not attacking you. He is probing to see if the number holds."
Assumption -- something you believe is true without checking it
"That is an assumption. The data says something else."
To follow up -- to check later that something has actually happened
"I will follow up with the exact figure by Friday."
Blind spot -- something everyone else can see that you cannot
"Every leadership team has a blind spot. Ours is the night shift."
Candid -- honest, even when it is uncomfortable
"Let me be candid: we are still in the middle of that process."
To hedge -- to make an answer less certain on purpose
"I am not hedging. I genuinely do not have the number today."
Takeaway -- the one thing people remember after the conversation
"If there is one takeaway from this meeting, it is the two sites."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Direct and Indirect Questions

GRAMMAR

A direct question inverts the subject and the verb. An indirect question puts a frame in front, and then the sentence goes back to normal order — subject before verb, and no do / does / did.

wh- questionCould you tell me...What is the rate? → Could you tell me what the rate is?
with an auxiliaryMay I ask...Which period are you asking about? → May I ask which period you are asking about?
yes/no questionwhether / ifDid the pilot work? → I was wondering whether the pilot worked.
question word = subjectnothing movesWho owns this? → Do you know who owns this?

The trap: the inversion survives inside the indirect question. "Could you tell me what is the rate?" is the most audible mistake an advanced Portuguese speaker makes in English. It is "what the rate is".

Quiz
You want to ask politely what the attrition rate is. Which sentence is correct?
A "Could you tell me what is the attrition rate?"
B "Could you tell me what the attrition rate is?"
C "Could you tell me what does the attrition rate?"
You want to know whether the pilot worked, politely. Which one?
A "I was wondering whether the pilot worked."
B "I was wondering did the pilot work."
C "I was wondering whether did the pilot work."

A "Sorry, my English is not good enough for that."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection
Think of the hardest question anybody has ever asked you at work — the one you could answer and did not. Record yourself: say the question in English, ask one indirect question back, and then answer it. Take the three seconds before you start. Do not apologize for anything.
