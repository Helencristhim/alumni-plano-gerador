Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Every art history student eventually discovers that description is not the easy part. The assumption that describing a painting simply means listing its contents is understandable, but it rarely survives a first assignment. A list tells a reader what is there. A description, in the sense that critics use the word, tells a reader why it matters that those things are there and not others.

Consider the difference between two sentences about the same picture. The first says that a woman is pouring milk in a kitchen and that the wall behind her is empty. The second says that the emptiness of the wall concentrates our attention on the woman's task. Both are accurate. Only the second makes a claim, and it does so largely through a single grammatical move: an adjective, empty, has become a noun, emptiness, and that noun can now act as the subject of a sentence and cause something to happen.

This move, usually called nominalization, is one of the defining features of academic and critical prose. It allows a writer to compress an entire observation into a phrase and then to evaluate it. Vermeer's refusal to decorate the wall, the modesty of the subject, her concentration, his handling of the light: each of these phrases packs a clause into a noun and leaves the rest of the sentence free to argue.

Used well, nominalization produces the density that readers of college essays and critical reviews expect. Used carelessly, it produces fog. A sentence such as "the utilization of illumination facilitates the enhancement of textural perception" contains no error that a spell checker would catch, yet it communicates almost nothing. The reader's frustration is justified, because the writer has hidden every actor and every action behind abstract nouns.

The skill, then, is judgment rather than habit. A useful test is to ask whether the noun lets you say something you could not say as easily with a verb. If the rediscovery of Vermeer in the 1860s is the subject of your paragraph, the noun phrase earns its place. If you write "the performance of an examination of the painting" when you simply mean looking at the painting, it does not.

Description, in the end, is where interpretation begins. The precision of your nouns is the first evidence a reader has that you have actually looked.

A A description is longer and uses more adjectives than a list.

C A description avoids making any claim about the work.
2. In the emptiness of the wall concentrates our attention, what does the nominalization make possible?
A It lets a quality act as the subject that causes something to happen.
B It makes the sentence shorter than the version that uses empty.
C It removes the need for a main verb in the sentence.
3. Why does the writer criticize the utilization of illumination facilitates the enhancement of textural perception?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Nominalization

GRAMMAR

Critical and academic prose turns actions and qualities into nouns. The noun becomes the subject of the sentence, and the rest of the sentence is free to evaluate it. Density is the tool, not the goal.

FormWhat it doesExample
verb → nounturns an action into a thing the sentence can evaluaterefuse → His refusal to decorate is deliberate.
adjective → nounturns a quality into a subject that can cause somethingempty → The emptiness of the wall focuses the eye.
the + noun + of + nounpacks who and what into one phrasethe handling of the light
possessive + nounkeeps the agent inside the noun phraseVermeer's restraint
The C2 trapstacking nouns until nobody acts. If a verb says it more clearly, use the verb.

A test before you keep a noun: can you say something with it that you could not say as easily with a verb? The rediscovery of Vermeer passes. The performance of an examination of the painting does not.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Admissions readers have a private name for a certain kind of sentence: the volume knob. It is the sentence that tries to make an experience sound larger by turning up the intensity of its words. Art is very important to me. The exhibition was really amazing. The class was extremely interesting. Each intensifier promises more feeling and delivers less information, because very, really and extremely can be attached to almost anything.

Precise writers work differently. Instead of raising the volume, they adjust the tuning. An adverb is chosen not to amplify the adjective but to specify it. A composition can be deceptively simple, which tells the reader that its simplicity is a surface and that something more complex lies beneath. A student can be acutely aware of a mistake, which suggests a sharp, almost painful awareness rather than a vague one. A lecture can be intellectually demanding and emotionally generous at the same time, and the two adverbs tell us exactly in which dimension each quality lives.

These pairings are not interchangeable. English has strong preferences about which adverbs keep company with which adjectives. We say bitterly disappointed but not bitterly important, deeply moving but rarely strongly moving, highly unlikely but not highly aware. A pairing that breaks these habits is not ungrammatical, yet a native reader hears it immediately as translated, and the essay loses a little of its authority each time.

The most sophisticated pairings often work by contrast. Quietly revolutionary and faintly disquieting both lower the volume of a strong adjective, and paradoxically that restraint makes the claim more believable. A reader trusts a writer who can say that something was slightly unsettling far more than one who insists that everything was the most incredible experience of a lifetime.

None of this means that an essay should be decorated with adverbs. The best application essays use them sparingly, perhaps three or four times, and each one earns its place by doing work that no other word could do. The test is simple: remove the adverb and ask what the sentence has lost. If the answer is nothing, it was only volume.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Adverb + Adjective Collocations

GRAMMAR

At C2, the adverb does not turn up the volume of an adjective; it tunes it. Choose the partner that says what kind of quality it is, and remember that English prefers some pairs to others.

FormWhat it doesExample
degree adverb + adjectivemeasures intensity preciselyprofoundly unsettled, acutely aware
contrastive adverb + adjectivecreates tension between surface and substancedeceptively simple, quietly revolutionary
viewpoint adverb + adjectivenames the dimension in which the quality is trueintellectually demanding, emotionally generous
fixed collocationspairs that native writers expectbitterly disappointed, deeply moving, highly unlikely
The C2 trapstacking intensifiers or pairing words that do not belong together. "Really extremely important" and "highly aware" weaken the essay they were meant to strengthen.

Before you keep an adverb, remove it and read the sentence again. If nothing is lost, it was only volume.
