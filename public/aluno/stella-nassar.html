Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Every art history student eventually discovers that description is not the easy part. The assumption that describing a painting simply means listing its contents is understandable, but it rarely survives a first assignment. A list tells a reader what is there. A description, in the sense that critics use the word, tells a reader why it matters that those things are there and not others.

Consider the difference between two sentences about the same picture. The first says that a woman is pouring milk in a kitchen and that the wall behind her is empty. The second says that the emptiness of the wall concentrates our attention on the woman's task. Both are accurate. Only the second makes a claim, and it does so largely through a single grammatical move: an adjective, empty, has become a noun, emptiness, and that noun can now act as the subject of a sentence and cause something to happen.

This move, usually called nominalization, is one of the defining features of academic and critical prose. It allows a writer to compress an entire observation into a phrase and then to evaluate it. Vermeer's refusal to decorate the wall, the modesty of the subject, her concentration, his handling of the light: each of these phrases packs a clause into a noun and leaves the rest of the sentence free to argue.

Used well, nominalization produces the density that readers of college essays and critical reviews expect. Used carelessly, it produces fog. A sentence such as "the utilization of illumination facilitates the enhancement of textural perception" contains no error that a spell checker would catch, yet it communicates almost nothing. The reader's frustration is justified, because the writer has hidden every actor and every action behind abstract nouns.

The skill, then, is judgment rather than habit. A useful test is to ask whether the noun lets you say something you could not say as easily with a verb. If the rediscovery of Vermeer in the 1860s is the subject of your paragraph, the noun phrase earns its place. If you write "the performance of an examination of the painting" when you simply mean looking at the painting, it does not.

Description, in the end, is where interpretation begins. The precision of your nouns is the first evidence a reader has that you have actually looked.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Admissions readers have a private name for a certain kind of sentence: the volume knob. It is the sentence that tries to make an experience sound larger by turning up the intensity of its words. Art is very important to me. The exhibition was really amazing. The class was extremely interesting. Each intensifier promises more feeling and delivers less information, because very, really and extremely can be attached to almost anything.

Precise writers work differently. Instead of raising the volume, they adjust the tuning. An adverb is chosen not to amplify the adjective but to specify it. A composition can be deceptively simple, which tells the reader that its simplicity is a surface and that something more complex lies beneath. A student can be acutely aware of a mistake, which suggests a sharp, almost painful awareness rather than a vague one. A lecture can be intellectually demanding and emotionally generous at the same time, and the two adverbs tell us exactly in which dimension each quality lives.

These pairings are not interchangeable. English has strong preferences about which adverbs keep company with which adjectives. We say bitterly disappointed but not bitterly important, deeply moving but rarely strongly moving, highly unlikely but not highly aware. A pairing that breaks these habits is not ungrammatical, yet a native reader hears it immediately as translated, and the essay loses a little of its authority each time.

The most sophisticated pairings often work by contrast. Quietly revolutionary and faintly disquieting both lower the volume of a strong adjective, and paradoxically that restraint makes the claim more believable. A reader trusts a writer who can say that something was slightly unsettling far more than one who insists that everything was the most incredible experience of a lifetime.

None of this means that an essay should be decorated with adverbs. The best application essays use them sparingly, perhaps three or four times, and each one earns its place by doing work that no other word could do. The test is simple: remove the adverb and ask what the sentence has lost. If the answer is nothing, it was only volume.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Every formal analysis eventually faces the same problem: a painting shows everything at once, but a sentence can only say one thing at a time. The writer must decide what the reader should notice first, and English offers a structure designed for exactly that decision. It is this structure that separates a description from an argument.

Consider a plain sentence: the light calls Matthew. It is accurate, but it treats the light as one detail among many. Rewrite it as it is the light that calls Matthew, and the sentence suddenly makes a claim. The frame it is ... that places the light under a spotlight and implies a contrast with something else, perhaps the gesture or the figure of Christ. Grammarians call this a cleft sentence, because the original clause has been split in two in order to isolate its focus.

A second pattern builds suspense instead. What unsettled Caravaggio's critics was not the story but the setting. Here the reader waits through the first half of the sentence, and the focus arrives at the end, where English naturally places new and important information. Critics use this pattern when they want the reader to feel the weight of a conclusion rather than simply receive it.

The most persuasive clefts correct an expectation. It was not piety but naturalism that provoked his contemporaries. A reader who assumed the objection was religious is gently redirected, and the writer gains authority by anticipating a misunderstanding. Similarly, all he needed was a single window emphasizes how little was required to produce so great an effect.

Clefts are powerful precisely because they are marked. A paragraph built entirely from them sounds like a series of announcements, and a cleft that points at something vague, such as what I think is that art is important, only draws attention to its own emptiness. Used once or twice in a paragraph, on the claim that genuinely matters, the structure does what good criticism always does. It tells the reader where to look.

A cleft sentence splits one clause into two parts so that one element receives the focus. In analysis, it tells the reader where to look and what to compare it with.

English word order is famously rigid. Subject, verb, object: the pattern is so strong that a reader notices immediately when it is broken. Skilled writers use that sensitivity deliberately. Never had the gallery seemed so large, one essay begins, and the reader feels the weight of the moment before knowing what the moment is.

The structure behind that sentence is called negative inversion. When a sentence opens with a negative or restrictive expression, such as never, rarely, seldom, hardly, only then or not until, the auxiliary verb moves in front of the subject, exactly as it would in a question. Rarely does a first draft find its best sentence. Only after the museum closed did she realize how long she had stayed. The inversion delays the main clause and places a spotlight on the condition that introduces it.

A related pattern intensifies a quality. So quiet was the room that the guard could hear her breathing. Such was her concentration that she missed the announcement. Here the adjective or noun is brought forward, the verb follows, and a result clause completes the thought. The effect is formal and slightly literary, which is precisely why it suits the reflective moments of a personal essay.

Fronting is the quieter relative of inversion. A writer simply moves a detail, a place or a comparison to the beginning of the sentence: into that silence walked my teacher; stranger still was the label beside the painting. Nothing grammatical is required of the reader, but the emphasis shifts to whatever arrives first.

These structures are effective because they are unusual, and they stop being effective the moment they become habitual. An essay with inversion in every paragraph sounds as if it were wearing a costume. One or two, placed where the moment genuinely deserves emphasis, are enough. And inversion after a positive adverb, as in often did I visit, belongs to another century; in modern English it sounds archaic rather than elegant.

The evidence suggests that opinion is changing is more defensible than opinion is changing, and arguably the most famous case admits, without apology, that a reasonable reader might name another. Some hedges also signal distance from a claim that belongs to someone else. A permit that was ostensibly granted, or purportedly issued, tells the reader that the writer has seen the claim but has not seen the proof.

Concession is the other half of the move. Instead of ignoring the strongest objection, the writer states it fairly and then answers it. Readers who find critical prose slow are often not struggling with vocabulary. They are struggling with compression. Academic writers routinely remove words that a reader is expected to supply, and a reader who does not recognize the missing pieces has to reread every sentence twice.

The most common form of compression is the participle clause. Painted outdoors and at speed, the canvases looked unfinished. The full version would be because they had been painted outdoors and at speed, the canvases looked unfinished, but the reader can reconstruct the subject and the relationship without help. A past participle, as in painted or rejected by the jury, usually carries a passive meaning. A present participle, as in working en plein air, carries an active one and often suggests that two actions happen at the same time. A perfect participle, as in having been derided, places one action clearly before another.

All three depend on a single condition: the participle must share the subject of the main clause. When it does not, the sentence acquires an unintended meaning. Walking into the gallery, the paintings seemed brighter tells the reader, absurdly, that the paintings were walking. Grammarians call this a dangling participle, and even experienced writers produce it when they revise in a hurry.

A second form of compression is ellipsis, the omission of words that have already appeared. In Monet painted the harbor; Morisot, the garden, the verb painted is understood in the second clause, and a comma marks the gap. Ellipsis works best in parallel structures, where the reader can easily see what has been removed.

Recognizing these patterns is the difference between reading critical prose and decoding it. Producing them sparingly, and correctly, is the difference between writing that explains and writing that moves.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Most English sentences are loose. They state their main idea first and then add details, one after another, the way a person naturally speaks. Tarsila became a defining figure of modern art, trained in Paris and admired in São Paulo. The reader knows the point immediately, and everything that follows is supporting information.

A periodic sentence reverses that order. It gathers its details first and delays the main clause until the very end. Trained in Paris, admired in São Paulo and ignored for decades by the museums of the north, Tarsila became a defining figure of modern art. The facts are identical, but the effect is not. The reader has to hold the details in suspension, and when the main clause finally arrives, it lands with more weight. Writers use periodic sentences for conclusions, turning points and the claims they most want remembered.

Periodic sentences almost always rely on parallelism, the repetition of the same grammatical form in a series. In the example above, trained, admired and ignored are all past participles followed by a phrase. That repetition creates rhythm, and rhythm helps the reader keep track of the accumulated details. Correlative structures work in the same way: neither a copy of Europe nor a rejection of it, or not what Paris expected but what neither city had imagined. What follows neither must match what follows nor, and what follows not must match what follows but.

When parallelism breaks, readers feel it even if they cannot name it. She liked to paint, drawing and sculpture sounds clumsy because it mixes an infinitive, a gerund and a noun. The repair is simple: to paint, to draw and to sculpt.

Both techniques lose their power through repetition. A paragraph made entirely of periodic sentences becomes exhausting, and constant tricolons start to sound like a political speech. Used once, at the moment the argument turns, a well-built periodic sentence is one of the most elegant tools an essay writer has.
