Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody you have just met asks how the weekend was, and you answer: "It was good." The sentence is correct, it is polite, and it kills the conversation, because the other person has nothing to hold on to. The repair is not a bigger vocabulary. The repair is one more sentence, and English gives you three little words to open it.

The first one is because, and it opens the reason: "It was good, because we finally got out of the city." After because comes a full sentence, with a subject and a verb. There is a shorter cousin, because of, and it is the one Brazilians get wrong: after because of comes a noun and nothing else. "We arrived at midnight because of the layover" is right. "Because of the flight was late" is not.

The second word is so, and it opens the result: "The beach was crowded, so we went back on Tuesday." Notice the order. The reason comes first, and the consequence comes after so. The third is that is why, and it does the same job with more weight: "My mother does not drive, and that is why we get around by bus." Never say for that here. It does not exist in English, and it is a direct translation from Portuguese.

One last thing. You can put the reason first: "Because it was crowded, we left early." And when the question is simply "Why?", the whole answer is a because sentence: "Why October?" — "Because my mother is free that week." Two sentences instead of one, and the person in front of you suddenly has somewhere to go.

1. The text says "it was good" kills the conversation. Why?
A Because the sentence is grammatically wrong.
B Because the other person has nothing to hold on to.
C Because the word good is too informal.
2. Why is "Because of the flight was late" wrong?
A Because of is followed by a noun, not by a full sentence.
B Because flight is a countable noun.
C Because the sentence needs so instead.
3. What comes after so, according to the text?
A The reason.
B The result, and the reason comes before it.
C A noun, exactly like after because of.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reason and Result

GRAMMAR

Because opens a reason. So opens a result. Because of takes a noun and stops there.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every policy page in the world does only four things, and English uses four little structures to do them. The first is obligation. Guests must show a photo ID when they check in, and they have to leave a deposit on a card. Must and have to mean the same thing here; must belongs to the written rule, and have to is what a person says out loud. After both of them the verb is bare: must show, never must to show.

The second is permission. Children under twelve are allowed to stay in the room free of charge, and every guest is allowed to leave the bags at the desk before three. The little word to is not optional: allowed to leave, never allowed leave. And the question is built the same way: Am I allowed to leave my bags here?

The third is prohibition, and the fourth is the trap. You must not smoke in the room means the hotel forbids it. You do not have to print the receipt means something completely different: nobody is forcing you, and you can print it if you want. In Portuguese the two sound close. In English they are opposites, and the difference on a non-refundable booking is money.

One last thing: in the past, all of this becomes had to. Last October we had to pay a fee to change the date, and the hotel never gave us a refund. There is no such word as musted, and the desk will always ask you for proof of what you say. Bring it.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The belt at baggage claim stops, everybody else walks away with a suitcase, and yours is not there. The first sentence you say at the desk decides how the next hour goes, and English has one tense for exactly this moment: a problem that started before now and is still open. "My bag has not arrived." No day, no hour, no story. Just the fact, and the fact is not finished.

Four small words live inside this tense. Just means a minute ago, and it sits between have and the verb: "They have just told me it is in Panama." Already means sooner than the other person expects, and it sits in the same place: "I have already filed a claim." Yet means not up to now, and it goes at the end, in a negative or in a question: "It has not arrived yet." "Have you filed a claim yet?"

The fourth is still, and it carries the impatience the other three do not: "It is nine in the evening and I still have not received it." Notice where it goes — in front of have not, not after it. Say that sentence at a desk and the agent hears how long you have been waiting without you having to complain.

One trap, and it is the Brazilian one. The moment you say when, English changes tense. "I filed the claim yesterday, at the airport" — simple past, because yesterday is finished. "I have filed a claim" — present perfect, because the case is open. Same claim, two tenses, and the difference is not the claim: it is whether you name the time. And when the reference number is in your hand and the bag is delayed, not missing, there is one more sentence worth saying: "What am I entitled to tonight?"

B Because the problem started before now and is still open, and no time is named.
C Because arrive is an irregular verb.
2. Where does still go, according to the text?
A In front of have not: "I still have not received it."
B At the end of the sentence, like yet.
C Between have and the verb, like just and already.
3. "I filed the claim yesterday, at the airport." Why is this not the present perfect?
A Because a claim is always in the simple past.
B Because the sentence names a finished time, and then English changes tense.
C Because the claim was filed at an airport and not at a hotel.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Reporting a problem is one sentence. Telling the story of it is a different skill, and English does it with two past tenses working together. The first one paints the scene: "It was raining." "Four other people were standing at the desk." Nothing here is a single event — it is what was going on around you, and the shape is always was or were plus the verb with -ing.

The second one is the event: "We arrived." "She gave us a voucher." One moment, finished, and the shape is the simple past. Put the two together and you have a story: "It was raining when we arrived." The long thing had already started; the short thing cut into it. That is what when does.

While is different: it holds two long things side by side, and both halves take -ing. "While he was making a fuss, the woman on duty was typing and saying nothing." Never while he shouted. And as soon as is different again: it puts two short events one straight after the other, so both take the simple past. "As soon as she saw the confirmation, she sorted it out."

Two words end a story instead of letting it stop. Eventually means after a long wait — and it is not the Portuguese word that looks like it. "Eventually he got the same room." And it turns out introduces what you discovered at the end: "It turned out the hotel had overbooked on purpose." Scene, interruption, end. Every story you will ever tell has those three pieces.

B Because rain is always in the continuous form.
C Because the sentence has two subjects.
2. What does while do, according to the text?
A It puts two short events one after the other.
B It holds two long things side by side, and both halves take -ing.
C It ends the story, like eventually.
3. "As soon as she saw the confirmation, she sorted it out." Why are both verbs in the simple past?

B Because see and sort are irregular verbs.
C Because both are short events, one straight after the other.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two guests ask for the same thing at the same desk, thirty seconds apart, and only one of them gets it. The difference is almost never the vocabulary. It is that the first one said "I need a late check-out" and the second one asked a question. A statement leaves the other person nowhere to go. A question does.

English has four sizes of question, and the longer the opening, the softer the request. For a thing: "Could I have a spare key?" For an action: "Could you double-check the name?" Note that after could the verb is bare — never could you to check. For something that costs the other person a little trouble: "Would you mind swapping seats?" And after would you mind, the verb always ends in -ing. Never would you mind to swap. That is the one form to learn by heart.

The softest of all is the longest: "I was wondering if you could hold the room until two." The past form here is not about time — it is distance, and distance is what makes it polite. The same works with "Is there any chance of an upgrade?"

Two more things, and they are worth more than the grammar. Give one short reason: "our flight is at nine in the evening", "we are short of time". A reason turns a favor into a problem somebody can solve. And when the answer is no, say "that is fine, thank you" and stay friendly. It sounds like it costs you the thing you wanted; in fact it is what gets you the call back twenty minutes later. One last trap: if somebody asks would you mind waiting and you are happy to wait, the answer is no. Not yes.

1. Why does "I need a late check-out" work less well than a question?
A Because need is an impolite verb in English.
B Because a statement leaves the other person nowhere to go, and a question does not.
C Because it does not contain the word please.
2. What shape does the verb take after would you mind?
A The -ing form: would you mind swapping.
B The infinitive with to: would you mind to swap.
C The bare verb, like after could.
3. Somebody asks "Would you mind waiting five minutes?" and you are happy to wait. You say:
A "Yes, of course."
B "No, not at all."
C "Yes, I do not mind."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Complaining tells the desk what the problem is. Negotiating hands them a yes they are allowed to give. The difference lives in one structure, and English uses it every time it talks about a real possibility in the near future: if plus the present, and then will plus the bare verb. "If you can move us to another hotel tonight, we will take it."

Look at the halves. The if half is about tonight, but the verb stays in the present. This is the trap for Portuguese speakers, because in Portuguese that half is a future form. In English, never if you will move us. The will lives in the other half, and only there. The two halves can also change places: "We will book somewhere ourselves if we do not hear anything by six."

Unless is the same structure in one word: it already means if not. "Unless you cover the taxi, we will have to pay for it ourselves." Because the not is already inside, you never add a second one. Never unless you do not cover.

Two more uses, and they are the ones that end the conversation well. The consequence half does not have to be will: it can be softer. "If that is everything you can authorise, could you ask a supervisor?" That sentence escalates without blaming the person in front of you. And a deadline is simply an if half with a time in it: "if we do not hear anything by six". Two reasonable options, one deadline, and the answer in writing. That is the whole conversation.

1. Why is "If you will move us tonight" wrong?
A Because the if half stays in the present, even when the sentence is about tonight.
B Because move is an irregular verb.
C Because the two halves are in the wrong order.
2. What does unless already contain?
A A future form, so the verb after it takes will.
B The word not, which is why you never add a second one.
C A deadline, so it always needs a time after it.
3. According to the text, what does "If that is everything you can authorise, could you ask a supervisor?" achieve?
A It tells the agent that they have not done their job.
B It moves the case up without blaming the person in front of you.
C It sets a deadline for the supervisor to answer.
