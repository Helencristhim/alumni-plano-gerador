Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

What You Actually Run -- Saying What the Company Does

Somebody at a trade fair asks what your company does, and every door after that one depends on the next two minutes. Key words: a group of companies, a managing director, a manufacturing plant, point-of-sale material, a retail chain, to supply somebody, to source something, a lead time, to scale up, in-house, a track record, to wear several hats. Structure: present perfect simple and present perfect continuous.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A group of companies -- several separate companies that are owned and directed together, as one business
"We are a group of companies: four in retail and one in transport."
A managing director -- the person who runs a company day to day and answers for the whole operation
"I am the managing director. If a plant stops, it is my problem."
A manufacturing plant -- a site with machines and a production line, where the products are actually made
"We have two manufacturing plants, about fifty miles apart."
Point-of-sale material -- the price tags, shelf labels and signs a shop uses to show a price or a promotion
"Everything you read on the shelf is point-of-sale material. That is what we make."
A retail chain -- a group of shops that belong to the same company and share a name
"We work with the largest supermarket chains and drugstore chains in the country."
To supply somebody -- to be the company that regularly provides another company with what it sells or uses
"We supply forty-seven of the fifty largest supermarket chains in Brazil."
To source something -- to find and buy a material or a product, often from another country
"We source part of our raw material in Asia and part in South America."
A lead time -- the time between placing an order and having it delivered
"Our lead time is fifteen days, and that is the reason the big chains stay with us."
To scale up -- to make an operation bigger so that it can produce much more
"We opened the second plant because we could not scale up in the first one."
In-house -- done by your own people inside the company, instead of bought from outside
"The design is in-house. We do not send it to an agency."
A track record -- everything a company has actually delivered so far, which is what a new client judges
"Twenty years and almost every large chain in the country: that is our track record."
To wear several hats -- to do several completely different jobs at the same time, in one role
"In a family group you wear several hats. Today I was in sales and in maintenance."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A man walks up to a stand at a retail fair and somebody asks what his company does. He says that they are a group of companies, and that four of them make point-of-sale material: the price tags and the shelf labels shoppers read without ever thinking about them. Then comes the number, because a buyer always wants the number first. They have supplied forty-seven of the fifty largest retail chains in the country. That sentence counts a result, so it takes the simple form: have plus the past participle.

The next sentence does a different job. They have been supplying most of those chains since 2016, and they are still supplying them this morning. Nothing there is finished, so the verb carries been and an -ing. The two sentences look similar and measure opposite things: one says how many, the other says how long.

The rest of the answer follows the same rule. He has been running the group for ten years, and in that time the group has opened a second manufacturing plant and has added a transport company. The design is in-house; only the raw material is sourced abroad, which is why the lead time is fifteen days and not thirty. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: never use the present tense with since or for. Not I am here since ten years, but I have been here for ten years.

1. "They have supplied forty-seven chains." Why the simple form and not "have been supplying"?
A Because supply is a verb that never takes -ing.
B Because the sentence counts a finished result, not a period still running.
C Because the sentence is about the past and is over.
2. Why is it "they have been supplying them since 2016" and not "they have supplied them since 2016"?
A Because the sentence measures a period that is still open today.
B Because there is more than one chain.
C Because "since" can only be used with the -ing form.
3. The text warns against "I am here since ten years". What is the problem?
A "Here" cannot follow the verb to be.
B Ten years is too long a period for one sentence.
C English does not use the present tense with since or for. It uses the present perfect.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect Simple and Continuous

GRAMMAR

One perfect, two jobs: counting what is finished, and measuring what is still running.

JobFormExample
How many, how muchhave / has + past participleWe have supplied forty-seven chains.
How long, still goinghave / has + been + verb-ingWe have been supplying them since 2016.
A result inside an unfinished periodsimple form, with this year / this monthThis year we have opened a second plant.
Since and forsince + a point in time · for + a length of timesince 2016 / for ten years
State verbsbe, have, know, own: simple form even for durationI have been a director for ten years.
QuestionHow many have you...? vs How long have you been...?How long have you been working with them?
Negativehave not + participle / have not been + -ingWe have not raised the price since March.

Never: I am the director since ten years · we are having two plants since 2019 · this year we have been opening a second plant · how long time you work with them? · we work with them since 2016.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Inside the Operation -- What a Buyer Looks At

A buyer walks your plant before the first big order, and what they check is not the newest machine. Key words: a production line, output, capacity, a shift, downtime, a batch, a bottleneck, scrap, an inspection, headcount, to be up and running, give or take. Structure: quantifiers, proportions and approximation.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A production line -- the sequence of machines and people that turns raw material into a finished product
"The second production line only runs in the high season."
Output -- how much a plant actually produces in a given period
"Output last week was thirty-eight thousand units."
Capacity -- the most a plant is able to produce when everything is running
"Capacity is around fifty-five thousand a week, but nobody runs at capacity."
A shift -- a block of hours worked by one team before another team takes over
"We weigh the scrap at the end of every shift."
Downtime -- the time a machine is stopped and producing nothing at all
"We lost four hours of downtime on the second line on Tuesday."
A batch -- a quantity of products made together, in one run of the line
"A changeover between one batch and the next takes forty minutes."
A bottleneck -- the slowest point in a process, which limits everything after it
"The bottleneck is the cutting machine, and everybody on the floor knows it."
Scrap -- material that comes off the line unusable and has to be thrown away
"Scrap is well below three per cent, and we weigh it every shift."
An inspection -- a formal look at a line to confirm that it meets an agreed standard
"They sent somebody for an inspection before the first big order."
Headcount -- the number of people a company has on the payroll, given as a figure
"Headcount is a hundred and twenty, plus a good deal more in high season."
To be up and running -- working normally again, after having been stopped
"The line was down for three hours and it is up and running again now."
Give or take -- used after a number to show it is close but not exact
"Forty thousand units a week, give or take."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A buyer arrives at a plant and asks the only question that matters to her: how much comes off the line in a week? The manager does not say forty thousand. He says in the region of forty thousand, and that small phrase is doing a great deal of work. It tells her the number is honest, that he knows it is an average, and that he has not rounded it up to sound bigger.

English keeps these hedges in a fixed place. Roughly, around, approximately and in the region of all go before the number. Just under and well below also go before it, and they add a direction: two per cent means close to two, just under two per cent means slightly less. Only one common hedge goes after the number, and it is give or take. Forty minutes, give or take.

Sometimes the honest answer is not a figure at all but a proportion. We run at two thirds of capacity. One in three orders is a repeat. And when the thing being measured cannot be counted — scrap, downtime, time, work — English uses much, a great deal of or plenty of, never many. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: do not end a business figure with more or less. It exists, but it sounds vague exactly where you need to sound careful.

1. Why does the manager say "in the region of forty thousand" instead of "forty thousand"?
A Because he does not know the real number.
B Because it signals the figure is an honest average rather than a rounded-up claim.
C Because English does not allow exact numbers in business.
2. Which hedge goes AFTER the number rather than before it?
A approximately
B give or take
C just under
3. The text says English uses "much" or "a great deal of" and never "many" for scrap and downtime. Why?
A Because they are always small quantities.
B Because they belong to the factory floor and take informal words.
C Because they cannot be counted one by one, so they take uncountable quantifiers.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Quantifiers, Proportion and Approximation

GRAMMAR

The number is half the sentence. How sure you are of it is the other half.

JobFormExample
Close to a figureroughly / around / approximately / about + numberOutput is roughly forty thousand units.
Close, formal registerin the region of / in the order of + numberCapacity is in the region of fifty-five thousand.
Just below or just abovejust under / just over / a little under / well belowScrap is well below three per cent.
Close — goes AFTERnumber + give or takeForty minutes, give or take.
Proportiontwo thirds of / a third of / one in threeWe run at two thirds of capacity.
Uncountable amountmuch / a great deal of / plenty of (never many)There is a great deal of scrap on that line.
Countable amountmany / a number of / plenty ofWe ran a number of small batches last month.

Never: forty thousand units, more or less · there is many scrap · downtime was two per cent just under · we run at two thirds capacity · the capacity are fifty-five thousand.

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

The Decision That Cost Money -- Talking About What You Would Have Done

Everybody who runs something has one decision they would take back. Key words: a trade-off, a margin, to write something off, a shipment, a tariff, a quote, upfront, to back out, a contingency, hindsight, to cut corners, a judgement call. Structure: third conditional and past modals.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A trade-off -- a choice in which you gain one thing and lose another, and cannot have both
"Cheaper material and a longer lead time. That is the trade-off."
A margin -- what is left of the price once every cost has been taken out of it
"The margin on that contract was thin before the tariff, and negative after it."
To write something off -- to accept that money or stock is lost, and take it out of the accounts
"We wrote off most of that shipment and moved on."
A shipment -- a quantity of goods sent together, in one delivery
"The first two shipments were fine. The third one was not."
A tariff -- a tax a country charges on goods that come in from abroad
"The tariff changed in the middle of the contract and the margin went with it."
A quote -- the price a company gives you in writing, before you place the order
"Their quote was eleven per cent below the supplier we had used for years."
Upfront -- paid or done at the beginning, before anything has been delivered
"They wanted forty per cent upfront, which should have told us something."
To back out -- to leave an agreement before it has been finished
"If they had backed out in March we would have had time. In June we did not."
A contingency -- a plan kept ready in case the first plan fails
"We had no contingency, and that was the real mistake."
Hindsight -- understanding of an event that you only have after it has happened
"In hindsight the eleven per cent was never eleven per cent."
To cut corners -- to do something faster or cheaper by skipping the steps that matter
"They were not cheaper. They were cutting corners, and we paid for it later."
A judgement call -- a decision made on experience, when the facts do not settle it
"It was a judgement call. It was wrong, but it was not careless."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A director moves half a large order to a new supplier because the quote is eleven per cent below the one he has used for years. Nobody visits the plant. Four months and three shipments later he finds out that the work was going somewhere else with somebody else's label on it, and the company writes off most of a delivery and pays a premium to bring the old supplier back.

Now look at how he talks about it. If we had visited the plant, we would have seen it in twenty minutes. That sentence describes a past that never existed: if plus had plus a participle in the first half, would have plus a participle in the second. The one thing English never allows is would inside the if half. In Portuguese the same idea takes a verb form that feels like a conditional, and that is exactly where the mistake comes from.

The second half of the story uses a different tool. We should have asked for a reference. That is not an alternative past, it is a judgement about the real one, and it stings. It could have been worse and we might have lost the client describe possibilities that did not happen. And in a formal report the if disappears altogether: Had we known about the tariff, we would have priced it in. The trade-off is that the price advantage was real, but nobody had put a number on the risk. In hindsight, it was a judgement call — and having no contingency was not.

1. Why is "If we had visited the plant, we would have seen it" and not "If we would have visited"?
A Because "visit" is an irregular verb.
B Because English never puts "would" inside the if half of the sentence.
C Because the visit actually happened.
2. What is the difference between "we would have seen it" and "we should have asked"?
A The first describes a past that did not happen; the second judges the past that did.
B The first is formal and the second is informal.
C They mean the same thing and are interchangeable.
3. In "Had we known about the tariff, we would have priced it in", what has happened to the sentence?
A A word is missing by mistake.
B It is a question rather than a statement.
C The "if" has been dropped and "had" moved to the front, which is the formal written form.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Third Conditional and Past Modals

GRAMMAR

One structure builds another past. The other judges the one you actually lived.

JobFormExample
A whole different pastIf + had + participle, would have + participleIf we had visited the plant, we would have seen it.
Criticism of the real pastshould have + participleWe should have asked for a reference.
A possibility that did not happencould have / might have + participleWe might have lost the client.
NegativeIf + had not + participle · would not have + participleIf the tariff had not changed, it would have worked.
Formal, without ifHad + subject + participle, ...Had we known, we would have priced it in.
Softeningin hindsight / with what we knew at the timeIn hindsight it was a judgement call.
QuestionWhat would have happened if...?What would have happened if you had visited?

Never: if I would have known · if we visited the plant, we would have seen it · we should have to ask · if had we known · it could have be worse.
