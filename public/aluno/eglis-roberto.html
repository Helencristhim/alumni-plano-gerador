Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Where Have You Been? -- Your Travels in Two Tenses

How to talk about the places you have been to, and then tell the story of one trip, the way travelers do in a hostel bar. Key words: to have been to, a backpacker, a hostel, a local, a stranger, to get lost, off the beaten track, to miss a train, jet lag, a must-see. Structure: have been for experiences with no date, and the past simple for the details of one trip.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To have been to -- to have visited a place at some time in your life
"I have been to Croatia, but I have never been to Greece."
A backpacker -- a person who travels with a backpack, usually cheaply and for a long time
"As a backpacker, I slept in hostels and took night buses."
A hostel -- a cheap place to stay, often with shared rooms, where travelers meet
"The best conversations of my trip happened in the hostel kitchen."
A local -- a person who lives in the place you are visiting
"Ask a local where to eat, not the hotel."
A stranger -- a person you do not know
"On the train, a stranger offered me half of his sandwich."
To get lost -- to not know where you are or how to find the way
"We got lost in Venice, and it was the best afternoon of the trip."
Off the beaten track -- in a place where few tourists go
"I like small towns that are off the beaten track."
To miss a train -- to arrive too late, after the train has left
"I missed the last train, so I slept at the station."
Jet lag -- the tired feeling after a long flight across time zones
"After the flight to Chicago, I had jet lag for three days."
A must-see -- a place or a thing that everybody says you have to visit
"The old town of Dubrovnik is a must-see."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Tom is from Canada, and he loves to talk about travel. “I have been to twenty countries. I have slept in train stations, and I have eaten some very strange things.” In this part, Tom does not give any dates. He is talking about his life experience, so he uses have plus the third form.

Then a woman at the bar asks him: “What was your best trip?” Now Tom tells one story, and the verbs change. “I went to Peru in 2019. I got lost in Cusco on the first night, and a local family helped me. They invited me for dinner.” This is one trip, with a date and details, so he uses the past simple.

At the end, the woman says: “I have never been to Peru. How long have you been here in Mexico?” Tom answers: “I have been here for two weeks.” He arrived two weeks ago and he is still in Mexico.

Why does Tom say "I have been to twenty countries" with no date?
A Because he is talking about his life experience, not one trip.
B Because he does not remember the dates.
C Because twenty is a big number.
Why does he say "I went to Peru in 2019" and not "I have been to Peru in 2019"?
A Because the woman asked him a question.
B Because he is telling one trip, and it has a date.
C Because Peru is far from Canada.
"I have been here for two weeks." What does it mean?
A He arrived in Mexico two weeks ago, and he is still there.
B He is going to Mexico in two weeks.
C He left Mexico two weeks ago.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Have Been or the Past Simple?

GRAMMAR

Your experience, with no date: have been. One trip, with a date or details: the past simple.

JobFormExample
Life experience, no datehave / has + third formI have been to Chile three times.
A question about experienceHave you ever + third form?Have you ever slept in an airport?
One trip, with a datepast formI went to Chile in 2022.
The details of that trippast formIt was cold, and I stayed in a hostel.
Started and still truehave been + for / sinceI have been back in Brazil since June.
Visit, not livebeen to (not been in)I have been to Croatia.

Never: I have been to Italy last year · Did you ever been to Peru? · I am in Brazil since June · I have been in Croatia (for a visit)

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Camino -- Telling a Story That People Want to Hear

How to tell the story of a long walk or a big trip so that people at the table want to hear the end. Key words: a pilgrim, to set off, a blister, a stage, exhausted, to keep going, to give up, to share, unforgettable, a turning point. Structure: the past simple for what happened, was or were plus -ing for the scene, and had plus the third form for what happened before.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A pilgrim -- a person who travels a long way to a special or holy place
"Every evening, the pilgrims ate together at one long table."
To set off -- to start a journey
"We set off at six in the morning, before the heat."
A blister -- a painful bubble on the skin, usually on your feet after a long walk
"By the fourth day, I had a blister on each foot."
A stage -- one part of a long route, usually one day of walking
"The longest stage was almost thirty kilometers."
Exhausted -- so tired that you have no energy left
"I was so exhausted that I fell asleep before dinner."
To keep going -- to continue, even when it is difficult
"My knee hurt, but I decided to keep going."
To give up -- to stop trying to do something
"On the second day, I almost gave up."
To share -- to have or use something together with other people
"We shared a room with twelve strangers."
Unforgettable -- so special that you will always remember it
"The view from the top of the hill was unforgettable."
A turning point -- the moment when something changes direction
"That walk was a turning point in my life."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Lucia is from Argentina. Two years ago, she walked the Camino from Porto to Santiago. Before the trip, she had never walked more than five kilometers, and she had never slept in a room with strangers. The past perfect, had never walked, tells us about her life before the Camino.

On the fifth day, she was walking along the coast and the sun was going down when she saw a small sign with a yellow arrow. Was walking and was going down are the scene, the background. Saw is the action that happened in the middle of it.

She followed the arrow and arrived at a tiny hostel. The other pilgrims had already cooked dinner, and they invited her to the table. “By the time I arrived in Santiago,” she says, “I had made ten new friends.”

"Before the trip, she had never walked more than five kilometers." What does it mean?
A She never walked again after the Camino.
B She walked five kilometers every day on the Camino.
C Until the Camino, five kilometers was her longest walk.
Which part of the story is the scene, the background?
A "she was walking along the coast"
B "she saw a small sign"
C "she followed the arrow"
"The other pilgrims had already cooked dinner." What happened first?
A Lucia arrived, and then they started to cook.
B They cooked dinner, and then Lucia arrived.
C They cooked together with Lucia.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Three Past Forms of a Story

GRAMMAR

What happened: the past simple. The scene: was or were plus -ing. What happened before: had plus the third form.

JobFormExample
What happenedpast formWe set off at six.
The scene, the backgroundwas / were + -ingIt was raining when we set off.
Earlier than another past momenthad + third formI had never walked that far before.
Up to a past momentby the time + past, had + third formBy the time I arrived, I had lost two kilos.
An experience in your life, no datehave + third formI have been to Spain three times.
Short form in speechI had = I’dI’d never seen so many stars.

Never: When I arrived, the group already left · I walked when it started to rain · Then I arrive and I see the cathedral · I had been to the USA (with no other past moment)

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Breaking the Ice -- From Small Talk to a Real Conversation

How to start a conversation with a stranger at a party, keep it going and find something in common. Key words: small talk, to break the ice, the host, to hit it off, to have something in common, awkward, to open up, to feel at ease, to catch someone’s name, to mingle. Structure: direct questions, follow-up questions, indirect questions, and short answers like So do I.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Small talk -- light, polite conversation about easy topics, often with people you do not know
"We made small talk about the music while we waited for drinks."
To break the ice -- to say or do something that makes people relax at the start of a conversation
"He broke the ice with a joke about the long line."
The host -- the person who invites people to a party and receives them
"I asked the host to introduce me to his friends from Chile."
To hit it off -- to like each other immediately
"We hit it off on the first night and talked until two in the morning."
To have something in common -- to share an interest, an experience or an opinion with another person
"We have a lot in common: we both love old films and long walks."
Awkward -- uncomfortable, when nobody knows what to say or do
"There was an awkward silence after his question."
To open up -- to start talking about your feelings or your life
"After an hour, she opened up and told me about her new job."
To feel at ease -- to feel relaxed and comfortable
"The host made everybody feel at ease."
To catch someone's name -- to hear and understand a person's name
"Sorry, I did not catch your name. Was it Marco?"
To mingle -- to move around a party and talk to different people
"After dinner, everybody stood up to mingle."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Paulo is at a dinner party in Madrid. He does not know anybody, so he walks to a woman near the kitchen and asks a direct question: “Do you know the host well?” She says: “Yes, we work together.” A direct question needs a helper verb: do, does, did, have, is.

Paulo does not stop there. He asks a follow-up question about her answer: “Oh, really? What do you two do?” Then he wants to know something more personal, so he makes the question softer: “Can I ask where you’re from?” In this indirect question, the order is like a normal sentence: where you are from, not where are you from.

She tells him she loves sailing. Paulo smiles: “So do I!” She adds: “But I have never sailed in the Caribbean.” “Neither have I,” he says. Two short answers, and now they have two things in common.

Why does Paulo ask "What do you two do?" after her first answer?
A Because he wants to end the conversation.
B Because a follow-up question shows he is interested.
C Because he did not hear her answer.
Which question is correct?
A "Can I ask where are you from?"
B "Can I ask where from you are?"
C "Can I ask where you are from?"
She says "I have never sailed in the Caribbean." Paulo has not sailed there either. He says:
A "Neither have I."
B "Me too."
C "So have I."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Questions That Make Friends

GRAMMAR

A direct question needs a helper verb. An indirect question keeps the order of a sentence. Short answers repeat the helper verb.

JobFormExample
Direct questionhelper verb + person + verbDo you live around here?
Follow-up questiona question about the answerOh really? How come?
Indirect questionCan I ask / Do you know + sentence orderCan I ask what you do?
I agree, positiveSo + helper verb + II love jazz. So do I.
I agree, negativeNeither + helper verb + II have never been to Asia. Neither have I.
Check, friendlysentence + tagGreat party, isn’t it?

Never: You like the music? · Where you live? · Can I ask what do you do? · I don’t like it. Me too.

Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Seen Anything Good Lately? -- Films, Series and Recommendations

How to describe a film or a series, recommend it and defend your opinion, without stopping in the middle of the sentence. Key words: the plot, a character, to be set in, a twist, gripping, overrated, to recommend, to binge-watch, the cast, a spoiler. Structure: who, which, that, where and whose to join two ideas in one sentence.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

The plot -- the story of a film, a series or a book: what happens
"The plot is simple, but the acting is brilliant."
A character -- a person in a film, a series or a book
"My favorite character is the old fisherman who never talks."
To be set in -- to happen in a certain place or time
"The film is set in Naples in the 1950s."
A twist -- a surprise in the story that changes everything
"Nobody expected the twist at the end."
Gripping -- so exciting that you cannot stop watching
"The first episode was so gripping that I watched three more."
Overrated -- less good than people say
"Everybody loved it, but I think it is overrated."
To recommend -- to tell somebody that something is good and they should try it
"Can you recommend a good series in English?"
To binge-watch -- to watch many episodes of a series one after another
"We binge-watched the whole season in one weekend."
The cast -- all the actors in a film or a series
"The cast is great, especially the two brothers."
A spoiler -- information about the story that ruins the surprise
"Do not tell me the ending. No spoilers!"

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Sofia is recommending a film to her friend. “It’s a film that everybody should see. It’s about an old man who decides to walk across his country.” With that and who, she joins two ideas in one sentence. She does not say “It is about an old man. The old man decides…”

“It’s set in a small village where nothing ever happens, and his only friend is a girl whose father owns the bar.” Where is for a place. Whose is for something that belongs to a person: the father of the girl.

Her friend asks: “Is it the one which won the prize in Venice?” “Yes! The director, who is only thirty, made it with almost no money.” In that last sentence, the part between the commas is extra information. You can take it out, and the sentence still works.

C Because the man is old.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Who, Which, That, Where, Whose

GRAMMAR

Join two ideas in one sentence. Do not repeat the person or the thing after the word.

JobFormExample
A personwho / thatan actor who never smiles
A thing or a filmwhich / thata series that I watched twice
A placewherethe town where it was filmed
Something that belongs to a personwhosea man whose wife is a pilot
Extra information, who / , which (with commas)The director, who is Italian, also wrote it.
Talking about a scenethe one where / the scene in whichIt’s the one where they cross the desert.

Never: the film what I saw · the actor that I like him · the city that he lives · a man who his wife is a pilot

TED Talk
Celeste Headlee: “10 ways to have a better conversation” (12 min)

A radio host who has talked to thousands of strangers gives ten simple rules. Rule number five is the lesson: ask open questions, and then follow what the other person says.

Tip: pick the two rules you break most often. Try one of them at your next party or with your sailing team, and tell your teacher what happened.

Watch on TED ↗
TED Talk
Julian Treasure: “How to speak so that people want to listen” (10 min)

A sound expert on why some voices make people lean in and others make them look away. The part about the music of your voice connects with the questions you practiced today.

Tip: listen to the part about pitch and pace. Then record your five party questions and listen: does your voice go down at the end of the wh- questions?

Watch on TED ↗
TED Talk
Marc Pachter: “The art of the interview” (15 min)

A museum director who interviewed famous people for years on how to get anybody to open up: the right question, at the right moment, and then silence while the other person talks.

Tip: write down the question he likes best. Use it with a real person this week, and bring the answer you got to class.

Lesson 4 — Seen Anything Good Lately?

TED Talk
Beeban Kidron: “The shared wonder of film” (17 min)

A film director on why watching a film together, in one room, can change how people see the world. This is Liam’s side of the argument, with real stories from schools and cinemas.

Tip: watch it with English subtitles. Then describe one film she mentions in one sentence with who, which or where.

Watch on TED ↗
Video
BBC Learning English: “Vocabulary Live! Movies”

A teacher from BBC Learning English with the words people really use to talk about films: the plot, the cast, a twist, a must-see. Clear British English, not too fast.

Tip: write down three new expressions and use them in your sixty-second recommendation this week.

Watch on YouTube ↗
Video
BBC Learning English: “Vocabulary Live: film awards”

The language of prizes, winners and the films everybody talks about after the awards. Useful for the next dinner where somebody asks you: seen anything good lately?

Tip: after the video, choose one film that won a prize and that you have seen. Say who is in it and why it won, in two long sentences.
