The Distance Between a Fact and a Verdict

Why an investigation is written the way it is written

Reading

It Would Appear That

It Would Appear That

The finding of an investigation is not a verdict, and the language it is written in exists to keep the two apart. A report may state that the sequence of events is established; it will rarely state that a person was negligent. Negligence is a legal conclusion, and it belongs to a court.

Consider three sentences about the same fact. The valve was closed. It would appear that the valve was closed before the transfer began. The witness stated that he had closed the valve. Only the first is a finding. The second is a hedge, and the reader is being told that the evidence supports it without compelling it. The third is testimony, which is what a person said. A report that mixes the three is useless to everybody who reads it afterwards.

Hedged language carries weight in the other direction as well. More often than not, a root cause is recorded as a contributing factor first, and is promoted only once the corrective action has been agreed. Investigators would rather understate a cause they cannot fully evidence than overstate one they can. The convention is deliberate: an overstated finding is attacked in court, and when it falls it takes the rest of the report down with it.

The advice given to young investigators has not changed in thirty years. If two explanations fit the evidence equally, you had better write both. If one of them can be tested, you may as well test it before you write anything at all. And if the recommendation you are about to make would have prevented this occurrence but not the next one, it seems that you have not yet found the root cause.

Chapter 4: The Code

How Far the Writer Stands from the Claim

It would appear that... / more often than not / would rather, had better, may as well

Grammar Discovery

States, or Suggests?

"It would appear that the valve was closed before the transfer began."

"More often than not, a root cause is recorded as a contributing factor first."

"Investigators would rather understate a cause they cannot evidence than overstate one they can."

"If two explanations fit the evidence equally, you had better write both, and you may as well test one first."

Take the hedge off the first sentence and read it again. The words are almost identical -- so what exactly did the writer stop being responsible for?

FormWhat it doesExample
it would appear / it seems thathedge: the evidence supports the claim without compelling itIt would appear that the valve was closed.
to some extent / more often than nota claim that carries its own limit inside itMore often than not, a root cause is called a contributing factor first.
would rather X than Ya stated preference between two available coursesInvestigators would rather understate a cause than overstate one.
had better + bare infinitivestrong advice with a consequence behind it. It is not the past of haveyou had better write both
may as well + bare infinitivethere is no reason not to, and some reason toyou may as well test it first
The reading trapa hedge is not a softer way of saying the same thing: it changes what the text CLAIMS. The text states and the text implies are two different questions, and the hedge is the only mark on the page that tells them apart.

Every wrong option in a comprehension question is built by moving a claim one step closer to the reader or one step further away. Hedges are where that move happens, and they are three words long.

One Question at a Time

1 / 6

It would appear that the valve was closed. Does the report state that it was closed?

  • No. It says the evidence supports it without compelling it.
  • An option beginning the text states that is wrong for this sentence.

Say, in exam register, that they are careful not to blame more than they can prove.

  • The attribution of cause is conservative.
  • They would rather understate a cause than overstate one.

What is the difference between a witness statement and a finding?

  • Testimony is what a person said. A finding is what the inquiry concluded from the evidence.
  • A report that mixes them is useless afterwards.

You had better write both. Is this the past of have?

  • No. Had better is present advice, with a consequence behind it.
  • It is followed by the bare infinitive: write, not to write.

When does a contributing factor become a root cause, according to the text?

  • Once the corrective action has been agreed.
  • Until then it is deliberately recorded at the lower level.

An option says: the text implies that negligence was involved. What would you look for on the page?

  • A hedge. It seems, it would appear, to some extent.
  • If the page says it flatly, the option should say states, not implies.

Listening 1

Thirty Years of Writing Carefully

A senior investigator on why she never writes the strong version. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she remember better than anything she got right, and why?
The two findings that were overturned in thirty years. In each case the fact was correct and the sentence was too strong.
2. What happens, in her account, the moment you write that a person was negligent?
You stop describing an event and start making a legal claim, and somebody will come and test it.
3. What is the second reason she gives for hedging?
A hedged finding is easier for the company to accept, and a finding nobody accepts changes nothing at all.
Listening 2

Before You Listen

“There is one question I ask about every recommendation I read, and it takes about four seconds.”
This is the first line. What do you think this audio is going to be about?
0:000:00
Listening 2

Would Have Prevented This One

A safety consultant on the test that tells you the root cause is wrong. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What is the first question he asks, and why is the answer almost always yes?
Whether the recommendation would have prevented the occurrence in front of him. Almost always yes, because people write recommendations backwards from the accident.
2. What is the second question, and what does a negative answer mean?
Whether it would have prevented the next one, which will not look like this one. If not, a contributing factor has been called a root cause.
3. What does he say he would rather do?
Send a report back three times rather than approve a recommendation that only works on an accident that has already happened.
