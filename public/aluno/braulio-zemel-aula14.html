Cinema and Culture

Everybody at the table saw the same film. What separates them is what they can say about it.

Chapter 1: The Film

What Was the Last Film That Stayed With You?

Not the best one you have seen. The one you were still arguing with two days later. What was it, and what exactly would you not let go of?

Where You Start

Three Things That Are Not an Opinion

I liked it

True, complete, and it ends the conversation in four words.

It was very good

Nobody can agree or disagree with this, so nobody answers.

It was boring

A verdict on you, not on the film. And impossible to argue with.

None of the three is wrong. They are simply finished. An English film conversation runs on opinions that are unfinished on purpose — specific enough to be interesting, and open enough that somebody can push back. That is the whole skill, and today it has a name.

Chapter 3: The Code

It Strikes Me That

How an educated opinion sounds in English

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"It strikes me that the ending is deliberately ambiguous."

"One could argue that the film is really about his father."

"To some extent, the whole genre is built on that one idea."

"There is a sense that the director does not trust the audience."

None of these four speakers is unsure. Each of them has a firm opinion and says it. So what are the blue openings actually for?

FormWhat it doesExample
It strikes me that...Presents a firm opinion as a personal impression, which invites an answer instead of ending the exchange."It strikes me that the ending is ambiguous."
One could argue that...Puts an argument on the table without owning it yet, so it can be tested before you commit to it."One could argue that it is his best film."
To some extent...Concedes a limit, which makes everything after it sound more confident, not less."To some extent, the genre is built on that idea."
There is a sense that...Reports a feeling that is in the room rather than only in you."There is a sense that he does not trust us."
I tend to think that...Marks a position as a habit of mind rather than a verdict, and leaves you room to move."I tend to think that the first hour is the film."

Hedging is not weakness and it is not politeness. A flat claim closes a conversation; a hedged one opens it, which is why educated English speakers hedge all evening and still say exactly what they mean.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"I think that maybe possibly the film is a little bit good."
"It strikes me that the film is a great deal better than its reviews."
"In my opinion, for me, I believe the ending is ambiguous."
"I tend to think the ending is deliberately ambiguous."
"One could to argue that it is his best film."
"One could argue that it is his best film."

Three separate traps. The first is stacking: one hedge is a signal, four in a row is a person who does not want to be quoted. The second is the same problem in the first person — in my opinion, for me and I believe all do the identical job, so pick one. The third is grammar and not style: could, like every modal, is followed by the bare verb. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Why Nobody in English Says I Liked It

Why Nobody in English Says That They Liked It

There is a moment at every dinner where somebody asks what you thought of something, and the honest answer is short. You liked it. It was good. In Portuguese that answer can carry a whole opinion, because the tone and the face do the rest of the work. In English it carries almost nothing, and the conversation stops there, politely, and moves on to somebody else.

The reason is not vocabulary, and it is not confidence. It is that an English opinion is expected to arrive unfinished. It strikes me that the ending is deliberately ambiguous. One could argue that the portrayal of the mother is the only honest thing in the film. To some extent the whole genre is built on that single idea. Each of those is a firm opinion, stated by somebody who means it, and each of them hands the other person somewhere to go.

This catches people out in a way they do not expect. A speaker who is careful and precise in his own language often produces the flattest sentences in his second one, because the machinery he uses to be careful has not been translated yet. He knows exactly what he thinks about the tension in a film and the political undertone running under it, and what comes out of his mouth is that it was very good. The gap is not between what he thinks and what he knows. It is between what he thinks and what he has the tools to hand over.

The machinery itself is small. Five openings, and the habit of naming one specific thing rather than the whole film. It strikes me that, one could argue that, to some extent, there is a sense that, I tend to think that. None of them means the speaker is unsure, and anybody who reads them that way has misread the room. They mean the speaker is still talking, which is the only thing an English dinner table is really asking of anybody.

Comprehension

About Claire

1. What does she ask him first, and how long has he had?
She asks what the film did to him -- and he has had two days since the screening.
2. What does she say happened among the critics that year?
They had the same argument all year, and half of them lost it.
3. What is her objection to the director?
That there is a sense he does not trust his audience -- he explains the last scene twice.
4. How does she push him to say more, in her second turn?
She says only two words, go on, and then asks smaller how -- she makes him do the work.

Before We Start Arguing

A film club host opening the evening. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he say the rule of the club is, and what is the one thing that is not allowed?
That any opinion is allowed as long as it comes with one reason -- the only thing not allowed is saying it was good and stopping there.
2. What does he say about the ending, and how does he present it?
That it strikes him as deliberately ambiguous -- and he presents it as his impression rather than as a fact, so the room can disagree.
3. What does he warn about the second half?
That it appears slow on a first viewing and that people who leave at that point miss what the whole film has been building.

Four Words Instead of Two

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she say is wrong with saying a film was good?
Nothing is wrong with it -- it is simply finished, and a finished sentence gives the other person nothing to answer.
2. What is the formula she gives?
One hedge, one specific thing in the film, and one reason -- in that order, and it takes about six seconds.
3. What does she say happens to people who use it?
They are treated as somebody worth talking to, regardless of how good their English is.
