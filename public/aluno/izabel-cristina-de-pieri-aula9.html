The Ledger of Roads Not Taken

The Ledger of Roads Not Taken

Every executive keeps two ledgers. The first records what the company actually did, and it is the one the auditors read: the contracts signed, the plants opened, the rivals answered on price. (1) It exists only in the conditional, and every entry in it begins with the word if.

Ask a chief executive about the offer she turned down, and you will hear a remarkably confident account of what would have followed. If we took that contract, she explains, the overhead would swallow the margin inside a year; if we held our nerve on price, the rival would blink first. (2) Nobody has run the experiment, nobody has collected the data, and yet the verdict is delivered with the calm of someone reading a balance sheet.

Psychologists call this counterfactual thinking, and they have found it to be far less reliable than it feels. When we imagine the road we did not take, we quietly hold everything else still: the market behaves, the competitors sleep, and nobody on the team resigns. (3) A hypothetical is not a forecast. It is a story told from a single point of view, and the person telling it is almost never a neutral witness.

None of this means the second ledger should be closed. (4) A leader who could not say what she would do if her largest client left tomorrow would be a leader without a plan. The value of the hypothetical lies not in its accuracy but in the discipline it imposes, because it forces a decision to be defended before it has to be made. The danger begins only when the imagined road is remembered as though it had been traveled.

Grammar Discovery

Same Odds, Different Choice

1a"If Jonas cuts his price, we will lose the tender."
1b"If Jonas cut his price, we would lose the tender."
2a"If you sign today, I will hold the lead time."
2b"If you signed today, I would hold the lead time."
Jonas can cut his price. You can sign today. Both are perfectly possible. So the speaker is not telling you what is likely. She is telling you something else.
  • 1Which version sounds like a warning, and which sounds like an idea being floated?
  • 2Which one is easier to walk back from tomorrow morning?
  • 3You are negotiating with someone you do not trust yet. Which do you use?
+ Open after she has answered all three

The second conditional is not only for impossible things. English speakers reach for it to create distance: it turns a commitment into a proposal, and a threat into a scenario. That is why it is the natural grammar of a negotiation.

First conditional = I am telling you what happens.  Second conditional = I am putting an idea on the table without standing behind it yet.

Common Mistake

The Three That Cost You Credibility

1 Never after "if"

If we would take the contract…  →  If we took the contract…

2 The result carries the distance

If they made a concession, I will sign.  →  If they made a concession, I would sign.

3 "Were" is the boardroom form

If I was in Priya's position…  →  If I were in Priya's position…

You will hear native speakers say "if I was" in a bar. You will not hear it in a board pack. Choose the register on purpose.
Precision

One Condition, Three Commitments

"If we took half the volume, we… keep the plant free."

would
Result · near certainty

"…we would keep the plant free." This is what happens. She is describing the consequence, not weighing it.

could
Option · ability

"…we could keep the plant free." It becomes available to us. Whether we do it is still open.

might
Possibility · low commitment

"…we might keep the plant free." Perhaps. She is refusing to promise, on purpose.

Drill: your teacher names the level of commitment, you produce the sentence. "Certain." · "Just an option." · "Do not pin me down."

Three people, one contract, and a contradiction

Dialogue

Before Anyone Opens the Numbers

A
Before we look at a single number, Izabel, I want your instinct. If the Meridian contract landed on your desk this afternoon, in full, what would you do with it?
I
My instinct would be to say no, and then spend a week arguing myself out of it. If we took it in full, we would be a different company by June, and not necessarily a better one.
A
That is a strong claim for something you have not costed. What would have to be true for you to say yes?
I
Two things. If they moved on delivery, and if I could phase the hiring across two quarters, I would take it. Without either concession, the whole risk would sit on our side of the table.
A
And if Jonas Lindqvist undercut you while you were still deciding?
I
I would let him. If we matched his price, we would win the contract and lose the margin that makes it worth having. I would rather compete on the one thing he cannot copy quickly.
A
Which is?
I
Our lead time. It is the only real leverage we have, so if I gave it away cheaply, I would have nothing left to trade.
Comprehension

What Was Andy Doing?

1. What does Andy ask for before they look at any numbers?
Her instinct, her first reaction to the contract arriving in full.
2. How does Andy challenge her first answer?
He points out that it is a strong claim about something she has not costed, and asks what would have to be true for her to say yes.
3. Which competitor scenario does Andy introduce?
Jonas Lindqvist undercutting her while she is still deciding.
4. Andy's last words are only two: "Which is?" What is he doing with them?
Refusing to let her stop at a vague claim. He forces her to name the leverage instead of gesturing at it, which is how she ends up committing to the lead time.

Three Accounts, One Contradiction

Andrea Kowalski · CFO · voicemail

"Izabel, it's Andrea. I ran the numbers you asked for, and I want you to have them before the board call. If we took the Meridian contract in full, we would need a second line by March, and the overhead would rise by about forty percent in the first year. If we took half, the overhead would barely move, but we would give up roughly two point one million in revenue. One more thing. Our lead time is currently nine days. Jonas Lindqvist's people are quoting fourteen. If I were you, I would treat those nine days as the whole negotiation. Call me back."

Rashid Haddad · Operations · voicemail

"Izabel, Rashid here. I have seen Andrea's numbers and I do not read them the same way. Her forty percent assumes we hire ahead of the volume, and I would not do that. If we phased the hiring over two quarters, the overhead would rise closer to twenty-five. But here is my real worry. Everyone keeps saying our lead time is the bargaining chip. If we took the full contract, our lead time would go from nine days to about sixteen, which is worse than Jonas Lindqvist's fourteen. So the thing we are planning to bargain with is the exact thing the contract would destroy. I would want that on the table before anyone signs."

Read both again with your eyes, not your ears. Find every figure, and every sentence that begins with if.
  • 1They do not disagree about the facts. What exactly do they disagree about?
  • 2Rashid says "the thing we are planning to bargain with is the exact thing the contract would destroy." What is that thing?
  • 3In the dialogue, you called the lead time your only real leverage. Is that still true after Rashid's call?
  • 4Which of the two is making the same argument as the article you read?
  • 5You have to answer the buyer this week. What would you do now, and what changed your mind?
+ Open at the very end, after question 5
  1. They disagree about an assumption, not a fact: whether the hiring happens ahead of the volume, or across two quarters.
  2. The nine-day lead time. Andrea calls it the whole negotiation; Rashid points out that winning the contract pushes it to sixteen.
  3. No. It only stays a bargaining chip if the contract stays small. Taking it in full would spend the very leverage she was planning to trade with.
  4. Rashid. He is doing exactly what the article demands: refusing to hold one variable still while another moves.
  5. Any defended answer is correct here. An undefended one is not.

Wrap-Up

What I Learned

  • I can describe an unreal situation and its result without slipping into "will".
  • I can explain why I chose a hypothetical instead of a plain prediction.
  • I can separate "would", "could" and "might" on purpose, not by accident.
  • I can soften a hard claim without losing the content of it.
  • I can find the sentence that fills a gap by following the cohesion, not the topic.
  • I can take notes on figures from a recording I only hear once.
  • I can spot where two colleagues disagree about an assumption rather than a fact.
  • I can defend a decision under pressure and say what would change my mind.
