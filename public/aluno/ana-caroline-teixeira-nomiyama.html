Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Have To, Need To, Must x Don’t Have To, Mustn’t

GRAMMAR

Use have to, need to or must when something is necessary (must has no to after it). Use don’t have to or don’t need to when something is not necessary: you can do it if you want. Use mustn’t when something is not allowed. In American English, people often say can’t for this.

JobFormExample
Necessary (a rule)have to / has to + verbWe have to submit the evidence by Friday.
Necessary (neutral)need to / needs to + verbThe client needs to sign the power of attorney.
Necessary (strong, formal)must + verb, without toAll documents must have a certified translation.
Not necessarydon’t / doesn’t have to, don’t need toWe don’t have to pay a filing fee.
Not allowedmustn’t + verb (in the U.S., people often say can’t)You mustn’t miss the deadline.
The trapno to after must; and mustn’t is not the same as don’t have toNot “We must to sign.” Say We must sign.

Never: We must to submit... · She have to sign... · I don’t must... · Do we need pay...?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous x Simple Past

GRAMMAR

Use was / were + -ing for an action in progress at a moment in the past (the background of the scene). Use the simple past for a short action that cuts in, or for actions one after the other. While usually goes with the long action; when usually goes with the short one. Verbs of state (know, like, want) stay in the simple past.

JobFormExample
Action in progress in the pastI / he / she / it + was + verb-ing; you / we / they + were + verb-ingI was reading in bed.
Short action that cuts insimple past...when the lights went out.
While + the long actionwhile + past continuous, simple pastWhile she was cleaning, she found a manuscript.
One action after the othersimple past, simple pastWhen I finished the book, I went to sleep.
Questions and negativesWas / Were + subject + verb-ing? · wasn’t / weren’t + verb-ingWhat were you doing when you found out?
The trapverbs of state (know, like, want, believe) do not take -ingNot I was knowing the end. Say I knew the end.

Never: I was read... · I was knowing... · While I read, the phone was ringing · When I was finding out the twist...

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past Perfect

GRAMMAR

When you tell a story in the past and one action happened BEFORE another, use had + past participle for the earlier action, and the simple past for the later one. Already, before and by the time often go with it. If the order is already clear (and then...), the simple past is enough.

Signal wordsalready, before, by the time, when, after, never ... beforeBy the time we filed, he had already left.
Negativehadn’t + past participleShe hadn’t seen him since the divorce.

The trapif the order is clear (one action after the other), the simple past is enoughHe signed the divorce and then transferred the house.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions

GRAMMAR

To sound polite, start with an opening: Could you tell me...? / Do you know...? Then use the order of a normal sentence: wh-word + subject + verb, with no do / does / did. For yes / no questions, use if: I was wondering if...

JobFormExample
Wh- questionsCould you tell me / Do you know + wh-word + subject + verb“Could you tell me where the cellar is?”
Yes / no questionsI was wondering if / Do you know if + subject + verb“Do you know if the winery is open on Sunday?”
No do / does / didthe verb takes its normal form: starts, ages, opened“Could you tell me when the harvest starts?”
With bethe subject goes before is / are“Do you know how old the barrels are?”
The punctuationCould you tell me / Do you know ends with ?; I was wondering if ends with a period“I was wondering if we could visit the cellar.”
The trapafter the opening, never question orderNot Could you tell me where is the cellar? Say where the cellar is.

Never: Could you tell me where is...? · Do you know when does...? · I was wondering if could we... · Could you tell me what means...?
