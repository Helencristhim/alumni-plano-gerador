Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Have To, Need To, Must x Don’t Have To, Mustn’t

GRAMMAR

Use have to, need to or must when something is necessary (must has no to after it). Use don’t have to or don’t need to when something is not necessary: you can do it if you want. Use mustn’t when something is not allowed. In American English, people often say can’t for this.

JobFormExample
Necessary (a rule)have to / has to + verbWe have to submit the evidence by Friday.
Necessary (neutral)need to / needs to + verbThe client needs to sign the power of attorney.
Necessary (strong, formal)must + verb, without toAll documents must have a certified translation.
Not necessarydon’t / doesn’t have to, don’t need toWe don’t have to pay a filing fee.
Not allowedmustn’t + verb (in the U.S., people often say can’t)You mustn’t miss the deadline.
The trapno to after must; and mustn’t is not the same as don’t have toNot “We must to sign.” Say We must sign.

Never: We must to submit... · She have to sign... · I don’t must... · Do we need pay...?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous x Simple Past

GRAMMAR

Use was / were + -ing for an action in progress at a moment in the past (the background of the scene). Use the simple past for a short action that cuts in, or for actions one after the other. While usually goes with the long action; when usually goes with the short one. Verbs of state (know, like, want) stay in the simple past.

JobFormExample
Action in progress in the pastI / he / she / it + was + verb-ing; you / we / they + were + verb-ingI was reading in bed.
Short action that cuts insimple past...when the lights went out.
While + the long actionwhile + past continuous, simple pastWhile she was cleaning, she found a manuscript.
One action after the othersimple past, simple pastWhen I finished the book, I went to sleep.
Questions and negativesWas / Were + subject + verb-ing? · wasn’t / weren’t + verb-ingWhat were you doing when you found out?
The trapverbs of state (know, like, want, believe) do not take -ingNot I was knowing the end. Say I knew the end.

Never: I was read... · I was knowing... · While I read, the phone was ringing · When I was finding out the twist...

Despite + noun / -ingcontrast, but NO subject + verb after itDespite the deadline / Despite receiving the complaint, he did not answer.
However,contrast between two sentences: period, However, commaHe has assets. However, he is hiding them.
Therefore,result between two sentences, formal: perfect for a motion or a briefThe money can disappear. Therefore, we request a hearing.
soresult inside one sentence, less formal: good for meetingsHe did not answer, so we filed a motion.
The trapdespite never takes of, and although never takes butNot Despite of the deadline or Although he owes, but he.... Say Despite the deadline / Although he owes, he...

Never: Despite of the deadline · Although he owes, but he... · Despite he was served · We requested for a hearing

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to say that the debtor did not pay, and this was a surprise. You say:
A "Although he promised to pay our client in six months, but he never paid anything."
B "Although he promised to pay, he never paid."
C "Despite he promised to pay, he never paid."
You are writing the last sentence of a motion. You write:
A "So that, we are requesting for an urgent hearing with the judge as soon as possible."
B "However, we request an urgent hearing."
C "Therefore, we request an urgent hearing."
The client asks what happens if the judge says no. You say:
A "If the judge doesn’t grant the motion, we can appeal."
B "If the judge grants it, we can appeal."
C "If the judge grants the motion, we appeal."
A colleague asks who the defendant is in your case. You say:
A "The defendant is the bank, because the bank started the lawsuit against the company in Florida."
B "The defendant is the company that didn’t pay."
C "The defendant is the bank."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Imagine you are explaining a (fictional) case to a U.S. judge. In one minute: give the facts, say what the problem is with although or however, explain the risk, and finish with your request using therefore.
