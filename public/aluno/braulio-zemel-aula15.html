Chapter 1: What Is Coming

What Will Be Different in Five Years?

One thing about the world and one thing about your own week. Not what you hope for. What you actually expect, and how sure you are that you are right.

Where You Start

The Problem Is Not the Tense

Will happen

A flat prediction. Nobody asks how sure you are, because you sounded certain.

Maybe will happen

Now nobody knows how sure you are, including you.

Will probably have happened

Certainty and shape, in three words, and it survives a bad phone line.

You already have the future tenses. What you do not yet have is the second layer: the small words that say how likely a thing is and what shape it has when it arrives. English carries that in the words. Portuguese usually carries it in the voice, which is exactly why it disappears when you switch languages.

Chapter 3: The Code

Will Probably Have

Two words that say how sure you are and what shape it has

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"By the time we land in Tokyo, I will probably have been studying Japanese for two years."

"I imagine I will still be getting used to retirement at the end of this year."

"She might well have changed her mind by the time we have that conversation."

"In twenty years, a city like Sao Paulo could well be dealing with a very different problem."

Every one of these four is a prediction, and every one of them puts an extra word between the modal and the verb. What is that extra word buying the speaker?

FormWhat it doesExample
will probably have + past participleHow likely, plus an action finished by a point in the future."I will probably have finished the book by June."
will still be + verb-ingAn action in progress at a future moment. Still quietly admits it is taking longer than planned."I will still be getting used to it in December."
might well have + past participleA strong possibility about something already completed by then. Well makes might heavier, not lighter."She might well have changed her mind."
could well be + verb-ingA strong possibility about an action in progress at a future point."They could well be dealing with that by 2040."
should have + past participle, by...An expectation that rests on a plan rather than on certainty."We should have confirmed it by next month."

English stacks two decisions into one verb phrase: a modal that says how likely, and a form that says what shape the action has. Portuguese usually carries the same nuance in the tone of voice, which is why it disappears the moment you switch languages.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"By June I will probably finished the course."
"By June I will probably have finished the course."
"She might well changed her mind by then."
"She might well have changed her mind by then."
"In twenty years the city could well deals with a different problem."
"In twenty years the city could well be dealing with a different problem."

One rule underneath all three. After a modal the verb is bare: never a past tense, never an -s. What changes after that is the shape you choose — have + participle for something finished by then, be + -ing for something still going on at that moment. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Dialogue

Nobody Pays Me to Be Right

D
Thirty years of long-range planning and I have never once been paid to be right. I was paid to be useful before anybody knew.
B
That is a distinction I would not have made. What is the difference in practice?
D
A prediction is a single answer. A forecast is a range, with the reasoning attached, so somebody can argue with it.
B
So when people ask you what is going to happen, what do you actually say?
D
I give them two scenarios and I say which one I would bet on. Then I say what would have to be true for me to be wrong.
B
By that standard I have never made a forecast in my life. I have only ever made predictions and defended them.
D
Most people have. And there is one more habit worth stealing. Never predict a date and a size in the same sentence.
B
Because one of the two will probably be wrong, and it takes the other one down with it.
Comprehension

About Diane

1. What does she say she was paid for, and what was she not paid for?
She was paid to be useful before anybody knew -- never to be right.
2. How does she distinguish a forecast from a prediction?
A prediction is a single answer; a forecast is a range with the reasoning attached, so that somebody can argue with it.
3. What does she actually say when people ask her what will happen?
She gives two scenarios, says which one she would bet on, and then says what would have to be true for her to be wrong.
4. What is the last habit she recommends, and why does it matter?
Never predict a date and a size in the same sentence -- because one of the two will probably be wrong and take the other down with it.

Listening 2

How Sure Are You, Exactly?

A short method clip. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he say is missing when a learner predicts something in English?
Not the tense -- the degree of certainty, because in Portuguese the voice carries it and in English the words have to.
2. What are the three levels he gives, from weakest to strongest?
It could be, it might well be, and it will probably be -- three degrees, three sets of words.
3. Why does he say this matters more in English than at home?
Because a written or a badly connected sentence keeps the words and loses the voice, so anything carried by tone alone disappears.
