Read for the Main Idea

Two Futures That Could Not Both Happen

Beatriz Nunes spent the whole of last year preparing for two futures that could not both happen. In the morning she studied for a national exam that eighty thousand people take for four hundred places. In the evening she wrote application essays for universities four thousand miles away. Her friends called it hedging. She called it arithmetic. “If I only prepare for one outcome,” she says, “I am not being confident. I am just refusing to look at the likelihood.”

The word she uses for the second plan is contingency, and she is precise about what it does not mean. A contingency is not a lesser thing you would settle for. It is a second real plan, prepared to the same standard, which exists because nobody controls which door opens. “If I get the place here, I will stay, and I will be happy. In the event that I do not, I will go, and I will also be happy. That is the whole design. Neither scenario is the sad one.”

What she found hardest was not the work. It was the second kind of sentence, the one that begins with if I weren’t. Saying out loud a life she is not living, in front of people who love her, felt to her like giving up. It took months to see it differently. “If I weren’t doing this, I would probably be working and studying at night,” she says now, without flinching. “Saying it does not make it happen. It only means I have looked at it, and I can tell you whether it is realistic.”

She got the place. She is in her first semester, and she still keeps the other file on her desktop, unopened. When somebody asks whether the essays were wasted, she is quick. “It all depends on what you think I was buying. I was not buying a second university. I was buying the ability to walk into that exam without being afraid of the result, and that is what let me pull it off.” Before any of it, she spent a month on what she calls the feasibility question: not what would be wonderful, but what she could actually envision herself doing for six years.

Chapter 4: The Code

The Plan and the Picture

One step back, and will becomes would

Grammar Discovery

A Plan, or a Picture?

"If I get the place here, I will stay, and I will be happy."

"If I do not get it, I will go, and I will also be happy."

"If I weren’t doing this, I would probably be working and studying at night."

"If I could choose any city in the world, I would live in the United States."

Both colors talk about a future. The red ones are futures that could actually happen this year. The green ones are futures you are only looking at. Notice what changes: the verb after if steps back one, and will becomes would.

JobFormExample
Real, and likelyif + present simple … will + bare verbIf I get the place, I will stay.
Real, but less certain… may · might · could + bare verbIf I pass, I might move to another city.
Imagined, and not happening nowif + past simple … would + bare verbIf I weren’t studying, I would be working.
Imagined, with the verb to beif + were, for every personIf I were you, I would apply to both.
Formal, in writing onlyin the event that + present simpleIn the event that the result is negative, the file stays open.
Word orderthe if half can come second, and then no commaI will stay if I get the place.
The trapnever will inside the if halfNot If I will pass. Say If I pass.

In one line: step the verb back one and will becomes would. That is the whole difference between a plan and a picture.

One Question at a Time

1 / 6

Say what will happen if Beatriz gets the place, in one sentence.

  • If she gets the place, she will stay and she will be happy.
  • Real and likely, so present simple after if, and will after the comma.

Now say what she would do if she could choose any city in the world.

  • If she could choose any city, she would probably study abroad.
  • Imagined, so the verb steps back and will becomes would.

Correct this out loud: If I will pass, I will move.

  • If I pass, I will move.
  • Never will inside the if half. This is the number one error from Portuguese.

Give advice with If I were you, about anything you like.

  • If I were you, I would apply to both.
  • Were for every person, not was, when the sentence is imagined.

  • If I get in, I will move closer to the university.
  • Real and likely: present simple after if.

  • If I were not studying for this, I would probably be training every day.
  • Imagined, so were, and would plus the bare verb after the comma.

Listening 2 · A Podcast

The Monday Afterwards

A researcher on why people refuse to name a second plan. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What are the first two reasons people give him?
That it feels like bad luck, as if naming the outcome could cause it, and that it looks like weakness in front of other people.
2. Why does he say the third reason, not having time, is not the real one?
Because the second plan would have cost about four per cent of the hours they actually spent.
3. What question does he say to ask instead, and why?
Not whether you will fail, but what you would do on the Monday afterwards — because that question is answerable, and once it is answered it stops taking up room.
