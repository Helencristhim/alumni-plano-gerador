Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every year, thousands of applicants send the same documents to the same competitive universities. Almost all of them have good grades, because good grades are what gets the file opened. What happens after that is a conversation about a person who does not exist yet: the student you would be if you were there. Admissions are not reading about your past. They are reading a prediction.

English keeps a separate structure for exactly this. When the situation is not real yet, the if half steps one tense backwards and the result half takes would. If I got a place there, I would major in law. Nobody has offered you anything; you are describing a world that does not exist, and the grammar says so before the sentence is finished. That is why it is called unreal: not because it is impossible, but because it is not true right now.

Change one word and you change what you are claiming. If I get in, I will study law is a different sentence, and it is not wrong — it treats the place as a real possibility with a real deadline attached. Both are correct English. Which one you choose tells the reader how close you think you are. An eleven-year-old writing about a university abroad is in the first sentence, and there is nothing weak about that; a candidate three weeks from a deadline is in the second.

One detail worth taking seriously, because it is the mark of somebody who reads. After I and he and she, careful writing keeps the old form were: if I were you, if he were older. You will hear if I was in speech every day, from native speakers, and nobody will correct them. In an essay that a committee reads, write were. The rule that matters more than either of them is the one nobody teaches: after if, never put would. If I would get in is not a possible sentence, and it is the single most common thing that goes wrong here.

1. According to the text, what does the second conditional describe?
A A plan that is already agreed and only needs a date.
B A situation that is not real now, and the result that would follow from it.
C Something that happened once in the past and finished.
2. Why does the text say if I get in, I will study law is a different sentence?
A Because it is wrong English.
B Because it treats the place as a real possibility, not as an imagined one.
C Because it can only be used about other people.
3. What does the text say about were in if I were you?
A It survives from an older form, and educated writing keeps it after I.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Open any airline manual and count the sentences that begin with a person. There are almost none. The route is filed. The fuel is calculated. The crew is briefed. The aircraft is cleared for takeoff. Nobody is hiding anything and nobody is being polite. The sentences are built that way because in a procedure the thing that has to happen matters more than whoever happens to be doing it that morning.

The machine is simple once you see it. Take the ordinary sentence, move the object to the front, put the right form of be in the middle, and finish with the past participle. Somebody files the route becomes the route is filed. The tense does not disappear; it moves into be. Present is is filed, past is was filed, and one step further back is had been filed. The participle never changes.

You can still say who did it, and English gives you one word for that: by. The fuel was calculated by a second person. Notice that the sentence is only worth writing that way because the second person is the whole point. If the agent adds nothing, leave it out, and that is why the checklist is read out loud is complete as it stands. Adding by the crew would tell the reader something already obvious.

Two things go wrong for almost everybody. The first is the missing be: the route filed yesterday is not a sentence, it is a label. The second is the participle, because English keeps a pile of irregular ones. Read is spelt like the infinitive and pronounced like red. Flown, taken, given, chosen, written are the ones you will meet first in this world. There is no rule for them and there is no shortcut: you meet them one at a time and you keep them.

1. According to the text, why does aviation English use the passive so much?
A Because it is more polite than naming the person.
B Because the procedure matters more than whoever carried it out.
C Because pilots are not allowed to say I on the radio.
2. What does the text say the agent tells you, when it is included with by?
A Who did it, and it is only added when that information changes something.
B When the action happened.
C Whether the action was finished or not.
3. Which sentence does the text call the natural one for a procedure?
A Somebody checked the fuel twice.
B The fuel was checked twice.
C They have been checking the fuel.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of asking the same thing, and the second one is longer on purpose. Where did you study? is the short one, and it is perfectly correct with a friend. Could you tell me where you studied? is the long one, and it is what people use in a room where they do not yet know each other. The extra words are not decoration. They are the distance the language puts between two strangers.

What happens inside is the part worth learning. When a question is buried inside a longer sentence, it stops behaving like a question. The subject goes back in front of the verb, so where did you study becomes where you studied. The little helper verb, do or does or did, disappears completely, and the tense it was carrying moves into the main verb. Why did you choose it becomes why you chose it, with the past now living in chose.

Some questions have no question word at all. Do you volunteer? starts with the helper verb and nothing else. When a question like that is buried, English lends it a word to hold the place: if or whether. Could I ask whether you volunteer? The two are interchangeable in speech, and whether is the one that survives in writing.

The last thing is punctuation, and almost everybody gets it wrong once. The question mark belongs to the OUTER sentence, not to the buried one. Could you tell me where the office is? ends in a question mark because could you tell me is itself a question. I would like to know where the office is. ends in a full stop, because nobody asked anything: that sentence is a statement about what you would like. Read your own sentence back, find the first three words, and let them decide the mark.

1. According to the text, what are the extra words in the long form for?
A They make the question easier to understand.
B They are the distance the language puts between two strangers.
C They show that the speaker is not sure of the answer.
2. What does the text say happens to the tense when did disappears?
A It is lost, and the sentence becomes present.
B It moves into the main verb, so choose becomes chose.
C It moves to the beginning of the sentence.
3. What decides whether the sentence ends in a question mark?
A The buried question, because that is the real question.
B Whether the speaker expects an answer.
C The outer sentence, and the text says to look at its first three words.

Vocabulary

A supplier -- the person or shop you buy your materials from
“The supplier who sells me the flour raised his price in March.”
A margin -- what is left after you take the cost out of the price

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has one small machine for saying WHICH thing you mean. You take a noun, and you hang a clause on it that narrows it down. The customers who come back every fortnight. Not all the customers, only those. That clause is doing real work, and if you delete it the sentence stops telling you who it is about. It takes no commas, and it is called a defining clause because it defines.

The same machine does a second job, and this is where the commas come in. My neighbour David, who buys two boxes every time, has never asked the price. There is only one neighbour called David, so nothing needs narrowing down. The clause is a comment you could delete without losing the point. Two commas mark it, one on each side, and it is called non-defining. Miss the second comma and the sentence hangs open.

The words are simple once the job is clear. Who for people, which for things, whose for something belonging to somebody, and where for places. That is the useful one and also the dangerous one: it can replace who or which, but only in the defining kind. After a comma, that is simply wrong, and no native speaker writes it.

One last shortcut. When the relative word is the OBJECT of its clause and there are no commas, you can leave it out entirely. The cakes that I made on Friday and the cakes I made on Friday are both correct. You cannot do it when the relative word is the subject: the customers come back is a different sentence from the customers who come back, and dropping who there breaks it. Read the sentence out loud. Where your voice pauses, a comma probably belongs, and where it does not pause, one probably does not.

2. Why does the text say the David sentence takes commas?
A Because the sentence is long.
B Because there is only one neighbour called David, so nothing needs narrowing down.
C Because the clause comes in the middle of the sentence.
3. When does the text say you can leave the relative word out?

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of talking about the past, and the difference is not how long ago it was. It is whether the time you are naming is closed or still open. I started the stall in 2024. The year 2024 is finished, the door is shut, and the past simple is the only form that fits. I have run it since 2024. That period began in 2024 and has not ended, because I still have the stall. The door is open, and English marks that with have plus the past participle.

The signal words are the fastest way in. Ago, last year, in March, yesterday, when I was nine all close the door and demand the past simple. Since, for, ever, never, so far, already, until now keep it open and demand the present perfect. Learn the words and the tense usually follows on its own, which is why they are worth memorising before the rule is.

There is a second use that has nothing to do with time. I have never asked my parents for money. Nobody is naming a moment here; the point is the experience up to now, and the door is open because my life is not over. The same with a result that still counts: I have learnt to write the number down matters because I am still doing it. Say I learnt it in March and you have turned it into a fact about March.

Two mistakes are almost universal among Portuguese speakers, and they are opposites. The first is putting have with a closed time: I have started the stall last year is wrong, because last year has already shut the door. The second is putting the past simple with since: I run it since 2024 is wrong, because since holds the door open and needs have. If you learn to catch only these two, most of your writing is already fixed.

1. According to the text, what decides which of the two tenses you use?
A How long ago the event happened.
B Whether the time you are naming is closed or still open.
C Whether the sentence is about you or about somebody else.
2. Why does the text say "I have never asked my parents for money" uses the present perfect?
A Because it happened a long time ago.
B Because nobody is naming a moment: it is the experience up to now, and life is not over.
C Because the sentence is negative.
3. What are the two mistakes the text calls almost universal, and how does it describe them?
