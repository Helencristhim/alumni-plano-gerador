Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Edinburgh, Take Two -- I’ve Been / I Went

Planning a spring trip to Edinburgh and talking about the places you have been to. Key words: a blizzard, closed to visitors, a guided tour, to book in advance, the off-season, crowded, breathtaking, a highlight, to make the most of, a bucket list. Structure: present perfect x simple past.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A blizzard -- a very bad snowstorm with strong wind
"We couldn’t go out because of the blizzard."
Closed to visitors -- not open for people to come in and see it
"The castle was closed to visitors that day."
A guided tour -- a visit where an expert shows you a place and explains it
"We took a guided tour of the old town."
To book in advance -- to reserve something some time before, not at the last minute
"In the summer, you need to book in advance."
The off-season -- the time of the year when few tourists visit a place
"Hotels are cheaper in the off-season."
Crowded -- full of people
"The streets were very crowded at New Year."
Breathtaking -- extremely beautiful; so beautiful that it surprises you
"The view from the hill was breathtaking."
A highlight -- the best or most exciting part of something
"The highlight of the trip was the concert."
To make the most of -- to use a situation or a time as well as you can
"We only had two days, so we made the most of them."
A bucket list -- a list of things you want to do or see in your life
"Machu Picchu was on my bucket list for years."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Mirela has been to many places: New York, Paris, London, Amsterdam and Machu Picchu, which was on her bucket list for years. But one trip was different. She went to Edinburgh for New Year, and there was a blizzard. The castle was closed to visitors, and she saw almost nothing.

Now she wants to go back in the spring. It is the off-season, so the city is not crowded, and the parks are green. She has never taken a guided tour there, so this time she wants to book one in advance.

Her friend Ana went to Edinburgh last April. “The view from the castle is breathtaking,” she says. “It was the highlight of my trip.”

1. Why does the text say “She went to Edinburgh for New Year” and not “She has been”?
A Because she went with her family, and has been is only for trips that you take alone.
B Because the sentence tells a specific time and a specific story.
C Because the trip was bad, and we use has been only for good trips.
2. What does “She has never taken a guided tour there” mean?
A She took a guided tour last year, but she did not like it very much.
B She does not like guided tours.
C Until now, in her whole life, she has not taken a guided tour in Edinburgh.
3. Why is the spring a good time for her trip?
A Because it is the off-season: the city is not crowded and the parks are green.
B Because the castle is free for visitors in the spring.
C Because there are no tourists at all in Edinburgh in April and May.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect x Simple Past

GRAMMAR

Use have / has + past participle for your life until now, without a date. Use the simple past when you say when, or when you tell one specific story.

JobFormExample
Life experience, no datehave / has + past participle“I’ve been to Peru.”
Asking about experienceHave you ever + past participle?“Have you ever been to Scotland?”
Never, until nowhave never + past participle“I’ve never seen the castle.”
A specific time or storysimple past“I went there in 2019. It snowed.”
Asking for detailsWhen / Where / How did you...?“When did you go?”
The trapa past time never goes with have + participleNot “I have been there in 2019.” Say I went there in 2019.

Never: I have been there in 2019. · Did you ever see...? · When have you gone? · I’ve never went

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Just One More Episode -- Avoid Watching, Decide to Stop

Why series are so hard to stop, and how to talk about your viewing habits. Key words: to binge-watch, addictive, to get hooked on, a cliffhanger, a plot twist, a sitcom, a documentary, dubbed, subtitles, to rewatch. Structure: verb + -ing x verb + to.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To binge-watch -- to watch many episodes of a series one after another
"We binge-watched the whole season on Saturday."
Addictive -- so enjoyable that it is very hard to stop
"This documentary is really addictive."
To get hooked on -- to start liking something so much that you want more and more
"I got hooked on the series after one episode."
A cliffhanger -- an exciting end of an episode that makes you need to know what happens next
"Every episode ends with a cliffhanger."
A plot twist -- a surprising change in the story
"Nobody expected the plot twist in the last episode."
A sitcom -- a funny series about the same characters in everyday situations
"Friends is my favorite sitcom."
A documentary -- a film or series about real people and real events
"I’m watching a documentary about a famous family."
Dubbed -- with the voices changed into another language
"I usually watch series dubbed into Portuguese."
Subtitles -- the words of the dialogue written at the bottom of the screen
"Try English subtitles: you read and listen at the same time."
To rewatch -- to watch something again
"I never rewatch a series, except Friends."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Mirela loves series, but she avoids starting them during the week, because they are addictive. When she gets hooked on a story, she wants to finish it, and every episode ends with a cliffhanger. She only watches one series at a time, and she never rewatches anything.

Well, almost anything. She enjoys watching old episodes of two sitcoms: Friends and The Big Bang Theory. She can start any episode, and it always makes sense. But she stopped watching another famous series, because it was too long and the story stopped making sense.

Today she watches everything dubbed, with Portuguese subtitles. Her new plan? She has decided to try English audio with English subtitles, one episode at a time.

1. Why does the text say “she avoids starting” and not “she avoids to start”?
A Because the verb avoid is always followed by a verb with -ing.
B Because the sentence is in the past, and the past always takes the -ing form after a verb.
C Because starting is a noun here, and nouns never come after to.
2. What does “she stopped watching another famous series” mean?
A She paused for a short time so that she could watch that series.
B She watched the whole series again, from the first episode to the last one.
C She does not watch that series anymore.
3. What is her new plan?
A To stop watching series.
B To watch with English audio and English subtitles, one episode at a time.
C To rewatch all the sitcoms she knows, dubbed and with Portuguese subtitles, every night.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Verb + -ing x Verb + to

GRAMMAR

Some verbs are followed by another verb with -ing (enjoy, avoid, keep, finish, mind). Others are followed by to + verb (want, decide, need, plan, hope). After a preposition, always use -ing.

JobFormExample
Verb + -ingenjoy, avoid, finish, keep, mind + -ingI avoid watching series at night.
Verb + towant, decide, need, plan, hope, would like + toI decided to stop after one episode.
After a prepositionof, about, at, before, after + -ingI’m tired of waiting for the next season.
Stop + -ingstop doing something: you do not do it anymoreI stopped watching This Is Us.
Stop + tostop to do something: you pause in order to do itI stopped to make some popcorn.
The trapavoid and enjoy never take toNot I avoid to watch. Say I avoid watching.

Never: I avoid to watch · I enjoy to watch · I decided stop · I’m tired of watch

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

If I Lived in New York -- If I Lived..., I Would...

Imagining a life in New York and talking about rent, neighborhoods and the cost of living. Key words: a neighborhood, the cost of living, to afford, the rent, a commute, lively, to settle in, homesick, a studio apartment, a borough. Structure: the second conditional.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A neighborhood -- a part of a city where people live, with its own character
"Brooklyn has very different neighborhoods."
The cost of living -- how much money you need for rent, food and transport in a place
"The cost of living in New York is very high."
To afford -- to have enough money to pay for something
"I couldn’t afford an apartment in Manhattan."
The rent -- the money you pay every month to live in a place that is not yours
"The rent is three thousand dollars a month."
A commute -- the trip you make every day between your home and your work
"My commute to São Paulo takes more than an hour."
Lively -- full of people, energy and things happening
"The neighborhood is lively at night."
To settle in -- to start to feel comfortable in a new home or a new place
"It took me a few months to settle in."
Homesick -- sad because you are far from your home and your family
"I was homesick during my first winter abroad."
A studio apartment -- a small apartment with one main room for living and sleeping
"She rents a studio apartment near the park."
A borough -- one of the five main parts of New York City
"Brooklyn and Queens are two of the five boroughs."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Mirela has been to New York many times, and she always says the same thing: “If I lived here, I would be happy.” But what would that life look like? She would probably choose a calm neighborhood in Brooklyn, one of the five boroughs, close to a park.

The problem is money. The cost of living is very high, and the rent for a studio apartment is almost three thousand dollars. If she had a job there, maybe she could afford it. Her commute would be short: twenty-five minutes by subway.

“Would you be homesick?” a friend asks. “Maybe at first,” she answers. “But the city is so lively that I think I would settle in fast.”

1. Does Mirela live in New York?
A No. The text imagines what her life would be like if she lived there.
B Yes, she moved there last year and she lives in a studio apartment in Brooklyn.
C Yes, but only during the summer vacation.
2. Why does the text say “If she had a job there” and not “If she has a job there”?
A Because the job was in the past, many years ago, before she went to New York for the first time.
B Because had is more polite than has.
C Because it is an imagined situation in the present, not a real plan.
3. What does she think about being homesick?
A She would never be homesick, because she does not like her city very much.
B Maybe at first, but she would settle in fast.
C She would be homesick for many years.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Second Conditional

GRAMMAR

To talk about an imagined situation in the present, use if + simple past, and would + verb for the result. For advice, use if I were you, I’d...

JobFormExample
The imagined situationIf + simple pastIf I lived in New York...
The imagined resultwould / ’d + verb...I would rent a studio.
The questionWould you + verb ... if ...?Would you live there if you could?
AdviceIf I were you, I’d + verbIf I were you, I’d visit in the fall.
Orderthe if part can come first or secondI’d be happy if I lived there.
The trapnever would in the if partNot If I would live there. Say If I lived there.

Never: If I would live there · If I had time, I will go · If I was you (for advice) · Would you go if you can?
