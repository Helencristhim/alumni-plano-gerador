Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

My name is Fernando Bottura and I have spent most of my career in advertising groups. I have been at the helm of finance for fourteen years, and I have worked in this industry for over twenty. Over that time I have led two consolidations, and I have never worked in a company without an international parent.

The dates tell a different part of the story. I joined a large advertising group in 2017 and I left at the end of last year. Before that, from 2011 to 2016, I reported directly to a CFO in New York, and I used English every single day. In my first year at the group we laid the groundwork for a shared services model, and in 2019 I spearheaded the consolidation of the back office across nine agencies.

Right now I am in transition. So far this year I have had three interview processes, and two weeks ago I did one for a CFO role in a Belgian group. I have not signed anything yet, because I am looking for the right role rather than the fastest one.

1. Why does the text say "I have worked in this industry for over twenty years" but "I joined a large advertising group in 2017"?
A Because the twenty years are still going on today, while joining in 2017 is a single finished event with a date.
B Because "have worked" and "joined" mean exactly the same thing in a career story.
C Because you use the present perfect for long periods and the past simple for short ones.
2. The text says "two weeks ago I did one" and not "I have done one". Why?
A Because interviews are always described in the past simple.
B Because "two weeks ago" is a closed point in time, and a closed time marker takes the past simple.
C Because the sentence already has another verb in the present.
3. Which words in the text signal that "have had three interview processes" is the right choice?
A "two weeks ago" and "in 2019"
B "so far this year", because the year is not over yet
C "right now", because it refers to the present moment only
4. In an interview, what does "I have led two consolidations" claim that "I led two consolidations in 2019" does not?
A That the experience is part of who he is today — a track record, not a dated episode.
B That the consolidations were bigger and more complex.
C Nothing. The two sentences carry exactly the same weight.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- A Closed Chapter or One Still Open

GRAMMAR

One question, two answers. That is the whole decision in this lesson — and it decides half of every answer you give about your career.

FormUseExample
Past simple
I joined / we closed		A closed chapter: a date, a company or a period that is over.I joined the group in 2017.
We closed the project in 2021.
Present perfect
I have worked		A chapter still open, or experience with no date attached to it.I have worked in agency finance for twenty years.
I have led two consolidations.
NegativeThe past simple uses did not. The present perfect uses have not or has not.I did not stay after the change.
I have not signed anything yet.
QuestionDid for the past simple, Have or Has for the present perfect.Did you report to Brussels?
Have you ever led a turnaround?
Time markersin 2017, last year, two weeks ago, when I was at the group point to the past simple. for, since, ever, never, already, yet, so far point to the present perfect. The sentence usually tells you before you have to decide.
for vs sincefor takes a length of time: for nine years, for over a decade. since takes a starting point: since 2017, since I left the group.
Why it matters in an interviewThe present perfect is what turns a list of jobs into a track record. "I have managed the finances of nine agencies" is a claim about you today. "I managed them in 2019" is a story about the past. Same fact, very different answer.

When you are not sure, choose the past simple with a date and keep going. A finished answer with the wrong tense is worth far more than a perfect tense you never say out loud.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. Do not translate — the example sentence is the meaning. Every word here describes a job somebody gave you, not a result you produced.

Mandate -- what an organization formally asks a leader to deliver
"My mandate was to bring nine separate finance teams under one reporting line."
Remit -- the area of work a role is responsible for
"Treasury was not in my remit until the second year."

Lean -- deliberately small in people and cost
"It is a lean function, so the CFO signs off and also builds the model."

"I took the headcount from forty-one to thirty-two without losing a close date."
To ramp up -- to increase something quickly and on purpose
"We ramped up the reporting cycle from quarterly to monthly in one year."
To scale back -- to reduce the size or the ambition of something
"We scaled back the project to the two entities that actually needed it."

"I had the board's backing on paper and none of it in the room."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

My mandate in that group was simple to say and hard to do: bring nine agencies onto one reporting line and make the month close predictable. What I inherited was three legacy systems that did not talk to each other and a headcount built for a group half that size. Treasury was not in my remit in year one; it came in later, when we ramped up the shared services model.

I waited eight months before I asked for more people, because I wanted the full picture first. If I had asked in month one, with the numbers I already had, I would have got the backing while the board was still listening. By the time I asked, the year was gone. If the board had said yes then, we would not have scaled back the project at all.

With hindsight, that was the turning point of my whole tenure there, and I missed it. The function was lean by design and stayed that way, which was survivable, but slower than it needed to be. If I had inherited a clean structure, the first year would have looked completely different — and I would have spent it on the business instead of on the plumbing.

1. In "If I had asked in month one, I would have got the backing", did he ask in month one, and did he get the backing then?
A No to both. The whole sentence describes something that did not happen.
B Yes to both. He asked and he got it.
C He asked, but the backing arrived later than he wanted.
2. Why is the verb in the "if" half "had asked" and not "would have asked"?
A Because both halves can take "would have" and this one simply chose not to.
B Because the condition always takes "had" plus a past participle, and only the result takes "would have".
C Because "ask" is an irregular verb and cannot follow "would".
3. What does "I inherited three legacy systems" tell a panel that "we had system problems" does not?
A That the problem existed before he arrived, so the panel can judge the result against the right starting point.
B That the systems were more expensive than average.
C Nothing. The two sentences carry the same information.
4. Which order does the text use to describe the role, and why does it work in an interview?
A Numbers, then mandate, so the strongest evidence comes first.
B Mandate, then what he inherited, then what happened — so the listener knows what good looked like before any figure appears.
C Strictly chronological, month by month, from the first day to the last.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Road You Did Not Take

GRAMMAR

One structure for the question every panel asks and almost nobody prepares: what would you have done differently?

PartFormExample
The conditionif + had + past participleIf I had asked in month one...
The resultwould have + past participle...I would have got the backing.
Negativehad not / would not haveIf I had not waited, we would not have lost the year.
QuestionWhat would you have + past participleWhat would you have done differently?
Order and commaEither half can come first. With the condition first you need a comma: If I had asked, I would have got it. With the result first you do not: I would have got it if I had asked.
In speechBoth halves contract to the same sound: If I'd asked, I'd have got it. The two 'd are identical, and only the verb after them tells you which is which. This is why the form is easy to read and hard to hear.
Why it matters in an interviewA panel does not ask this to find a weakness. It asks because a leader who has never revised a decision has either not made enough of them or is not being honest. The answer that works has three parts — what you did, the condition, the result — and then stops. One turning point is an answer; three is a confession.

Never put "would" in the "if" half. It is the single most common mistake with this structure, and it is audible immediately.

Decoding a long question, buying three seconds, and answering the thing that was actually asked. Structure: modal perfects for saying what somebody must have meant.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. Do not translate — the example sentence is the meaning. Half of these you will hear from a panel; half of them you will say back to it.

To probe -- to keep asking in order to get past the first answer
"He probed until I gave him a number instead of a principle."
A follow-up -- a second question that goes deeper into the same answer
"The follow-up is where the interview really starts."
To unpack -- to explain something complicated one part at a time
"Let me unpack that, because there are two decisions inside it."
To drill down -- to move from the general point to the specific detail
"Can we drill down into the second quarter for a moment?"

To touch on -- to mention something briefly, without developing it
"She touched on the reporting line and moved straight to the team."
The gist -- the main idea of what somebody said

To paraphrase -- to say the same thing again in different words

"I am not sure what you are driving at — could you put it another way?"

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Looking back at that interview, the recruiter must have wanted something more concrete than what I gave him. He kept probing, and a person only probes when the first answer has not landed. He might have been testing how I handle a follow-up, or he may have been checking whether I could drill down from the principle to the number. I still do not know which, and I do not need to.

What I do know is that it cannot have been the vocabulary. I know those words. The problem came earlier: I did not get the gist of the question, because I was listening to every word instead of listening for the instruction. I should have asked him to repeat the last part, and I could have paraphrased it back to him in one line. Either one would have taken four seconds.

Now I do it differently. When a question is long, I say what I think the person is driving at, and I ask them to bear with me while I unpack it. If there are two questions inside one, I answer the second and circle back to the first. Nobody has ever objected. As the search consultant put it, she has never once written down that a candidate asked for a repeat.

1. "He must have wanted something more concrete." How certain is the speaker?
A Almost certain. It is a strong conclusion drawn from the evidence.
B It is an obligation: the recruiter was required to want that.
C It is a possibility, no stronger than "might have wanted".
2. Why does the text say "it cannot have been the vocabulary" rather than "it was not the vocabulary"?
A Because "cannot have been" is simply a more formal way of saying the same thing.
B Because it is a conclusion he is drawing, not a fact he witnessed — near certainty, arrived at by reasoning.
C Because the event is still happening now.
3. What is the difference between "I should have asked him to repeat" and "I could have asked him to repeat"?
A "Should have" says it was the right thing to do and he did not do it. "Could have" says it was simply available to him.
B "Should have" is about the past and "could have" is about the future.
C There is no difference in meaning, only in register.
4. According to the text, where did the problem actually start?
A In the answer, because the words did not come.
B Before the answer: he listened to every word instead of listening for the instruction, so he missed the gist.
C In the recruiter's accent, which was too fast to follow.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Talking About a Room You Have Already Left

GRAMMAR

Modal + have + past participle. One shape, four different degrees of certainty about something that is already finished.

FormMeaningExample
must have + participleAlmost certain that it happenedHe must have wanted a number.
might / may / could havePossible, and nothing stronger is claimedShe might have been testing me.
cannot have + participleAlmost certain that it did NOT happenIt cannot have been the vocabulary.
should have + participleIt was the right thing, and it did not happenI should have asked her to repeat.
ContinuousSomething in progress at that momentHe must have been looking for the order.
Question and negativeCould he have meant the group figure? — I should not have started with the context.
Never "had"The modal is always followed by have, never by had, and what comes after is the participle: gone, not went.
Why it matters in an interviewThis is how you discuss a room you have already left without pretending to certainty you do not have. "They must have been looking for a turnaround profile" sounds like analysis. "They did not like me" sounds like a wound. The same event, and a completely different candidate.

In speech, all of these contract to the same sound: must've, might've, should've. That is why they are easy to read and hard to hear — and why the listening task in this lesson is built on them.

Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Spotlight

GRAMMAR

English lets you take any part of a sentence and move it to the front. The part you move becomes the point — and everything else quietly becomes background.

PatternPlain versionWith the spotlight

It was ... thatThe sequence saved the margin.It was the sequence that saved the margin.
The thing that ... is / wasThe reporting line changed everything.The thing that changed everything was the reporting line.
All ... is / wasI needed six weeks of visibility.All I needed was six weeks of visibility.
AgreementAfter what the verb is singular, even when the thing that follows is plural: What I bring is twenty years, never are.
ConnectorAfter it was the connector is that (or who for people), never what. It was the sequence that saved it, never what saved it.

One spotlight marks one takeaway, and an answer only has one takeaway.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Inversion, and Why It Buys You Time

GRAMMAR

When a sentence opens with a negative or limiting phrase, English flips the subject and the auxiliary. It is emphasis — and it is also four words that commit you to nothing while your brain finds the rest.

Opening phrasePlain versionInverted
Not only ...We did not only protect the margin.Not only did we protect the margin, we also closed earlier.
At no point ...The board never asked me to reverse it.At no point did the board ask me to reverse it.
Never ...I have never reported to that kind of board.Never have I reported to that kind of board.
Only when ...I decided to leave when the line moved.Only when the line moved did I decide to leave.
Rarely / Seldom ...We rarely closed later than day five.Rarely did we close later than day five.
The mechanicsAfter the opening phrase the sentence behaves exactly like a QUESTION: auxiliary, then subject, then the main verb in its base form. did we protect, have I reported, did the board ask. If there is no auxiliary in the plain sentence, you supply do, does or did.
Why it belongs in this lesson"Not only did we" is four words that mean nothing on their own. You say them at full speed while the rest of the sentence assembles itself — and to the room it sounds like emphasis, not like hesitation. Use one per answer. Two in a row stops sounding senior and starts sounding rehearsed.

The single most common mistake is forgetting the flip: "Not only we protected" instead of "Not only did we protect". It is audible immediately to a native ear.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Two Facts, One Sentence

GRAMMAR

A relative clause hangs a second fact on a noun without starting a new sentence. The comma decides whether that fact is essential or extra — and in speech the comma is a pause.

TypeWhat it doesExample
Defining
no commas		Tells you WHICH one. Remove it and the sentence loses its meaning.The entity that was losing money went first.
Non-defining
commas		Adds a fact about something already identified. Remove it and the sentence still works.The group, which owned nine agencies, reported to Brussels.
that vs whichDefining clauses take that or which. Non-defining clauses take which and never that. For people, who works in both.
whosePossession, for people and for things: the controller whose team did the same reconciliation four times a month.
Quantifier + of whichThe most useful pattern for a group: four subsidiaries, two of which had never been integrated. Also most of whom, none of which, all of whom.
No second subjectThe relative pronoun IS the subject. The entity that was losing money, never the entity that it was losing money — a direct import from Portuguese.
In speechYou cannot hear a comma, but you can hear a pause. A non-defining clause is spoken between two small pauses, on a slightly lower pitch. If you do not pause, the listener hears a defining clause and the meaning changes.

With this structure a whole org chart fits into four sentences. Without it, it takes twelve — and the listener is gone by the fifth.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two thousand nineteen is the year I get asked about. We went into it with a gross margin of about thirty one per cent and an overhead base built for a group twice the size it had been two years earlier. In February we lost our largest account, roughly a fifth of the top line.

I remember telling the board we would not chase revenue to replace it, and I remember the silence afterwards. We tried cutting discretionary spend first, which bought us almost nothing, so we stopped doing that and went on to rebuild the overhead base itself. Protecting the margin meant reducing that base by roughly a fifth, and it meant telling people, one at a time, before the market told them.

By the fourth quarter the margin was thirty four and a half, on a smaller top line. Cash conversion went from seventy four per cent to ninety one. And we took no write-offs at all that year, which in a year like that is the number I am actually proud of. I regret not telling the board about the account two weeks earlier, when I first knew, and I have said so.

1. "We tried cutting discretionary spend." Did they cut it?
A Yes — they did it as an experiment, and it did not achieve much.
B No — they attempted it and were unable to do it.
C The sentence does not say either way.
2. What would change if the text said "we stopped to do that"?
A Nothing — the two forms are interchangeable after "stop".
B It would mean they interrupted something else in order to do it — the opposite of ending the activity.
C It would make the sentence more formal but keep the same meaning.
3. "I remember telling the board" versus "I remembered to tell the board". What is the difference?
A The first is a memory of something he did. The second is about carrying out an obligation he had not to forget.
B The first is informal and the second is formal.
C The first is about the present and the second about the past.
4. Revenue fell by a fifth and the margin rose. What does the text say made that possible?
A Cutting discretionary spend, which delivered most of the improvement.
B Rebuilding the overhead base itself, by roughly a fifth.
C Replacing the lost account with two smaller ones.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- When the Form Decides the Fact

GRAMMAR

Most verbs take either the -ing form or the to form and nothing changes. With these six, the choice changes what actually happened — which is why a finance panel notices.

Verb+ -ing+ to
stopYou ended the activity.
We stopped reporting monthly.		You interrupted something else in order to do it.
We stopped to report.
remember / forgetThe memory of a past event.
I remember telling the board.		The obligation, and whether you carried it out.
I remembered to tell the board.
tryYou did it, as an experiment.
We tried cutting spend.		You attempted something difficult.
We tried to cut spend.
meanIt has this consequence.
It meant cutting forty roles.		You intended it.
I meant to cut forty roles.
regretYou are sorry about a past action.
I regret telling them so late.		You are about to give bad news.
I regret to tell you...
go onYou continued the same thing.
We went on reporting weekly.		You moved to the next thing.
We went on to rebuild the base.
The principleThe -ing form points at the activity itself. The to form points at a purpose or an intention. Everything else follows from that, including the cases you have never been taught.

With these six verbs, the wrong form does not sound wrong — it sounds like a different fact. That is a much more expensive mistake in an interview than an accent.
