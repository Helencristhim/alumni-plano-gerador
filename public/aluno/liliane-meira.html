Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Where the Work Actually Is -- Explaining Research as Practice

A curator walks into your studio, finds no finished pieces and asks where the work is. Key words: practice-based research, a body of work, an inquiry, a premise, to sit with a question, a strand, to unfold, an outcome, field notes, to underpin, tentative, to frame something. Structure: present perfect simple and present perfect continuous.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Practice-based research -- research where making the work is the method itself, not a report written about it afterwards
"Practice-based research is hard to explain to people who expect an object at the end."
A body of work -- everything an artist has made over the years, seen as one continuous project
"My body of work has been growing around a single question since 2015."
An inquiry -- a question you keep asking over years, rather than a problem you solve once
"This is an inquiry, not a project with a delivery date."
A premise -- the idea a whole project stands on, accepted at the start and tested by everything that follows
"The premise is that illness produces a kind of knowledge medicine has no room for."
To sit with a question -- to keep a question open for a long time instead of forcing an answer out of it
"I have been sitting with this question for ten years, and I am in no hurry."
A strand -- one line of thinking inside a larger project, running beside the others
"The photographs are one strand. The recorded voices are another."
To unfold -- to develop slowly, with each stage revealing the next one
"The project unfolds. I cannot plan the ending in advance."
An outcome -- what a piece of research produces, which is not always an object
"The outcome is that somebody else can enter the question now."
Field notes -- the writing a researcher keeps while observing, before anything has been made
"My field notes are not preparation. They are already part of the work."
To underpin -- to be the foundation that holds an argument or a project up
"One idea underpins everything I have made in the last ten years."
Tentative -- not final; offered as a first attempt that may still change
"That is a tentative title, and I am comfortable with that."
To frame something -- to present an idea in a way that shows how it should be read
"How you frame the work decides how a curator reads it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An artist opens her studio to a curator who has come a long way to see it. There is nothing on the walls. What she does is practice-based research, and she has been working on the same inquiry since 2015: that illness produces a form of knowledge clinical language cannot hold. That idea underpins everything in the room, and it is the premise the whole project stands on.

The curator asks what has changed in ten years. The premise has not changed at all. The strands have. She started with photographs; for the last three years she has been recording voices and filling notebooks, and those field notes are not preparation for the work, they are the work. Nothing has been finished in the usual sense, because the project unfolds instead of ending.

Then comes the question a board always asks: what is the outcome? She has given the same answer three times this year, and she gives it again. The outcome is that somebody else can now enter the question. It is a tentative answer, and she would rather offer a tentative one than a tidy one. How she chooses to frame that answer, she knows, decides how the curator reads the whole body of work.

1. "She has been working on the same inquiry since 2015." What does the -ing form add here?
A That the work was interrupted and started again.
B That the activity started in 2015 and is still running now.
C That the work was finished in 2015.
2. Why is it "the premise has not changed" and not "has not been changing"?
A Because the sentence is about a result up to now, not about an activity in progress.
B Because negative sentences never take the -ing form.
C Because the premise belongs to the past and not to the present.
3. "She has given the same answer three times this year." Why not "has been giving"?
A Because the year has finished.
B Because give is a state verb.
C Because the sentence counts completed occasions, and how many never takes the -ing form.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect Simple and Continuous

GRAMMAR

One tense, two jobs: the years you have spent, and what those years produced.

JobFormExample
The activity, still runninghave / has been + verb-ingI have been researching it since 2015.
The result, already countablehave / has + past participleI have written three essays.
How longusually the -ing formHow long have you been working on it?
How manynever the -ing formThe premise has changed twice.
Negativehave / has not + participle (+ yet)I have not published anything yet.
State verbsknow, believe, own: no -ingI have known his work for years.
Short answerYes, I have. / No, not yet.Have you published it? Not yet.

Never: I research this since 2015 · I am researching this since 2015 · I have been writing three essays · How long time are you working on it? · I have not wrote it yet.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Words That Carry Suffering -- Reading Narrative Medicine

A dense page on why the story a patient tells counts as evidence. Key words: an illness narrative, a caregiver, suffering, testimony, to bear witness, a bedside, to alleviate, empathy, chronic, a diagnosis, stigma, to dismiss. Structure: relative clauses, defining and non-defining.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An illness narrative -- the story a patient tells about their own illness, in their own words
"An illness narrative rarely begins on the day of the diagnosis."
A caregiver -- the person who looks after somebody who is ill, often for years and without pay
"The caregiver is usually standing in the corridor while the decision is made."
Suffering -- what a person goes through, which is wider than pain and much harder to measure
"Pain has a scale from one to ten. Suffering does not."
Testimony -- an account somebody gives of what they lived through, offered so that others hear it
"Testimony is not a courtesy. It is an instrument."
To bear witness -- to stay with somebody's account and confirm that it happened
"Half of what a clinician can offer is to bear witness."
A bedside -- the place beside a patient's bed, where the real conversation happens
"Nothing that matters is decided at the bedside in under a minute."
To alleviate -- to make pain or difficulty less severe, without removing it
"Nothing here alleviates the pressure clinicians work under."
Empathy -- the capacity to feel what another person feels, from inside their situation
"Empathy is a skill that can be trained, not a personality trait."
Chronic -- lasting for years, with no expectation of a cure
"A chronic condition does not present one moment to be diagnosed."
A diagnosis -- the name a doctor gives to a condition after examining the evidence
"The diagnosis arrived four years after the first appointment."
Stigma -- the shame a society attaches to a condition, which the patient ends up carrying too
"The stigma is often heavier than the symptoms."
To dismiss -- to treat somebody's account as unimportant and not worth listening to
"A patient whose testimony is dismissed stops offering one."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A condition that lasts for years is called chronic, and it changes more than a body. It reorganizes a household. The person who takes on that reorganization is the caregiver, and the hours they give are almost never recorded anywhere.

Narrative medicine, which grew out of clinical practice rather than out of literary theory, treats the illness narrative as evidence. Arthur Kleinman, whose work is now read far outside medicine, asks a different question at the bedside: not what is the matter with you, but what matters to you. The account a patient gives in answer is the one any treatment will have to work inside.

None of this alleviates the pressure clinicians work under. But a patient whose testimony is dismissed stops offering one, and the silence that follows is not the same as an absence of information. Empathy, in this view, is not a personality trait: it is a skill that can be trained, and part of it is simply the willingness to bear witness to what somebody says.

1. "A condition that lasts for years is called chronic." Why is there no comma before that?
A Because the clause says WHICH condition we mean. Remove it and the sentence loses its meaning.
B Because that never takes a comma in any sentence in English.
C Because the sentence is in the present simple.
2. "Kleinman, whose work is now read far outside medicine, asks a different question." What does whose do here?
A It asks a question about who Kleinman is.
B It shows possession: the work belongs to Kleinman, and the information is extra, so it sits between commas.
C It replaces the word because.
3. "Narrative medicine, which grew out of clinical practice, treats the narrative as evidence." Could which be replaced by that?
A Yes, which and that are always interchangeable.
B Yes, because the subject is a thing and not a person.
C No. After a comma, English uses which, who or whose, never that.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

The sentence inside the sentence. Two commas change everything.

TypeFormExample
Defining -- says WHICH oneno commas; that / which / who / whoseA condition that lasts for years is chronic.
Non-defining -- adds extratwo commas; which / who / whose, never thatKleinman, whose work I read, is an anthropologist.
People and thingswho for people, which for thingsthe clinician who listened; a field which grew
whosepossession, for people and thingsa patient whose testimony is dismissed
Dropping the pronounonly when it is the object, and only if definingthe story (that) the patient lives
where and whenfor places and timesthe bedside where the conversation happens

Never: the clinician which examined her · Kleinman, that wrote the book · a condition who lasts for years · a patient whose the testimony · the book that I told you about it.

Complementary Activities

Optional extras to enjoy outside class. Mark each one as done after you watch or listen to it.

Lesson 1 — Where the Work Actually Is

Talk
Taryn Simon -- "The stories behind the bloodlines" (TED, 17 min)

An artist describing four years of research whose outcome is an archive rather than a single object. She gives, in seventeen minutes, exactly the account you were building today: the premise, the method, the strands, and what she still cannot resolve. Her English is slow, precise and rich in the vocabulary of this lesson.

Tip: open the transcript beside the video and mark every present perfect she uses. Then write the one sentence in which she states her premise, and compare it with yours.

Film
Art21, Extended Play -- Stephanie Syjuco: "Making Time"

A short film in which an artist walks through a project while it is still unfinished, in her own studio, with the notes and the false starts visible. This is what a studio visit sounds like from the inside, and it is the register you will need when a curator is standing in your own room.

Tip: watch it twice. The second time, stop after each sentence in which she explains why she is doing something, and say the same idea about one of your own strands.

Watch on Art21 ↗
Podcast
A brush with... Danh Vo (The Art Newspaper)

Ben Luke asks artists the questions you are learning to answer: what the work is asking, where it came from, and what art is for. Danh Vo builds his work out of documents and objects that arrive from somewhere else, so the whole conversation runs on the language of research, sources and long projects.

Tip: listen to the first fifteen minutes only, and write down three expressions he uses to say that something is still in progress. Bring them to the next class.

Listen on The Art Newspaper ↗

Lesson 2 — The Words That Carry Suffering

Talk
Rita Charon -- "Honoring the stories of illness" (TEDxAtlanta, 18 min)

The physician who founded narrative medicine at Columbia, explaining in her own words the argument you read today: that close reading and close listening are the same skill, and that a patient's account is clinical information. Slow, deliberate speech, and the vocabulary of this lesson in almost every sentence.

Tip: turn on the English subtitles and write down every relative clause she uses in the first five minutes. There will be more than you expect.

Podcast
The Nocturnists -- "Fly Away with Ann Schrooten"

A short episode in which a mother tells one night of the fifteen years she spent caring for her son. It is the caregiver's side of what you heard in class, told without a single technical word, and it shows how much a testimony can carry when nobody interrupts it.

Tip: listen once with your eyes closed, then read the transcript on the page and mark the three sentences you would quote if you were writing about it.

Tip: you do not have to finish it. Take the first twenty minutes, then say out loud, in English, one thing you would ask him if you were in that room.
