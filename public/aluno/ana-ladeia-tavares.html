Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

What Brought Us Here -- Opening a Mediation

Two sides sit down and somebody has to say, out loud and in order, what actually happened. Key words: a dispute, a mediator, a party, to represent somebody, a claim, a settlement, common ground, a deadlock, to walk away, an opening statement, in good faith, to reach an agreement. Structure: past simple and present perfect.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A dispute -- a serious disagreement that two sides cannot settle on their own
"The dispute is about a song used in a film without permission."
A mediator -- a neutral person who helps two sides reach their own agreement, without deciding for them
"The mediator does not say who is right. She helps them find an answer they both accept."
A party -- one of the two sides in a case: a person, a group or a company
"Both parties agreed to sit down before going to court."
To represent somebody -- to speak and act for somebody in a meeting, a case or a negotiation
"I represent the songwriter. Everything I say today, I say for her."
A claim -- what one side says it is owed, or has a right to
"Her claim is simple: the song was used without permission and she was never paid."
A settlement -- an agreement that ends a dispute before a judge decides it
"Most cases like this end in a settlement, not in a courtroom."
Common ground -- the points that both sides already agree on, before anything else is discussed
"They both want the film to stay online. That is the common ground."
A deadlock -- a point where neither side will move, so the talk stops going anywhere
"We reached a deadlock over the number, and the mediator called a break."
To walk away -- to end a negotiation and leave without an agreement
"If they offer nothing, my client is ready to walk away and go to court."
An opening statement -- the short speech each side gives at the start, before any discussion
"In her opening statement she said who she was, who she represented, and what happened."
In good faith -- honestly, with a real intention to reach an agreement
"We came here in good faith. We are not looking for a fight."
To reach an agreement -- to arrive at a point where both sides say yes to the same thing
"They reached an agreement in two hours: a credit on screen and a fee."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A songwriter and a small film company are sitting in a room with a mediator. He opens by saying what he is not: he is not a judge, and he will not decide who is right. Then he asks the first side to say what happened. In March the company used one of her songs in a short film. Nobody asked her. She found the film in July and she wrote to them twice. Every one of those sentences names a finished moment, so every verb is one word: that is the past simple, and it is how the story is told step by step.

Then the mediator asks a different question: where do things stand this morning? The answer sounds similar and is built differently. They have not answered her. She has never been paid for the use. Nothing there is a date, because the point is not when it happened; the point is what is still true right now. That is the present perfect: have or has plus the past participle.

The rest of the session runs on the same rule. Her claim is a fee and a credit on screen. She has never asked for the film to come down, which means the two sides already have common ground. If they reach a deadlock on the number, the mediator will stop them. And one warning for a Portuguese speaker: a named, finished time kills the present perfect. Not we have signed it yesterday, but we signed it yesterday.

1. "In March the company used her song." Why the past simple and not "has used"?
A Because "use" is a verb that has no participle.
B Because the sentence names a finished moment, and a named finished time takes the past simple.
C Because the company is not the person speaking.
2. Why is it "they have not answered her" and not "they did not answer her"?
A Because the sentence describes what is still true this morning, not one finished moment.
B Because the sentence is negative, and negatives take the present perfect.
C Because there is more than one person on that side.
3. The text warns against "we have signed it yesterday". What is the problem?
A "Sign" cannot be used in the present perfect at all.
B A named finished time such as "yesterday" cannot sit with the present perfect. It takes the past simple.
C "Yesterday" has to come at the beginning of the sentence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Simple and Present Perfect

GRAMMAR

Two pasts: the one that names the moment, and the one that describes what still stands.

JobFormExample
A finished moment, and you name itpast simpleThey used the song in March.
The story, step by steppast simple, one event after anotherShe wrote, they ignored it, she called a lawyer.
The situation as it stands nowhave / has + past participleThey have not answered her.
A period that is still openpresent perfect, with this year / so farWe have met twice this month.
Experience, with no dateever / never + present perfectHave you ever mediated a case like this?
Since and forpresent perfect — never the present tenseI have worked on this case since January.
Irregular participles that trip peoplewrite / wrote / written · speak / spoke / spoken · send / sent / sentShe has written twice. Not has wrote.

Never: we have signed it yesterday · I work on this case since January · did you ever mediate a case like this? · she has wrote to them · they are in deadlock since July.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Things That Could Have Gone Differently -- The Past That Did Not Happen

Nobody read clause seven, and two years later four people are in a room because of eleven words. Key words: a clause, evidence, a witness, testimony, a breach, to disclose, to assert, a compromise, liable, an outcome, in hindsight, as it turned out. Structure: third conditional.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A clause -- one numbered part of a contract, which says one thing
"Clause seven limited the licence to the hardback edition."
Evidence -- the facts and documents that can be shown to support what somebody says
"The evidence was one email, sent at eleven at night and never answered."
A witness -- a person who saw or heard what happened and can describe it
"The designer was the only witness who had been in the room that morning."
Testimony -- what a witness says on the record, as a formal account of what happened
"Her testimony was three pages long and it changed nothing."
A breach -- the act of breaking a rule, a promise or an agreement
"Printing the paperback was a breach of the licence, even if nobody meant it."
To disclose -- to make information known, especially information somebody would rather keep quiet
"They never disclosed that a second print run had already been ordered."
To assert -- to state something firmly, as a right or as a fact, without softening it
"She asserted her right to be credited on every edition, not only the first."
A compromise -- an agreement where each side gives up part of what it wanted
"The compromise took two hours: a fee for the paperback and a credit from now on."
Liable -- legally responsible for something that went wrong, whether or not you meant it
"The publisher was liable for the print run, and the designer was not."
An outcome -- the way a situation finally ends, after everything has been decided
"The outcome was better than either side expected, and it took one afternoon."
In hindsight -- looking back, now that you know how the whole thing ended
"In hindsight, one phone call in March would have saved two years."
As it turned out -- used to introduce what really happened, against what everybody expected
"As it turned out, the photographer had never wanted the book withdrawn."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A publisher put a photograph on a book cover. The licence was signed, the fee was paid, and everything was in order for the hardback. Clause 7.2 said, in eleven words, that the licence covered that edition and no other. Nobody read it. When the paperback came out in March and the e-book in September, the photographer found out from a bookshop window. That was the breach, and it happened without a single person deciding to commit it.

Two years later, both sides were in a room with a mediator, and the most useful ten minutes of the afternoon were spent on sentences that were not about what happened. If somebody had read clause 7.2, the paperback would never have carried the photograph. If the house had disclosed the second print run, the photographer would have found out in March instead of two years later. Both sentences have two halves: the half with if takes had plus the past participle, and the other half takes would have plus the past participle.

The other half is not always would. She could have proved her case in a minute if she had kept the original email — that was possible, not certain. And the question the mediator asked at the end took the same shape: would the outcome have been different if anyone had picked up the phone? One warning for a Portuguese speaker: if never takes would in English. Not if I would have known, but if I had known. In hindsight, that single line is what the whole case turned on.

1. "If somebody had read clause 7.2, the paperback would never have carried the photograph." What does this sentence tell you about what really happened?
A Somebody read the clause, and the paperback was stopped.
B Nobody read the clause, and the paperback carried the photograph.
C It is still not clear whether anybody read it.
2. Why is it "she could have proved it" and not "she would have proved it"?
A Because the result was possible, not certain, and "could have" is what marks that difference.
B Because "prove" is a verb that cannot follow "would".
C Because the sentence is about the photographer and not about the publisher.
3. The text warns against "if I would have known". What is the problem?
A "Know" cannot be used in a conditional sentence.
B In English the half that starts with "if" never takes "would". It takes "had" plus the participle.
C The two halves have to be in the opposite order.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Third Conditional

GRAMMAR

One sentence, two halves: the past that did not happen, and what it would have changed.

JobFormExample
The condition that did not happenif + had + past participleIf somebody had read clause seven...
The result that did not happenwould have + past participle...none of this would have happened.
A result that was possible, not certaincould have / might have + past participleShe could have proved it in a minute.
Ordereither half can come first; comma only when if opensShe could have proved it if she had kept the email.
Spoken formcontractions everywhereIf she’d known, she wouldn’t have signed.
Questionwould / could / might + subject + have + participleWould the outcome have been different?
The two trapsnever would after if; never a past simple after hadNot if I would have known, not if I had knew. Say if I had known.

Never: if I would have known · if I had knew · nothing would happened · she could prove it if she had kept the email · the outcome has been different if anyone had read it.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The mediator asks where the whole thing could have stopped. You answer:
A "If somebody would have read clause seven, nothing happened."
B "If somebody had read clause seven, none of this would have happened."
C "If somebody had read clause seven, nothing would happened."
You want to say the result was possible, not certain. You say:
A "She could have proved it in a minute if she had kept the email."
B "She could prove it in a minute if she had kept the email."
C "She would prove it in a minute if she kept the email."
The other side says it was an honest mistake. You want to say they are still legally responsible. You say:
A "You are guilty of the paperback."
B "Nobody says it was deliberate. The house is still liable for the breach."
C "The breach is not important if it was a mistake."
You want to open the part of the conversation about what nobody expected. You say:
A "In the end of the day, the things happened."
B "For the surprise of everybody, the book was not withdrawn."
C "As it turned out, neither side had ever wanted the book withdrawn."
