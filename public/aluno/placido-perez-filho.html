Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Questions After the Presentation -- What If?

After the presentation, the questions start, and many of them begin with what if. Key words: a Q&A session, to elaborate, a ballpark figure, off the top of my head, to get back to someone, to back up, a quote, a spec sheet, lead time, to scale up, reliable, a trade-off. Structure: the second conditional.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A Q&A session -- the part of a presentation when the audience asks questions
"The Q&A session was longer than the presentation."
To elaborate -- to give more details about something you said
"Could you elaborate on the delivery times?"
A ballpark figure -- a number that is approximately right, not exact
"I can give you a ballpark figure today and the exact price tomorrow."
Off the top of my head -- from memory, without checking any notes
"Off the top of my head, we import about forty percent."
To get back to someone -- to contact someone later with an answer
"Let me check the numbers and get back to you by Friday."
To back up -- to support an idea or a claim with facts or numbers
"Our clients back up what I said about quality."
A quote -- a written price for a specific order
"We asked three suppliers for a quote."
A spec sheet -- a document with the technical details of a product
"The spec sheet shows the thickness and the alloy."
Lead time -- the time between the order and the delivery
"The lead time from Asia is about ten weeks."
To scale up -- to make something bigger, or to produce more of it
"If demand grew, could you scale up production?"
Reliable -- that you can trust to work well every time
"Our clients need a reliable supplier more than a cheap one."
A trade-off -- a balance between two good things that you cannot have at the same time
"There is always a trade-off between price and lead time."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At the end of his presentation, a buyer asks Plácido a hypothetical question: “If we ordered twice the volume, would you lower the price?” He answers with care: “It would depend on the lead time. If it were shorter, we could offer a better price.”

The buyer asks him to elaborate. Plácido does not have the numbers with him, so he gives a ballpark figure, off the top of his head, and promises to get back to the buyer with the exact quote.

At the end, the buyer says: “If I were you, I would send the spec sheet with the quote. Good numbers back up a good presentation.”

1. Why does the buyer say “if we ordered” and not “if we order”?
A Because the order happened last year.
B Because the buyer is British.
C Because it is an imagined situation, not a real plan yet.
2. Why does Plácido give a ballpark figure?
A Because he does not have the exact numbers with him.
B Because the buyer asked for a fixed price.
C Because the price is a secret.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A “If I was you, I would send the spec sheet.”
B “If I were you, I would send the spec sheet.”
C “If I would be you, I send the spec sheet.”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Second Conditional -- If + Past, Would + Verb

GRAMMAR

Use it for situations that are imagined, not real: a hypothetical order, a price that could change, advice. The verb after if looks like the past, but the meaning is present or future.

JobFormExample
Imagine a situationIf + past simple, would + verbIf we ordered more, would you lower the price?
Be, for every personif I / he / she / it wereIf the lead time were shorter...
Give adviceIf I were you, I would / I’d...If I were you, I’d ask for a quote.
A possibilitycould or might instead of wouldWe could buy more from you.
The order can changewould-part first, no commaWhat would happen if the price went up?
The trapno would in the if-partNot if we would order. Say if we ordered.

Never: If I was you · If we would order more · If the price were lower, we will buy · I’ll get back you

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Book in Fifteen Minutes -- Ikigai

A short summary of a book about purpose and long life. Key words: a purpose, longevity, to figure out, to give up, to carry on, to take up, to look forward to, to slow down, a sense of community, flow, resilience, to keep someone going. Structure: separable and inseparable phrasal verbs.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A purpose -- the reason why you do something, or the reason your life is important to you
"For many people, work gives them a purpose."
Longevity -- a long life
"The island is famous for the longevity of its people."
To figure out -- to understand something or find the answer after thinking about it
"It took him years to figure out what he really wanted."
To give up -- to stop doing something, often because it is difficult
"She never gave up her morning walk."
To carry on -- to continue doing something
"Many people there carry on working after eighty."
To take up -- to start a new hobby or activity
"He took up painting when he was seventy."
To look forward to -- to feel happy about something that is going to happen
"I look forward to my walk in the park every afternoon."
To slow down -- to do things less quickly; to live at a calmer pace
"The book says we should slow down and enjoy the day."
A sense of community -- the feeling that you belong to a group of people who help each other
"A strong sense of community helps people live longer."
Flow -- a state when you are so focused on an activity that you forget the time
"When I read, I get into flow and forget the clock."
Resilience -- the ability to recover after a difficult time
"Resilience is not about never falling. It is about getting up."
To keep someone going -- to give someone the energy or the reason to continue
"What keeps you going on a difficult day?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Phrasal Verbs -- Where the Object Goes

GRAMMAR

Some phrasal verbs open in the middle; with a pronoun, they must. Others never open, and three-part verbs take -ing after them.

JobFormExample
Separable, with a nounverb + particle + noun, or verb + noun + particletake up painting / take painting up
Separable, with a pronounthe pronoun always goes in the middlefigure it out · gave it up
Inseparablethe object goes after the whole verbcarry on with it
Three-part verbsnever separate; a verb after them takes -ingI look forward to reading it.
No objectthe phrasal verb stands aloneDon’t give up. Slow down.
The trapno pronoun at the end of a separable verbNot figure out it. Say figure it out.

Never: figure out it · give up it · look forward to see · look forward the book to

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Catching the Fast Talk -- Films and Series

Films and series at native speed. Key words: a plot, a cliffhanger, subtitles, to binge-watch, a spoiler, a twist, to mumble, to make out, to pick up, to catch on, slang, to rewind. Structure: ’d = would or had; gonna, wanna, gotta.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A plot -- the story of a film, a series or a book
"The plot is simple, but the dialogue is very fast."
A cliffhanger -- an exciting end of an episode that makes you want to see the next one
"Every episode ends with a cliffhanger."
Subtitles -- the words of the dialogue, written at the bottom of the screen
"I watch with English subtitles the first time."
To binge-watch -- to watch many episodes of a series, one after the other
"We binge-watched the whole season in one weekend."
A spoiler -- information that tells you what happens before you see it
"Don’t tell me the end. No spoilers!"
A twist -- a sudden, surprising change in the story
"I didn’t see the twist coming."
To mumble -- to speak quietly and not clearly
"The main actor mumbles, so I need the subtitles."
To make out -- to manage to hear or see something, with difficulty
"I couldn’t make out the last line."
To pick up -- to learn something, like a word or an accent, without studying it
"I picked up a lot of slang from that series."
To catch on -- to begin to understand something
"It took me a few episodes to catch on to the jokes."
Slang -- very informal words that a group of people uses
"British slang is hard to understand at first."
To rewind -- to go back to an earlier part of a video or a recording
"I rewind the same scene three times."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Fast Speech -- Would, Had and Gonna

GRAMMAR

At native speed, would and had both become ’d. The next word tells you which one it is. Gonna, wanna and gotta are normal in speech, but you write going to, want to and have got to.

JobFormExample
’d + base verb= wouldI’d watch it again.
’d + past participle= hadI’d seen it before.
’d better + verb= had better (strong advice)You’d better turn on the subtitles.
gonna= going to (only in speech)I’m gonna start season two.
wanna / gotta= want to / have got toDo you wanna watch it? I gotta go.
The trapyou hear gonna, you write going toIn an email, not I’m gonna send: I’m going to send.

Never: I’d saw it · I’d watched it again tomorrow · You’d better to go · gonna in a formal email
