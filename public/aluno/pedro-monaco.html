Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Last June I went to my first international conference. I had a badge with my name on it, a lanyard, and no idea what to do with either. I have been in the legal team of a media company since 2023, and by then I had drafted a fair number of contracts in English — with a lot of help from a machine, which is a separate problem. So I could follow the sessions. What I could not do was talk about them.

The keynote that morning was excellent and far too short. The speaker touched on cross-border compliance for about four minutes and moved on, and four minutes was exactly the part of my job that nobody ever explains. My one takeaway from the whole day was that question: how do you build a rulebook that works in two countries at once. If somebody had unpacked that on stage, I would have paid for the ticket twice.

Then, in the coffee break, a man I had never met turned to me and asked what had struck me most in the talks. It is not a difficult question. It is not even a legal question. And I went completely blank — not because I did not have an answer, but because I had never practised giving one. I said something about the venue. He said something polite. That was the end of it.

I have thought about that thirty seconds many times since then. The lesson was not about vocabulary. Anybody who can draft a contract has the words. The lesson was that an open question needs a shape, decided in advance: one thing, why it stayed with you, what changes on Monday. Thirty seconds. I have never been caught by that question again, and I have not forgotten the name of the man who asked it, either — he is now a counterpart of mine in London, and we have worked on two deals together.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

Portuguese has one past where English has two. That is the whole difficulty: the choice is not a translation, it is a decision about the timeline. One question settles almost every case — does the sentence name a finished time?

FormWhat it doesExample
have / has
+ past participle		Something that started in the past and is still true now, or an experience with no time attached. The moment is irrelevant — the result is not."I have worked here since 2023."
"I have drafted contracts in English."
past simpleA finished action at a finished time. If the sentence says when — yesterday, last year, in June, two hours ago — it is past simple, always."I drafted it last year."
"The keynote finished twenty minutes ago."
for · sinceFor + a length of time. Since + the point where it started. Both belong to the present perfect. "I work here since 2023" is the single commonest error a Portuguese speaker makes in English."for two years"
"since March 2023"
just · already
yet · ever · never		Recent, earlier than expected, still expected, or at any point in a life. Ever and never are the words of the experience question — and they cannot be built with did."We have just opened an office."
"Have you ever worked with an American client?"
been x goneOne letter apart, opposite meanings. Been = went and came back. Gone = went and is still there."She has been to London." (she is back)
"She has gone to London." (she is there)

One habit worth building this week: every time you say since or for about your own work, stop and check that the verb in front of it is have or has. Nine times out of ten the sentence is about you, it is true right now, and the present perfect is the only tense that says so.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Read any corporate document in English and you will notice the same thing: almost nobody does anything. The subsidiary was incorporated. The filings were made. The account is subject to review. Somebody made every one of those decisions, and the sentences are built so that you do not have to say who. That is the passive voice, and in business and legal English it is not decoration. It is the default.

There are three honest reasons for it. Sometimes the person is unknown — nobody can say which officer looked at the form. Sometimes the person is obvious, and naming them wastes the reader's time. And very often the ACTION is what matters: what a contract cares about is that notice shall be given in writing, not about the individual who prints the letter. Add by only when the agent is new information: "all filings are reviewed by outside counsel" is worth writing, and "the letter was signed by a person" is not.

The dishonest reason is worth knowing too, because you will meet it. "Mistakes were made" is the most famous sentence in corporate English precisely because it is a confession with the confessor removed. When a report tells you that a decision was taken and never says by whom, the grammar is doing work that the writer did not want to do in the open.

The form itself is simple, and it is where the errors live. Only be carries the tense: is signed, was signed, has been signed, will be signed, can be signed. The participle never changes. After by, English uses the object pronoun — by us, never by we. And a company with no track record in a market is treated differently from one that has been there for ten years, which is a sentence you will read in some form every week for the rest of this expansion.

1. According to the text, why is the passive the default in legal and corporate English?
A Because it is more polite than the active voice.
B Because the action, not the person, is usually what matters.
C Because English has no other way of saying it.
2. When does the text say you should add "by"?
A Always, so that the sentence is complete.
B Never, in formal writing.
C Only when the agent is new information for the reader.
3. Why does the text call "mistakes were made" a dishonest use of the passive?
A Because it admits the fact while removing the person responsible for it.
B Because the grammar is wrong.
C Because it is too informal for a report.
4. Which part of the passive carries the tense?
A The past participle.
B The verb be.
C The agent after by.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Passive Voice

GRAMMAR

Portuguese solves the same problem with se and with the third person plural, so the IDEA of the passive will feel familiar. The mistakes are all in the FORM — the participle, the pronoun after by, and the fact that only the verb be ever changes.

FormWhat it doesExample
be + past participlePuts the ACTION first. Use it when the doer is unknown, obvious or irrelevant — or when you would rather not name them."The subsidiary was incorporated in Delaware."
by + agentAdd by only when the doer is NEW information. And after by the pronoun is the object form: us, them, him — never we."reviewed by outside counsel"
"signed by us"
the tense sits on BEThe participle is frozen. Time, modality and negation all happen to be. Learn the chain once and every tense comes free."is signed · was signed · has been signed · is being signed · will be signed · can be signed"
contracts and filingsThe standard register of the documents you already read every day. Shall be is obligation; is to be is an instruction."The Buyer shall be notified in writing."
"Payment is to be made within thirty days."
get-passiveSpoken, informal and usually bad news. Right in a corridor, wrong in a contract."The filing got rejected."

One habit for this week: when you read a clause in English, find the verb be and ask who is missing. Half the time the answer is "nobody, and that is fine". The other half is where the interesting questions in your job are.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ask a lawyer what they do and you usually get one of two answers: a job title that explains nothing, or four minutes that explain far too much. The useful answer sits between them, and it almost always contains a relative clause. In a nutshell, I decide what the company can sign. The contracts that carry real risk come to me first. Two sentences — and the second one is doing the work, because it tells you WHICH contracts without starting again.

That second sentence is a defining relative clause, and it takes no commas. Take the commas seriously, because they change what you are saying. The clauses that protect us are rewritten in plain English means some clauses protect us, and those are the ones being rewritten. The clauses, which protect us, are rewritten in plain English means all of them protect us and all of them are being rewritten. The comma is not decoration. It is the difference between some and all, and in a contract that difference is the whole argument.

Non-defining clauses are the ones you could delete without damage. They always take commas, and they never take that. Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand the clause in one sentence. Delaware, where the subsidiary was incorporated, has its own company law. A partner whose filings are always late is telling you something before the due diligence gets a chance to.

Two habits will make you sound like somebody who speaks English rather than somebody who writes it. First, drop the pronoun when it is the object of its own clause: the safeguard we rely on, not the safeguard which we rely on. Second, do not name the object twice. The safeguard that we rely on it is the commonest mistake a Portuguese speaker makes here, because in Portuguese the pronoun stays. In English it goes, and the sentence is already finished.

2. What is the difference between "the clauses that protect us" and "the clauses, which protect us"?
A The first means only some of them; the second means all of them.
B There is no difference; the comma is decoration.
C The second one is simply more formal.

4. According to the text, what is the commonest mistake a Portuguese speaker makes here?
A Using who for things instead of which.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

Portuguese has one word — que — where English chooses between who, which and that, and where a comma decides whether you mean SOME or ALL. The idea will feel familiar; the choices are where the work is.

FormWhat it doesExample
defining (no commas)Tells the reader WHICH one you mean. Delete it and the sentence loses its point. No commas — and here that is allowed."The clause that protects us is the one nobody reads."
non-defining (commas)Adds information about something already identified. Delete it and the sentence still stands. The commas are obligatory, and that is never allowed."Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand it."
dropping the pronounWhen the relative pronoun is the OBJECT of its clause, leave it out. This is the single change that makes written English sound spoken."the safeguard we rely on"
not "the safeguard which we rely on"
whose · where · whenwhose = of whom, of which (people AND things). where = in which place. when = at which time."a partner whose filings are late"
"Delaware, where the subsidiary was incorporated"
the prepositionFormal register puts it before the pronoun; everybody else leaves it at the end. Both are correct, and only one of them sounds like a person talking."the rule on which the policy is based"
"the rule the policy is based on"

One habit for this week: every time you write a sentence with and it or and this in the middle, try joining it with which or who instead. Half of your paragraphs will get shorter, and the half that does not was two ideas all along.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are four words in an English contract that decide what your company is committed to, and every one of them is short. Shall and must create an obligation: this has to happen. May creates a permission: this is allowed, and nobody is forcing it. Shall not is a prohibition. And is entitled to states a right that belongs to one party whether the other side is happy about it or not.

The dangerous one is may. In Portuguese, pode carries permission and possibility at the same time, so a clause that GRANTS something reads like a clause that REQUIRES it. Either party may terminate the agreement on thirty days notice means nobody is locked in. Translate that as an obligation and you have written a contract that forces both sides out of a relationship they wanted to keep. That mistake is usually found two years later, by which time it is somebody else's problem.

The form is simple and it is where the errors live. After shall, must and may the verb is bare: shall indemnify, never shall to indemnify. There is no third person -s: may not accept, never may not accepts. And the passive is everywhere, because the obligation matters more than the person: Notice must be given in writing, payment is to be made within thirty days.

One last habit worth building. When you read a provision, say out loud which of the three things it does — obliges, permits or prohibits. If you cannot say which, the clause is either badly drafted or you have not finished reading it. That single question is what separates reading a contract from looking at one, and it takes about four seconds.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways to ask the same question in English, and the polite one is not simply longer. Where are we on the indemnity? is direct and perfectly correct. Could you tell me where we are on the indemnity? is the version that opens a conversation instead of demanding an answer — and the moment you add that opening, something has to move inside the sentence.

What moves is the word order. Once the question has already been asked by Could you tell me, the second half goes back to being a normal statement: subject, then verb. where we are, not where are we. The auxiliary goes too: Do you know what he wants?, never what does he want. The tense is not lost — it moves onto the main verb, which is doing the work all along.

For a yes/no question there is nothing to move, so English adds a word instead: if or whether. I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed. Not that, which is the commonest mistake here, because Portuguese uses que in both places and nothing warns you. Whether is safer in writing and obligatory before or not; if is what everybody says out loud.

The reason any of this matters is speed. In a meeting you have about two seconds to take the turn, and Sorry, can I jump in? and Could you tell me... are prefabricated: you start speaking before the sentence is finished in your head, and the grammar buys you the rest. That is the real function of these openers. They are not manners. They are time.

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Saying that something is more expensive is almost no information at all. The useful sentence says HOW MUCH more, and English does that with a small set of words that sit in front of the comparative. Far, much, a lot and considerably mean the difference is big. Slightly, a bit and a little mean it is small. Rent in London is far higher than in Sao Paulo; a ten per cent pay rise buys you slightly less than it sounds.

The word that does not belong there is very. In Portuguese one single word grades both the plain adjective and the comparative, so very more expensive comes out of a Brazilian mouth at speed and sounds completely wrong to an English ear. Very expensive is fine. Far more expensive is fine. Very more expensive does not exist in English at all.

The same grading works in the negative. Not nearly as and nowhere near as say the difference is big, from the other direction: food is nowhere near as expensive as housing. Note what follows: as ... as, never as ... than. And when you are counting things rather than measuring them, the word changes: far fewer flats, far less space. That distinction is one of the clearest signals of a C1 writer in a TOEFL essay.

Finally there is the structure the whole reading is built on: the further out you live, the longer you commute. Two comparatives locked together, each pulling the other. It is worth practising out loud because it is short, it sounds fluent, and it lets you say a whole trade-off in one sentence — which is exactly what an examiner and a colleague both want from you.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a kind of answer that is completely correct and still fails. Somebody asks how the negotiation went and you say it went well. Nothing is wrong with it. The problem is that it is finished: there is nothing in it for the other person to build on, no reason, no example, nothing that shows how you think. In an exam it gives the examiner nothing to award marks for. In a meeting it makes the room move on.

The fix is not more vocabulary. It is deciding, in advance, that every answer has three parts. First the answer. Then ONE of these: the reason (mainly because...), the example (a case in point is...), or the concession (in fairness..., that said...). Then the consequence, which is what closes it: the upshot is..., on balance..., all things considered...

The concession is the move worth practising most, because it is the one that sounds senior. Saying the point that works against you — before the other person says it — does not weaken your position; it shows that you have already thought about it. To some extent that is true, although not in this contract. In fairness, nobody had seen that structure before. Both of those are stronger than a flat disagreement, and both buy you the right to keep talking.

Two small warnings. Markers are not decoration, so never use two in a row: however, on the other hand, that said... is three promises and no content. And beware of actually, which does not mean at the moment — it means in fact. For time, English uses currently or at present. That single word has confused more Brazilian professionals than any grammar point in this course.

2. What are the three parts of an answer, according to the text?

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Discourse Markers and the Three-Move Answer

GRAMMAR

Nothing here is new grammar. It is a decision made before you open your mouth: answer, then one of the four moves, then close. Three sentences is roughly forty seconds, which happens to be exactly what a TOEFL speaking task is looking for.

FormWhat it doesExample
the reasonWhy it is true. The default second move, and the one that costs nothing to prepare: mainly because, the reason being, largely down to."It went well. Mainly because we had the structure agreed first."
the exampleOne concrete case. Stronger than a reason, because it cannot be argued with: a case in point, take the bank account, for instance."The process is slow. A case in point is the bank account."
the concessionThe point against you, said before somebody else says it: that said, in fairness, to some extent, admittedly. This is what makes an answer sound senior."We got most of it. That said, the cap is still open."
the consequenceWhat follows from all of it. This is the move that CLOSES an answer instead of letting it fade: the upshot is, on balance, all things considered."The upshot is that nothing can be signed this month."
where the marker goesFront position with a comma is the safe default and works for all of them. Mid position sounds more written; end position is speech only. Never two markers in a row."That said, the cap is open."
"The cap, however, is open."

One habit for this week: after every answer you give in English, count to two in your head before you let yourself stop. If a second sentence has not started by then, use mainly because and one will.

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to a complete three-move answer, then put the parts in the order they were said.

?The question is asked: how is the American operation going?
?The answer comes first, in one short sentence: slower than planned.
?A reason or an example follows: the bank account alone took four months.
?A point against the speaker is conceded: in fairness, nobody had seen that structure before.
?The consequence closes it: the upshot is that nothing can be signed until the cap is agreed.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Somebody has just asked you a question. Choose the answer that does not stop too early.

"How did the negotiation go?"
A "It went well."
B "It went well. Mainly because we had the structure agreed before anybody talked about money."
C "Good, good, very good."
You want to admit a point that works against your own argument:
A "You are completely right, then."
B "In fairness, nobody on their side had seen that structure before."
C "That is not true at all."
You want to close an answer instead of letting it fade:
A "So, yeah."
B "And that is more or less it, I think."
C "The upshot is that nothing can be signed until the cap is agreed."
You want to say what is happening at this moment:
A "Actually we are in the third round of negotiation."
B "Currently we are in the third round of negotiation."
C "In the actuality we are in the third round."
You want to give one concrete example of a slow process:
A "Everything is slow, in general, all of it."
B "A case in point is the bank account, which took four months."
C "For example many things, like documents and other things."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Choose one open question about your work this year — something a stranger might genuinely ask — and answer it out loud for sixty seconds without stopping. Use all four moves at least once: a reason, an example, a concession and a consequence. Then listen back and count how many sentences you actually produced.
