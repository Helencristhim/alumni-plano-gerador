Who’s Who on the Call -- Your Role and Your Current Work

A partnership call starts and everybody says who they are. Key words: to be in charge of, to coordinate, a member company, a partnership, a counterpart, to be based in, a stakeholder, to reach out to, a point of contact, a background. Structure: present simple vs present continuous.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To be in charge of -- to be the person responsible for a team, a task or an area
"I am in charge of the relationship with our member companies."
To coordinate -- to organize people and tasks so that they work well together
"I coordinate meetings between Brazilian and Canadian companies."
A member company -- a company that pays to belong to an organization, like a chamber of commerce
"A new member company joined the chamber last month."
A partnership -- an agreement between two organizations to work together
"We are discussing a partnership with a trade association in Toronto."
A counterpart -- a person in another organization who has a role similar to yours
"My counterpart in Toronto also works with events and partnerships."
To be based in -- to have your main office in a city or country
"Our partner is based in Montreal, but she travels a lot."
A stakeholder -- a person or group that is interested in a project or affected by it
"Before the meeting, we talk to every stakeholder."
To reach out to -- to contact someone, often for the first time
"I reached out to the consulate to ask for a meeting."
A point of contact -- the person you talk to about a specific subject
"I am your point of contact for the partnership."
A background -- your past studies and work experience
"She has a background in marketing and international trade."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The call starts at ten. Twelve people are online, from five countries, and the host asks for a quick round. The first person says: “I coordinate international programs at a trade association in Toronto, and at the moment we are preparing a visit to Brazil.”

The second person says: “I am in charge of events at a chamber of commerce. I talk to our member companies every week. This month we are organizing a business breakfast.”

Notice the pattern. When the sentence describes the job, the verb is simple: coordinate, talk. When the sentence describes the task of the week or the month, the verb takes am, is or are plus -ing.

1. Does the first person prepare visits to Brazil every week of the year?
A Yes, that is the permanent job.
B No. The job is to coordinate programs; the visit is what the team is preparing now.
C The text does not say.
2. Why does the second person say “I talk to our member companies” and not “I am talking”?
A Because it is a routine: every week.
B Because it happened once, in the past.
C Because it is a task only for this month.
3. Which sentence is correct?
A “At the moment I prepare a call with Toronto.”
B “At the moment I am preparing a call with Toronto.”
C “At the moment I am prepare a call with Toronto.”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple vs Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

The simple form says who you are and what is always true. The -ing form says what you are doing now.

JobFormExample
Your job, always truepresent simple: I / we + verbI coordinate partnership meetings.
A routine, how oftenpresent simple + every month / usuallyWe meet our members every month.
Something happening nowam / is / are + verb-ingAt the moment I am preparing a call.
A task for this week or montham / is / are + verb-ing + this weekThis week we are organizing an event.
The two questionsWhat do you do? / What are you working on?What do you do? — What are you working on now?
The trapstate verbs stay simpleNot I am knowing her. Say I know her well.

Never: I am knowing Daniel · Every month I am coordinating the meeting · At the moment I prepare a call · What are you doing? (to ask about a job, say What do you do?)

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Agenda -- Planning a Meeting

A good meeting starts days before the call, with one short page. Key words: an agenda, an agenda item, to run a meeting, an objective, a time slot, an attendee, to circulate, the minutes, to reschedule, to prioritize. Structure: will vs going to vs present continuous.

▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An agenda -- a list of the subjects that people will discuss in a meeting
"I will send the agenda to everybody on Monday."
An agenda item -- one subject on the list of a meeting
"The first agenda item is the new partnership program."
To run a meeting -- to lead a meeting and control the time and the subjects
"Daniel is going to run the meeting on Thursday."
An objective -- the result you want to get from a meeting or a project
"The objective of the call is to choose a date for the event."
A time slot -- a fixed period of time for one activity
"Each agenda item has a time slot of ten minutes."
An attendee -- a person who is present at a meeting or an event
"We are expecting twelve attendees from four countries."
To circulate -- to send a document to all the people in a group
"Please circulate the presentation before the call."
The minutes -- the written record of what people said and decided in a meeting
"Who is going to take the minutes today?"
To reschedule -- to change the date or time of a meeting or an event
"We need to reschedule the call because of the holiday."
To prioritize -- to decide which things are the most important and do them first
"We have little time, so let us prioritize the partnership items."

GRAMMAR

Three ways to talk about the future. The difference is when you made the decision, and whether there is a day and a time.

JobFormExample

An arrangement in the calendaram / is / are + verb-ing + timeWe are meeting on Thursday at ten.
A prediction from what you seegoing to + verbThe meeting is going to be long.
An offerShall I...? / I will...I will take the minutes, if you want.
The trapafter if, no willIf Daniel agrees, we will reschedule.

Never: If Daniel will agree, we reschedule · We will discuss the program (decided last week) · I am going to send it now (decided this second) · We meet the partners Thursday at ten

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Taking the Floor -- Joining the Conversation Politely

You understand the discussion and you have a point: now you need the phrase that opens the door. Key words: to chip in, to go ahead, to bring up, to clarify, to rephrase, to hold on, to speak up, to be on mute, to catch, to take turns. Structure: polite requests with can, could, would, may and would you mind + -ing.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To chip in -- to add a comment to a conversation that other people started
"Can I chip in here? I have some information about that company."
To go ahead -- to start or continue speaking, often after someone gives permission
"Sure, go ahead, Juliana. We are listening."
To bring up -- to start talking about a subject in a meeting
"I would like to bring up the date of the event."
To clarify -- to make something easier to understand by explaining it again
"Could you clarify the second point, please?"
To rephrase -- to say the same idea again with different words
"Let me rephrase that. I mean the partners from Montreal."
To hold on -- to wait for a short time
"Hold on a second. I am opening the document."
To speak up -- to speak louder, or to give your opinion when others are quiet
"Could you speak up a little? The sound is low."
To be on mute -- to have your microphone off in an online call
"Sorry, I was on mute. Can you hear me now?"
To catch -- to hear or understand what someone says
"Sorry, I did not catch the last part."
To take turns -- to speak one after the other, in order
"Let us take turns, so everybody can speak."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The call is fast. Daniel is talking about a visit from Canada, and Juliana has a question. She does not wait for a silence. “Could I just chip in here?” she says. Daniel stops. “Sure, go ahead.”

Juliana did not catch the number of companies. “Would you mind repeating it?” she asks. Daniel repeats it, and she asks one more thing: “Could you clarify the date of the first visit?”

Notice the pattern. After can, could, would and may, the verb is simple, with no to. After would you mind, the verb takes -ing.

1. Why does Juliana say “Could I just chip in here?”
A To end the call.
B To join the conversation politely, without waiting for a silence.
C To ask Daniel to speak louder.
2. What did Juliana not catch?
A The number of companies.
B The name of the city.
C The time of the call.
3. Which question is correct?
A “Would you mind to repeat it?”
B “Would you mind repeating it?”
C “Would you mind repeat it?”

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Polite Requests and Permission

GRAMMAR

Can, could, would and may make a question polite. After them the verb is simple; after would you mind, the verb takes -ing.

JobFormExample
Permission, informalCan I + verb?Can I ask a question?
Permission, more politeCould I / May I + verb?Could I add something?
Asking someone to do somethingCould you / Would you + verb?Could you send me the list?
Very polite requestWould you mind + verb-ing?Would you mind repeating the date?
Keeping your turnCan I just + verb?Can I just finish my point?
The trapno to after can, could, would, mayNot Could you to send. Say Could you send.

Never: Could you to send me the list · Would you mind to repeat · May I to add something · Repeat, please (to a new partner: say Could you repeat that, please?)

Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Where We Are Now -- Giving a Status Update

In every partnership meeting somebody asks: where are we now? Key words: progress, a milestone, to be on track, to fall behind, a status update, pending, a draft, to sign off on, a setback, to wrap up. Structure: present perfect (already, yet, just, so far) vs past simple.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Progress -- the movement of a project toward its end
"We have made good progress on the partnership this month."
A milestone -- an important point or date in a project
"Signing the agreement was the first milestone."
To be on track -- to be doing what the plan says, at the right time
"The event is on track for November."
To fall behind -- to be slower than the plan
"We have fallen behind because of the holidays."
A status update -- a short report that says where a project is now
"Could you send me a status update by Friday?"
Pending -- waiting for a decision or an action
"Two contracts are still pending."
A draft -- a first version of a document, not the final one
"I have just sent you the draft of the agreement."
To sign off on -- to give final approval to a plan or a document
"The director has signed off on the budget."
A setback -- a problem that stops or slows progress
"The visa problem was a small setback."
To wrap up -- to finish a task or a meeting
"We wrapped up the first phase last week."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect vs Past Simple

GRAMMAR

Use have / has + participle for progress up to now, with no date. When you say the date, use the past simple.

JobFormExample
Progress, no datehave / has + past participleWe have sent the invitations.
Done before nowalready (middle of the sentence)We have already signed the agreement.
Not done, but expectednot + yet (end)The director has not signed it yet.
Very recentjustI have just sent you the draft.
A specific time in the pastpast simple + on Monday / last week / in MayWe sent them on Monday.
The trapno present perfect with a dateNot I have sent it yesterday. Say I sent it yesterday.

Never: We have sent them last Monday · The director has signed it yet · So far, twelve companies confirmed · I have sent you already the draft
