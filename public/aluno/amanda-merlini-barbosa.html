Stage 3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

A company can buy its law from outside, or it can keep it inside. A law firm has many clients and sells hours. An in-house lawyer has one client, the company, and sells judgement: the answer has to arrive before the business moves, not after.

The team that sits inside is the legal department, and its work falls into three groups. The first is contracts: the lawyers draft the first version, negotiate the clauses which protect the company, and review every document before anybody signs it. The second is litigation, the disputes that end up in court. In a large group the in-house team rarely goes to court itself: it chooses the firm that represents the company, and it controls the strategy and the cost.

The third group is advisory work. Here nobody is in court yet. A manager asks a question, the lawyer gives an opinion, and the business decides. Much of that opinion is about compliance: the law, the rules of the sector and the internal policies of the group, which are often stricter than the law itself.

The industry changes the work. In agribusiness, a lawyer who reads a supply contract is also reading the weather, the harvest and the port. A late ship is a legal problem, and so is a dry season. And a department that handles everything from the field to the buyer still has limits: in most groups, employment law goes to a specialist firm outside.

Stage 4: Grammar Tip -- who, which and that

GRAMMAR

Two short sentences say less than one long one. A relative clause joins them: it adds information about a noun without starting a new sentence. This is the fastest way to stop answering in four words.

WordWhat it points toExample
whoa personthe lawyer who reviews the contract
whicha thing, a company, an ideathe clause which protects the seller
thata person or a thing; the natural choice when you speakthe group that exports grain
Do not repeat the subjectthe relative word already IS the subject: "the lawyer who reviews", never "the lawyer who she reviews".
Do not repeat the object"the contract which I signed yesterday", never "the contract which I signed it yesterday".
What it is for"I am a lawyer. I work for an agribusiness group." becomes "I am a lawyer who works for an agribusiness group."

Quick test before you speak: person or thing? Person takes who. Thing takes which. When you hesitate, that works for both.

Stage 3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

Every lawyer is asked the same question at some point: so, tell me about yourself. And most answers fail in the same way. They are a list. The person says a job, then another job, then a third one, and the listener learns nothing except the order.

A story is not a list. It has a shape, and in English that shape is carried by two tenses. Look at what happens when a lawyer tells it well. "I started in a law firm in two thousand and thirteen, and I stayed for three years." Those years are closed: they have a beginning, an end and a date, so the verbs sit in the past simple. "Then I moved in-house, and I have never gone back." That sentence is different. It has no date, and it is still true today.

The same split runs through the whole story. "I have been the legal manager since two thousand and twenty-one" is a period that reaches this morning. "I have worked in agribusiness for eleven years" is a length of time that is still growing. But "the buyer stopped paying in two thousand and twenty-two" is finished, and so is the case that followed it.

The most useful move in a career story is the one that uses both in a row. First the closed event, then what it left behind. "The jurisdiction clause sent us abroad, and the case was slow and expensive. Since then, I have read that clause first, in every contract." The past simple gives the evidence. The present perfect gives the person listening the thing they actually want: what you do now because of it. That is the difference between a track record and a list of employers.

1. According to the text, why does a list fail as an answer?
A The listener learns nothing except the order of the jobs.
B A list is too long for a thirty-minute call.
C A list uses too many tenses at the same time.
2. Why does the text put "I stayed for three years" in the past simple and "I have worked for eleven years" in the present perfect?
A Because the first one is shorter than the second one.
B Because the three years are closed and the eleven years are still growing.
C Because the first one is about a law firm and the second one about a company.
3. What does the text call the most useful move in a career story?
A Saying the years in the right order, from the first job to the last one.
B The closed event in the past simple, and then what it left behind in the present perfect.
C Ending with the reason why you want the new role.

Stage 4: Grammar Tip -- present perfect vs past simple

GRAMMAR

There is only one question to ask, and it is not about the verb. It is about the time: is the time in this sentence finished?

FormUseExample
Present perfect
have / has + past participle		the time is not finished, or you do not say whenI have worked here for four years.
Past simplethe time is finished, and usually said out loudI joined the company in 2016.
for / sincehow long / when it startedfor eleven years · since 2021
already / yet / justalready before the verb; yet at the end of negatives and questions; just before the verbI have already read it. I have not signed it yet.
ever / neverexperience in a life that is not overHave you ever worked on a carbon project? I have never gone back.
Watch outa date kills the present perfect. Say "I joined in 2016", never "I have joined in 2016". And a period that reaches today kills the present simple: say "I have worked here since 2021", never "I work here since 2021".

Quick test before you speak: is there a finished time in this sentence? Yes, past simple. No, present perfect.

Running Your Part of the Meeting

Stage 1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Four words for the structure of a meeting, four for what you do inside it. Listen to each one and read it in context.

agenda -- the list of points a meeting will cover, in order
"There are three items on the agenda, and the third one is mine."
action item -- one task the meeting produced, with an owner and a date

Stage 3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

In a meeting held in a second language, the hardest moment is not the difficult question. It is the ordinary one: so, where are we on this? The answer has to do two things at once, and most people do only one.

The first thing is the job. What your department does is permanent: it does not change because it is Wednesday. "I oversee the legal department." "I liaise with the commercial team every day." These sit in the present simple, and they answer the question who are you in this room.

The second thing is the week, and it needs a different tense. "We have been reviewing the contract since Friday." "The vessel has been waiting at the port for six days." Work that started earlier, is still going, and is the reason the item is on the agenda at all. That is what have been plus -ing is for, and it is the sentence that buys you the room, because it explains why there is no answer yet without sounding like an excuse.

Then the item has to close. A meeting that ends in agreement and not in action items produces nothing: in three weeks nobody remembers who owned what, and the minutes are the only proof. So the last thirty seconds belong to whoever is willing to say the words. "Let me wrap up. Two action items: I send the notice today, and you confirm the date by Friday. I will follow up on the answer myself."

Nothing in that sentence is difficult English. What is difficult is deciding that closing the item is your job, and not the job of whoever is most senior in the room.

2. What does the text say "have been + ing" is for, in a meeting?

Stage 4: Grammar Tip -- present perfect continuous

GRAMMAR

Two things get confused in every status update: the job and the week. The job takes the present simple. The week takes have or has been plus -ing.

FormUseExample
Present simplethe job: true every weekI oversee the department.
Present perfect continuous
have / has been + ing		work that started earlier and is still goingWe have been reviewing it since Friday.
Negative and questionhave not been + ing / have you been + ingWe have not been waiting. Has the team been calling the agent?
Result or activity"I have read the contract" = finished, you know what is in it. "I have been reading the contract" = still in the middle, and that is why there is no answer yet.
Never continuousknow, want, need, understand, mean. Say "I have known this buyer for years", never "I have been knowing".
Watch outthe present continuous alone does not carry "since". Say "we have been reviewing it since Friday", never "we are reviewing it since Friday".

Quick test before you speak: is this the job, or is this the week?
