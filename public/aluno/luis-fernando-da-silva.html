Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A member of the board lands once or twice a year, walks in with a coffee, and asks the same four words: so, what do you do? The answer starts with what is true every single week. He works in credit and collections. He is responsible for the growers and the dealers in one region, and he deals with about two hundred customers. Nothing there is finished, so the verb stays in its base form. Only the third person is different: his colleague handles the north, and a grower pays after the harvest. That small -s is the one thing English asks for in return.

Then the visitor asks the second question, and the verb changes shape. How did you end up in credit? He studied business, he started in customer service, and he moved into credit two years later. Each of those happened once and is over, so each verb carries the past form. The clue is usually next to it: in 2011, two years later, before that.

The rest of the answer follows the same two rules. The company makes crop protection products and is based in the interior. He reports to the finance director and is currently the only analyst in the region, which is true today and may change next year. He covers a wide range of customers, from a family farm to a group with an office in the capital, and his background is in numbers even though the job turned out to be about people. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: the past mark goes on ONE part of the verb only. Not Where did you studied?, but Where did you study?

1. "He works in credit and collections." Why the base form and not "he worked"?
A Because "work" is a verb that has no past form.
B Because it describes what is true every week, not something that finished.
C Because the sentence has no time expression in it.
2. Why is it "his colleague handles the north" and not "his colleague handle the north"?
A Because in the present the third person adds an -s to the verb.
B Because "the north" is singular.
C Because "handle" is an irregular verb.
3. The text warns against "Where did you studied?". What is the problem?
A "Where" cannot start a question about the past.
B The past is marked twice. "Did" already carries it, so the second verb stays in its base form.
C "Study" cannot be used in a question.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A credit analyst opens the same report every Monday. He has worked in credit for eight years and he has had this region since 2019. Both of those periods started in the past and are still open this morning, so the verb comes in two parts: have plus the past participle. The words next to them are the clue. For measures how long the period is; since says the point where it started.

Then the same person talks about the events that are closed. He joined the company in 2011 and he worked in customer service before that. Those happened once, on a date that is finished, so the past simple does the job. English is strict here: as soon as a closed time expression appears, the two-part verb is wrong. Not I have joined the company in 2011, but I joined the company in 2011.

The report itself is written in the same two tenses. Most of the portfolio is current, and most of the receivables are money the company has invoiced and is still waiting for. Four accounts have been overdue since March, and all four are the same kind of customer: a distributor that supplies a whole area and lives on the gap between two dates. Two of them have asked for a higher credit limit this season, which the analyst can only approve inside compliance. Nobody in the region has defaulted in three years, and nobody has had to chase a payment for long, because the payment terms follow the harvest. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: never say I work here since 2011. The present tense cannot carry since or for.

1. "I have worked in credit for eight years." Why not "I work in credit for eight years"?
A Because "work" cannot be used in the present tense.
B Because the period started in the past and is still open, and English will not let the present tense carry "for" or "since".
C Because eight years is too long a period for the present tense.
2. What is the difference between "for" and "since" in this text?
A "For" gives the length of the period; "since" gives the point where it started.
B "For" is formal and "since" is informal.
C "For" is used with years and "since" with months.
3. Why is it "I joined the company in 2011" and not "I have joined the company in 2011"?
A Because "join" has no past participle.
B Because the company still exists today.
C Because "in 2011" is a closed time, and a closed time takes the past simple.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody sits down next to you at a company dinner. They have already opened the red and the food has not arrived yet. The safest first move is not a fact about grapes. It is a question: have you ever tried a wine from this region? Notice what is not in that sentence. It does not say when, and it does not have to, because the question is about experience at any point in a life. That is the whole job of this tense.

The answers work the same way. I have never been to Portugal, so I would not know. I have just tried it, and it is drier than I expected. Quite bold, actually, and it would go well with the beef. Four small words do most of the work here. Ever asks about any time in a life. Never is already negative, so it never takes a second not. Just means a very short time ago. Already means sooner than expected, and yet lives in negatives and questions, at the end of the sentence.

Then comes the moment where English changes lane. As soon as you say WHEN, you leave this tense and take the past simple. We tried that pairing at a dinner last December. I read the tasting notes after we finished the bottle. That is the only real trap, and it catches almost everybody. The rest is easier than it looks: say whether you like it, say what it reminds you of, and hand the question back. Say that very sweet wine is not really my thing, or that you are genuinely into the history and the regions, and you have broken the ice without pretending to know anything. That is what a good table sounds like. Not one that says I have tried it last year, but one that says I tried it last year, and then asks a question.

1. Why is "Have you ever tried a wine from this region?" better than "Did you ever try a wine from this region?" at a dinner?
A Because the question is about experience at any point in a life, and that is what this tense is for.
B Because "did" is impolite in English.
C Because "try" cannot be used in the past simple.
2. What is wrong with "I have not never been to Portugal"?
A "Portugal" needs an article.
B "Never" is already negative, so the sentence does not take a second "not".
C "Been" should be "gone".
3. According to the text, what makes English change lane into the past simple?
A The sentence becoming a question.
B The subject changing from I to we.
C Saying WHEN it happened, for example "last December".

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A visitor from headquarters arrives once a quarterly cycle and asks small questions on purpose. If the harvest is late, the payments will move with it. Look at that sentence carefully, because it contains the whole rule. The first half describes a condition that is possible but has not happened. The second half says what follows. And here is the part that catches every Portuguese speaker: the first half stays in the present, even though it is obviously about the future. Never if the harvest will be late.

The same shape carries the plan for the meeting itself. If I do not catch the question, I will ask him to rephrase it. If I am still not sure, I will repeat back what I understood, so that we are on the same page. If a number matters, I will confirm it and follow up in writing. The two halves can also change places, and then the comma disappears: I will send an update if the figure changes.

There is one more version, and it is not about the future at all. If I do not follow up, customers do not pay. Nothing there is a possibility: it is simply how the world works, every season, and so both halves stay in the present. That is the difference to listen for. A possibility takes will in the second half; a permanent truth does not. And when something is certain rather than possible, English changes the word itself: not if the harvest comes, but when the harvest comes. Get those three apart and a hard question stops being dangerous: you can break down the revenue, name the target, and say exactly what you will do if it slips.

1. Why is it "if the harvest is late" and not "if the harvest will be late"?
A Because the harvest is not important enough for the future tense.
B Because the if-half stays in the present, however far in the future the situation is.
C Because "late" is an adjective and adjectives cannot take will.
2. "If I do not follow up, customers do not pay." Why is there no "will" in this one?
A Because it is not a possibility. It describes what always happens, so both halves stay in the present.
B Because the sentence is negative.
C Because "customers" is plural.
3. According to the text, when should you use "when" instead of "if"?
A When the sentence is about work rather than about life.
B When you want to sound more formal.
C When the thing is certain to happen, not merely possible.

The if-half stays in the present, however far in the future it is.

JobFormExample
A real possibility in the futureif + present simple, ... will + base verbIf the harvest is late, the payments will move.
The order can be swappedno comma when the if-half comes secondI will send an update if the figure changes.
Something that is always trueif + present simple, ... present simpleIf I do not follow up, customers do not pay.
Softer than willmay / might / can, in the second halfIf the price falls, we may have to extend the terms.
An instructionif + present simple, ... imperativeIf he calls again, put him through to me.
When, not ifuse when for what is certain, if for what is only possibleWhen the harvest comes · if the harvest is late
The trapnever will inside the if-halfNot if the client will not pay. Say if the client does not pay.

Never: if the client will not pay · if the harvest is late, the payments move with it in April · if the harvest comes in June, we will invoice then · can you repeat again more slowly?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

A visitor asks a question and you only catch half of it. You say:
A "Yes, exactly. That is our situation."
B "Sorry, could you say that again in a different way?"
C "Repeat again please, more slowly."
You want to say what happens if a big customer does not pay in April. You say:
A "If he does not pay in April, we will hold the next delivery."
B "If he will not pay in April, we hold the next delivery."
C "Case he not pay in April, we will hold the next delivery."
The visitor asks whether the region is under control. The answer that earns trust is:
A "Yes, everything is under control, no problem."
B "It is complicated, but we are working very hard on it."
C "Four accounts are late, all four are dealers, and I will know more after the harvest."
You do not have the exact figure in front of you. You say:
A "I think it is around forty, more or less, I am not sure."
B "Bear with me. I will confirm the figure and follow up this afternoon."
C "Sorry, my English is not good enough for this question."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.
