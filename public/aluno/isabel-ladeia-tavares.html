Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Back in the Rehearsal Room -- Talking About a Past That Ended

You walk into a rehearsal room you left two years ago, and somebody asks what you used to do there. Key words: a rehearsal, the cast, a script, a cue, to project, stage fright, to go blank, a warm-up, to drop out, backstage, an audition, rusty. Structure: used to and would for things that were true then and are not true now.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A rehearsal -- a meeting where the actors practice a play, long before any audience sees it
"We had a rehearsal every Saturday morning for three months."
The cast -- all the actors who perform in one play, taken together as a group
"Half the cast had never been on a stage before."
A script -- the written text of a play, with every single line the actors have to say
"I lost a whole page of the script in front of six hundred people."
A cue -- the word or the movement that tells you it is your turn to speak or to enter
"I could see the other actor waiting for the cue, and I had nothing."
To project -- to push your voice so the back row hears every word, without ever shouting
"You have to project, or the last three rows lose half the play."
Stage fright -- the fear that hits you in the minutes before you walk on stage
"People assume you grow out of stage fright. You do not."
To go blank -- to lose everything you knew, for a few seconds, while people wait
"I still go blank in the wings about twice a season."
A warm-up -- the exercises you do with your voice and your body before you perform anything
"Come twenty minutes early so you have time for a warm-up."
To drop out -- to leave a course or a group before it finishes
"I dropped out twice, and neither time was about the acting."
Backstage -- the part of the theater the audience never sees, on the other side of the curtain
"I get backstage an hour early now, and I do not negotiate with that."
An audition -- a short performance in front of a director, so that you can be given a part
"Auditions run on Tuesday and Wednesday, from six until half past nine."
Rusty -- out of practice, so that what you used to do easily now costs effort
"If you have not performed in a while and you feel rusty, come anyway."

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Isabel used to have a rehearsal every Saturday morning. The group would meet at nine, spend the first hour on a warm-up, and only then open the script. She used to be the youngest person in the cast, and the quietest one as well.

The first time she went blank, the other actor was already waiting for the cue, and there was nothing in her head at all.

1. "She used to be the youngest person in the cast." Why not "she would be"?
A Because would is only used in questions.
B Because that is how she was, not something she did. Would only works for actions that happened.
C Because would is informal and used to is formal.
2. "The group would meet at nine." How often did this happen?
A Regularly, over a period in the past. It was the normal pattern then.
B Once, on one specific Saturday.
C It has not happened yet, but it is planned.
3. "She did not use to get stage fright at that age." What does the sentence tell you about now?
A That she has never had stage fright in her life.
B That things changed. It was not true then, and the sentence suggests it is true now.
C Nothing at all. It is only about the past.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Four Paragraphs, Four Englishes -- The Anatomy of an Abstract

An abstract looks like one block of text and is really four boxes in a fixed order, each written in a different English. Key words: an abstract, a finding, a sample, a baseline, to assess, a trial, significant, to account for, peer-reviewed, a limitation, to rule out, to conclude. Structure: the sentence with nobody in it, which is how every method section is written.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Three of them mean something different from what they mean in everyday English.

An abstract -- the short paragraph at the top of a paper that tells you the whole study in four moves
"I open about thirty abstracts a week and read two papers properly."
A finding -- something a study discovered, reported as a result rather than an opinion
"The main finding was that the effect disappeared below ten degrees."
A sample -- the group of people, animals or things that a study actually looked at
"The samples were collected in March and stored at minus twenty."
A baseline -- the measurement taken before anything is done, so that later numbers mean something
"Two hundred seedlings were assessed at baseline and again after six weeks."
To assess -- to measure or judge how large, how strong or how good something is
"Growth was assessed every seven days by the same two researchers."
A trial -- a controlled test of a treatment or a method on a group, run under fixed conditions
"The data were analyzed in three independent trials."
Significant -- in a paper, unlikely to be the result of chance. It does not mean large or important
"The difference was significant, although it was only two percent."
To account for -- to be the cause of part of a result. In a paper it is not an excuse, it is a cause
"Temperature accounted for most of the variation between the two groups."
Peer-reviewed -- checked by other specialists before publication. It is a filter, never a guarantee
"Peer-reviewed does not mean correct. It means somebody qualified read it first."
A limitation -- a weakness that the authors admit their own study has
"A paper with no limitations is not stronger. It is quieter."
To rule out -- to show that an explanation is not possible, and remove it from the list
"Contamination was ruled out by running the whole thing again."
To conclude -- to state, at the end, the judgement that the results support
"The authors conclude that the evidence suggests a seasonal effect."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The abstract of a paper is four boxes in a fixed order, and the second box is the one that frightens most readers. It should not. Look at how it is written: samples were collected at four sites in March. Two hundred seedlings were assessed at baseline and again at six weeks. The data were analyzed in three independent trials.

Nobody appears in any of those sentences. The thing that had something done to it is standing at the front, and the person who did it is gone, because in a method section who did it is not the point. When the person does matter, one small word brings them back: growth was assessed by the same two researchers, and contamination was ruled out by repeating the procedure with fresh material.

The third box is where the numbers arrive, and where two words are easy to misread. The difference was significant, and it was only two percent: significant means unlikely to be chance, not large. Temperature accounted for most of the variation. The fourth box is the most careful English in the paper. The authors conclude that the evidence suggests a seasonal effect, they name one season as a limitation, and they never say that anything has been proved. Note also that the paper is peer-reviewed, which means it was read by specialists first, and nothing more than that.

1. "The samples were collected in March." Who collected them?
A The samples collected themselves.
B The sentence does not say, and in a method section that is on purpose.
C The reader of the paper.
2. "Growth was assessed by the same two researchers." What does the word by add?
A It says where the growth happened.
B It brings the person back, because here who did it is part of the point.
C It makes the sentence more formal, with no change in meaning.
3. The difference was two percent and the paper calls it significant. Why is that not a contradiction?
A Because significant is about chance, not size. A tiny difference can be significant.
B Because two percent is a large number in biology.
C Because the authors made a mistake in the results box.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Sentence With Nobody In It

GRAMMAR

Put the thing first, add was or were, and use the third form of the verb.

UseFormExample
Finished action, one thingwas + third formThe sample was collected.
Finished action, many thingswere + third formThe samples were collected.
Still relevant nowhas / have been + third formThe effect has been observed three times.
When the person matters+ by + the person or the thingGrowth was assessed by two researchers.
Negativewas / were not + third formThe plants were not watered for six days.
QuestionWas / Were + thing + third formWere the samples stored at minus twenty?

Never: The samples was collected · The data were analyze · Two hundred seedlings assessed at baseline · The seedlings were grown by in a greenhouse · The effect has observed three times.

The Choice You Have Not Made Yet -- Research or Teaching

Somebody asks what you are going to do with a biology degree, and you have three years before you have to answer. Key words: a career path, a trade-off, a fallback, rewarding, daunting, to specialize, a degree, to keep your options open, to weigh up, to put somebody off, to be cut out for something, to commit to something. Structure: talking about a life you have not chosen.

▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Five of the twelve are expressions.

A career path -- the sequence of jobs a person moves through over the years, in one direction
"My supervisor told me I was throwing away a research career path."
A trade-off -- something you give up in order to get something else. You cannot have both
"The trade-off is two extra semesters for a qualification I might never use."
A fallback -- the second plan, the one you keep for the case where the first one does not work
"People treat teaching as a fallback, and that is exactly the wrong reason to do it."
Rewarding -- giving you real satisfaction, even when the work itself is hard or slow
"Everybody says teaching is rewarding, and almost nobody says what it costs."
Daunting -- frightening because it is large or difficult, before you have even started it
"Choosing a life at eighteen sounds daunting until somebody explains what you are actually choosing."
To specialize -- to become an expert in one narrow area, and to stop doing the rest
"You do not have to specialize in the first year, and most people who do regret it."
A degree -- the formal qualification you receive when you finish a university course
"Doing both adds between two and three semesters to your degree."
To keep your options open -- to avoid deciding on purpose, so that more than one road stays possible
"I am keeping my options open, and that is a decision, not a lack of one."
To weigh up -- to compare the advantages and the disadvantages before you decide
"I have been weighing up two extra semesters against a qualification for life."
To put somebody off -- to make somebody stop wanting to do something, usually by what you say about it
"Everybody tried to put me off teaching, and none of them had ever taught."
To be cut out for something -- to have the right qualities and temperament for a particular kind of work
"I am not sure I am cut out for six hours of pipetting a day."
To commit to something -- to promise yourself to one course of action and stop considering the others
"Nobody is asking you to commit to one life at eighteen."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At this university nobody chooses a track in the first year. Isabel has three years before she has to declare, and she can declare both. That does not stop people asking, and the question sounds daunting until you notice what is really being asked. If she chose the teaching track tomorrow, she would stay two more years. If she did both, her degree would take three more semesters. That is the trade-off, and it is about time, not about identity.

What she is doing instead is weighing up two things at once. She is not going to commit to anything this year, and she is not going to specialize early either. If a friend asked her for advice, she would say the same: if I were you, I would sit in on one class from each track before signing anything.

The thing that annoys her is the word people use. They call teaching a fallback, as if it were what you do when the career path you wanted closes. Everybody who says that has tried to put somebody off it, and almost none of them has ever taught. She is not sure she is cut out for six hours of pipetting a day, and she is not sure she is cut out for a classroom either, and both of those are honest answers. So she is keeping her options open, which people hear as indecision and which is in fact the most rewarding thing she could be doing with a first year.

1. "If she chose the teaching track tomorrow, she would stay two more years." Has she chosen?
A Yes, she chose it yesterday.
B No. The sentence is imaginary, which is why the first half is in the past.
C The sentence does not say anything about her decision.
2. "If I were you, I would sit in on one class." Why were and not was?
A Because this is the fixed form for giving advice about an imaginary situation.
B Because the speaker is talking about more than one person.
C Because it happened in the past.
3. Why does the text say that keeping her options open is a decision?
A Because she has secretly already decided on the research track.
B Because the university does not allow first-year students to decide.
C Because she is refusing to close a road on purpose, which is a choice and not an absence of one.

Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

Read the Protocol, Do Not Guess -- The Recipe on the Bench

A protocol has no opinions and almost no subjects, and one word buried in the middle of a step can cost you an afternoon. Key words: a protocol, a reagent, to dilute, to incubate, a batch, to discard, a hazard, to label, to rinse, to weigh out, to make up a solution, thoroughly. Structure: the sentence that starts with a verb, and the small words that decide the order.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. You already do all twelve of these things every week.

A protocol -- the written set of steps that tells you exactly how to run an experiment
"Do not run the protocol the way it is printed. Step four changed last month."
A reagent -- a substance you add in order to make a reaction happen or to test for something
"This reagent must not be handled outside the fume hood."
To dilute -- to make a liquid weaker by adding water or another liquid to it
"Dilute the sample one to ten before you measure anything."
To incubate -- to keep something at a fixed temperature for a fixed time, so a process can happen
"Once the mixture is clear, incubate the tubes for twenty minutes."
A batch -- a whole group of samples prepared and processed together, at the same time
"The batch came out cloudy every single time."
To discard -- to throw something away, deliberately and in the right place
"Waste must be discarded in the marked container by the door."
A hazard -- something with the potential to cause harm, before anything has gone wrong
"The warning tells you where the hazard is, and it is written as must not."
To label -- to write on something what it is, so that the next person is not guessing
"Label every tube before you fill it, not after."
To rinse -- to wash something with clean water in order to remove what is left on it
"Do not rinse the glassware until the whole batch is finished."
To weigh out -- to measure out the exact quantity of a solid that a step asks for
"First, weigh out the powder. Then make up the solution."
To make up a solution -- to prepare a solution by mixing the substance with liquid to a set volume
"Make up the solution with the buffer from the fridge, not the one on the shelf."
Thoroughly -- completely and carefully, leaving nothing behind. The opposite of quickly
"Rinse thoroughly, except after the final incubation."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Here is protocol 7B, and every line of it is doing a different job. Before you start, label every tube. Weigh out two and a half grams of the powder. Make up the solution with the buffer from the fridge, not the one on the shelf. Notice what is missing from all three: there is no I, no you and no we. The sentence opens on the verb, because the protocol is not describing what somebody did. It is telling the next person what to do.

Allow the tubes to stand for five minutes before adding the reagent. Dilute each sample one to ten. Once the mixture is clear, incubate at thirty-seven degrees for twenty minutes. Those small words carry the whole experiment. Once and after mean that something has to have happened already; before and until mark where a step stops. Do not rinse the glassware until the batch is complete.

Then there are the two lines that are not steps at all. This reagent must not be handled outside the fume hood, and waste must be discarded in the marked container. Must is not should: it marks the hazard, and it is in the document because somebody was hurt. Finally, the line that catches everybody: rinse thoroughly, except after the final incubation. One word, in the middle of a step, and if you read at speed that is the word you miss.

1. "Label every tube." Who is being told to do it, and where is that word?
A The author of the protocol, and the word is missing by mistake.
B Whoever is reading it. The word is not there on purpose, because instructions have no subject.
C Nobody. The sentence is a description of what usually happens.
2. "Do not rinse the glassware until the batch is complete." When do you rinse?
A While the batch is running, whenever there is time.
B After the batch is finished. Until marks where the prohibition stops.
C Never, in this protocol.
3. Why does the text say that must is not the same as should?
A Because must marks a hazard and is a rule, not advice. It is there because somebody was hurt.
B Because must is more formal, and the meaning is the same.
C Because should is used in speech and must in writing.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Sentence That Starts With a Verb

GRAMMAR

The verb comes first, the small word decides the order, and must is never a suggestion.

UseFormExample
An instructionbase verb, no subjectDilute the sample.
A prohibitionDo not + base verbDo not rinse the glassware yet.
OrderFirst, then, after that, finallyFirst weigh out the powder. Then make up the solution.
Something must already be trueonce / afterOnce the mixture is clear, incubate.
Where a step stopsbefore / untilDo not rinse until the batch is complete.
The rule you cannot breakmust / must not + be + third formWaste must be discarded in the marked container.

Never: You must to weigh out the powder · Not rinse the glassware yet · Until the mixture is clear, incubate · This reagent should not be handled outside the fume hood · Waste must discarded in the container.

The Week That Does Not Fit -- Saying No Without Apologising

Somebody asks for two hours you do not have, and the long apology you improvise is not politer than one clear sentence. Structure: the buffer, the clear no, and the real alternative.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Five of the twelve are expressions.

A deadline -- the moment after which something is late, and there is no version of it that is not late
"I am afraid I do need an answer before Friday. That is a real deadline."
A clash -- two things booked at the same hour, where only one of them can actually happen
"We have a clash next week and I need to sort it out before Friday."
A slot -- a defined piece of time in a diary, kept for one thing and no other
"There is a slot on Thursday afternoon, same room, same length."
A workload -- the total amount of work somebody is carrying, which can grow while the job stays the same
"My workload has doubled and my hours have not."
To prioritize -- to decide what gets done first when there is not enough time for all of it
"Prioritizing is not choosing what matters. It is choosing what waits."
To reschedule -- to move something that was already agreed to a different day or time
"I would much rather reschedule something once, properly."
To push something back -- to move something to a later date, keeping it on the list
"We push the lab session back to Thursday afternoon, same room."
To fit something in -- to find room for something in a week that had no room in it
"I cannot fit it in this month, but I could in April."
To take something on -- to accept new work on top of what you are already carrying
"I am afraid I cannot take that on this month."
To double-book -- to promise the same hour to two different people, by accident
"I have double-booked myself twice this month, and both times on a Monday."
To be stretched thin -- doing so many things at once that none of them gets done properly
"It ends with me stretched thin and everybody slightly disappointed."
Overcommitted -- having agreed to more things than the hours in your week can hold
"Being overcommitted is not a personality. It is a diary problem with a fix."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Daniel asks Isabel for two extra afternoons a week, starting Monday, for six weeks. She has a lab placement four mornings a week, lectures until eleven every evening, and an outreach programme on Monday afternoons. The two hours he wants are not in her week. What she says is: I am afraid I cannot take that on before April, but I could do it then.

Three parts, in that order, and nothing else. I am afraid is the buffer: it tells him a no is coming, before it arrives. I cannot take that on is the no itself, said plainly, so nobody is left guessing. And but I could in April is the alternative, which is the part that stops it being a door closing. Notice what is not there: a reason. She never mentions the workload, the placement or the lectures.

That is not rudeness, it is arithmetic. An excuse invites a negotiation, because every reason is something the other person can try to solve for you. When she does need to explain, she keeps it to the shape of the diary rather than the shape of her life: Monday afternoons are a clash, and she would rather not double-book herself and let him down halfway through. And when she wants something changed, the same grammar goes the other way: would it be possible to push the whole thing back a month, and could we keep the same slot on Thursdays? A person who says all of that in four sentences is not being difficult. She is being easy to plan around.

1. "I am afraid I cannot take that on." Is she actually frightened?
A Yes. She is nervous about her supervisor’s reaction.
B No. It is a buffer, and its only job is to warn the listener that a no is coming.
C No, it is simply an old-fashioned way of saying sorry.
2. Why does the text say that giving no reason is kinder, not ruder?
A Because an excuse invites a negotiation: every reason is something the other person can try to solve.
B Because reasons are considered private in English-speaking workplaces.
C Because the other person is not really interested in the answer.
3. "Would it be possible to push the whole thing back a month?" What is this doing?
A Refusing the request a second time, more firmly.
B Asking for a change, with the same softened grammar pointed the other way.
C Asking permission to leave the project completely.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Shape of a Polite No

GRAMMAR

Buffer, clear no, real alternative — and no reasons.

UseFormExample
The bufferI am afraid… / Unfortunately…I am afraid I cannot take that on.
A soft preferenceI would rather not + base verbI would rather not commit to Tuesdays.
Asking for a changeWould it be possible to + base verbWould it be possible to push it back?
The alternativebut I could / but I can do X instead…this month, but I could in April.
Softer than cancould / would in place of can / willCould we look at Thursday?
LengthOne sentence beats fourA long excuse is not politer. It is an opening.

Never: I would rather not committing · Would it be possible push it back · No, I am very busy · Sorry, I have lectures and the placement and my project, so I really cannot.
