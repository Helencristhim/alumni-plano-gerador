The line at passport control moves slowly. When your turn comes, the officer asks four short questions, always in the same shape. "What is the purpose of your trip?" "How long are you staying?" "Where are you staying?" "Do you have anything to declare?" Notice what happens in every one of them: the little word is, are or do jumps in front of the subject. In Portuguese it stays behind, and that is exactly why a question in English often comes out sounding like a statement.

1. In "Where are you staying?", what comes immediately after the question word?

B The auxiliary: "Where are you staying?"

B "How long stay you in the country?"

B Nothing. A question mark is enough.
C Is, at the front: "Is you have anything to declare?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The order of a question

GRAMMAR

A question in English always puts the auxiliary in front of the subject.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- There is / there are and could

GRAMMAR

One structure says what exists in a place. The other asks for what you want.

StructureUseExample
There is + singularOne thing existsThere is a safe in the room.
There are + pluralTwo or more things existThere are two beds and one sofa.
Is there / Are thereAsk whether it existsIs there a pool on the roof?
Could I / Could you + base verbAsk politelyCould I have a late check-out?

Portuguese uses one verb for both ideas, and that is the trap. In English, have is only for what somebody owns ("I have a reservation"). For what exists in a place you always need there is or there are.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

A server hears the same three sentences all evening. "I would like a table for two." "I would like some water." "I would like to see the menu." Look at what changes and what does not. Would like never changes: it is the polite way to say what you want, and it works for every person. What changes is the word right after it. A table is one thing you can count, so it takes a. Water is not something you count, so it takes some. And when what you want is an action, not a thing, you need to before the verb. Say "I want" instead and the sentence is still correct English, but it sounds like an order, not a request.

1. Why do we say "some water" and not "a water"?
A Because water is always plural in English.
B Because water is uncountable, so it cannot take a or an.
C Because water is a drink and drinks never take a.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "I would like see the menu, please."
B "I would like to see the menu, please."
C "I would like to a menu, please."
3. According to the text, what is the difference between "I want" and "I would like"?
A "I want" is wrong English in a restaurant.
B They mean different things.
C Both are correct, but "I want" sounds like an order and "I would like" sounds like a request.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Would like + countable and uncountable

GRAMMAR

The word you put after would like depends on whether you can count what you are asking for.

You can count itYou cannot count itAn action
I would like a table.I would like some water.I would like to see the menu.
I would like an appetizer.I would like some rice.I would like to order now.
I would like two main courses.I would like some bread.I would like to pay by card.

Questions use the same shape: "Would you like a side dish?" The short answers are "Yes, please" and "No, thank you". And if you really need a number for something uncountable, count the container: "two glasses of water", "three cups of coffee".

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Directions in English are built from very small words, and each one belongs to one of two families. The first family tells you where something is: the station is on the corner, the crosswalk is between the bank and the hotel, your friend is waiting at the entrance. Nothing moves in those sentences. The second family tells you where you go: walk across the park, go along the avenue, get off at the second stop. Something is travelling from one point to another. When you are not sure which word to use, ask yourself one question: is anything moving here? If the answer is no, you need a word from the first family.

1. In "The station is on the corner", why do we use a place preposition?
A Because corners always take on.
B Because nothing is moving: the sentence says where the station is.
C Because the station is a building and buildings take on.
2. Which sentence uses a movement preposition?
A "My office is between two banks."
B "Walk across the park to the museum."
C "She is waiting at the entrance."
3. According to the text, what is the quickest way to choose the right preposition?
A Learn a list of nouns and the preposition each one takes.
B Ask whether anything is moving in the sentence.
C Use in when you are not sure.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Place and movement

GRAMMAR

Two families of prepositions, and one question that tells them apart: is anything moving?

FamilyWordsExample
Placein, on, at, between, opposite, next toThe station is on the corner.
Movementto, into, across, along, through, up, downWalk across the park.
Public transportget on / get offGet off at the second stop.
Cars and taxisget in / get out ofGet into the taxi at the door.

Two more traps for a Brazilian speaker: you arrive at a station, an airport or an office, and in only for a city or a country. And along, not in, is the word for following a street: "walk along this avenue", never "walk in this avenue".

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Nobody solves a problem before they explain it, and explaining it means putting every verb in the past. Some verbs are easy: I waited at the belt, I filled out the form, they promised me an answer. You add -ed and you are done. Others have their own past and you simply learn them: the airline lost my bag, the agent said nothing, I went to lost and found. Then something interesting happens in the negative and in the question. As soon as did appears, it takes the past for itself and the main verb goes back to its base form: "they did not give me a voucher", "did you speak to the manager?". The past is marked once, never twice.

1. Why is it "give" and not "gave" in "They did not give me a voucher"?
A Because give is a regular verb.
B Because did already carries the past, so the main verb goes back to the base form.
C Because the sentence is negative and negatives never use the past.
2. Which question is correct?
A "Did you filled out the form?"
B "Did you fill out the form?"
C "You filled out the form?"
3. Which sentence has an irregular past?
A "I waited at the belt for forty minutes."
B "The airline lost my bag in Chicago."
C "They promised me an answer."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past simple

GRAMMAR

Four shapes, and one rule that covers all of them: the past is marked once.

ShapeFormExample
Positive, regularverb + -edI filled out the form.
Positive, irregulara new word to learnThe airline lost my bag.
Negativedid not + base verbThey did not give me a voucher.
QuestionDid + subject + base verbDid you speak to the manager?

The irregular verbs you need tonight: go → went, lose → lost, take → took, say → said, give → gave, find → found, pay → paid. And be is the only verb with two past forms: was for I, he, she, it, and were for you, we, they.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Comparing in English depends on one thing only: how long the adjective is. Short adjectives take an ending. The second plan is cheaper than the first, and this shop is the cheapest in the street. Long adjectives keep their shape and borrow two words instead. That model is more expensive than this one, and it is the most expensive in the catalogue. You choose one system or the other, never both, and more cheaper is the single most common mistake a Brazilian speaker makes here, because in Portuguese the comparative always needs an extra word. Two adjectives refuse both systems and have forms of their own: good becomes better and the best, and bad becomes worse and the worst.

1. Why do we say "more expensive" and not "expensiver"?
A Because expensive is about money.
B Because it is a long adjective, so it takes more instead of an ending.
C Because it is an irregular adjective.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "This plan is more cheaper than that one."
B "This plan is cheaper than that one."
C "This plan is more cheap that that one."
3. What is the superlative of "bad"?
A the baddest
B the most bad
C the worst

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and superlatives

GRAMMAR

The length of the adjective decides the form. Two things take a comparative; one against all takes a superlative.

AdjectiveTwo thingsOne against all
cheap, big, longcheaper thanthe cheapest
expensive, reliablemore expensive thanthe most expensive
goodbetter thanthe best
badworse thanthe worst

Spelling: one syllable ending in one vowel plus one consonant doubles the consonant (big → bigger). An adjective ending in -y turns it into -i (easy → easier). And the word after the comparative is always than, never that.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

There is one question shape that almost never gets a one-word answer. "Have you ever been to Brazil?" "Have you ever worked with an American team?" It asks about a whole life, with no date attached, and the other person has to look back and find something. That is why it keeps a conversation alive when "Do you like your job?" kills it. The answer comes in two parts. If it is no, the word is never, and the verb stays positive: "I have never seen a race live." If it is yes, the moment you add a date the sentence changes tense: "Yes, I went there in 2019." Have and a date cannot live in the same sentence.

1. Why does "Have you ever been to Brazil?" work better than "Do you like Brazil?"
A Because it is a longer question.
B Because it asks about a whole life, so the other person has to find an experience to tell.
C Because it is more polite.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "I have not never worked abroad."
B "I have never worked abroad."
C "I never have not worked abroad."
3. What happens when you add a date?
A Nothing. "I have seen that race last year" is correct.
B The sentence goes into the past simple: "I saw that race last year."
C You have to add ever as well.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present perfect with ever and never

GRAMMAR

Experience with no date. Have or has plus the past participle.

ShapeFormExample
QuestionHave / Has + subject + ever + participleHave you ever been to Brazil?
Answer: nosubject + have / has + never + participleI have never seen it.
Answer: yesYes, + past simple for the detailYes, I went there in 2019.
Third personhas, never haveShe has never tried it.

The participles you need most here: be → been, see → seen, do → done, go → gone, eat → eaten, write → written. Regular verbs simply take -ed: worked, tried, enjoyed. And never already means no, so the verb after it never carries a second negative.
