Chapter 3: The Code

The Question That Turns Around

Indirect and embedded questions

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Could you tell me where we are on the indemnity?"

"I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed."

"Do you know who signs this on your side?"

"Can I ask what made you change the scope?"

Turn each coloured part back into a direct question and say it out loud. Something moves in every one of them, and in two of them a word disappears completely. What is it?

FormWhat it doesExample
no inversionInside the second half, the word order goes back to normal: subject, then verb. This is the whole rule, and it is the one that gets forgotten under pressure."Could you tell me where we are?"
not "where are we"
no do / does / didThe auxiliary belongs to the direct question and does not survive the move. What is left is the plain verb, carrying the tense itself."Do you know what he wants?"
not "what does he want"
if · whetherA yes/no question needs one of these to attach. Whether is the safer one in writing and before or not; if is everywhere in speech."I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed."
who · what as subjectWhen the question word IS the subject, nothing changes at all — there was never an inversion to undo. This is why these two feel easy and the others do not."Do you know who signs this?"
the openersFour that cover almost every meeting: Could you tell me..., Do you know..., I was wondering..., Can I ask.... Learn them as one block, not as grammar."I was wondering whether we could circle back to that."

Portuguese keeps the same word order in both versions, so nothing in your first language warns you that something has to move. That is why this survives so long in otherwise excellent English: the sentence sounds finished, and it is the one thing an American ear notices immediately.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Could you tell me where are we on the indemnity?"
"Could you tell me where we are on the indemnity?"
"Do you know what does he want from us?"
"Do you know what he wants from us?"
"I was wondering that the deadline is fixed."
"I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed."

The first keeps the inversion after it has already been used once. The second keeps does, which cannot survive the move — the tense goes onto the verb itself. The third uses that for a yes/no question: English needs if or whether there, and Portuguese uses que in both places, which is where the habit comes from.

Dialogue

Sorry — Can I Jump In?

M
Before we start, I have a hard stop at the top of the hour, so let us take the decisions first.
P
Sorry, can I jump in on that? I was wondering whether we could take the indemnity before the timeline.
M
We can. Go ahead — and please chime in whenever something on your side does not match.
P
Thank you. Could you tell me where we are on the cap? Our last version had a number and yours does not.
M
That was deliberate. We took the number out and left the mechanism, and I think we can find a workaround.
P
I would push back on that. Without a figure, the clause does not do the one thing we need it to do.
M
Fair. Do you know what number your board would actually accept, even as a ballpark figure?
P
I would rather not guess on the call. Can I run a figure by them today and come back to you before Friday?
Comprehension

About Megan

1. What does Megan say at the very beginning, and why does it matter?
She has a hard stop at the top of the hour, so the decisions have to come first.
2. What does she invite Pedro to do during the call?
To chime in whenever something on his side does not match.
3. What does she say happened to the number in the cap?
It was taken out deliberately; they left the mechanism and removed the figure.
4. What does she ask Pedro for at the end?
A number his board would actually accept, even as a ballpark figure.

As agreement, or as absence — not as politeness.

Because there is no gap: the meeting has forty minutes and eleven items.

Listening 2

What Was Actually Decided

A project lead on the last two minutes of every meeting. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What is the habit, and where did he get it?
Saying out loud at the end of every call what was decided, who is doing it and by when — from a colleague in Dublin.
2. Why does he say it matters?
Because half the room heard a different meeting: somebody heard a decision where there was only a discussion, or a maybe where there was a no.
3. What two things happen to the person who does it?
The room corrects them immediately, and their version of the meeting becomes the one that ends up in writing.
