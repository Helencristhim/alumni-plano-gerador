Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody asks who you are, and suddenly a whole life has to fit into four pieces of time. The first piece is closed. I grew up in the interior, and I played for a team in Franca. It happened, it finished, and English marks that with one shape: the past simple. Nothing in the sentence says whether it still matters. It only says it is over.

The second piece is open, and it is small. Right now I am studying six days a week. That is not who you are — it is who you are this year. English keeps a separate shape for that, with am or are in front and -ing behind, and it is the shape a Brazilian speaker uses least, because in Portuguese the same sentence can stay in the simple present. The third piece is the widest: your whole life up to today. I have traveled abroad six times. I have never lived outside Brazil. No date, no year — the counting starts at your birth and ends at this second.

The fourth piece holds what is simply true. I am eighteen. I come from a small city. I work out four times a week. Here the trap is famous, and it costs a Brazilian speaker the first ten seconds of every conversation: age never uses have in English. Not I have eighteen years. I am eighteen. And when the sentence has to say that a skill is rusty rather than gone, this is the piece of time it lives in: my English is rusty, in the present, because it is true right now.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of talking about the past, and choosing between them is not a question of grammar. It is a question of whether there is a fence around the story. He moved to Lisbon at sixteen. He trained there for four years. Both sentences carry a time you can point at — an age, a number of years — and the moment a time is named, the story closes. Nothing more can be added to it.

Take the fence out and the sentence behaves differently. He has never described those years as unhappy. He has not missed a deadline in three years. There is no date here, and that is the point: the counting started somewhere and it is still running as you read this. That is why since and for live in this shape and nowhere else. I have lived in Sao Paulo since last year: the living has not stopped. In Portuguese the same idea sits comfortably in the simple present, which is exactly why I live here since March is the most common mistake a Brazilian speaker makes in English, and one of the easiest to hear.

The trap runs in the other direction too. Once a sentence names its time — last year, in 2023, at sixteen, when I was a child — the present perfect becomes impossible. Not Last year I have traveled. Last year I traveled. And one more thing worth having automatic: with ever and never, the shape is always the open one, because both words mean at any point in a whole life. Have you ever given something up like that? The answer, if it comes with a date, closes: Yes, I did, in 2023.

B Because the sentence names a time, and a named time closes the story.

A Saying I live here since March instead of I have lived here since March.
B Saying he trained instead of he has trained.

B Because give is an irregular verb.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A routine and a day are not the same thing, and English uses two different shapes for them. The routine is what happens every day, and it lives in the present simple: I get up at twenty to six, I eat breakfast on the bus, I never study after half past nine. Notice that the continuous does not belong here. I am waking up at six every day is one of the most common Portuguese shadows in English, because in Portuguese the continuous can carry a habit. In English it cannot: a habit is simple, always.

Today is a different question. Today is a place you are standing in, somewhere between the beginning and the end, and three small words do all the work. Already says something is finished, and finished sooner than the listener expects: by seven I have already had breakfast. Just says it finished seconds ago: I have just finished a four-hour block. Yet says it has not happened and is still expected, and it only appears in negatives and questions: I have not started the biology unit yet.

The position of these three words is where the mistake actually happens, and it is worth more attention than the tense itself. Already and just go inside, between have and the verb. Yet goes at the very end. Not I have finished already the unit. Not Yet I have not started. One more pair worth fixing today: for takes a length of time and since takes a starting point. I have not had a day off for three weeks, and I have not had a day off since March. Both are correct. Since three weeks is not.

A Because wake is an irregular verb.
B Because a habit always takes the present simple in English, even though the continuous can carry one in Portuguese.
C Because six in the morning is too early to be a habit.
2. Where do already and just go, and where does yet go?
A Already and just go at the end, and yet goes inside.
B All three go at the end of the sentence.
C Already and just go between have and the verb. Yet goes at the very end.
3. Which pair does the text say is correct?
A for three weeks and since March
B since three weeks and for March
C for March and since three weeks

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of talking about a future that has not happened, and they are not levels of politeness. They are levels of belief. The first one is for a future you actually expect: if plus the present simple, then will. If I get the place, I will stay. If I do not get it, I will go. Both halves are about January, both are possible, and nothing in the sentence says which is more likely.

The second one is for a future you are only looking at. Here the verb after if steps back one, into the past, and will becomes would. If I weren’t studying for this exam, I would probably be training every day. The step back is not about time — the sentence is about now. It is a signal, and what it signals is: this is not happening. That is also why the verb to be takes were for every person in this shape. If I were you, not if I was you. You will hear was in speech, and you will never see it in an exam.

Two traps, and both come straight from Portuguese. The first is putting the future inside the if half: If I will pass. English does not allow it — the if half stays in the present, always. The second is the mirror image: If I would have more time. The would belongs in the other half. Say If I had more time, I would sleep more. One last thing, small and constant: in English something depends on something, never depends of.

B That the situation is not happening, even though the sentence is about now.

2. Why is “If I will pass, I will move” wrong?

B Because the if half stays in the present in English, always.
C Because you cannot use will twice in one sentence.
3. The text says you will hear one form in speech and never see it in an exam. Which?

B He said he had already finished it.
C He said me he already finished it.
Somebody asked you: “Do you agree?” You report it as:
A She asked me did I agree.
B She asked whether I agreed.
C She asked me do I agree.
Your coach said: “Push through the pain.” You report it as:
A He told me to push through the pain.
B He said me to push through the pain.
C He told to me push through the pain.
You want to name the source of a claim that is not yours. You say:
A For my teacher, the second day is harder.
B According to my teacher, the second day is harder.
C In according with my teacher, the second day is harder.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Report a real conversation from this week: what somebody said, what they asked you, what you answered, and one thing you were told to do. Use at least four different reporting verbs.
