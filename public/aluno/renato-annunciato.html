Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

The Role You Run -- Introducing Yourself to the Global Team

Your job, your scope and your people, in forty seconds of English. Key words: line manager, direct report, headcount, to oversee, scope, stakeholder, peer, cross-functional, in the field, account, to roll out, to be based in. Structures: present simple for the job and present continuous for what is happening right now.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. You already run every one of these in Portuguese.

Line manager -- the person you report to, one level above you
"My line manager is the Commercial Director in Sao Paulo."
Direct report -- a person who works for you and answers to you directly
"I have seven direct reports across the South and Southeast."
Headcount -- the number of people working in a team or a company
"The headcount in my area has not changed since last year."
To oversee -- to watch over work or people and make sure it goes well
"I oversee the commercial operation in two regions."
Scope -- everything your job covers, and the point where it stops
"Pricing is outside the scope of my role."
Stakeholder -- anyone with an interest in the result of your work
"The doctors are our most important stakeholders."
Peer -- someone on the same level as you, in another team or area
"I share the forecast with my peers in the other regions."
Cross-functional -- involving people from several different departments
"It is a cross-functional project with medical and marketing."
In the field -- away from the office, where the clients actually are
"I spend two days a week in the field with the team."
Account -- a client organisation that the company sells to and looks after
"Our largest account is a clinic group in Sao Paulo."
To roll out -- to start using something new across a whole region or company
"We are rolling out a new coverage plan this quarter."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the profile and answer the questions. Notice which verbs describe the job and which describe this quarter.

Renato is based in Sao Paulo and oversees the commercial operation for the South and Southeast of Brazil. He reports to the Commercial Director in Brazil, and he has seven direct reports, all of them consultants working in the field. The headcount in his area does not change very often. His peers run the other regions of the country, and they share the same targets.

The scope of his role covers coverage of the territory and protection of the key accounts; pricing sits with another area. The stakeholders in this market are the doctors who prescribe the product. At the moment his team is rolling out a new coverage plan for the largest accounts. It is a cross-functional project, so this quarter he is working with medical and marketing much more than usual, and he is covering part of the south himself while a new consultant is being hired.

1. The text says "he oversees the commercial operation" but "his team is rolling out a new plan". Why the difference?
A Overseeing the operation is the permanent job, so it takes the present simple. The roll-out started and will finish, so it takes the present continuous.
B "Oversee" is irregular and "roll out" is a phrasal verb.
C The present continuous is simply more formal in a company profile.
2. Why is it "he has seven direct reports" and not "he is having seven direct reports"?
A Because the number seven is fixed and numbers block the continuous.
B Because "have" here is a state verb, and state verbs stay in the simple form.
C Because the sentence is about the past.
3. "This quarter he is working with medical and marketing much more than usual." What does the tense tell the reader?
A It is temporary. It is not how his week normally looks, and it will go back to normal.
B It is a permanent change to his job description.
C It happened once, last quarter.
4. Which sentence would you use to describe the job to a global director?
A "I am overseeing two regions and I am reporting to the Commercial Director."
B "I oversee two regions and I report to the Commercial Director."
C "I oversee two regions and I am reporting to the Commercial Director every day."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous at Work

GRAMMAR

One question decides it every time: is that the job, or is that a project that will finish?

FormWhen you use itExample
present simpleThe job. True every week, with or without you thinking about it.I oversee two regions. I report to the Director.
am / is / are + verb-ingA project or a temporary situation. It started and it will finish.We are rolling out a new plan this quarter.
Third person, simpleHe, she and it take an -s: oversees, reports, covers.He reports to the Commercial Director.
Markers that pull the simpleevery week · usually · normally · twice a month · always · in general
Markers that pull the -ingat the moment · currently · this quarter · right now · this year · these days
Verbs that stay simpleState verbs never take -ing: know, have, need, understand, believe, own.I know this market. Never I am knowing.

The Territory and the Team -- Structure, Coverage and Comparison

Two regions, seven consultants and the language that compares them. Key words: territory, to cover, coverage, workload, to allocate, to rank, headquarters, gap, reach, backlog, to split, benchmark. Structures: comparatives and superlatives, and the as ... as pattern.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words on every territory slide you already build in Portuguese.

Territory -- the area and the clients one consultant is responsible for
"The Southeast is our largest territory."
To cover -- to be responsible for visiting and serving an area
"Three consultants cover the South."
Coverage -- how completely an area is actually visited and served
"Coverage in the South is ninety-one percent."
Workload -- the amount of work one person has to carry
"The workload in the Southeast is heavier."
To allocate -- to decide who gets a resource, and how much of it
"We allocate four consultants to the Southeast."
To rank -- to put people or things in order, from first to last
"Consultants are ranked on coverage and growth."
Headquarters -- the main office of the company, where the big decisions are made
"Headquarters has approved one additional position."
Gap -- a place where something needed is missing
"The main gap is in the interior of Sao Paulo."
Reach -- how many clients you are actually able to get to
"Reach is lowest in the interior of the state."
Backlog -- work that is still waiting and has not been done yet
"The backlog of unvisited accounts is longest there."
To split -- to divide something into two or more parts
"We will split the territory before the end of the year."
Benchmark -- a result you compare your own result against
"Our benchmark is one visit a month for every key account."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the review and answer the questions. Notice every comparison and what it is comparing.

Renato runs two territories. The Southeast is bigger than the South and carries the heaviest workload in the country: four consultants cover four states, and each of them looks after around sixty-two accounts. The South is smaller, with three consultants and around thirty-four accounts each.

The interesting part is that the bigger territory is not the better one. Coverage in the South is better than in the Southeast, ninety-one percent against eighty-four. The benchmark is one visit a month for every key account, and the Southeast is the furthest from it. Reach is lowest in the interior of Sao Paulo, where the backlog of unvisited accounts is the longest they have. That is the biggest gap in the country, and it is not as easy to close as it looks.

Headquarters has approved one more position, so Renato will split the territory this year. Consultants will be ranked on coverage first, growth second and backlog third, and the new person will be allocated to the interior.

1. Why is it "bigger than" and not "more big than"?
A Because "big" is a short adjective, and short adjectives take the ending -er, never "more".
B Because "more" is only used in questions.
C Because the sentence compares three or more things.
2. "The heaviest workload in the country." How many workloads is this comparing?
A Two — the Southeast and the South.
B All of them — the superlative points at one extreme inside a whole group.
C None — it is a description, not a comparison.
3. Why is coverage described as "better than" and not "gooder than"?
A Because "good" is a long adjective.
B Because "good" is irregular: good, better, the best.
C Because percentages always take "better".
4. "It is not as easy to close as it looks." What does this structure mean?
A The two things are not equal — closing it is harder than it appears.
B The two things are exactly equal.
C Closing it is impossible.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

One question decides the form: are you comparing two things, or pointing at one extreme in a whole group?

TypeComparative (two things)Superlative (the extreme)
One syllablebig → bigger than · long → longer thanthe biggest · the longest
Ending in -yheavy → heavier than · easy → easier thanthe heaviest · the easiest
Three syllables or morecomplex → more complex than · expensive → more expensive thanthe most complex · the most expensive
Irregulargood → better · bad → worse · far → furtherthe best · the worst · the furthest
Saying they are equalas ... as — "The South is as important as the Southeast." Negative: not as ... as.
Making it strongermuch / far / a lot + comparative · by far + superlative — "Sao Paulo is by far the largest."
Three trapsNever more big. Never bigger that — the word is than. Never more better — one comparison per adjective.

Reporting the Numbers -- Targets, Growth and the Quarter

The headline number, what drove it and what happens next quarter. Key words: target, quota, forecast, to hit, to miss, growth, market share, pipeline, revenue, shortfall, to outperform, year to date. Structures: present perfect for the open period and past simple for the finished one.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. You manage every one of these in Portuguese, every month.

Target -- the result a team is expected to reach in a period
"The target for the region this year is forty million."
Quota -- the individual share of the target that one person carries
"Each of my seven consultants has a monthly quota."
Forecast -- what you expect to sell in the months ahead
"The forecast for the fourth quarter is above the target."
To hit -- to reach a number you were expected to reach
"In March the team hit the quota for the first time."
To miss -- to finish below a number you were expected to reach
"Last month we missed the forecast in Rio de Janeiro."
Growth -- the increase from one period to the next
"Growth came from the clinics in the interior."
Market share -- the slice of the whole market that belongs to your product
"Our market share has risen two points since the launch."
Pipeline -- all the deals you are working on that have not closed yet
"There are three large clinics in the pipeline for next quarter."
Revenue -- the money the business brings in from what it sells
"Revenue in the South is stable year on year."
Shortfall -- the amount by which you finished below the number
"The shortfall sits in one territory, not across the region."
To outperform -- to do better than the number, or better than the others
"Two consultants outperformed their quota this quarter."
Year to date -- from the first of January until today, with the year still open
"Year to date we have reached ninety-four percent of the target."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the report and answer the questions. Notice which verbs take have and which do not.

Renato opens the quarterly review with the headline number. Year to date the region has reached ninety-four percent of the target, and revenue in the South is stable. Two things explain it. In April two consultants outperformed their quota in the interior, and since January the market share of the product has risen two points. That is where the growth came from.

Then he flags the part nobody asked about. Last month the team missed the forecast in Rio de Janeiro, and the whole shortfall sits in that one city. The board has not decided on the extra investment yet, so the fourth quarter depends on the pipeline: three large clinics, two of which would be enough. If they close, the region hits the target in December. Renato has been working on those three since Monday.

1. The text says "year to date the region has reached" but "in April two consultants outperformed". Why the difference?
A "Year to date" is still open, so it takes the present perfect. "In April" is a finished, named month, so it takes the past simple.
B "Reach" is irregular and "outperform" is regular.
C The present perfect is simply more formal in a report.
2. Why is it "since January the market share has risen"?
A Because "since" can take either tense in a business report.
B Because "since" marks a period that runs from a past point up to now, and that always takes the present perfect.
C Because market share is a percentage, and percentages take the present perfect.
3. "The board has not decided on the extra investment yet." What does the tense tell the reader?
A The decision is still open and still expected — it matters right now.
B The decision was rejected at some point in the past.
C The decision belongs to a period that is already closed.
4. Why is it "Renato has been working on those three since Monday"?
A Because the work finished on Monday.
B Because "work" cannot be used in the past simple.
C Because it started in the past and is still going on today, which is what have been + verb-ing shows.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect and Past Simple in a Report

GRAMMAR

One question decides it every time: is that period finished, or are you still inside it?

FormWhen you use itExample
have / has + past participleThe period is still open, or the result matters right now.Year to date we have reached ninety-four percent.
past simpleThe time is finished and named, or both sides know which time you mean.In March the team hit the quota.
have been + verb-ingStarted in the past and still going on today.I have been working on the forecast since Monday.
Markers that pull haveyear to date · so far · this quarter · since January · over the last six months · already · not yet
Markers that block itlast quarter · last month · in March · in 2025 · yesterday · two weeks ago
Negative and questionhave not / has not + participle · Have you...?The board has not decided yet. Have you seen the forecast?
Two trapsNever We have hit the target in March — a named finished month takes the past simple. Never I am working on it since Monday — since takes have been working.

The Client Is a Doctor -- Prescribers, Advocacy and Access

The doctors who prescribe, the ones other doctors follow, and the report that comes after the visit. Key words: prescriber, key opinion leader, advocacy, referral, clinic, private practice, to endorse, indication, consultation, waiting list, protocol, credibility. Structures: relative clauses with who, which, that, where and whose.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. Every one of these is a word you already use in Portuguese, in every cycle meeting.

Prescriber -- a doctor who decides which medicine a patient will take
"She is the strongest prescriber in the territory."
Key opinion leader -- a doctor other doctors listen to and follow
"She is a key opinion leader, not simply a large account."
Advocacy -- public support from a doctor who recommends your product
"Her advocacy has cooled since March."
Referral -- when one doctor sends a patient to another doctor
"She wants a referral pathway to three colleagues."
Clinic -- a place where doctors see patients, smaller than a hospital
"Our largest clinic is in the west of the city."
Private practice -- a doctor's own business, not owned by a hospital or the state
"She runs a private practice in the west of Sao Paulo."
To endorse -- to say publicly that you support a product or a method
"She endorsed the protocol publicly at the congress."
Indication -- a medical condition that a product is approved to treat
"Two of the indications she treats are covered by a competitor."
Consultation -- an appointment where a doctor sees one patient
"Her consultations are booked eight weeks ahead."
Waiting list -- the patients who are waiting for an appointment or a procedure
"The waiting list for the procedure is the longest in the region."
Protocol -- the set of steps a doctor follows to treat a condition
"The protocol we presented last year is still the one she follows."
Credibility -- the quality of being believed and trusted by other people
"Her credibility with younger specialists is worth more than one account."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the visit report and answer the questions. Notice every clause that describes a person, a thing or a place.

Doctor Prado, who runs a private practice in the west of Sao Paulo, is the strongest prescriber in the territory. She is a neurologist whose consultations are booked eight weeks ahead, and the waiting list for the procedure is the longest in the region. She is also the doctor who other specialists call when they are not sure, which is what makes her a key opinion leader.

The protocol that we presented at the congress last year is still the one she follows, and she endorsed it publicly at the time. What changed is not the science. Two of the indications she treats most often are now covered by a competitor programme that pays for the first consultation, so her advocacy has cooled since March.

She asked for a summary of the safety data which she can show to the residents she trains, and for a referral pathway to three clinics in the east. The clinic where she sees her patients is small, but her credibility with younger specialists is worth more to us than any single account.

1. "Doctor Prado, who runs a private practice, is the strongest prescriber." Why are there commas around the clause?
A Because we already know which doctor she is — the clause only adds extra information.
B Because the sentence is long and commas make it easier to read.
C Because the clause tells us which doctor we are talking about.
2. "She is a neurologist whose consultations are booked eight weeks ahead." What does "whose" replace here?
A The word "which", because consultations are things.
B The possessive "her" — her consultations are booked eight weeks ahead.
C The word "who", with no change of meaning.
3. "The protocol that we presented last year is still the one she follows." Could you remove "that we presented last year"?
A No — without it the reader does not know which protocol. It is a defining clause, so there are no commas.
B Yes — it is only extra information, like the clause about Doctor Prado.
C Yes — clauses with "that" can always be removed.
4. "The clinic where she sees her patients is small." Why "where" and not "that"?
A Because "clinic" is a place, so it always takes "where".
B Because something happens in the clinic — she sees patients there. If the clinic were the object of the verb, it would be "that".
C Because "that" cannot be used after a noun.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

Two questions decide everything: what am I pointing at, and do I need this clause to know which one?

WordWhat it points atExample
whoPeople: doctors, consultants, patientsThe doctor who prescribes the most is in Campinas.
which / thatThings: a protocol, an account, a programmeThe protocol that we presented last year.
whereA place, when something happens thereThe clinic where she sees her patients.
whosePossession — it replaces his, her, theirThe consultant whose territory is the interior.
Defining (no commas)The clause says which one. Take it away and the sentence stops making sense. that is allowed.
Non-defining (commas)The clause only adds a detail about somebody already named. Use who or which — never that.
Dropping the pronounWhen the clause has its own subject, you can leave the pronoun out: "the summary (that) she asked for".
Three trapsNever the doctor which. Never the clinic where I visited — you visit the place itself, so it is the clinic that I visited. Never the consultant that his territory — use whose.

"Doctor Bennett, runs the largest clinic, will speak at the congress."
"Our largest account, is a clinic group, is on the same protocol."

Stage 2: Put the Visit Report in Order

Order

Listen first, then put the four parts of the visit report in the correct order.

?She asked for a written summary of the safety data that she can show to her residents.
?I visited Doctor Prado, who runs a private practice in the west of Sao Paulo.
?I committed to mapping the three clinics she refers her patients to.
?She is the strongest prescriber in the territory, but her advocacy has cooled since March.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen to each sentence, then record yourself saying it. Aim for the rhythm, not for speed.

Doctor Prado, who runs her own private practice, is our strongest prescriber.
She is the doctor other specialists call when they are not sure.
Her advocacy has cooled since March, and I wanted to understand why.
She asked for a summary that she can show to her residents.
I will confirm whether those clinics qualify, and I will come back either way.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

Your director asks who you visited. You start with:
A "Doctor Prado, who runs a private practice in the west, is our strongest prescriber."
B "Doctor Prado, that runs a private practice in the west, is our strongest prescriber."
C "Doctor Prado, which runs a private practice in the west, is our strongest prescriber."
You want to say which protocol you mean. You say:
A "The protocol, that we presented last year, is still in use."
B "The protocol that we presented last year is still in use."
C "The protocol where we presented last year is still in use."
A doctor is a big prescriber, but nobody asks for her opinion. She is:
A A key opinion leader.
B A strong prescriber, but not a key opinion leader.
C A referral.
She says she will not support the product publicly until she reads the data. She will not:
A Prescribe it.
B Endorse it.
C Refer it.
You have twenty minutes with a doctor between consultations. The best opening is:
A A full presentation of every indication the product treats.
B One question about what changed since the last visit.
C A request for her public support at the next congress.
She asks something you cannot answer. You say:
A "I think it is probably yes, but I am not completely sure."
B "I do not know yet. I will check and come back to you this week, either way."
C "That is a question for another department, not for me."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the prompt below. There is no right or wrong answer — bring the recording to class.

Report a real visit in sixty seconds, using the three layers: who the doctor is and what they run, what they asked for or objected to, and what you committed to do next, with a date. Use at least one clause with who and one with that.
