Priming 1: The text

Input

Read it once, straight through, without a dictionary. You are not being tested on it. It is here so that the language is already in your ear when the negotiation starts.

When the earn-out went quiet

In cross-border deals, the clause that causes the most litigation is rarely the one the parties argued about. It is the earn-out: the part of the price that the seller only receives if the business hits certain targets after closing. On paper it looks like a fair compromise, and it is often sold to the seller as a vote of confidence. In practice it hands the buyer control of the very numbers that determine what the seller gets paid.

A common pattern goes like this. The seller accepts a lower headline price in exchange for an earn-out worth thirty per cent of the total. The buyer, now running the company, decides to invest heavily in a new market. The investment is defensible, even sensible, and it depresses profit for exactly the two years the earn-out is measured over. The seller receives nothing, and nothing improper has happened. There is no bad faith to point at, no clause that was broken, and therefore very little to litigate.

Experienced counsel therefore stop negotiating the number and start negotiating the definitions. What counts as profit. Whether central overheads allocated from the parent company are deducted before the calculation. Who prepares the accounts, and what happens if the seller disagrees with them. Whether the buyer owes any duty to operate the business so as to give the earn-out a fair chance. English courts have been reluctant to imply that duty where the contract is silent, which means silence is a choice with consequences.

There is a second trap that arrives later. Even where the definitions are tight, the seller usually has no mechanism to see the workings. A clause that promises audited accounts within ninety days is worth little if the seller cannot ask a question about how a line was built. Practitioners who have lived through one of these disputes tend to insist on two unglamorous things: an independent expert named in advance, and a right of access to the underlying records, exercisable without a court.

The practical lesson is uncomfortable, because it asks the party with less power to spend what little leverage it has on something that feels procedural. In an earn-out, that party should spend its remaining leverage not on price, but on the machinery that measures performance. Ask who holds the pen after closing. Ask who checks the arithmetic, and who pays for the check. Whatever the answers are, write them down.

Priming 2: Twelve moves you will meet

Vocabulary

Do not study these. Listen to each one once and move on. They are not vocabulary items to learn — they are moves, and you will discover what they do in class, after you have already needed them.

I'd want to understand what sits behind that number.
Probes without accusing anyone of anything.
Help me understand the rationale.
Same job, warmer register.
That's workable in principle, provided that...
A yes that costs the other side something.
On that basis, yes.
Four words. Closes a point without reopening it.
Let me push back on one thing.
Announces the disagreement before making it.
I'm not going to be able to agree to that as drafted.
A no that blames the drafting, not the person.
Where does that leave my client if...?
Tests the downside out loud, so the other side hears it too.
What happens in the scenario where...?
Same move, more neutral.
I can live with the number if we fix the definition.
Trades the thing you can lose for the thing you actually want.
We could move on that, but I'd need something back.
Never concede for free, and say so out loud.
Can we park that and come back to it?
Defers without conceding. Buys thinking time.
Let me take that away and revert.
Ends a call without saying yes or no.

Priming 3: Notice, do not answer

Input

Two things to look for while you read. There is no answer key, and none of this is checked in class — noticing is the whole exercise.

1. Go back to the third and fourth paragraphs. The writer lists the questions experienced counsel ask. Underline them. How many are about the number, and how many are about who controls it?
2. None of the twelve moves above says a plain yes or a plain no. Read them once more with that in mind. What are they doing instead? Do not write an answer — just hold the question until class.

Priming 4: Say them once

Speaking

Two only. This is not about meaning — it is about your mouth having been there before. Both are long, and both are the kind you will need in one breath while somebody waits.

That's workable in principle, provided that the accounts are reviewed independently.
I can live with the number if we fix the definition.
Where does that leave my client if the buyer opens a new market?

Priming 5: Bring the deal

Reflection

The task in class is a negotiation with a real shape. It works if you arrive with a real one in your head.

Think of a deal you have worked on where the seller had less leverage than the buyer. Record 60 seconds in English: what the seller wanted, what they had to give up, and the one clause you would fight harder for if you did it again. No legal analysis — talk as if to a client who is not a lawyer.
One sentence, out loud, in English: when you negotiate in your second language, what is the first thing you lose — precision, speed, or the willingness to disagree? Say it and record it. Your teacher will come back to this at the end of the class.
