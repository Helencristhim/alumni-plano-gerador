Lesson 01 — Pre-class

More Than My Name

Ten words that keep an introduction going (be into, hang out, get along with, in common, spare time, at the moment, most of the time, actually, an only child, How about you?) and the choice between the present simple and the present continuous.

Stage 1.1: Ten Words for a First Conversation

Vocabulary

Tap Listen, then say the word out loud. Ten words, three minutes.

Be into — to like something a lot and do it often
"I'm really into volleyball, but I don't play in a team."
Hang out — to spend free time with friends, with no plan
"After school we just hang out at somebody's house."
Get along with — to have a good relationship with somebody
"I get along with my cousins better than with my classmates."
In common — something two people both like or both have
"We have one thing in common: neither of us can cook."
Spare time — the hours when you are free, after school
"In my spare time I watch a lot of series."
At the moment — in this period of your life, not forever
"At the moment I'm learning two languages at the same time."
Most of the time — almost always, but not always
"Most of the time I walk home, but on Fridays I don't."
Actually — you say it to add a fact people do not expect
"Everyone thinks I'm from here. Actually, I moved last year."
An only child — a person with no brothers and no sisters
"I'm an only child, so the dog gets all the attention."
How about you? — the question that gives the turn back
"So that's my week. How about you?"

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the introduction. Then answer the questions.

Hi, I am Ruby and I live in Manchester. I am twelve. I am really into drawing, and I play the drums on Tuesdays. Most of the time I hang out with my cousin, because she lives on the same street and we get along with each other really well. At the moment I am learning Spanish at school, and honestly it is not going very well. I am also reading a book in Spanish this month, which was a bad idea. Actually, I am an only child, so my cousin is basically my sister. That is me. How about you?
Why is it "she lives on the same street" and not "she is living on the same street"?
A Because it is a permanent fact about her, not something happening this month.
B Because "live" can never take the -ing form.
Which one is only true for now, and will probably change?
A "I play the drums on Tuesdays."
B "I am reading a book in Spanish this month."
Why does she start a sentence with "Actually"?
A To add a fact the reader was not expecting.
B To say that everything before it was false.
What does the last line do?
A It closes the conversation politely.
B It gives the turn to the other person, so the conversation continues.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Present simple × present continuous

One tense is for your life. The other is for your week.

Present simple

I play volleyball.
She plays volleyball.
I don't play in a team.
Do you play every week?

Present continuous

I am learning English.
She is learning Spanish.
I am not learning French.
Are you learning it now?

Use the simple for routines, facts and the person you are: every week, on Saturdays, usually, most of the time, never.
Use the continuous for what is happening now or around now, and will change: at the moment, right now, this week, this year, today.
Do not forget the -s in the third person of the simple: she plays, he lives, it works. It sounds like a Z.
In questions the simple needs do or does: What do you do in your spare time? — never What you do...?

The Club Profiles

Stage 1.1: Ten Words About Routines

Vocabulary

Tap Listen, then say the word out loud. Ten words, three minutes.

Member — a person who belongs to a club or a group
"She's the newest member, so everyone asks her questions."
Join — to become part of a group
"I joined the club last month and I still don't know everyone."
Weekday — any day from Monday to Friday
"On weekdays I get up at six. On weekends, never."

"On weekdays my routine hardly ever changes."

"We swap books by mail, which takes three weeks each way."

"I don't know the word, but I'll give it a go and describe it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the profile. Then answer the questions.

I joined this club last month, so I am the newest member. On weekdays my routine hardly ever changes: I always get up at six, and I am always at school by half past seven. I play basketball twice a week and I am quite good at it, although I never watch it on television. I can't stand losing, which my brother says is a problem. Once in a while I swap books with a friend in another country, and it takes three weeks each way. I sometimes want to stop, but then I give it a go one more time.
Why is it "I always get up" but "I am always at school"?
A The adverb goes before a normal verb, but after the verb "to be".
B Both are possible and they mean different things.
Why does "twice a week" come at the end of the sentence?
A Because it is a negative expression.
B Because frequency expressions with a number go at the end, not before the verb.
Does the writer ever watch basketball on television?
A No — "never" means zero times.
B Yes, but almost never.
Which is more frequent: "hardly ever" or "once in a while"?
A "Hardly ever" — it is closer to "sometimes".
B "Once in a while" — "hardly ever" is almost never.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Adverbs of frequency — where the little word goes

It is not about the adverb. It is about the verb next to it.

Position

I always get up early.
I am always tired.
She never watches it.
She is never late.
I swim twice a week.

The scale

always — 100%
usually — almost every time
often — a lot
sometimes — now and then
hardly ever — almost never
never — 0%

Before a normal verb: I usually walk home. After the verb "to be": I am usually at home by five.
Expressions with a number go at the end: twice a week, three times a month, every single day. They can also open the sentence: Once in a while, we swap books.
Never is already negative. Say I never watch it — never I don't never watch it.
Questions: the adverb stays after the subject. Do you usually walk home? And to ask about frequency: How often do you...?

Lesson 03 — Pre-class

Landing in Athens

You have just landed in Athens: gate C12, a bag that has not arrived, and no euros yet. Before the lesson: eight airport words — boarding pass, customs, currency, gate, shuttle, landmark, overbooked, layover — and the difference between telling the experience (I have been to Athens) and telling the story (I went in July).

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Eight airport words. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Boarding pass -- the thing you show before you get on the plane
"My boarding pass says gate C12, but the screen says C7."

"The flight was overbooked, so they put me on a later one."
Layover -- the wait between two flights of the same trip
"I had a three-hour layover in Madrid."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read about my cousin. The verbs in bold switch between the experience and the story.

My cousin flies a lot. She has been to nine countries and she has never missed a flight. Last summer she flew to Lisbon on her own for the first time.

The flight was overbooked, so they put her on a later one with a three-hour layover in Madrid. She waited at the gate and asked somebody where to change her currency. Since that day she always asks. She says the worst part is still customs, and that she hasn't found a way to enjoy that bit yet.

Why is it "she has been to nine countries" and not "she went to nine countries"?
A It is her experience so far, with no date attached.
B Because it happened a long time ago.
C Because nine is a big number.
And why "last summer she flew to Lisbon"?
A Because she does it every summer.
B There is a date, so it is the story, not the experience.
C Because she is still in Lisbon.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

The experience, then the story

Nothing new today — it is the present perfect you already know, next to the past simple.

  • Present perfect — have / has + past participle. The experience, no date.
    "I've been to Athens." · "My bag hasn't arrived yet."
    friends of this tense: ever, never, yet, already
  • Past simple — the past form. The story, with a date.
    "I went in July." · "They changed the gate an hour ago."
    friends of this tense: yesterday, last July, an hour ago

The trap. Once you say the date, the present perfect is gone. Not "I have been there last July" — either "I've been there" or "I went there last July".

The order that sounds natural: experience first, story second. "I've been to Athens. I went in July and it was forty degrees."

Vocabulary

Eight restaurant words. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Recommend -- to tell somebody that something is good and they should try it

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the advice. The words in bold are your plan B, said before you need it.

Here is what I tell my cousin before every trip. Say the difficult thing FIRST, at the door, before you sit down. If you have an allergy, you will need one sentence, and you will need it in the first thirty seconds.

Then it gets easy. If the table isn't ready, you 'll wait outside. If the dish you want isn't available, you 'll ask what they recommend. If the portion is enormous, you 'll share it. And if the plate comes out wrong, you 'll say so in one sentence — you do not have to complain, you just have to say it.

"If the table isn't ready, you'll wait outside." Is this a dream or a real possibility?
A Real — it can easily happen tonight.
B A dream, like winning the lottery.
C Something that already happened.
Why is it "if the table isn't ready" and not "if the table won't be ready"?
A Because it is about the past.
B After if we use the present, even when we mean the future.
C Both are fine.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

First conditional — your plan B

For things that can really happen, tonight, in that restaurant.

  • If + present simple  →  will / 'll + verb
  • "If the table isn't ready, we'll wait outside."
  • "If they don't have it, I'll try something else."
  • The other half can be an instruction: "If you have an allergy, say it first."

The trap. Never will after if. Not "if the table will not be ready" — the if half stays in the present, even though you are talking about later.

And the politeness scale

  • "Give me..." — rude, do not use it
  • "I want..." — sounds like a child
  • "Could I have..." — this is the one you want
  • "I was wondering if I could..." — very formal, for special requests
