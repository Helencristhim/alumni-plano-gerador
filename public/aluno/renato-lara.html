Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

The verb to be — and why it is never missing

Every English sentence needs a verb. To say who you are, where you are or how you feel, that verb is am, is or are.

Full form

I am
You are
He / She / It is
We / They are

Short form

I'm
You're
He's / She's / It's
We're / They're

+ The swell is big today.
− The swell is not big today. (short form: isn't)
? Is the swell big today? (the verb moves to the front — never "Do you are")
→ Yes, it is. / No, it isn't.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Present simple — things that are always true

Routines, habits and facts. The verb never changes — except after he, she and it, which add an -s.

I / you / we / they

I check
I don't check
Do you check?

He / she / it

He checks
He doesn't check
Does he check?

Only one -s per sentence. If does or doesn't is there, the verb goes back to normal.
Spelling: watch → watches, finish → finishes, go → goes, study → studies.
Where the adverb goes: I always check. He never surfs on Sunday. (before the verb)

The form

I am watching
He is paddling
They are cheering
He isn't winning
Are you watching?

Which one, when

every day, always, never
→ he surfs
now, right now, at the moment
→ he is surfing

Spelling: ride → riding · win → winning · sit → sitting · watch → watching.
In fast speech: he's paddling, they're cheering, what's happening? (the verb is small, but it is there)

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Was and were — the past of the verb to be

In the present you choose between three words. In the past there are only two.

was

I was
He / She / It was
I wasn't

were

You were
We / They were
They weren't

? Was it a good final? / Were you there? (no "did" — the verb jumps to the front, exactly like in Lesson 1)
→ Yes, I was. / No, we weren't.
There: There was a delay. / There were two rookies in the final.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Past simple — it already happened

Most verbs add -ed. Some change into a new word. The form is the same for I, you, he, we and they.

Regular

paddle → paddled
borrow → borrowed
rescue → rescued

Irregular

break → broke
take → took
give up → gave up

+ My leash broke and I lost the board.
− I didn't give up. (not "didn't gave")
? Did you borrow a board? (not "Did you borrowed")
One past per sentence. If did has it, the verb does not.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Read the note. Then answer the questions.

Welcome! A few things. The kitchen is open all day, so help yourself — you don't have to ask. You can use the bikes in the garage any time. You have to put the bins out on Tuesday night and you have to do your own laundry on Sunday. On school nights you have to be home by nine thirty, but at the weekend you can stay out later if you send a message. You don't have to cook, although you can help if you want. A The first one is free choice; the second one is forbidden.
B There is no real difference.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Can, can't, have to, don't have to

Four ways to talk about rules. The verb after all of them is bare: can go, have to go.

can → it is allowed. You can surf before school.
can't → it is forbidden. You can't take the car.
have to → it is obligatory. You have to lock up. (he / she / it: has to)
don't have to → it is not necessary, but you may. You don't have to cook.
? Can I use the kitchen? / Do I have to do my own laundry?
Careful: never "can to", never "cans", never "don't can". It is can, can't, and that is it.

He has won two titles.
