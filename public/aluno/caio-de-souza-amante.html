Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A buyer in New York asks a founder what his company does, and the founder answers well — the numbers are right, the story is clear, the strategy holds. And still, something in the room moves half a step back. Later nobody can say exactly what it was. It was one letter. Dataside specialize in data, he said. The buyer want a faster close. Three or four times in ten minutes, and each time the sentence arrived a little less finished than the thought behind it.

English marks one person in the present simple, and only one. When the subject is he, she or it, the verb carries an -s: she wants, he runs, it costs. Everybody else takes the bare verb: I run, we specialize, they want. The trap for a Portuguese speaker is not the rule — the rule is simple and most speakers can recite it. The trap is the subject that does not look like a person. Dataside has three hundred people, because a company is an it. Revenue comes from consulting, because revenue is an it too. The private equity firm wants three years of numbers. Every one of those subjects is a single thing, and every one of them takes the -s.

In the negative and in questions the -s moves house. It leaves the main verb and goes to do: he does not move on price, and does the buyer need more time? Saying she does not wants puts it in both places, which is the second most common version of the same mistake. And there is a habit that travels from Portuguese with it: depends of instead of it depends on. Neither one changes what a founder means. Both change how finished the founder sounds, and in a first meeting that is the only thing a buyer has to go on.

B Because the founder had the numbers wrong.
C Because the founder spoke too quickly for the room.
2. Why does “Dataside” take a verb with -s?
A Because it is a long word.
B Because a company is a single thing, so it counts as it.
C Because it is a foreign name.
3. What happens to the -s in a negative sentence, according to the text?

B It stays on the main verb and is repeated on do.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a moment in every first meeting when a buyer stops asking what the company does and starts asking what it has done. The two questions sound similar and they are not. The first one can be answered by anybody with a website. The second one can only be answered by a company with a history, and English keeps a separate shape for it. Dataside has been operating for fifteen years. We have delivered more than four hundred projects. We have never lost a client to a competitor. None of those three sentences carries a date, and that is the whole point.

The shape is have or has plus the past participle, and it does one job: it says the count is still running. Four hundred projects today, four hundred and one next month. Compare that with we opened our second office in 2018. The year is named, the sentence closes, and nothing about it can change. That is the past simple, and it is the right shape whenever the time is on the table. The rule underneath both is short enough to carry into a meeting: name the time and the sentence closes; leave the time out and you are still counting. With for and since the count is explicit — for takes a length of time, since takes a starting point — and both of them require the present perfect, always.

Two traps cost a Portuguese speaker the most, and they are both traps of translation rather than of grammar. The first is since with the simple present. In Portuguese the equivalent sentence sits comfortably in the present tense, so the direct translation arrives as I am CEO since 2009. English will not take it: it has to be I have been CEO since 2009. The second trap is a named time glued to a present perfect — Last year we have opened an office — and the fix is to drop back to the past simple: Last year we opened an office. And underneath both, the permanent one: a company is an it, so it takes has. Not Dataside have grown. Dataside has grown.

A Because the numbers are estimates and could be wrong.
B Because the count is still running, and naming a time would close the sentence.
C Because dates are considered impolite in a first meeting.
2. What is the difference between for and since?
A For takes a length of time; since takes a starting point.
B For is formal and since is informal.
C Since takes the simple present and for takes the present perfect.
3. The text calls one mistake permanent. Which one is it?
A Saying Last year we have opened an office.
B Saying I am CEO since 2009.
C Saying Dataside have grown instead of Dataside has grown.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a question a serious buyer asks that a curious one never does. Not what do you do, and not even what have you done, but how long has it been running. The reason is not politeness. A result can be a lucky quarter; a process that has not stopped for two years is much harder to fake, and much harder to lose. So the shape English uses for duration is the shape that fills a due diligence call: we have been building the engine since 2022, the pipeline has been growing every quarter since March, they have been running diligence for six weeks.

The form is have or has plus been plus the -ing verb, and the test that chooses it is one question: can you answer how many, or only how long? We have signed four contracts answers how many, so it is the ordinary present perfect — a counted result. We have been signing contracts since March answers how long, so it is the continuous — an activity still under way. Both are true about the same three months. They simply point at different things, and a buyer listens for which one you reach for first.

Two things trip a Portuguese speaker here. The first is that Portuguese covers this ground with the simple present tense, so the direct translation arrives as we are doing this since six weeks, which is wrong twice over — wrong tense and wrong preposition. It has to be we have been doing this for six weeks, with for before a length and since before a starting point. The second is that some verbs refuse the -ing entirely because they describe a state rather than an activity: be, have in the sense of owning, know, need, want, mean. Not I have been knowing him for ten years. I have known him for ten years. And underneath all of it, the permanent one: the pipeline, the company and the market are each an it, so they take has, never have.

A Because a result can be a lucky quarter, and a process that has not stopped is harder to fake.
B Because durations are easier to verify in documents.
C Because buyers are not interested in results.
2. Which question decides between the two perfect shapes?
A Whether the sentence is positive or negative.
B Whether you can answer how many, or only how long.
C Whether the subject is singular or plural.
3. Why is we are doing this since six weeks wrong twice over?
A Wrong tense and wrong preposition -- it needs have been doing and for.
B Wrong word order and wrong subject.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are only three things you can do when somebody puts a condition on the table. You can accept it, you can refuse it, or you can trade. Accepting costs money. Refusing costs information, because the moment you say a flat no the other side stops proposing, and their proposals were the only thing telling you what they actually want. Trading costs nothing, and English has one sentence shape built for it: if you move on the threshold, we will move on the timetable.

The shape is if plus the present simple, then will plus the bare verb. Both halves are about the future, and that is exactly what makes it counterintuitive: the if half stays in the present anyway. If you accept, not if you will accept. This is the single most expensive mistake a Portuguese speaker makes in a negotiation, because Portuguese marks the future in both halves and the direct translation puts will where English will not take it. There is also a quieter version: unless, which simply means if not, and which draws a hard line while sounding like a condition. We will not sign anything unless the clause protects the team is a refusal that nobody in the room will hear as a refusal.

One shape close to it does something different. If revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays in full keeps both halves in the present, and that is not an offer at all — it is a rule that fires by itself, the way a contract clause does. Use it for what is already agreed, never for what you are still negotiating. And the permanent target does not go away just because there is an if in front of it: after if, he, she and it still take the -s. Not if the buyer accept. If the buyer accepts.

2. Why does the text call if you will accept the most expensive mistake?
A Because Portuguese marks the future in both halves, so the direct translation puts will where English will not take it.
B Because accept is an irregular verb.
C Because will is too direct for a negotiation.
3. When should you keep both halves in the present?

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences about the same ninety days. We will deliver the transition in ninety days. We can deliver the transition in ninety days. One word apart, and they are not variations of each other — they are different sizes of promise. The first is a commitment somebody can hold you to, and in a committee meeting it goes into the minutes as one. The second says you are capable, which is a statement about your company rather than a promise about the future. Founders reach for the first when they mean the second, usually because it sounds more confident, and six months later somebody reads it back to them.

English has a small set of these words — will, can, could, may, might, must, should — and each one sets a level. Could, may and might are possibilities that cost you nothing and buy you room: three of the eleven might take a call from a competitor admits a risk without promising an outcome, and it makes the rest of what you say more credible rather than less. Should is almost certain, based on evidence. Must is a rule. And there is one mechanical relief in all of this: after any of these words the verb is bare. No -s, no to, no ending at all. She can deliver, never she can delivers.

Two habits travel here from Portuguese and both are audible. The first is stacking two of them together, because the idea arrives as a single word in Portuguese and needs two in English: not we will can deliver, but we will be able to deliver. The second is must pointed at another person. In American business English you must send me the analysis sounds like an order from a superior, which is almost never what the speaker meant. For obligation that comes from outside, use have to; for a request, use could you; and keep must for rules and compliance, where it belongs.

1. Why does the text say founders reach for will when they mean can?
A Because will is grammatically simpler.
B Because it sounds more confident -- and six months later somebody reads it back to them as a commitment.
C Because can is considered informal in a committee.
2. What does the text say admitting a possibility does to the rest of what you say?
A It makes it more credible rather than less.
B It weakens the position and should be avoided.
C It has no effect, as long as the numbers are right.
3. Why is you must send me the analysis a problem in American business English?
A Because must is only used in writing.
B Because it needs a bare verb after it.
C Because it sounds like an order from a superior, which is almost never what the speaker meant.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An investor asks what your company would look like in five years. Nobody believes the forecast, including the person who wrote it, so the question is not really about the fifth year. It is a test of whether you have ever seriously imagined a company other than the one you already decided to build. Answering it needs a sentence shape that describes something untrue: if we opened an office in New York, we would need a different kind of leader.

The shape is if plus the past simple, then would plus the bare verb. Nothing in that sentence is about the past. The past form is not marking time here — it is marking distance: this has not happened, and it may never happen. That is the whole difference from the shape you already know. If we open in New York, we will need a leader there is a plan somebody is going to decide. If we opened in New York, we would need a leader there is a picture nobody has agreed to yet. Same office, completely different meeting.

Two details finish the structure. With the verb be, this shape takes were for every person, including I and it: if I were you, if the market were stronger. And when the result is a maybe rather than a certainty, could or might replaces would: if we spun it off, we might lose the platform team.

Two traps, both expensive. The first: would never follows if. Portuguese carries this idea with a dedicated verb form in the first half and a conditional in the second, and the direct translation puts would in both halves — a mistake that marks a speaker as translating rather than thinking. Say if we opened, not if we would open. The second is the permanent target turned inside out: after would, the verb takes no ending at all. Not he would needs. He would need.

A Whether the founder can produce an accurate five-year forecast.
B Whether the founder has ever seriously imagined a company other than the one he already chose.
C Whether the founder is willing to open an office abroad.
2. What does the text say the past form is doing in if we opened?
A Marking an event that happened before the meeting.
B Marking distance, not time: the thing has not happened and may never happen.
C Making the sentence sound more polite than the present form.
3. Why does the text call if we would open an expensive mistake?
A Because it comes from translating a structure that marks the idea in both halves, and it marks the speaker as translating rather than thinking.
B Because open is an irregular verb.
C Because investors dislike hypothetical answers.
