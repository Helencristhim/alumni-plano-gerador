Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A buyer in New York asks a founder what his company does, and the founder answers well — the numbers are right, the story is clear, the strategy holds. And still, something in the room moves half a step back. Later nobody can say exactly what it was. It was one letter. Dataside specialize in data, he said. The buyer want a faster close. Three or four times in ten minutes, and each time the sentence arrived a little less finished than the thought behind it.

English marks one person in the present simple, and only one. When the subject is he, she or it, the verb carries an -s: she wants, he runs, it costs. Everybody else takes the bare verb: I run, we specialize, they want. The trap for a Portuguese speaker is not the rule — the rule is simple and most speakers can recite it. The trap is the subject that does not look like a person. Dataside has three hundred people, because a company is an it. Revenue comes from consulting, because revenue is an it too. The private equity firm wants three years of numbers. Every one of those subjects is a single thing, and every one of them takes the -s.

In the negative and in questions the -s moves house. It leaves the main verb and goes to do: he does not move on price, and does the buyer need more time? Saying she does not wants puts it in both places, which is the second most common version of the same mistake. And there is a habit that travels from Portuguese with it: depends of instead of it depends on. Neither one changes what a founder means. Both change how finished the founder sounds, and in a first meeting that is the only thing a buyer has to go on.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a moment in every first meeting when a buyer stops asking what the company does and starts asking what it has done. The two questions sound similar and they are not. The first one can be answered by anybody with a website. The second one can only be answered by a company with a history, and English keeps a separate shape for it. Dataside has been operating for fifteen years. We have delivered more than four hundred projects. We have never lost a client to a competitor. None of those three sentences carries a date, and that is the whole point.

The shape is have or has plus the past participle, and it does one job: it says the count is still running. Four hundred projects today, four hundred and one next month. Compare that with we opened our second office in 2018. The year is named, the sentence closes, and nothing about it can change. That is the past simple, and it is the right shape whenever the time is on the table. The rule underneath both is short enough to carry into a meeting: name the time and the sentence closes; leave the time out and you are still counting. With for and since the count is explicit — for takes a length of time, since takes a starting point — and both of them require the present perfect, always.

Two traps cost a Portuguese speaker the most, and they are both traps of translation rather than of grammar. The first is since with the simple present. In Portuguese the equivalent sentence sits comfortably in the present tense, so the direct translation arrives as I am CEO since 2009. English will not take it: it has to be I have been CEO since 2009. The second trap is a named time glued to a present perfect — Last year we have opened an office — and the fix is to drop back to the past simple: Last year we opened an office. And underneath both, the permanent one: a company is an it, so it takes has. Not Dataside have grown. Dataside has grown.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a question a serious buyer asks that a curious one never does. Not what do you do, and not even what have you done, but how long has it been running. The reason is not politeness. A result can be a lucky quarter; a process that has not stopped for two years is much harder to fake, and much harder to lose. So the shape English uses for duration is the shape that fills a due diligence call: we have been building the engine since 2022, the pipeline has been growing every quarter since March, they have been running diligence for six weeks.

The form is have or has plus been plus the -ing verb, and the test that chooses it is one question: can you answer how many, or only how long? We have signed four contracts answers how many, so it is the ordinary present perfect — a counted result. We have been signing contracts since March answers how long, so it is the continuous — an activity still under way. Both are true about the same three months. They simply point at different things, and a buyer listens for which one you reach for first.

Two things trip a Portuguese speaker here. The first is that Portuguese covers this ground with the simple present tense, so the direct translation arrives as we are doing this since six weeks, which is wrong twice over — wrong tense and wrong preposition. It has to be we have been doing this for six weeks, with for before a length and since before a starting point. The second is that some verbs refuse the -ing entirely because they describe a state rather than an activity: be, have in the sense of owning, know, need, want, mean. Not I have been knowing him for ten years. I have known him for ten years. And underneath all of it, the permanent one: the pipeline, the company and the market are each an it, so they take has, never have.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text -- tap Listen to hear each paragraph -- then answer the questions.

There are only three things you can do when somebody puts a condition on the table. You can accept it, you can refuse it, or you can trade. Accepting costs money. Refusing costs information, because the moment you say a flat no the other side stops proposing, and their proposals were the only thing telling you what they actually want. Trading costs nothing, and English has one sentence shape built for it: if you move on the threshold, we will move on the timetable.

The shape is if plus the present simple, then will plus the bare verb. Both halves are about the future, and that is exactly what makes it counterintuitive: the if half stays in the present anyway. If you accept, not if you will accept. This is the single most expensive mistake a Portuguese speaker makes in a negotiation, because Portuguese marks the future in both halves and the direct translation puts will where English will not take it. There is also a quieter version: unless, which simply means if not, and which draws a hard line while sounding like a condition. We will not sign anything unless the clause protects the team is a refusal that nobody in the room will hear as a refusal.

One shape close to it does something different. If revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays in full keeps both halves in the present, and that is not an offer at all — it is a rule that fires by itself, the way a contract clause does. Use it for what is already agreed, never for what you are still negotiating. And the permanent target does not go away just because there is an if in front of it: after if, he, she and it still take the -s. Not if the buyer accept. If the buyer accepts.

The same three moves happen again on paper, and on paper they have their own vocabulary. A payment that arrives only if something else happens first is contingent on it. A document that legally forces both sides to do what it says is binding, and most of a term sheet is not binding at all, apart from the confidentiality section and the non-disclosure agreement both sides have signed weeks before anything else. Closing is the day the deal legally completes and the money moves, and every condition in the document points at that date. If a payment is contingent on a number, the number decides. If it is contingent on a person, you have handed that person the deal.

And there is one condition you write for yourself, before the first meeting. Decide what you will walk away from, and write it down. If you have not decided that, every condition they propose sounds reasonable, because you have nothing to measure it against. If revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays in full is a rule you have already accepted; if you move on the threshold, we will move on the timetable is still an offer. The founders who negotiate well are not the ones who talk the most. They are the ones who know, before the room fills up, which line they will not cross.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In a negotiation, what was agreed is not what was said in the room. It is what was written afterwards. Somebody leaves the meeting, sends an email, and three weeks later everybody argues from that email and nobody remembers the call. So the person who can repeat other people accurately, in English, controls the record — and the person who cannot has handed that job to the other side.

The mechanism is one step backwards. Everything the person said moves one tense further into the past. We are ready becomes he said they were ready. The pool was agreed becomes she said the pool had been agreed, because it was already past when she spoke. The modals move with it: can becomes could, will becomes would, must becomes had to. The pronouns move too, and so does the time: tomorrow becomes the next day.

A reported question stops being a question. It loses the auxiliary, it loses the question order and it loses the question mark: can you hold the price? becomes he asked whether we could hold the price. Keeping the original order — he asked what did we want — is the single most common mistake in this structure, and it is audible immediately. The other one is smaller and just as visible in writing: tell needs a person after it and say does not. He told me. He said that. Never he said me.

There is one exception worth knowing, because it belongs to your world. When the thing reported is still true at the moment you are speaking, the step back becomes optional: he said the threshold is fourteen percent is correct if it still is. And the permanent target survives here too: in the present, the reporting verb keeps its ending. She says, not she say. Finally, one rule that is not grammar at all. Report the sentence, not your feeling about the sentence. If the buyer said the margin was too thin, say he said the margin was too thin. You are allowed to disagree in the next sentence; you are not allowed to edit his on the way home.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences can contain the same facts and be worth very different amounts. We serve large clients across many verticals is true and unusable. The two verticals where we have the deepest experience are banking and logistics is the same claim with a half-sentence attached, and that half-sentence is the whole difference: it turns a description into something a buyer can go and verify. In a room where somebody is deciding what your company is worth, a claim nobody can check is a claim nobody pays for.

The half-sentence starts with a small word that points back at the noun in front of it. Who for people, which or that for things, where for places and situations, whose for what belongs to somebody. The partner who ran the call. The platform that the incumbent could not replace. The verticals where we are strongest. The engineers whose tenure is over five years. When the pronoun is the object of its own clause, you can drop it entirely: the client we onboarded in March is complete English.

Then there is the punctuation, and it is not decoration. Without commas, the clause is telling the reader which one: the client that arrived through a referral has renewed four times means there are several clients and this is the one being talked about. With commas, the thing was already unique and the clause only adds a detail: our largest client, which arrived through a referral, has renewed four times means there is exactly one largest client. And in that second kind, that is not allowed — after a comma it is always which or who.

Two traps. The first is small and audible: the subject is not repeated after the pronoun. Portuguese allows the speaker to pick the subject up again inside the clause, and the direct translation produces the engineers who they built the platform. The pronoun already is the subject. The second trap is the permanent target in a new place: the verb inside the clause agrees with the noun in front of it, not with the pronoun. The client who has renewed. The clients who have renewed.
