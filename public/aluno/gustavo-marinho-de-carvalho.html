Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A client you have never met opens the call and asks two questions. They sound like the same question and they are not. The first one is: what does your company do? We work with clients in several countries. I handle the international side. That answer was true last year, it is true today and it will be true in June. English marks it with the plain form of the verb, and nothing in the sentence says anything about this week.

The second question is: what are you working on at the moment? This week I am putting together the proposal for your team. At the moment we are reviewing the timeline for the first quarter. Both sentences are true now and both will stop being true, and English marks that with am, is or are in front and -ing behind. This is the shape a Brazilian speaker uses least in a work call, because in Portuguese the plain present covers the two jobs on its own.

Then there is a third group, and it is where the most expensive mistake lives. I agree with you. I think the deadline is realistic. I need the payment terms in writing. These are not actions at all: they are what goes on inside your head, and they never take -ing. And agree is already the verb, so I am agree does not exist in English, in any tense. If you say it on a call with a client, nothing breaks and everybody understands you — but it is the one sentence that makes a fluent speaker sound like a beginner, and it costs nothing to fix.

1. “We work with clients in several countries.” What does this shape tell you?
A That it was true last year, it is true today and it will be true in June.
B That it is only true this week.
C That it started this morning and is not finished yet.
2. Why does the text say a Brazilian speaker uses “I am putting together” least in a work call?
A Because the verb is irregular.
B Because in Portuguese the plain present covers both jobs on its own.
C Because it is too formal for a call with a client.
3. The text calls one mistake the most expensive one. Which is it?
A Saying I am agree instead of I agree.
B Saying a supplier instead of a partner.
C Saying at the moment instead of now.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways of talking about the same piece of news, and English keeps them apart with the verb. The first way puts the event in a finished moment. The vote took place on Tuesday. I read about it on Friday morning. She pointed out the problem in the call yesterday. Tuesday, Friday morning and yesterday are all over, and the sentence says so.

The second way says nothing about when. It says that something is different now. Prices have gone up since January. They have already announced the new plan. I have read three articles about it this week. Nobody is telling you the day, and that is the point: what matters is the result you are living in, not the moment it started. This week is not finished either, so it belongs here too.

And then there is the mistake that a Brazilian speaker makes almost every time, because Portuguese lets you do it. I have read it yesterday does not exist in English. The moment you name a finished time — yesterday, last Tuesday, in 2022 — you have to use the first shape: I read it yesterday. Nothing else in the sentence changes. It is not a question of politeness or of level: the two shapes simply cannot share a finished time.

1. “Prices have gone up since January.” What does this shape tell you?
A That it happened in January and then stopped.
B That something is different now, and the sentence does not name the day.
C That it will probably happen next January.
2. Why does the text say “this week” belongs with the second shape?
A Because a week is longer than a day.
B Because news is always weekly.
C Because this week is not finished yet.
3. According to the text, what makes “I have read it yesterday” impossible?
A The sentence names a finished time, and that shape cannot take one.
B The verb read is irregular.
C It is correct, but too formal for a call.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every promise you make at work has a condition hidden inside it, and English puts that condition in a shape of its own. If you send the brief today, I will have the draft ready by Friday. Two halves: the condition, and what follows from it. The strange part for a Brazilian speaker is that the condition half stays in the present, even though the whole sentence is about next week.

That is where the most common mistake in this structure lives. In Portuguese the future is already inside the verb of the condition, so the hand wants to write if you will send. English does not allow it, in any register, in any variety. The rule has no exceptions worth learning at this level: after if, present. If we run into another problem, I will call you the same day.

Two other words do the same job with a twist. Unless is if not in one word: unless we hear from them before noon, we will keep the old date. Because the not is already inside it, unless you do not send it says the opposite of what you mean. And as long as is the condition you are willing to promise on: as long as nothing else comes up, Tuesday is realistic. Notice what that last sentence really does. It is not a weaker promise. It is a clearer one, because it names the only thing that could break it.

1. Why does the condition half stay in the present?
A Because the sentence is about something that already happened.
B Because that is the shape English uses, even when the sentence is about the future.
C Because the condition is less important than the promise.
2. According to the text, why is “unless you do not send it” wrong?
A Because unless already contains the not, so the sentence says the opposite.
B Because unless can only be used in writing.
C Because unless needs will after it.
3. What does the text say “as long as nothing else comes up” does to a promise?
A It makes the promise weaker, so it should be avoided with a client.
B It only works in writing, never on a call.
C It makes the promise clearer, because it names the only thing that could break it.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Last lesson you learned the shape for a condition that might really happen this week: if you send it today, I will have it ready. Present in one half, will in the other. Today the sentence moves one step away from reality, and English marks that move by putting the condition in the past.

If we moved to a four-day week, we would need far fewer meetings. Nobody is moving. If it were my decision, I would start with the buses. It is not my decision. The past tense here has nothing to do with the past: it is a distance marker, and the distance is exactly what makes the sentence usable in an argument. It puts the idea one step away from the person saying it, so the other side can disagree with the idea without disagreeing with you.

Two traps, and a Brazilian speaker walks into both. The first is would in the wrong half: in Portuguese the conditional ending appears twice, so the hand wants to write if I would have more time. English allows it in exactly one half. Say if I had more time, I would read it properly. The second is if I was. In careful English it is if I were — for every person, including I and it. And one bonus: the most useful sentence of the lesson has no if at all. It would make sense to start smaller is an opinion, a suggestion and an invitation to answer, in seven words.

1. What does the past tense do in “if we moved to a four-day week”?
A It says the move happened last year.
B It marks distance from reality, not time.
C It makes the sentence more formal, with no change in meaning.
2. According to the text, why is the hypothetical useful in an argument?
A It puts the idea one step away from the person, so the other side can disagree with the idea and not with you.
B It hides what you really think, which avoids conflict.
C It is the only polite form in British English.
3. Why does the text say a Brazilian speaker writes “if I would have more time”?
A Because the verb have is irregular.
B Because in Portuguese the conditional ending appears in both halves.
C Because English allows it in speech but not in writing.

Lesson 05 -- Pre-class

What You Must, What You Should -- The Rules Nobody Wrote Down

Every workplace runs on two sets of rules, and only one of them is written down. Key words: an expectation, to be expected to, a rule of thumb, to get away with, unwritten, to be frowned upon, to take the initiative, a heads-up. Structure: modals of obligation, advice and possibility.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

An expectation -- what people believe is going to happen, or should happen
"Nobody set that rule. It is just an expectation, and everybody has it."
To be expected to -- to have other people believing that you will do something
"You are expected to reply the same day, and nobody will ever say so."
A rule of thumb -- a practical guide that is usually right, but not exact
"As a rule of thumb, if it needs three emails, it needs a call."
To get away with -- to do something wrong or unusual and have nothing happen
"You can get away with it once. The second time somebody says something."
Unwritten -- not written down anywhere, and everybody knows it
"That is an unwritten rule here, and it took me a year to learn it."
To be frowned upon -- to be considered wrong or rude by most people, without being forbidden
"Emailing at nine at night is not banned. It is just frowned upon."
To take the initiative -- to act first, without waiting to be asked
"Nobody asked her to call the supplier. She took the initiative."
A heads-up -- a warning that gives somebody time to prepare
"Give her a heads-up before the meeting, not during it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Before you choose the word, ask yourself one question: where is the rule coming from? If it comes from outside — a contract, a client, the law — English uses have to or need to. We have to confirm every date in writing. If the obligation is one you put on yourself, English prefers must. I must call her before the end of the day. Nobody is forcing you. You decided.

And then there is the pair that costs a Brazilian speaker the most, because Portuguese covers both with the same expression. You do not have to reply tonight means there is no obligation: you may reply, and nothing happens if you do not. You must not reply tonight means it is forbidden. They are not two versions of the same idea. They are opposites, and if you say the second one to a client when you mean the first, you have just prohibited something you were trying to permit.

The rest is easier. Should and ought to are advice, and the other person can still decide otherwise. Might, may and could are possibility, not certainty, and they are the safest way to suggest something to somebody senior. One shape rule holds for all of them: after a modal, no to and no -s. I must send it, never I must to send it. She should give him a heads-up, never she shoulds.

1. According to the text, when does English prefer must over have to?
A When the obligation is one you put on yourself.
B When the obligation comes from a contract.
C When you are talking to a client rather than a colleague.
2. What is the difference between “you do not have to reply” and “you must not reply”?
A The second is simply a more formal version of the first.
B The first says there is no obligation. The second says it is forbidden. They are opposites.
C There is no real difference in spoken English.
3. What shape rule does the text give for all modals?
A They always take an -s in the third person.
B They always need to before the next verb.
C After a modal there is no to and no -s.

Stage 2: Put the Briefing in Order

Order

You are explaining how your side works to somebody new. Put the six lines in a logical order.

?"Let me tell you how things work on our side."
?"We have to confirm every date in writing. That one is not negotiable."
?"You should give us a heads-up when something is at risk."
?"You do not have to reply outside working hours. Nobody expects it."
?"And there is one unwritten rule that nobody will ever tell you."
?"What is it like on your side? I would rather ask than guess."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The client asks whether she needs to answer your email tonight. It can wait. You say:
A You must not reply tonight.
B You do not have to reply tonight, it can wait until the morning.
C You should not reply tonight, it is forbidden.
There is a rule in the contract about written confirmation. You say:
A We have to confirm every date in writing.
B We should to confirm every date in writing.
C We must to confirm every date in writing.
You want to suggest something to somebody senior, without pushing. You say:
A You have to check with them first.
B It might be worth checking with them first.
C You must checking with them first.
Your client mentions that something you did is “quietly frowned upon” there. The best response is:
A Apologise at length and promise it will never happen again.
B Ask what else you should know that nobody would ever write down.
C Explain that in your country it is completely normal, and leave it there.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Ninety seconds, no notes. Three unwritten rules of your own workplace, for somebody who has never been there. Say what each one is, how you learned it, and which one you would change if it were up to you.
