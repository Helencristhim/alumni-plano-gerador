The First Call with London -- What You Do and What You Are Doing

A client in England opens the call and asks two questions that sound like one. Key words: to get in touch, a supplier, to catch up, straightforward, a deadline, to be on the same page, a follow-up, to handle. Structure: present simple vs present continuous.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To get in touch -- to contact someone by phone, email or message
"I got in touch with them on Monday about the new contract."

To be on the same page -- to understand a situation in the same way as the other person
"Let me repeat the three points, so we are on the same page."
A follow-up -- a short message or meeting that comes after a first contact
"I will send a follow-up with the three points we agreed on."
To handle -- to be responsible for something and take care of it
"I handle the international side, so you can write to me directly."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A client you have never met opens the call and asks two questions. They sound like the same question and they are not. The first one is: what does your company do? We work with clients in several countries. I handle the international side. That answer was true last year, it is true today and it will be true in June. English marks it with the plain form of the verb, and nothing in the sentence says anything about this week.

The second question is: what are you working on at the moment? This week I am putting together the proposal for your team. At the moment we are reviewing the timeline for the first quarter. Both sentences are true now and both will stop being true, and English marks that with am, is or are in front and -ing behind. This is the shape a Brazilian speaker uses least in a work call, because in Portuguese the plain present covers the two jobs on its own.

Then there is a third group, and it is where the most expensive mistake lives. I agree with you. I think the deadline is realistic. I need the payment terms in writing. These are not actions at all: they are what goes on inside your head, and they never take -ing. And agree is already the verb, so I am agree does not exist in English, in any tense. If you say it on a call with a client, nothing breaks and everybody understands you — but it is the one sentence that makes a fluent speaker sound like a beginner, and it costs nothing to fix.

1. “We work with clients in several countries.” What does this shape tell you?
A That it was true last year, it is true today and it will be true in June.
B That it is only true this week.
C That it started this morning and is not finished yet.
2. Why does the text say a Brazilian speaker uses “I am putting together” least in a work call?
A Because the verb is irregular.
B Because in Portuguese the plain present covers both jobs on its own.
C Because it is too formal for a call with a client.
3. The text calls one mistake the most expensive one. Which is it?
A Saying I am agree instead of I agree.
B Saying a supplier instead of a partner.
C Saying at the moment instead of now.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple vs Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

The shape you pick is not grammar. It is how long the sentence is going to stay true.

JobFormExample

What is on your desk this weekam / is / are + verb-ingThis week I am putting together the proposal.
A change that is happening nowam / is / are + verb-ingCosts are going up again this year.

The signal wordsusually, every week / this week, at the momentUsually I travel in March. This year I am staying here.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Reading the News Together -- What Happened and What Has Changed

One story, two countries, two different conversations. Key words: a headline, coverage, to report, a source, an outcome, to take place, widespread, to point out. Structure: past simple vs present perfect.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A headline -- the title of a news story, printed in large letters at the top
"Most people never read the article. They read the headline and stop there."
Coverage -- the amount of attention the news gives to a subject
"The story got three days of coverage and then it disappeared."
To report -- to tell people about something that has happened
"Two newspapers reported the same numbers and reached opposite conclusions."
A source -- the person or document that information comes from
"She never publishes anything without a second source."
An outcome -- the result at the end of a process or an event
"Nobody knows the outcome yet, and the vote was six weeks ago."
To take place -- to happen, at a particular time or in a particular place
"The meeting took place on Tuesday morning, in their London office."
Widespread -- happening in many places, or among many people
"The problem is not in one city. It is widespread."
To point out -- to say something so that other people notice it
"She pointed out that the date had already been moved twice."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways of talking about the same piece of news, and English keeps them apart with the verb. The first way puts the event in a finished moment. The vote took place on Tuesday. I read about it on Friday morning. She pointed out the problem in the call yesterday. Tuesday, Friday morning and yesterday are all over, and the sentence says so.

The second way says nothing about when. It says that something is different now. Prices have gone up since January. They have already announced the new plan. I have read three articles about it this week. Nobody is telling you the day, and that is the point: what matters is the result you are living in, not the moment it started. This week is not finished either, so it belongs here too.

And then there is the mistake that a Brazilian speaker makes almost every time, because Portuguese lets you do it. I have read it yesterday does not exist in English. The moment you name a finished time — yesterday, last Tuesday, in 2022 — you have to use the first shape: I read it yesterday. Nothing else in the sentence changes. It is not a question of politeness or of level: the two shapes simply cannot share a finished time.

1. “Prices have gone up since January.” What does this shape tell you?
A That it happened in January and then stopped.
B That something is different now, and the sentence does not name the day.
C That it will probably happen next January.
2. Why does the text say “this week” belongs with the second shape?
A Because a week is longer than a day.
B Because news is always weekly.
C Because this week is not finished yet.
3. According to the text, what makes “I have read it yesterday” impossible?
A The sentence names a finished time, and that shape cannot take one.
B The verb read is irregular.
C It is correct, but too formal for a call.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Simple vs Present Perfect

GRAMMAR

The question is not when it happened. The question is whether the sentence names the moment.

JobFormExample
A finished moment, and you say whenpast simpleThe vote took place last Tuesday.
A finished moment, and everybody knows whenpast simpleI read it on Friday morning.
It started before and it is still truehave / has + past participlePrices have gone up since January.
News that is new to the listenerhave / has + past participleThey have announced a new plan.
Your experience up to nowhave / has + past participleI have never been to Manchester.
The signal wordsyesterday, last week, in 2022 / since, so far, already, yet, this weekI saw it yesterday. I have not seen it yet.
The trapa finished time never goes with have / hasNot I have read it yesterday. Say I read it yesterday.

Never: I have read it yesterday · The vote has taken place last Tuesday · Since January prices went up · I never went to Manchester in my life

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

The client asks whether you saw the story. It was published on Monday. You answer:
A Yes, I have read it on Monday.
B Yes, I read it on Monday, and I have been following it since then.
C Yes, I am reading it on Monday.
You want to say that the situation is different now, without naming a day. You say:
A A lot has changed since the vote.
B A lot changed since the vote.
C A lot is changing since the vote.
The client brings up a political story from your country and asks what you think. The safest strong move is:
A Give your verdict immediately, so that she knows where you stand.
B Say that you never talk about the news and change the subject.
C Say what happened, say what has changed since then, and ask what the reaction was there.
You have only read the headline and nothing else. The honest and useful answer is:
A I have only seen the headline, so tell me what the coverage has been like there.
B Nothing, because you should never admit that you have not read it.
C I read all about it and I agree with the newspaper.
