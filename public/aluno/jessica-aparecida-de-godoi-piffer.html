Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A conference room empties slowly. The presentations were good, the numbers held, nobody asked a question anybody could not answer. And then the schedule says lunch and networking, ninety minutes of it, and half the room quietly reaches for a phone. The part of the day that decides who remembers whom is the part nobody rehearses.

What makes that part hard is rarely vocabulary. It is a choice English forces on you every time you talk about yourself. I have worked with teams in three countries and I worked with a German team in 2020 are both true, and they are not the same sentence. The first one is a life so far, with no date, and English marks it with have plus the participle. The second one is a closed moment, and it has a date sitting in it, so the verb goes into the past simple. In Portuguese one form covers both, which is why the choice feels invisible until somebody points at it.

The test is short. Look for a finished time word: in 2020, last August, yesterday, three years ago. If one is there, the past simple wins, and no amount of have will rescue the sentence. If there is none, and the point is the experience rather than the day it happened, use the perfect. The words ever, never, already and before pull the perfect towards them, which is why Have you ever been to Chicago? is the natural question at a coffee table and Did you ever go to Chicago? sounds like the start of an investigation.

There is a second habit worth naming, and it has nothing to do with grammar. A conversation dies when an answer contains exactly what was asked and nothing more. Small talk stays alive on the extra detail: not two years, but two years here, and before that a German team and a Dutch one. That extra clause is the door. Somebody walks through it, finds that you have something in common, and forty minutes later you are no longer a name on a badge.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences, both correct, and they do not mean the same thing. She worked at the firm for twenty years. She has worked at the firm for twenty years. The first one says she left. The second one says she is still there, at her desk, today. Nothing in the words tells you that. The tense does.

This is the choice English makes that Portuguese does not, and it is the reason a fluent speaker can still write a sentence that says the opposite of what was meant. The past simple closes a period. It goes with the words that name a finished moment: in 2009, last quarter, three days later, ago. The present perfect leaves the period open onto now, and it goes with the words that refuse to close it: since, so far, already, yet, ever, never.

Put the two together and the sentence breaks. I have sent the report on Tuesday is not English, because Tuesday closes a period that have sent is trying to keep open. Say I sent the report on Tuesday if the day matters, and I have sent the report if the only thing that matters is that it is done and nobody has to chase it. The second one is what a manager wants at eight in the morning.

The same rule runs through an entire story. When Dana Reyes came across the first duplicated receipt, she corrected it and moved on — three finished events, in order, all in the past simple. When she describes what those weeks left behind, she switches: I have never seen a large discrepancy that did not have a small one in front of it. That sentence has no date in it, and it is not supposed to. It is the result the twenty-two years produced, and it is still true this morning.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences, four words apart. When I arrived, she was writing the report. When I arrived, she wrote the report. In the first one she had been at it for a while and you walked into the middle of it. In the second one she had not started, and your arrival is what got her going. Same people, same room, and a completely different story about who caused what.

This is the difference the past continuous exists for. Was or were plus the -ing form describes something that was already running: the background. The past simple describes what arrived: the event. That is why while naturally attaches to the background — while she was waiting for the lift — and when naturally attaches to the event — when the phone rang. Put two backgrounds side by side and both go into the continuous: they were presenting while I was checking the numbers.

Two things trip people up. The first is that a finished sequence needs no continuous at all: the meeting took place at nine and finished at ten is three closed events in a row, and nothing was interrupted. The second is that some verbs refuse the -ing form entirely, because they are not actions you can be in the middle of: know, believe, understand, want. You did not say I was knowing. You knew.

Why it matters outside a grammar exercise: a timeline is built out of exactly this. An interviewer asks what you were doing, never what you saw, because the background gives you somewhere to stand. And when your answer is vague — around ten — you will be asked again later, and the second answer will contradict the first. It is cheaper to say you cannot recall the time than to invent one to be helpful.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Here is a sentence about tomorrow: If the storm moves east, we will reroute everything. Both halves are about the future, and only one of them says so. The half with will in it is the result. The half after if is in the present simple, even though nothing about it has happened yet. That is the whole of the first conditional, and it is one of the few rules in English with no exceptions worth learning.

For a Portuguese speaker this is not a matter of memory. Portuguese has a verb form reserved for exactly this job, and English simply does not. So the brain reaches for the nearest future marker it owns, and out comes if it will rain. It is the fastest signal of direct translation there is, and it disappears the moment you accept that English fills the gap with the present.

Three details finish the picture. The result does not have to be a promise: we might need a backup plan and I can take the call are both first conditionals, and both sound less absolute than will. The order of the halves is free, and the comma only appears when if comes first. And unless already contains the negative — it means if not — so a second negative after it breaks the sentence: unless we decide today, never unless we do not decide today.

The same present-tense rule runs after when, as soon as, until and before. I will send the summary as soon as the meeting finishes. And there is one habit worth stealing from people who do this professionally: attach a time to every condition. A duty controller writes decide by 06:45 next to each line, because a condition with no deadline never goes ahead and never gets called off. It is not a decision. It is a worry.

B Because they have not memorised the list of irregular verbs.
C Because "rain" can be both a noun and a verb.
2. According to the text, when does the comma appear?

B Only when the if half comes first.
C Only when the result uses might instead of will.
3. What does the text say turns a condition into a decision?
A Writing it down in a formal document.
B Getting somebody senior to approve it.
C Attaching a time to it. Without a deadline it is a worry.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences that look almost the same. If I have time this weekend, I will finish the report. If I had more time, I would rewrite the whole process. The first one might really happen: it is a plan with a condition on it. The second one will not: it is an opinion wearing the clothes of a plan. Nothing in the words says which is which. The tense does.

The move is small and it does all the work. Step the verb after if one tense back — have becomes had, move becomes moved — and put would in the other half. The past there is not about the past. It marks distance from reality. That is why if I won the lottery tomorrow is in the past tense and about tomorrow at the same time, and why nobody finds that strange.

One phrase is worth learning as a single block: if I were you. English keeps were for every person here — if I were, if she were, if it were — and this is the standard way to give advice without giving an order. Alongside it sit the softeners: I would probably…, I might consider it, I could ask. In English an unsoftened opinion lands harder than the speaker usually intends, and that matters most when the other person is senior to you.

The trap for a Portuguese speaker is precise. Portuguese marks this situation with a past subjunctive of its own, and the English word that looks like its natural equivalent is would. So out comes if I would have more time. It is wrong in both conditionals for the same reason: would never appears on the if side of the sentence. Say if I had more time, and let would do its job in the other half.

1. What does the past tense mark in "If I had more time"?
A That the situation happened some time ago.
B Distance from reality — the situation is not true.
C That the speaker is being formal rather than casual.
2. Why does the text say "if I would have more time" is a Portuguese-speaker error?
A Because Portuguese uses the imperfect subjunctive there, and would looks like the natural equivalent — but would never goes on the if side.
B Because have is an irregular verb.
C Because the sentence needs unless instead of if.
3. According to the text, why do softeners matter?
A They make the sentence grammatically correct.
B They are required in written English but not in speech.
C Because an unsoftened opinion lands harder than the speaker intends, especially with somebody senior.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two sentences about the same afternoon. We submitted the report on Tuesday. The report was submitted on Tuesday. The second one has lost a person and gained two words. The thing that was acted on has moved to the front, and the verb has become be plus a past participle. That is the whole shape of the passive, and there is nothing else to it.

The detail worth memorising is where the tense lives. It never sits in the participle — it sits in be. The figures are checked every month. The notice has been issued. The file will be reviewed on Friday. Three tenses, one participle each, and only the first word changing. This is why the notice has issued is wrong: the been is not decoration, it is the verb.

The agent — the person who acted — comes back only with by, and only when it matters: it was flagged by the tax team. Most of the time it is left out, and for four honest reasons: nobody knows who did it, it does not matter who did it, it is obvious who did it, or the sentence is about the thing rather than the person. A safety notice is about the product, so no name appears in it. The internal record about the same incident names everybody, because that document has the opposite job.

There is a fifth reason, and everybody recognises it: mistakes were made. Same grammar, different purpose. And there is one hard limit worth knowing before you write anything: a verb with no object cannot go into the passive at all. Something happened, it arrived, it went wrong. Never it was happened. If there is nobody the action was done to, there is no passive to build.

1. Where does the tense live in a passive sentence?
A In the past participle.
B In be — is, was, has been, will be.
C In the word that follows by.
2. Why is "it was happened last week" impossible?
A Because "happen" is an irregular verb.
B Because the verb has no object, so there is nothing to move to the front and no passive to build.
C Because the sentence is missing by.
3. According to the text, why does a safety notice name nobody while the internal record names everybody?
A The two documents have opposite jobs: one is about the product, the other is about who approved what.
B Because public documents are not allowed to contain names.
C Because the internal record is written in a different tense.
