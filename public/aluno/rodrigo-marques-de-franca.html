Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Twenty Years in Two Minutes -- Saying What You Do

Somebody at a seminar abroad asks what you do, and the answer has to fit in two sentences before it fits in two minutes. Key words: a caseload, a track record, a stint, a turning point, demanding, hands-on, to take something on, to juggle, to wind down, to be based in, to wear many hats, back on track. Structure: present perfect simple and past simple.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A caseload -- all the work a professional is handling at the same time
"My caseload is heavier this year than it was last year."
A track record -- everything somebody has achieved so far, seen as proof of what they can do
"They chose the firm for its track record in international arbitration."
A stint -- a fixed period of time somebody spends doing one particular job
"After a short stint in tax, he moved to the corporate side."
A turning point -- the moment when a situation changes direction and does not go back
"Moving to São Paulo was the turning point in my career."
Demanding -- needing a lot of time, effort and attention, so that little is left over
"It is a demanding practice, and the hours show it."
Hands-on -- doing the practical work yourself instead of only supervising it
"I am still hands-on. I read the contracts myself."
To take something on -- to accept a new piece of work or a new responsibility
"I have taken on two new matters since January."
To juggle -- to handle several demanding things at the same time, without dropping any
"On a bad week I am juggling four matters that are all urgent."

"The matter is winding down. We should close the file in June."
To be based in -- to have your main place of work in a particular city or country
"I am based in São Paulo, but I grew up in Bahia."
To wear many hats -- to have several different roles inside the same job

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At a seminar in Lisbon, somebody turns to the lawyer beside her and asks the standard question. He has worked in corporate law for twenty years, so the answer could take an hour, and he gives it in two sentences instead. He studied in a small city in the nineties, when the work there did not need English at all, and he moved south in 2005. That was the turning point.

Since then he has been at the same firm, and his caseload has grown every year. He is still hands-on: he reads the contracts himself instead of only signing off on them, and on a bad week he is juggling four matters that all became urgent on the same afternoon. It is a demanding practice. He has taken on two new matters this year and he has not turned down anything yet, which he admits is a problem.

She asks whether he has ever argued a case in English. He has, once. He argued it in 2019, and he says it was the longest afternoon of his professional life. He stopped studying the language for a year when a stint on an impossible file took over his calendar, and this month he is back on track. His track record speaks for itself; what he wants now is for his English to speak as clearly as it does.

1. "He has worked in corporate law for twenty years." Why not "He worked"?
A Because twenty years is a very long time.
B Because the period is still open: he started twenty years ago and he is still there.
C Because corporate law is a permanent profession.
2. "He moved south in 2005." Why is this not "He has moved south in 2005"?
A Because 2005 is a closed year, and a closed time takes the past simple.
B Because moving is a short action and short actions never take have.
C Because he moved only once.
3. "She asks whether he has ever argued a case in English. He has, once. He argued it in 2019." Why does the tense change in the third sentence?
A Because the second speaker is different from the first.
B Because ever and once cannot appear in the same paragraph.
C Because the date arrives. Experience with no date takes have; as soon as the year is named, the sentence moves to the past simple.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

One decision, made by the time expression: is the period still open, or did the clock stop?

JobFormExample
A period still openhave / has + past participleI have worked here for twenty years.
A time that is closedpast simpleI studied there in the nineties.
Life experience, no datehave / has + participleI have never argued a case in English.
The date arrivesswitch to past simpleI argued one in 2019.
Not yet, up to nowhave / has not + participleI have not signed it yet.
Time words that take havefor, since, ever, never, yet, so far, this yearI have taken on two matters this year.
Time words that take the pastin 2005, last week, when I was, agoWe signed it last Thursday.

Never: I work here since 2005 · I am working here since 2005 · I have moved to São Paulo in 2005 · We have not wrote it yet · How long time are you working on this?

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Weekly Read -- How a Long Article Builds an Argument

The kind of piece you already read every week, taken apart: the claim, the caveat, and the long sentences that carry them. Key words: a case in point, to bear out, a caveat, sweeping, to hinge on, entrenched, a proxy, to gain traction, to lag behind, at odds with, a downside, to overstate. Structure: relative clauses, defining and non-defining.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A case in point -- an example that proves exactly what has just been said
"A case in point is software, where the week is already built around tasks."
To bear out -- to confirm that an earlier claim was in fact true
"The numbers bear out the basic claim, but only in one industry."
A caveat -- a warning that limits how far a claim can be taken
"I agree with the piece, with one caveat about the sample."
Sweeping -- covering far too much at once, with no room for exceptions
"That is a sweeping statement, and the exceptions are the interesting part."
To hinge on -- to depend completely on one single thing
"The whole case hinges on how you measure output."
Entrenched -- fixed so deeply in place that it is very hard to change
"The habit of counting hours is entrenched for a reason."
A proxy -- something you measure because you cannot measure what you actually want
"Hours worked is a proxy for effort, and a poor one."
To gain traction -- to start being accepted, or to start actually working
"The idea has gained traction fastest where it was needed least."
To lag behind -- to move more slowly than the others and fall further back
"Everywhere the work is tied to presence, the idea has lagged behind."
At odds with -- in conflict with something else, so that the two do not fit together
"The reform is at odds with the way we decide who is working hard."
A downside -- the disadvantage that comes with something otherwise good
"The downside of a proxy is that people start managing the proxy."
To overstate -- to describe something as bigger or more certain than it really is
"It would be easy to overstate the objection, so let me be careful."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every few years an argument appears that claims the five-day week is over. The trials that get the headlines are the ones in which output held steady while hours fell, and the numbers do bear out the basic claim. Iceland, which ran the largest trial, published everything in full, and Japan, which followed two years later, found something similar.

The caveat is the sample. A firm whose work is easy to count can lose a day and make it up by cutting meetings; a hospital cannot. So the sweeping version of the argument — the one that says the shorter week simply works — starts to come apart. The idea has gained traction fastest in the industries that needed it least, and has lagged behind everywhere the output is the hour itself. Software is a case in point.

It would be easy to overstate the objection. The honest position is that the case hinges on measurement, and that our habit of counting hours is entrenched because hours are the one thing everybody agrees on. The downside of any proxy is that it eventually gets managed instead of the thing it stands for — which is why a serious version of this reform is at odds with the way most organizations currently decide who is working hard.

1. "The trials that get the headlines are the ones in which output held steady." Why are there no commas around that get the headlines?
A Because the clause is short.
B Because the clause identifies which trials. Delete it and you no longer know which trials are meant.
C Because commas are never used after the word trials.
2. "Iceland, which ran the largest trial, published everything in full." Why which and not that?
A Because the clause is inside commas, and a clause inside commas never takes that.
B Because Iceland is a country and countries always take which.
C Because the verb ran is in the past.
3. "A firm whose work is easy to count can lose a day." What does whose tell you here?
A That the firm belongs to somebody.
B That the writer does not know which firm is meant.
C That the work belongs to the firm. Whose marks possession, and it works for things as well as people.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Relative Clauses

GRAMMAR

One test decides everything: delete the clause. If you still know which one, it takes commas.

JobFormExample
Identifies which oneno commas · that or whichThe trials that measured output were the easy ones.
Only adds informationcommas · which, never thatIceland, which ran the largest trial, published it.
Peoplewho (or that, when identifying)The staff who stayed were cheaper to keep.
Possessionwhose — people and thingsA firm whose output is easy to count.
After a prepositionin which · at which · for whomThe point at which the argument comes apart.
You may drop itonly as object, only without commasThe article (that) I read this morning.
Never repeat the objectone pronoun onlyThe article that I read (not: read it).

Never: Iceland, that ran the trial · The staff which stayed · The article that I read it · The trials, that measured output, were... · The firm which its output is easy to count

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Say That Again -- The Call That Fails and the Polite Repair

The line breaks up, two people speak at once, and you need a sentence ready. Key words: patchy, to break up, to follow, a heads-up, a rundown, to run through something, to loop somebody in, to talk over someone, to drop off, to chime in, to get back to someone, to be on mute. Structure: indirect questions and polite requests.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Patchy -- coming and going, never steady -- said of a line or a signal
"Sorry, your line is a little patchy. Could you say that again?"
To break up -- when a call becomes impossible to hear because the connection fails
"You are breaking up a bit. I got the first half."
To follow -- to understand what somebody is saying while they are still saying it
"I am not sure I followed the last point. Could you take me through it again?"
A heads-up -- a warning given in advance, so that nobody is caught by surprise
"Thanks for the heads-up. I will have the draft ready before the call."
A rundown -- a short summary of the main points, given quickly
"Could you give me a quick rundown of what I missed?"
To run through something -- to go over the points of something quickly, one by one
"Let me run through the agenda before we start."
To loop somebody in -- to add a person to a conversation they should be part of
"I will loop our tax partner in before we answer that."
To talk over someone -- to keep speaking while another person is still speaking
"Sorry, I talked over you. Please go ahead."
To drop off -- to lose the connection and leave a call without meaning to
"I dropped off for a second. What did I miss?"
To chime in -- to add a short comment to a conversation that is already running
"May I chime in here for a second?"
To get back to someone -- to reply later, once you have found the answer
"I do not have that figure here. Can I get back to you this afternoon?"
To be on mute -- speaking with the microphone turned off, so nobody hears you
"I think you are on mute. We cannot hear you."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nine in the morning in São Paulo, one in the afternoon in London. The organizer opens the call and says she will run through the three points first, because she has a hard stop at half past. The line from her side is patchy from the first minute, and a warning arrives with it: if it breaks up, say so immediately.

She says a revised clause went out on Tuesday. He did not see it, and he does not say "I did not receive it". He says: "Could you tell me when exactly you sent that?" — a question with no inversion inside, and no accusation either. Then: "Would you mind copying our tax partner?", because he wants to loop the partner in before anybody answers on the indemnity. He was in a hearing all Tuesday, so he will check and get back to her before lunch.

Somebody on the New York line has been silent for ten minutes, and it turns out he is on mute. Another participant talks over the organizer twice, and then drops off completely for a minute. When he returns he asks for a quick rundown, because he could not follow the last exchange at all. At the end Rodrigo closes the way he always closes now: "So, to confirm: have I got that right?" Nobody has ever been annoyed by that question. It is the cheapest sentence on the call.

1. "Could you tell me when exactly you sent that?" Why is it not "when exactly did you send that"?
A Because the sentence is about the past.
B Because the question is now inside another sentence, so the word order goes back to subject before verb and the auxiliary disappears.
C Because could you tell me cannot be followed by a question word.
2. "Would you mind copying our tax partner?" Why copying and not copy?
A Because would you mind is always followed by the -ing form.
B Because the action is happening right now.
C Because the tax partner is a person and not a document.
3. He asks when the clause was sent instead of saying he never received it. What does the indirect question buy him?
A Nothing: the two sentences are identical in effect.
B It hides the fact that he did not read his email.
C It gets the same information without putting blame on anybody, which keeps the call moving instead of turning it into a defence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Indirect Questions and Polite Requests

GRAMMAR

The moment a question goes inside another sentence, it stops looking like a question.

JobFormExample
Direct questionverb before subjectWhen did they send it?
Indirect questionnormal order, no auxiliaryCould you tell me when they sent it?
Yes or no insideif / whetherI was wondering whether the clause is still there.
The openersCould you tell me / Do you know / I was wondering / Do you happen to knowall take normal order after them
Would you mind+ verb-ingWould you mind repeating that?
Softer requestCould / Would rather than CanCould you send it by Friday?
PunctuationI was wondering... is a statementI was wondering whether it arrived.

Never: Could you tell me when did they send it · Do you know where does it is · Would you mind to repeat · I was wondering if is the clause there? · Repeat, please (as your only repair)
