Whose English Is It?

Whose English Is It?

There are roughly four hundred million people who grew up speaking English. There are around one and a half billion who speak it and did not. That second number is the one that matters for your working life, because it means most conversations in English today happen between two people who both learned it.

This has a practical consequence that nobody tells you in a language course. The English you will actually hear is not the English of films. It is a Norwegian speaking carefully, a Dutch colleague going straight to the point, an Indian colleague with a rhythm that puts the stress in a different place. None of them is wrong. All of them are English.

It also changes what good listening means. The native speaker is not the target: understanding is. And understanding does not require catching every word. It requires catching the gist first, and only then going back for detail. Most learners do the opposite. They stop at the first word they miss, lose the thread, and by the time they recover, the speaker has moved on.

The other half of the skill is not listening at all. It is asking. People who work internationally interrupt to check constantly, and nobody thinks less of them for it. The sentence that keeps a meeting on the rails is not a clever one. It is just: sorry, I did not catch that, could you say it again?

Chapter 4: The Code

Asking Again, Without Sounding Rude

The same request, four levels of directness

Discovery

Same Request, Four Distances

"Sorry, what was that?"

"Sorry, I did not catch that."

"Could you repeat that, please?"

"Sorry, would you mind saying that again?"

All four ask for exactly the same thing. What changes is how much distance the sentence puts between you and the other person — and how much distance that person expects.

SentenceDistanceWho it fits
Sorry, what was that?CloseAmericans, Australians, colleagues you know
Sorry, I did not catch that.NeutralWorks everywhere. Your default.
Could you repeat that, please?PoliteFirst meetings, clients, larger groups
Would you mind saying that again?CarefulNordic colleagues, formal settings, seniority
Could you give me the short version?NeutralWhen you caught the words but missed the point
Just to check, are you saying ...?NeutralBefore you agree to anything that matters
What? on its own—Nobody you do not know well. It is not neutral.

In one line: the more distance the culture expects, the longer the sentence gets.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"What?"
"Sorry, I did not catch that."
"Repeat, please."
"Could you repeat that, please?"
"Sorry, I did not listen you."
"Sorry, I did not hear you."
Nodding and saying nothing when you did not understand.
"Sorry, I lost you at the part about phase one."

The first three are language. The fourth one is not, and it is the expensive one: every meeting where you nodded instead of asking is a meeting where a decision was made without you in it.

Comprehension

About Priya

1. What word did Priya have to explain, and how did she explain it?
Sequencing. She rephrased it in simple words: the order — which item comes first, second, third.
2. What is Priya's point about the list?
That agreeing the list is not enough — they have to agree the order today, or they only think they are on the same page.
3. How many times does Ana ask for help, and does the conversation suffer?
Three times: to repeat a word, to confirm a decision, and to get the short version. The conversation moves faster because of it, not slower.

Ask on the Spot

An American colleague says something you did not hear. You are on friendly terms. Ask again.

  • Sorry, what was that?
  • Say again? works with Americans and sounds relaxed, not rude.

A Norwegian colleague says something you did not catch. Ask again, in a register that fits.

  • Sorry, I did not catch that. Could you say it again?
  • Never just What? with a Nordic colleague -- it lands as impatient.

You caught the words but not the point. Ask for the gist, not for a repeat.

  • Sorry, could you give me the short version?
  • Just to check -- what is the main thing you want from me here?

You think you understood, but the decision matters. Confirm before agreeing.

  • Just to check, are you saying we move the deadline to the fifteenth?
  • So if I understood you correctly, ...

Somebody is talking over you and you have not finished your sentence.

  • Sorry, may I just finish this point?
  • Hold on one second, I was not quite done.

You lost the thread three sentences ago and the meeting has moved on. Recover without pretending.

  • Sorry, I lost you at the part about phase one. Could we go back to that?
  • Naming WHERE you lost it is what makes this work.
