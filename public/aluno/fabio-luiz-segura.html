Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two people stop at the same stand, one minute apart. The first one says: I work in wholesale banking. My division handles foreign exchange, trade services and trade finance. Every word is correct and the sentence is well built. The person behind the counter nods, smiles, and forgets the conversation before the end of the day. The second one says the same thing, and then adds one line: and this year we are replacing a settlement platform. That second person leaves with a meeting on Thursday.

English has two ways of talking about the present, and they do different jobs. The present simple is for what is always true: the role, the product, the structure of the company. I oversee innovation. A remittance to Europe takes two days. The bank serves large corporations. The present continuous — am, is or are plus the verb with -ing — is for what is happening around now, this quarter, this year. We are replacing a platform. My team is testing three providers. Right now I am looking for a counterpart in Germany.

The trap for a Portuguese speaker is that Portuguese uses one present for both jobs. Translated word by word, a sentence about a project that started in March comes out as this year we replace the platform — which in English sounds like something the bank does every single year, as a routine. The project disappears. On an exhibition floor that is expensive, because the only sentence that separates you from four hundred other visitors is the one about what is on your desk right now. Two more things to remember. In the negative and in questions the continuous keeps am, is or are — we are not testing that yet, are you working with any bank in Latin America? And a small group of verbs never take -ing, even about this exact moment: know, want, need, mean, cost. Not I am wanting a faster settlement. Say I want a faster settlement.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two people meet in the queue at the registration desk. One says: I attended this conference last year. The other says: I have attended this conference for eleven years. Both sentences are about the past, both are true, and they do different work. The first one closes: it names a year, and the year is over. The second one is still running — no finished time is mentioned, so next autumn it becomes twelve.

That is the rule. Use the past simple when the sentence names a finished time: last year, in 2023, on Tuesday, three weeks ago. Last year I attended the same conference. In 2023 we sent two people and booked nothing. Use the present perfect — has or have plus the past participle — when there is no finished time and the situation is still true today. And two small words do most of the work here. For answers how long: I have worked in trade finance for nine years. Since answers from when: I have led this team since 2019. A length of time takes for. A starting point takes since.

There are two traps, and a Portuguese speaker meets both. The first: naming a finished time makes the present perfect impossible, so it is never Last year I have attended. The second is more fossilised, because Portuguese uses a present tense in this exact sentence: since never goes with the present simple. Not I am in this role since 2019, but I have been in this role since 2019. Finally, remember the small words that pull the present perfect with them: just, already, yet, ever, never. I have just come from the panel. We have not launched there yet. Have you been to this conference before?

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A presentation in a second language fails for a reason nobody says out loud: not because the words are wrong, but because the listener does not know where they are. Somebody starts talking about a country, then a division, then a system, then a number, and after ninety seconds the person opposite has stopped following and started waiting. The fix costs three words at a time, and it is not vocabulary. It is a set of short phrases whose only job is to tell the listener what kind of sentence is coming next.

There are five that carry almost everything. To give you some context means background is coming, so do not expect the point yet. Let me walk you through means several steps, in order — and note the form: in English you walk a person through something. You never explain somebody something; you explain something to somebody, or, far more naturally in a meeting, you walk them through it. What that means in practice is announces the plain version of the technical sentence you just said, and it is the phrase that saves you when the other side does not know your jargon. The key challenge we are facing is tells everybody to stop taking notes and look up. And So that is what we are looking for says you have finished, which is how the other person knows it is their turn.

Inside those signposts, the two past tenses keep doing their work. A named year takes the past simple: Bradesco launched this division in 2018. The same story with no finished time takes the present perfect: Since then we have expanded into digital assets. Putting them the wrong way round is the most common slip in a company presentation, and it is worth saying why it matters here. The past simple closes the story: it says the division was launched and that was that. The present perfect keeps it open: it says the portfolio is still growing, which is exactly the impression you want to leave with somebody deciding whether to work with you.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every proposal you will ever be handed was written to be agreed with. That is not dishonesty; it is the job of the document. Which means that reading one well is not a matter of suspicion, but of grammar: you take each claim on the page and you turn it into a condition. Integration typically takes twelve weeks becomes If the integration runs past sixteen weeks, what happens to the fee? The claim was a statement and could only be agreed with or doubted. The condition is a question, and it has to be answered.

The structure is the first conditional, and it has one shape: if plus the present simple, then will plus the verb. If the volume triples, we will need two more people. The half after if is about the future, but it never takes will — that is the single most common mistake for a Portuguese speaker, because in Portuguese the future is allowed there. Not if the volume will triple. And the second half does not have to be a promise: very often it is simply a question. If the regulator changes the reporting rule in March, who absorbs the work?

Around the conditional sit four modal verbs that decide how hard the question lands. Could and might say something is possible without saying it is likely: That could work for the first corridor, but it might not survive the audit. Would is hypothetical, which is exactly why it is the polite one, and why the most useful question in a vendor meeting is built with it: What would you need from my side that you have not asked for yet? Should makes a recommendation without giving an order: We should agree the exit terms before the pilot. All four take the bare verb after them — never could to work, only could work.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is one structure in English that lets you give advice without giving an order, disagree without hardening a table, and ask a hard question without anybody losing face. It is the second conditional, and it works by a small trick: it uses a past form to talk about the present. If I were in your position, I would look at the regulatory angle first. Nothing in that sentence is in the past. The past form is simply the distance, and the distance is the politeness.

Compare the two shapes. The first conditional is for things that may really happen: if plus the present simple, then will. If the panel finishes early, I will come and find you. That is a real plan for tonight. The second conditional is for things you are only imagining: if plus the past simple, then would. If we expanded into that market, we would need a local partner. Nobody is expanding into anything; you are thinking out loud, and everybody at the table knows it.

Three details decide whether it sounds natural. First, the verb be in the if half is were for everybody, including I and he: if I were you, if he were in charge. You will hear native speakers say if I was you in casual conversation, but in a professional room the expected form is were. Second, would never appears in the if half. Not if I would be you — that mistake comes straight from Portuguese and it is the most audible one. And third, the past form is not about the past at all: if we expanded is about now, and about a decision nobody has taken.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Half of what you will say at a technology conference is a comparison between how something worked before and how it works now, and English has a structure built for exactly that. Used to plus the bare verb says that something was a habit or a state, and that it has stopped — without you having to say when, or having to explain that it ended. Banks used to send short coded messages. Compliance teams used to check everything by telephone. We used to reconcile by hand. In every one of those sentences, the listener knows the practice is over, and nobody had to add a date.

Compare it with the past simple, which is for one finished event with a time attached: last year we tested two providers. That is one thing that happened once. We used to test every provider ourselves is a habit that lasted years and then stopped. The two are not interchangeable, and choosing the wrong one changes the story you are telling about your own institution.

Two mechanical details. In the negative and in the question, the d disappears, because did is already carrying the past: did not use to, did you use to — never did not used to. And there is a lookalike that means something completely different: be used to, which means to be accustomed to something, and which takes a noun or a verb with -ing. I am used to meetings in English, or I am used to working in English — never I am used to work. One sentence says a habit ended; the other says you are comfortable with something now. In a meeting, saying the wrong one turns a story about progress into a statement about yourself.

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

English has two ways of saying the same event, and choosing between them is not a matter of style. Our team identified the gap in 2022 puts a person first. The gap was identified by our team in 2022 puts the fact first and the person second. Both are correct; they do different jobs. The second one is the passive voice, and its form is simple: the verb be, in whatever tense you need, plus the past participle.

Use it when the fact is what matters and the author does not. The pilot was approved last quarter. Nobody in the room needs the name of the committee. Payments are settled in two days on that corridor. That is how the world is, not something a person did. And forecasts live almost entirely here: trade flow is projected to grow again this year has no author at all, which is honest, because a projection belongs to a model rather than to a person.

Now the part most people get wrong after they discover the structure. The passive is for data and for institutional facts. It is not for ownership. The model was built by me and the numbers are owned by my team is grammatically perfect and it sounds like somebody hiding. Say I built the model and my team owns the numbers. A presentation entirely in the passive sounds like a report nobody wrote, and the moment you need to be accountable, you switch back to the active and stay there.

One mechanical warning. The passive needs the participle, not the past. It is never the gap was identify and never the pilot approved last quarter when what you mean is was approved. And a habit worth building alongside it: a figure said out loud gets a baseline in the same breath. Not volume grew forty per cent, but volume grew forty per cent year on year, from a two-year base, on a corridor that grew nine.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In a room where somebody runs three systems, the word system is almost useless on its own. English fixes this with a very small piece of machinery: one word that attaches a description to a noun and turns it into exactly one thing. This is the system that handles our cross-border settlement. Without that, the listener has to guess which of the three you mean. With it, there is nothing to guess.

Which word you use depends on what you are identifying. For a thing, use that or which: the dependency that decides the timeline. For a person, use who: the vendor who presented yesterday. For a place, use where: the two places where somebody still works by hand. That last one matters more than it looks, because a Portuguese speaker will reach for that in all three cases, and the place that we lose the file sounds wrong to every listener without any of them being able to say why.

There is one mechanical trap and it comes straight from Portuguese. The relative word already does the job of the pronoun, so you do not repeat it. It is never the system that it handles, only the system that handles. In Portuguese the extra pronoun is natural; in English it is the single clearest sign that somebody is translating in their head.

Finally, commas. If the description is what identifies the thing, there are no commas: the system that handles settlement tells you which one. If the thing is already identified and the description is extra, the commas go in: the reporting change, which was approved last quarter, decides the timeline — there is only one reporting change, and the middle part is additional information. The test is simple: take the clause out. If you still know which thing is meant, use commas. If you do not, leave them off.

1. Why does the text say the word "system" is almost useless on its own?
A Because it is too technical.
B Because in a room where somebody runs three systems, the listener has to guess which one you mean.
C Because it has no direct translation.
2. What does the text call the clearest sign that somebody is translating in their head?
A Repeating the pronoun: "the system that it handles".
B Using which instead of that.
C Forgetting the commas.
3. What is the test for whether to use commas?
A Whether the sentence is long.
B Take the clause out: if you still know which thing is meant, use commas.
C Whether you are describing a person or a thing.

Order

The projector has failed and somebody asks how your payment flow works. Put your explanation in a logical order.

?"Money leaves the client, sits in three places on the way, and arrives two days later."
?"I can take you through any of the three places, in any order."
?"This is the system that handles the first leg, and the instruction leaves in under a second."
?"Most of the two days sits in the correspondent chain, which is outside our control."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to identify one of your three systems. You say:
A This is the system that it handles our settlement.
B This is the system that handles our cross-border settlement.
C This is the system, it handles our settlement.
You want to name the two spots where somebody still works manually. You say:
A There are two places that we work by hand.
B There are two places where somebody still works by hand.
C There are two places which them we work by hand.
A question comes fast and you need five seconds. You say:
A Sorry, I did not understand anything.
B So if I understand you correctly, you are asking about the second leg.
C Yes, that is a very good question.
Somebody asks for a number you do not have. You say:
A It is around eight or nine per cent, I think.
B I do not have that number. Marina owns it, and I will send it on Thursday.
C I will have to check that.
