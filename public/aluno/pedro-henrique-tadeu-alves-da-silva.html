Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

The Room Where the Song Gets Made

Your job sits between two departments and has no clean name. This lesson gives you the words for it. Key words: a session, a take, a cue, to mix, a rehearsal, the crew, a backing track, to book, a shortlist, lyrics, to pitch, on the side. Structure: present simple for what is true every season, present continuous for what you are in the middle of this week.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A session -- a block of studio time when people record or mix music together
"The session starts at ten and the singer arrives at eleven."
A take -- one complete attempt at recording a performance, from beginning to end
"We kept the third take, because the first two were too careful."
A cue -- a short piece of music, or a signal, that tells someone exactly when to start
"The cue runs four seconds long, so the picture team has to cut something."

"I mix at night, because nobody calls me after nine."
A rehearsal -- the practice that happens before the real performance or recording
"You find the real problems in the rehearsal, never in the meeting."

"Half of the crew has worked on this show since the first season."
A backing track -- the recorded music a singer performs over on stage
"I am building the backing tracks for the live shows this week."
To book -- to reserve a room, a studio or a person in advance
"We book the studio two weeks before we need it."
A shortlist -- the small group chosen from many candidates
"Four thousand people send a video, and the shortlist has forty names."
Lyrics -- the words of a song
"I read the lyrics before I listen, and then I listen again."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody asks what you do, and the honest answer has two halves. The first half is the part that is true every season, and English puts it in the base form of the verb. He works between audiovisual production and music production. He mixes at night. He books the studio two weeks in advance. His team produces music shows. Nothing there is happening right now as he speaks; it is simply the shape of the job. Only the third person is different, and it costs one letter: his colleague runs the picture side, the singer performs over a backing track.

The second half is the part he is in the middle of, and there the verb changes shape completely. Right now he is building the backing tracks for a new season. This week he is sitting in rehearsals with a notebook. Today he is arguing with the picture team about a cue that runs four seconds long. These are temporary. They started, they have not finished, and in three weeks they will be somebody else's problem. The clue is usually in the sentence: right now, this week, at the moment, today.

The same two halves organize everything else he could say. He produces his own projects on the side, which is permanent. He is pitching a new format for the auditions, which is not. He always keeps the third take; he is comparing three of them this afternoon. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: a few verbs almost never take the continuous form, even when the moment is now. Not I am knowing the answer, but I know the answer. The same goes for want, need, believe and understand.

1. "He mixes at night." Why the base form with -s, and not "he is mixing at night"?
A Because "mix" is a verb that has no continuous form.
B Because it describes the general shape of his work, not something he is in the middle of.
C Because the sentence has no object after the verb.
2. "This week he is sitting in rehearsals." What does the continuous form add here?
A That it is temporary: it started, it is not finished, and it will stop.
B That it happens every week of the year.
C That it is more polite than the base form.
3. The text warns against "I am knowing the answer". What is the problem?
A "Know" can only be used in questions.
B The sentence needs a time expression such as "right now".
C "Know" describes a state, not an action in progress, so English keeps it in the base form.

Ten Years In

The four lines that follow you everywhere: how long you have been doing this, and what has come out of it. Key words: a credit, a showreel, self-taught, to run something, a milestone, to specialize in, a gap, to turn into, steady, word of mouth. Structure: present perfect with since for the starting point and for for the length of time.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A credit -- your name in the list of the people who made a song, a film or a show
"I have three album credits, and two of them are records nobody bought."
A showreel -- a short collection of your best work that you send to people who might hire you
"My showreel is two minutes long, and the first ten seconds do all the work."

"I am self-taught, which means I learned the software before I learned the theory."

"I have run the music side of that show for four seasons."

"The hundredth live show was a milestone, and it took me eight years."
To specialize in -- to make one narrow area the thing you are known for
"I have specialized in live music shows, and that is why people call me."
A gap -- a period when nothing happened, between two things that did
"There is a gap in 2020, and everybody in this industry has one."
To turn into -- to change slowly into something different
"The projects I do for fun have turned into half of my income."
Steady -- continuing at the same rate, without stopping and without surprises
"The work has been steady since 2018, which in this industry is a luxury."
Word of mouth -- the way work arrives when one person tells another that you are good

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a paragraph that follows you everywhere: under your photo, at the top of an email to a stranger, next to the link to your showreel. Every sentence in it does the same job, and English gives that job one shape: have or has plus the past participle. He has worked in television. He has specialized in live music shows. He has run the music side of a show for four seasons. All of it started in the past, and all of it is still true today, which is exactly why the past simple does not fit here.

Then the sentence usually needs a measurement, and there are only two. Since points at the beginning: since 2016, since January, since he left college. For measures the distance: for eight years, for six months. The work has been steady since 2018. The projects he does on the side have turned into half of his income. He has been building backing tracks for eight years — and that longer form, have been plus -ing, says the activity is still running right now.

Some sentences carry no date at all, and they are the strongest ones. He has delivered more than a hundred live shows. He has been nominated twice. He is self-taught, and almost every job he has had has arrived by word of mouth. Each of those is a milestone or a fact, not a story, so it needs one line and no year. And the gap? There has been one, in 2020, and he names it in six words. Two warnings for a Portuguese speaker: never the present simple with since (not I work here since 2016), and never the continuous with for (not I am working here for ten years).

1. "He has worked in television since 2016." Why not "He worked in television since 2016"?
A Because "work" has no past simple form.
B Because it started in the past and is still true today, and the past simple would close it.
C Because "since" can only be used with "have".
2. What is the difference between "since 2016" and "for eight years"?
A "Since" points at the moment it began; "for" measures how long it has lasted.
B "Since" is formal and "for" is informal.
C "Since" is for work and "for" is for study.
3. The text says "He has delivered more than a hundred live shows" is one of the strongest sentences. Why?
A Because it uses a longer verb form than the others.
B Because it explains how he got the job.
C Because it is a result that needs no date at all, so one line is enough.

Saying What You Think

Somebody plays you their work and then goes quiet. This lesson fills that silence with something useful. Key words: a draft, to stand out, subtle, to overdo, a hook, to land, clunky, to tighten, a reference, blunt, to hold up, a nitpick. Structure: present perfect when the time does not matter, past simple the moment a finished time appears.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A draft -- an early version that is not finished yet
"This is a draft, so tell me about the structure and not about the mix."
To stand out -- to be noticeably better or different from everything around it
"The bass line stands out, and everything else is doing the same thing."
Subtle -- small and quiet, and easy to miss, but it changes everything
"It is subtle: the drums come in half a bar late, and that is why it breathes."
To overdo -- to use so much of it that it stops working
"The strings are overdone in the last chorus, and the voice disappears."
A hook -- the short part of a song people remember without trying
"The hook is four notes long and I have not stopped hearing it since yesterday."
To land -- to have the effect you wanted, on the person listening
"The first chorus lands. The second one repeats it and nothing happens."
Clunky -- heavy and awkward, so the listener notices the machinery
"The transition into the bridge is clunky, and you can hear the edit."
To tighten -- to make something shorter and more exact, with nothing loose
"If you tighten the bridge by four bars, the whole thing holds up."
A reference -- another track you point at to explain the sound you want
"Send me a reference, because warm means six different things."
Blunt -- saying the true thing directly, with no soft edges
"Can I be blunt? The second verse is where I stopped listening."
To hold up -- to still work well after you hear it many times
"I have played it twenty times this week and it still holds up."
A nitpick -- a very small criticism that does not change the whole thing
"One nitpick, and then I will stop: the fade at the end is two seconds long."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody plays you two minutes of their own work and then goes quiet. Every sentence you are about to say sits on one decision, and the decision is not about politeness. It is about whether the sentence names a finished time. "I have listened to it three times" carries no date, and that is the point: the fact matters, the moment does not. "I listened to it last night" names a time that is over, and the verb goes straight to the past form. Same event, two different sentences, and the only thing that changed is last night.

In a feedback round the two forms usually arrive together, in this order. The question opens with the present perfect: "Have you heard the new version?" The answer explains with the past simple: "Yes, I heard it yesterday." The same pattern covers experience. "Have you ever worked with a live band?" asks about a whole life up to now, so there is no date. "I have never mixed an orchestra" says the same thing from the other side. Three irregular participles do most of the work in this room: write becomes written, hear becomes heard, send becomes sent.

Then there is the content of the note itself. The hook lands or it does not. The bass line stands out. The transition is clunky. The strings are overdone and the voice disappears under them. One change is subtle and already works; one problem would go away if you tighten the middle; one thing is only a nitpick and you say so out loud, so the writer knows how much it weighs. And if you cannot describe the sound you want, send a reference instead of an adjective. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: never put a finished time next to the present perfect. Not I have heard it yesterday.

1. Why is it "I listened to it last night" and not "I have listened to it last night"?
A Because "listen" is an irregular verb.
B Because the sentence names a finished time, and a finished time sends the verb to the past form.
C Because the present perfect cannot be used with the verb "listen".
2. According to the text, what order do the two forms usually arrive in during a feedback round?
A The question opens with the present perfect, and the answer explains with the past simple.
B The past simple always comes first, because it is shorter.
C They never appear in the same conversation.
3. The text says to send a reference instead of an adjective. Why?
A Because adjectives are informal in a feedback round.
B Because a reference is faster to send than a sentence.
C Because an adjective like "warm" means six different things, and another track shows exactly what you mean.

Inside a Lyric

A song has three minutes and no room to explain anything, so it shows instead. Key words: a verse, a line, an image, a twist, to hint at something, plain, to set a scene, a narrator, bittersweet, to unfold. Structure: the past continuous is the room, and the past simple is what happens in it.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A verse -- one section of a song that carries the story, with new words each time
"The second verse says the same thing as the first, and that is the problem."

"The best opening line I read last year was six words long."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A song has about three minutes and no room to explain anything, so the first lines do not report events — they set a scene. The rain was falling. She was standing in the kitchen with her coat still on. Both of those were already running when you arrived, and the form that says so is was or were plus -ing. It opens a window on something in progress, and the listener walks in halfway.

Then the events arrive, and they arrive in the past form, one after another, like beats. She put the keys on the table. He asked where she had been. She did not answer. Each of those is finished, and finished is what moves a story forward. The two forms do different jobs, and a good verse uses both in one breath: while the radio was playing in the other room, he said the one sentence he could not take back. The background keeps running; the event lands on top of it.

The third tool is what the words refuse to do. A plain line such as she put the keys on the table hints at everything and states nothing, and that absence is the craft: the listener supplies the feeling. The best lyrics are full of images and almost empty of explanation. The narrator is not the writer either, and can know less than you do. And near the end something turns: the coat was never taken off, because she was never staying. That is the twist, the reason the song unfolds instead of ending, and it usually leaves the listener somewhere bittersweet. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: the background needs the -ing form. Not the rain fell and she stood in the kitchen as an opening.

1. Why is it "the rain was falling" and not "the rain fell" at the start of a song?
A Because "fall" is an irregular verb and needs the longer form.
B Because it was already running when the listener arrived, and the -ing form is what says so.
C Because the past simple cannot be used for the weather.
2. "While the radio was playing, he said the one sentence he could not take back." What does each half do?
A The first half is the background that keeps running; the second half is the event that lands on top of it.
B Both halves report finished events, one after the other.
C The first half is more polite than the second.
3. Why does the text say a plain line works better than one that names the feeling?
A Because short lines are easier to sing.
B Because the listener supplies the feeling, and a feeling you supplied yourself is stronger.
C Because adjectives are not allowed in songs.

GRAMMAR

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Almost every pitch that works opens in the past, because before anybody hears your idea they need to believe that you understand the thing you want to change. So the first thirty seconds describe the old normal. We used to run the auditions on paper. Four thousand people would send a video, and a team of six would sit in a room for three weeks. Both forms say the same thing — that this was the routine and it is over — but they are not interchangeable. Used to works with anything. Would only works with repeated ACTIONS.

That limit is the whole grammar of this lesson. There used to be one reviewer who watched everything first, and you cannot say there would be, because be is a state and not an action. The same applies to have, know and live. The negative and the question hide a second trap: after did, the -d disappears. We did not use to record anything. Did you use to print the sheets by hand? And a third one, which is a different structure altogether: I used to work here means it is finished, while I am used to working here means it feels normal to me now.

Once the old way is on the table, the rest of the pitch is three moves. Name the bottleneck, because the lead time is usually waiting and not work. Show the proof of concept before you ask for the budget — that order is the one thing almost nobody does. Then name the trade-off yourself, out loud, before the decision-maker finds it. Expect somebody to push back; a fair objection deserves a design, not an argument, which is why you roll out the new system beside the old one. And remember that a signature is not buy-in: the people who have to live with the change are the ones who decide whether it survives.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every studio hands you two printed pages on the first day, and almost nothing that matters is in them. You must wear the pass. You must not bring food into the control room. Studio two is off limits after eleven. Those are real, but you can read them in four minutes and then forget them, because the room enforces them for you. The rules that decide whether you are invited back are the etiquette, and nobody will ever tell you what they are.

English marks where a rule comes from, out loud. Must usually comes from inside the building: the studio decided it. Have to often comes from outside it — the broadcaster, the client, the contract. We have to deliver on Thursday because somebody else owns the slot. Should is advice, and it is the form the unwritten rules arrive in, which is exactly why they are easy to ignore. You should flag a problem the same day, in one sentence. You should not cut in while somebody is listening back. Can and may are permission, and the difference between them is mostly how formal the room is.

Two traps. The first is that must not and do not have to look similar and mean opposite things: you must not stay is a prohibition, and you do not have to stay means it is simply not required. The second is that none of these modals ever takes to, and none of them is followed by -ing. Not you must to wear, not we have to delivering. And one piece of vocabulary that carries its own rule: if you build a workaround, you must tell somebody, because a mandatory deadline, a night of overtime and a weekend on call are all survivable, but a workaround nobody knows about is not.
