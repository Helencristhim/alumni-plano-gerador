The First Minute -- Who You Are and What You Are Doing Now

The ninety seconds that open every conversation in English: who you are, what you do, and what you happen to be doing this week. Key expressions: to be based in, a colleague, to take care of, at the moment, busy, to deal with, a client, to cover for, a break, straight away. Structure: present simple versus present continuous.

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Janderson works at a bank in Sao Paulo. He is based in the same building every day, on the eighth floor, and he takes care of investments for about forty families. He deals with clients who put money outside Brazil, and if one of them writes to him, he answers straight away. That is his job, and it does not change from one month to the next.

This week, however, three things are different. He is covering for a colleague who is on holiday, so he is dealing with two desks instead of one. He is also starting an English course, twice a week, at half past eight in the evening. And on Thursday he is taking the whole afternoon off, which almost never happens.

On Tuesday he is the most busy, so he never books anything after six. The team takes a break at eleven and everybody walks to the coffee machine at the end of the corridor. He does not drink coffee in the afternoon. At the moment he is drinking tea instead, and his colleagues find this very funny.

If you meet him by the coffee machine and ask him what he does, he has about ninety seconds to answer. Two sentences about the job that never changes, one sentence about the week that will be over on Friday, and one question back. That is the whole first minute.

1. "He works at a bank" and "he is covering for a colleague." Why are the two verbs in different forms?
A Because the second sentence is about the past.
B Because the job is permanent and covering for the colleague is only this week.
C Because the second sentence is a question.
2. Which verb in the text takes an S, and why?
A Works, takes and deals, because the subject is he.
B All of them, because the text is in the present.
C None of them, because English never adds an S to a verb.
3. What does the text say about Tuesday?
A He takes the whole afternoon off.
B It is the day he is most busy, so he books nothing after six.
C The team takes a longer break.
4. According to the text, what does a good first minute contain?
A A long explanation of the whole company.
B Two sentences about the job, one about this week, and one question back.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

The question is never "when?" It is always "permanent or temporary?" Answer that first and the verb chooses itself.

FormWhat it saysExample
Present simple — positivePermanent. Your job, your routine, the facts about you."I work at a bank."
"She takes care of the visitors."
Present simple — negative and questionThe sentence borrows do or does, and the main verb loses its S."I don't work on Saturdays."
"Does she deal with clients?"
Present continuous — positiveTemporary. True this week, this month, this project, and it will stop."This week I am covering for a colleague."
Present continuous — negative and questionNothing is borrowed. Am / is / are simply moves."I am not covering for anybody."
"Are you working from home?"
The words that give it awayEvery day, usually, never, on Tuesdays → simple. At the moment, this week, right now, today → continuous."I usually answer straight away."
"At the moment I am reading it."
The trapA small group of verbs about the mind never takes the -ing form: understand, know, want, need, mean, remember. They are not actions.NOT "I am not understanding"
"I don't understand."

In a first conversation you will almost always need both, one after the other: what you do, and what you happen to be doing this week. That second sentence is what makes you sound like a person and not a job title.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past Simple

GRAMMAR

One tense covers everything that already finished. The whole difficulty is one question: is the past inside the verb, or inside did? It is never inside both.

FormWhat it saysExample
Regular verbs: verb + EDMost verbs simply take -ed, and the form is the same for every subject. No S anywhere."We booked a table."
"He walked away."
Irregular verbs: a new wordAbout a hundred common verbs change completely. The ten most common cover half of what you say every day.go → went · take → took · have → had
find → found · eat → ate · sit → sat
Negative: did not + plain verbDid carries the past, so the main verb loses it."I did not go anywhere."
NOT "I did not went"
Question: did + subject + plain verbSame idea, and English needs that did. A rising voice is not enough, as it is in Portuguese."Did you take the day off?"
NOT "You took the day off?"
The exception: to beWas and were work alone. They never borrow did."The restaurant was not open."
"Were you on your own?"
The words that give it awayYesterday, last weekend, two hours ago, in twenty nineteen, when I was twenty — all of them close the door on the present."Last weekend I took a day off."

You already recognise every one of these forms when you read them. The work now is producing them at speed, in a corridor, before you have had time to think.

While he was waiting in the car, something small and good happened. He ran into nobody, obviously, because he was alone in a car, but the client called him — and the client was in the same jam, six cars behind. So they did the whole meeting on the telephone, from two cars that were going nowhere.

Luckily he made it to the office before the client did, and the meeting started only twenty minutes late. His colleague in London was about to call him when he finally walked in. She asked the question that gave this lesson its title: what were you doing all that time?

1. "He was reading an email when he realised he had no signal." Which action started first?
A Reading the email — it was already happening, so it takes was + -ing.
B Realising — because it comes second in the sentence.
C Both started at exactly the same moment.
2. "What were you doing all that time?" Why is there no did in this question?
A Because it is an informal question.
B Because were is already the auxiliary, so nothing needs to be borrowed.
C Because the question is about the future.
3. What does "while he was waiting in the car" tell you?
A That the waiting finished before the next action started.
B That the waiting was the background, and something else happened inside it.
C That he waited only for a second.
4. Why does the text say he was lucky?
A Because the road opened again quickly.
B Because he made it to the office before the client did.
C Because the meeting was cancelled.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

The past continuous is not about time. It is a frame: it paints the scene, and the past simple is what walks into it.

FormWhat it saysExample
was / were + verb-INGThe long action. It was already running when the story starts."I was driving to the office."
Past simpleThe interruption. It happened once, quickly, and it finished."… when the road closed."
when and whileWhen usually introduces the short action; while the long one. Both orders mean the same."I was reading when he called."
"While I was reading, he called."
Two long actionsIf both were happening at the same time, both take -ing."While I was waiting, she was working from home."
Negative and questionMove was or were, or add not after it. Nothing is borrowed — there is no did here."I was not driving."
"Were you waiting long?"
The trapVerbs about the mind still refuse the -ing form, exactly as in the present.NOT "I was not knowing"
"I did not know."

One practical use of this structure at work: it explains a delay without sounding like an excuse. I was on my way when the road closed gives the reason and the timing in six words.

He took a day to make up your mind — his words again, and we will fix the pronoun in class — and in the end he decided to go for the second one, because it was better value for money. Neither hotel was better in general. One was better for him, that week, at that price.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Three shapes cover every comparison in English, and the only technical decision is the length of the adjective.

FormWhat it saysExample
Short adjective + ER + thanOne or two syllables, comparing exactly two things.cheap → "cheaper than"
big → "bigger than"
easy → "easier than"
MORE + long adjective + thanThree syllables or more. The adjective itself never changes."more convenient than"
"more reliable than"
THE + adjective + EST / THE MOSTThree or more things. The word the is obligatory."the cheapest of the three"
"the most expensive of the three"
AS + adjective + ASEqual. With not, the first one is less."as fast as the train"
"not as reliable as the train"
The three irregularsGood, bad and far refuse both rules and you simply learn them.good → better → the best
bad → worse → the worst
far → further → the furthest
Making it stronger or softerOne word in front does all the work. Never use two comparative forms together.much cheaper · far more reliable
slightly more expensive · by far the best
NOT "more cheaper"

The word after a comparison is always than — never that, and never of. That single word is the most common comparison mistake made by Portuguese speakers, and it is a one-second fix.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Janderson is going to fly to Miami on Tuesday. He booked the whole thing three months in advance, because it is much cheaper, and it is a round trip: he is back on Thursday night. He always checks in online the night before and he travels with hand luggage only, so he never waits at the belt.

The schedule is tight. He lands at half past ten, he has three meetings the same afternoon, and there is a connection in the middle of the journey that gives him fifty minutes. On paper it works. On paper everything works.

At the desk the woman tells him the flight is going to take off ninety minutes late. There is a delay on the incoming aircraft and nobody can do anything about it. He looks at the board and does the arithmetic. He is going to miss the connection; that is not an opinion, it is a queue and a clock.

So he decides, right there, in front of her. I will call the office now, he says, and I will ask them to move the first meeting. She says I will put you on the later flight and print a new boarding pass. And a colleague in Miami has already written to say: do not worry, I will pick you up at the airport, whatever time you land.

1. "He is going to fly on Tuesday." Why not will?
A Because the decision was made months ago, when he bought the ticket.
B Because Tuesday is close.
C Because flying always takes going to.
2. "He is going to miss the connection." What kind of future is this?
A An opinion with no proof.
B A prediction based on evidence he can see: the queue and the clock.
C A promise to somebody else.
3. "I will call the office now." Why will?
A Because it is in the far future.
B Because the decision is being taken at that moment, in the conversation.
C Because calling is a routine.
4. What is the difference between the two futures, according to the text?
A How far away the action is.
B When the decision was taken -- before the conversation, or during it.
C Whether the sentence is positive or negative.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Going To and Will

GRAMMAR

Both are futures and neither is more polite. The question is never when will it happen. It is when did you decide.

FormWhat it saysExample
am / is / are going to + verbThe decision already existed before this conversation. Plans, tickets, arrangements."I am going to fly on Tuesday."
"She is going to check in tonight."
will + verbThe decision is being taken now: an offer, a promise, an instant reaction."I will call him now."
"I will pick you up."
going to for evidenceYou can see the proof in front of you."Look at that queue. We are going to miss it."
will for opinionYou think or believe it, with no proof. Often after I think."I think it will be fine."
Negative and questionGoing to moves the verb to be. Will becomes will not or won't."Are you going to check in online?"
"I won't be late."
The trapThere is always a form of to be in front of going to.NOT "I go to travel"
"I am going to travel."

One practical rule for a meeting: everything already in your calendar takes going to, and everything you promise during the meeting takes will. That single distinction makes you sound organised instead of vague.
