Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

The question is never "when?" It is always "permanent or temporary?" Answer that first and the verb chooses itself.

FormWhat it saysExample
Present simple — positivePermanent. Your job, your routine, the facts about you."I work at a bank."
"She takes care of the visitors."
Present simple — negative and questionThe sentence borrows do or does, and the main verb loses its S."I don't work on Saturdays."
"Does she deal with clients?"
Present continuous — positiveTemporary. True this week, this month, this project, and it will stop."This week I am covering for a colleague."
Present continuous — negative and questionNothing is borrowed. Am / is / are simply moves."I am not covering for anybody."
"Are you working from home?"
The words that give it awayEvery day, usually, never, on Tuesdays → simple. At the moment, this week, right now, today → continuous."I usually answer straight away."
"At the moment I am reading it."
The trapA small group of verbs about the mind never takes the -ing form: understand, know, want, need, mean, remember. They are not actions.NOT "I am not understanding"
"I don't understand."

In a first conversation you will almost always need both, one after the other: what you do, and what you happen to be doing this week. That second sentence is what makes you sound like a person and not a job title.

GRAMMAR

One tense covers everything that already finished. The whole difficulty is one question: is the past inside the verb, or inside did? It is never inside both.

FormWhat it saysExample
Regular verbs: verb + EDMost verbs simply take -ed, and the form is the same for every subject. No S anywhere."We booked a table."

Irregular verbs: a new wordAbout a hundred common verbs change completely. The ten most common cover half of what you say every day.go → went · take → took · have → had

Negative: did not + plain verbDid carries the past, so the main verb loses it."I did not go anywhere."
NOT "I did not went"
Question: did + subject + plain verbSame idea, and English needs that did. A rising voice is not enough, as it is in Portuguese."Did you take the day off?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

The past continuous is not about time. It is a frame: it paints the scene, and the past simple is what walks into it.

FormWhat it saysExample
was / were + verb-INGThe long action. It was already running when the story starts."I was driving to the office."
Past simpleThe interruption. It happened once, quickly, and it finished."… when the road closed."
when and whileWhen usually introduces the short action; while the long one. Both orders mean the same."I was reading when he called."
"While I was reading, he called."
Two long actionsIf both were happening at the same time, both take -ing."While I was waiting, she was working from home."
Negative and questionMove was or were, or add not after it. Nothing is borrowed — there is no did here."I was not driving."
"Were you waiting long?"
The trapVerbs about the mind still refuse the -ing form, exactly as in the present.NOT "I was not knowing"
"I did not know."

One practical use of this structure at work: it explains a delay without sounding like an excuse. I was on my way when the road closed gives the reason and the timing in six words.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Three shapes cover every comparison in English, and the only technical decision is the length of the adjective.

FormWhat it saysExample
Short adjective + ER + thanOne or two syllables, comparing exactly two things.cheap → "cheaper than"
big → "bigger than"
easy → "easier than"
MORE + long adjective + thanThree syllables or more. The adjective itself never changes."more convenient than"
"more reliable than"
THE + adjective + EST / THE MOSTThree or more things. The word the is obligatory."the cheapest of the three"
"the most expensive of the three"
AS + adjective + ASEqual. With not, the first one is less."as fast as the train"
"not as reliable as the train"
The three irregularsGood, bad and far refuse both rules and you simply learn them.good → better → the best
bad → worse → the worst
far → further → the furthest
Making it stronger or softerOne word in front does all the work. Never use two comparative forms together.much cheaper · far more reliable
slightly more expensive · by far the best
NOT "more cheaper"

The word after a comparison is always than — never that, and never of. That single word is the most common comparison mistake made by Portuguese speakers, and it is a one-second fix.

FormWhat it saysExample
who / whichWho for people, which for things."the client who signed"

thatWorks for both, and it is what people actually say. Never after a comma."the client that signed"
