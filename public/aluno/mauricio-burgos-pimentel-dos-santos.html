Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

I joined this bank in two thousand and one, when I was twenty-six. For the first four years I worked in the back office and I did not speak to a single client. Then I got my first promotion. They gave me an assignment in another city for a year, and that was the real turning point — I came back with clients of my own. In two thousand and fifteen I took over the commercial team. A Four years.

B Because he came back with clients of his own.
Why is it "I have been here for twenty-three years" and not "I am here since twenty-three years"?

B Because twenty-three is a big number.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Normally my team handles around forty deals a month. I oversee the commercial side and I coordinate with risk and with legal every week. That is the normal picture. B Ten.

B Finishing the migration and closing the budget.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the text. Then answer the questions.

I am going to spend two weeks at the programme in November, and I have a plan. Before I leave, we are going to close the annual budget, because I am not going to answer emails about it from California. The agenda has four sessions a day; a professor will facilitate each one, so I am going to have to participate and not just listen. On the last Thursday there is a panel, and my goal is to ask one question a day, even a bad one. Last time I asked zero. The outcome I want is five people I can call afterwards. I am committing to that now, out loud, so somebody can check.
What is going to happen before he leaves?
A They are going to close the annual budget.
B He is going to answer emails from California.
Why is it "a professor will facilitate" and not "is going to facilitate"?
A Because it is a fact about the programme, announced by someone else — not his own plan.
B Because professors always use will.
What is his goal?
A To meet forty people.
B To ask one question a day.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Two futures — the difference is when you decided

Decided before this conversation → going to. Deciding while you speak → will.

going to — the plan

I’m going to present a case.
We’re going to close the budget.
I’m not going to miss a session.

will — right now

I’ll take that question.
I’ll send it tonight.
I’ll ask him for you.

? Are you going to present? — not "Do you going to present?"
× "I will to close the budget" — after will the verb is bare: I will close.
× "I go to participate" — the plan needs going to: I am going to participate.

In real speech: gonna. You do not have to say it — but you will hear it constantly, so recognise it.

Checkpoint — Review and Your First Pitch

Eight phrases that hold the floor while you build the next sentence (in short, that said, roughly, to be honest, it depends on, as far as I know, I would say, the point is) plus a review of the four shapes from lessons 1 to 4. No new grammar. Around 30 minutes, in one sitting.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Phrases That Buy You Time

Vocabulary

Tap Listen, then say each word out loud with your own numbers. Eight words, three minutes.

In short — a phrase to start a summary
"In short, we are running behind."
That said — to add something that goes against what you just said
"It is hard. That said, we will finish."
Roughly — about, not exactly
"Roughly twelve hundred clients."
To be honest — to say what you really think, not the polite version
"To be honest, I was not ready."
It depends on — the answer changes with the situation
"It depends on the client."
As far as I know — you think it is true, but you are not certain
"As far as I know, the deadline has not moved."
I would say — a softer way to give your opinion
"I would say it is the biggest risk."
The point is — to bring the listener back to your main idea
"The point is, we need two more people."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the text. Then answer the questions.

In short, I run a commercial desk: eight people, roughly twelve hundred corporate clients. I have been at the bank for twenty-three years — I joined in two thousand and one, when I was twenty-six. At the moment we are restructuring the workflow, and to be honest, it is the hardest quarter I remember. That said, we are only nine deals behind target. As far as I know, the deadline has not moved, so we will close it. In November I am going to present our case at a programme in California. The point is, I want feedback from people who have never worked in a bank. Is it going to be comfortable? It depends on how much I practise between now and then. I would say not very.
Which phrase does he use to add the good side after a complaint?
A That said.
B In short.
Which phrase does he use when he is not completely certain?
A The point is.
B As far as I know.
The text uses four different tenses. Which one describes the quarter?
A are restructuring — temporary, happening now.
B have been — still true since 2001.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

No new grammar today — four shapes, one map

Every question in November lands on one of these four. Know which one, and the sentence builds itself.

The job · the career

I oversee a desk. (always true)
I joined in 2001. (finished)
I have been here for 23 years. (still true)

The quarter · November

We are restructuring. (now)
I’m going to present. (planned)
I’ll take that one. (decided now)

To open a summary: In short, … · Basically, …
To turn the argument: That said, … · On the other hand, …
To hedge, instead of inventing: As far as I know, … · It depends on … · Roughly …
To land the plane: The point is, …

These eight are the reason fluent people never seem to pause. They pause — but they pause inside a phrase.
