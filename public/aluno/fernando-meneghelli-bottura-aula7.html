Chapter 3: The Code

Two Sentences, Opposite Facts

"We stopped reporting" and "we stopped to report" are not the same thing

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"We stopped reporting monthly."  vs  "We stopped to report monthly."

"I remember telling the board."  vs  "I remembered to tell the board."

"We tried cutting discretionary spend."  vs  "We tried to cut it."

"It means cutting forty roles."  vs  "I meant to cut forty roles."

In each pair, only one version describes what actually happened. Which, and why?

Verb+ -ing+ to
stopYou ended the activity.
We stopped reporting monthly.		You interrupted something else in order to do it.
We stopped to report.
remember / forgetThe memory of a past event.
I remember telling the board.		The obligation, and whether you carried it out.
I remembered to tell the board.
tryYou did it, as an experiment.
We tried cutting spend.		You attempted something difficult.
We tried to cut spend.
meanIt has this consequence.
It means cutting forty roles.		You intended it.
I meant to cut forty roles.
regretYou are sorry about a past action.
I regret telling them so late.		You are about to give bad news.
I regret to tell you...
go onYou continued the same thing.
We went on reporting weekly.		You moved to the next thing.
We went on to rebuild the base.

With these six verbs the form is not style, it is fact. Choose the wrong one and you have told a finance panel that something different happened — and they will notice.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"We stopped to report monthly and moved to weekly."
"We stopped reporting monthly and moved to weekly."
"I remember to tell the board we would not chase revenue."
"I remember telling the board we would not chase revenue."
"Protecting the margin meant to cut the overhead base."
"Protecting the margin meant cutting the overhead base."

The -ing form points at the activity itself. The to form points at a purpose or an intention. With these verbs, that difference is a difference of fact.

2. What happened

Dialogue

Billings Is Not Revenue

I
Your last group did four hundred million. That is a bigger business than ours, so help me understand the comparison.
F
That figure is billings, not revenue. Most of it is media passing through us. Billings were four hundred million and revenue was fifty five, and fifty five is the number for this conversation.
I
Thank you. That saves us twenty minutes. So, fifty five of revenue. What was the gross margin?
F
Thirty one per cent going into 2019, on an overhead base built for a group twice the size two years earlier.
I
And then you lost the account. Walk me through what you did, in order.
F
We tried cutting discretionary spend first, which bought almost nothing, so we stopped doing that and went on to rebuild the overhead base itself.
I
Where did the year finish, and what did it cost you in write-offs?
F
Thirty four and a half by the fourth quarter, on a smaller top line. And no write-offs at all that year, which in that year is the number I am actually proud of.
Comprehension

About Ines

1. What assumption does Ines make at the start, and why?
That the four hundred million is revenue, so the last group was bigger than hers.
2. How does she react to the clarification?
She thanks him and says it saves twenty minutes — then goes straight to the gross margin.
3. What are the two things she asks for at the end?
Where the year finished, and what it cost in write-offs.
