Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Most executives believe the problem at a dinner table is vocabulary. It rarely is. The problem is where the important word lands. In a room where English is everyone’s second language, people listen for the end of the sentence, because that is where the voice falls. If the key idea is buried in the middle, it disappears.

English has a family of structures that solve this. The first is the wh-cleft: instead of “Investors want stability,” you say “What investors want is stability.” The sentence takes longer, and that is the point: the room waits for the last word. A variation signals that you have thought about it: “What I have come to realize is that the pipeline is rarely the problem.” Notice the word that. It cannot disappear.

When you want to give a personal view without sounding weak, use “The way I see it, greenfield is still where the risk sits.” Never “the way how I see it” — that is Portuguese in English clothes. When you want to make a request smaller and harder to refuse, use All: “All we need from the regulatory framework is a date.”

Finally, when the action is the news, put it in focus with do: “What the ministry did was postpone the auction for the concession.” None of these structures is decoration. Each one decides which word the investor from Abu Dhabi will remember when the dinner is over.

What the Pipeline Actually Looks Like

Ask a minister how the country’s infrastructure programme is going and you will hear a number: how many projects are in the pipeline. Ask a banker the same question and you will get a much shorter list, because the two of them are counting different things. What the minister counts is announcements. What the banker counts is deals a lender would actually sign.

The gap between those two lists is where project finance lives. A scheme is financed against nothing but the cash it is expected to generate, which means the lender is buying a forecast. That is why the first question is never how big the asset is, but how predictable it is. A toll road that has been open for nine years, with nine years of traffic data behind it, is a different proposition from one that exists on paper.

The sector has two words for that difference. Brownfield describes the asset that is already built and already earning; greenfield describes the one that still has to come out of the ground. Almost all the money wants the first and almost all the need is in the second, and no amount of enthusiasm has yet closed that gap.

Who is holding the money matters as much as how much of it there is. A pension fund answers to members who will retire decades from now, and its mandate is written to keep it out of trouble rather than to make it rich. A sovereign wealth fund answers to a finance ministry, and can be told to take a view a pension fund would never be allowed to take. When a government sets out to bring in private capital, it has to know which of the two it is talking to.

The instrument that usually does the work is a concession: the state keeps the asset and hands over the right to operate it, and to collect from it, for a fixed number of years. Where the state wants to share the risk rather than hand it over, the arrangement is called a public-private partnership instead. Both stand or fall on the same thing, which is a regulatory framework that will still say what it says today after the next election.

None of this is hard to follow once somebody is willing to unpack it. The difficulty in a room of investors is rarely the content. It is that the person presenting has not decided what the one sentence is. To connect the dots for a committee you have to know which dot matters, and the number that would move the needle is almost never the number on the first slide. If there is a single takeaway here, it is that one.

How One Solar Cluster Got Built
A case note written for an investment committee · ~490 words

In 2019 a consortium put forward a four-hundred-megawatt solar cluster on the dry plateau of a mid-sized economy. The engineering was the least interesting part of it. Panels are panels, and the contractor had already built three similar plants on two continents. What took two years to solve was everything that had to happen before the first panel arrived, and all of it was financial.

The sponsors began by removing the question nobody could answer, which was what the electricity would eventually be worth. A twenty-year off-take agreement with the national utility fixed the price per megawatt-hour for the life of the plant. Nothing about the desert had changed, but a forecast had become a contract, and a contract is something a lender can lend against.

That single document rearranged the whole structure. The senior banks agreed to advance seventy per cent of the cost, a leverage ratio the sector would have called optimistic for an unbuilt project five years earlier. The remaining thirty was divided between the developer's equity stake, which would be paid last and blamed first, and a layer of subordinated debt priced to reward whoever was willing to stand behind the banks.

Even then the deal sat still for eight months. The pension funds that had been approached liked the asset and could not buy it: their fiduciary duty is owed to members who will retire in 2050, and an unbuilt plant in an unfamiliar jurisdiction does not survive that test. The regional development bank solved it in two moves. It offered concessional lending for the first five years, below market terms and openly so, and it wrapped a partial guarantee around the senior bond. The guarantee delivered credit enhancement worth two notches of rating, and that was the de-risking mechanism the funds had been waiting for. Public money on worse terms, so that private money can arrive on ordinary terms, is what the sector calls blended finance; the bank said plainly that it expected to be judged not by what it spent but by what arrived behind it.

One problem remained, and it was arithmetic rather than engineering. The plant would earn in local currency and repay in dollars. A currency hedge covering the first eight years cost roughly a fifth of the projected margin, and the sponsors bought it anyway, on the grounds that a committee will forgive a thin return and will never forgive a surprise.

The book opened in March. The first commitment came from a pension fund that took a quarter of the bond and asked for nothing extra for going first; as an anchor investor what it contributed was not really money but a name on a page. Within four months the issue was three times oversubscribed, and the yield compression that followed left the final pricing well below what the sponsors had modelled. The developer's risk-adjusted return ended up lower than the one it had promised its own board, and it signed regardless. Its chief executive put it plainly at the closing dinner: two years had taught the consortium that capital deployment is not the hard part of this business. Finding something worth deploying it on is.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Speakers who open conferences for a living use one structure that most people only ever meet in writing. It is called inversion, and it works like a spotlight: it takes a limiting word, puts it at the front of the sentence, and forces the room to wait for the rest.

Compare “Capital has not been this cautious since 2008” with “Not since 2008 has capital been this cautious.” The facts are the same. The second version marks an inflection point. Notice what happens after the limiting phrase: the auxiliary comes before the subject, exactly as in a question. “Rarely do we see every stakeholder at the same table.” “Only by fixing the contracts can we move the money.”

If the verb has no auxiliary, English lends it one: do, does or did. “Not only did the auction close, but it closed early.” Forgetting the auxiliary is the most common mistake: “Rarely we see” sounds like a sentence that stopped halfway.

The last rule is about taste, not grammar. One inversion sets the tone. Two in a row sound like a sermon. Use it once, in the sentence you want remembered, pause after it, and move on to a show of hands, the rhetorical pivot or the moment you hand over to the first panelist.

The First Ninety Seconds

Conference programmes are written backwards. The organisers decide who will convene the session, who will deliver the keynote and who will sit for the fireside chat, and only then does somebody ask what the event is actually for. By the time the room fills, the answer has to arrive in the first ninety seconds or not at all.

That is the job of a curtain-raiser. It is not a welcome, and it is not a reading of the agenda. Its function is agenda-setting: to tell four hundred people which of the twenty things they could argue about this week is the one that matters. Get it right and the corridors talk about that question for two days. Get it wrong and every panel drifts back to whatever the room already believed before it arrived.

The tools are older than the conference circuit. A framing device is any sentence that tells an audience how to hear what follows: a date, a number, a single contrast. A rhetorical pivot is the turn that moves the room off the comfortable half of the argument and onto the uncomfortable one, usually somewhere around the third minute.

Openings also carry administrative weight, and the good ones dispose of it quickly. A housekeeping note about timings and microphones belongs in one sentence, not four. A show of hands is a useful instrument and a dangerous habit: it wakes a room up once and bores it twice.

The measure of an opening is not applause. It is whether the room begins to coalesce around one question. Chairs who understand this set the tone on purpose, and they choose the hardest version of the question rather than the most agreeable one, because agreement produces nodding and disagreement produces attendance.

And then they stop. The hardest discipline in the first ten minutes is knowing when to open the floor and when to hand over to the first speaker. A chair who keeps talking past that point has already told the room that the session is about the chair. If the sector really is at an inflection point, the opening is where the room finds out, and it finds out in ninety seconds.

When you tell an investor what a regulator said, you are doing more than repeating words. You are telling them how firm the promise was, and whether it is still true. English does both jobs with two tools: the reporting verb and the tense.

What the Regulator Did Not Say

Every regulated contract is read twice: once for what it promises, and once for what it leaves to somebody’s judgement. The second reading is the one that sets the price. A concession can be beautifully drafted and still be unfinanceable if too much of it rests on administrative discretion.

The instruments themselves are not mysterious. A tariff review sets out when and how the price the operator charges may be recalculated. A grandfathering clause protects contracts signed under the old rules when new ones arrive. A rebalancing claim is what the operator files when something outside its control has made the original arithmetic impossible. Force majeure covers the events nobody could have prevented, and it is a great deal narrower than most people assume.

Some of the machinery protects the state rather than the operator. A step-in right lets the authority take over operation if the concessionaire fails badly enough, and what counts as badly enough is defined by a material breach. Where the state ends the contract for its own reasons, the compensation is a termination payment, and the formula for it is negotiated years before anyone imagines using it.

Then there is what sits off the balance sheet. A contingent liability is an obligation that only becomes real if something specific happens, which is precisely why finance ministries like it, and why a fiscal framework written to be taken seriously puts a ceiling on how many of them a government may carry at once.

When a panel gets heated, the moderator has two jobs at once: keep the argument alive and keep it from becoming personal. The language that does both is surprisingly calm.

The first move is to restate the attack before answering it: “If I understand you correctly, what you are saying is that the contract, not the country, is the risk.” The second is to concede a point without giving up the argument: “That is a fair point, and yet the numbers tell a different story.” Neither sentence is weak. Both show the room that you heard the pushback and are not afraid of it.

The third move turns blame into a hypothesis, and English has a formal way to do it: the conditional without if. “Had the framework been clearer in 2019, we would have seen more capital” talks about a past that did not happen. “Were the agency to revise the model, the risk would shift” imagines the present or future. “Should the ministry accept international arbitration, the fund would move” describes a possible event.

In all three, if disappears and the auxiliary moves to the front. Never use both at once (“If had the rules been”), and never contract (“Hadn’t the rules been”). Used calmly, the structure does something useful: it moves a red line from an accusation to a proposal, and people can discuss a proposal without losing face.

The Question You Were Not Expecting

There is a moment in almost every panel when the prepared material runs out. Somebody asks the question that was not on the list, and the next forty seconds decide how the room reads you for the rest of the hour.

Most people reach for a talking point. It is the safest instrument and the most easily detected: an audience can hear a rehearsed sentence arriving from a long way off. Worse, if the point does not fit the question, what you have produced is a non-answer, and a room forgives almost anything faster than it forgives that.

The alternative is not to answer everything. It is to be visible about what you are doing. To sidestep a question openly — “I am not going to give you a number, and I will tell you why” — costs far less than pretending. To concede a point early costs almost nothing, and it buys the right to hold everything else.

The useful techniques are small ones. To reframe is to accept the subject and change the terms: the question was about cost, and you answer about who carries it. To push back is to disagree on the record, which is sometimes exactly what a room wants to watch. To play devil’s advocate lets you put an argument on the table without owning it, although it wears thin if you do it twice in an hour.

Two traps. The first is a straw man: answering a weaker version of the question than the one you were actually asked. Everyone notices, starting with the person who asked it. The second is the unattached caveat, the qualification that swallows the sentence it was attached to, until nobody in the room could say what was claimed.

What the good ones do is decide in advance where a red line really sits, and then say everything else. They know which numbers they cannot give, and they give the rest freely, which is why they sound open rather than evasive. They look for common ground before they need it, they steer the discussion without appearing to, and when the temperature rises they rein in the argument rather than the person making it. The skill is not winning the exchange. It is to take the heat out of it and still leave the question answered.

The Statement Nobody Wanted to Write

Every organisation discovers, sooner or later, that it has to say something before it knows what it thinks. The instrument for that is a holding statement: three sentences confirming what is already public, declining what is not, and promising more later. It is written in advance, it sits in a drawer, and its only job is to buy forty-eight hours.

The rest of the machinery is more deliberate. A press statement is issued by one party in its own words. A joint statement is issued by several, and takes five times as long, because every clause has to survive stakeholder alignment before anybody outside reads it. The compromise is usually visible in the text.

Timing is governed by an embargo: the material goes out in advance on the condition that nothing is published before a stated hour. It is an old convention held together by nothing but professional habit, which is exactly why breaking one is treated so seriously.

What may be said, and by whom, is not a matter of taste. Disclosure obligations require a listed company to publish material information — anything a reasonable investor would want to know before dealing — promptly, and to everyone at the same time. A remark made on background, unattributed, is still a remark; a remark made on the record belongs to the journalist. A spokesperson who is unclear about which of the two is in play should not be in the conversation.

The craft of it is small and ruthless. A lede that buries the news in the third line is a lede that somebody else will rewrite, and you will not care for their version. Boilerplate at the foot of the release is not decoration: it is the paragraph the legal team fought over, and it is supposed to be dull.

The hardest of all is forward guidance, which is the art of saying what you expect without promising it. It has to be specific enough to be useful and vague enough to survive a bad quarter, and getting the balance wrong in either direction means the next statement is a correction. Before any of it goes out, somebody has to be able to say a key message in one sentence, out loud, without looking at the page.

Advanced speakers rarely make grammar mistakes in front of a noun. They make rhythm mistakes. “A bilateral long-term robust infrastructure framework” is built from correct words, and a native listener still stops, because English puts those words in a fixed order.

How a Room of Sixty Reaches One Sentence

The hardest format in this business is not the keynote. It is the closed session: sixty people around a horseshoe, four nationalities, three languages, and ninety minutes to produce something that can be written down afterwards.

It begins before anybody speaks, with the lineup. A good one is not a list of the most senior people available; it is a list chosen so that the disagreements already in the room are represented on the floor. If everyone at the table would answer the question the same way, the session has failed before it opens and the chair has ninety minutes to fill.

Then come ground rules, and they are worth the two minutes they cost. Who may be quoted. Whether a follow-up question is allowed from the floor or only from the chair. How long a speaking slot runs. A chair who says none of this will spend the hour negotiating it one intervention at a time.

Simultaneous interpretation changes everything about pace. The interpreter is roughly eight seconds behind, which means a joke lands twice and an interruption lands on top of somebody else’s sentence. Experienced chairs time-box each intervention on purpose and say the number out loud, because a delegate who knows she has four minutes takes four minutes, and one who does not takes eleven.

The machinery in the middle is familiar enough. A breakout session takes twenty people into a smaller room where they will actually speak. A lightning round puts one question to everybody in turn, sixty seconds each, and it is the fastest way to find out where a room really is. A rapporteur writes down what was said, which is a defined job and not a favour asked of the youngest person present.

The end is where most sessions are lost. A closing round in which everyone offers a parting thought is pleasant and produces nothing at all. The Closing Address That Is Worth Staying For

By the last afternoon of a three-day event the room has heard forty speakers and can remember four. A closing address that simply lists what happened is the reason people book flights for five o’clock.

The ones that work do three things and then stop. They recap without summarising: not what each panel said, but the two or three sentences that turned out to matter. They name a through line — the argument that ran underneath the whole programme, usually visible only from the end. And they say what happens next.

That last part is where most closings collapse into weather. A call to action that asks everybody to continue the conversation is not a call to action; it is a goodbye with a verb in it. What makes it real is a date. By this time next year we will have published the framework. Between now and March the working group will be meeting every month.

The apparatus that holds that together is dull and necessary. An action item has an owner and a date, and without both of them it is a wish. A roadmap puts those items in sequence, so that a room can see which one is blocking the others. A scorecard is what you open at the next event to find out how many of them survived contact with the year.

A benchmark and a milestone are not the same thing, and the confusion is expensive. A benchmark is a standard you are measured against, usually somebody else’s performance. A milestone is a point on your own route. You can hit every milestone you set and still be behind the benchmark, and a room that has not understood the difference will applaud the wrong chart.

None of it survives without a mechanism. A debrief within the week, while people can still remember what was actually said. A written retrospective that is honest about what did not work. Stage 2: Put the Closing Address in Order

Order

Put the moves of a strong closing address in a logical order.

?"If there is one idea to take home from these two days, it is that the region lacks trusted contracts, not capital."
?"So let me turn our agreements into commitments."
?"By the end of March, the ministry will have published its draft concession model."
?"This time next year, we will be discussing projects under construction, not frameworks on paper."
?"My call to action is simple: follow through on what you said on this stage."
?"And now, with real gratitude, thank you to our sponsors and to all of you."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

You want to end a summit well. You start with:
A "Thank you to our twenty sponsors, one by one."
B "The one idea that connected these two days."
C "That is all for today."
You want to turn an agreement into a commitment. You say:
A "By the end of March, the ministry will have published its draft."
B "Until March the ministry publishes the draft maybe."
C "The ministry will publish sometime."
You want to put the room in the future. You say:
A "Next year we discuss projects."
B "Next year we will discussing projects."
C "This time next year, we will be discussing projects, not frameworks."
A topic was not resolved. You say:
A "We will circle back to arbitration in June."
B "We will do the arbitration again in June."
C "We will turn around to arbitration in June."
