Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

The Second Sentence -- Saying What Your Company Does

Somebody at a conference asks what your company does, and the answer has to carry a second idea before it stops. Key words: to be in charge of, to handle, to look after, to roll out, payroll, compliance, a provider, headcount, straightforward, a pain point, to walk somebody through something, to go live. Structure: defining relative clauses with who and that.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To be in charge of -- to be the person who answers for a team, an area or a decision
"I am in charge of the commercial side and of the partner relationships."
To handle -- to deal with something as part of your job, and answer for how it goes
"My team handles every client outside Brazil."
To look after -- to take care of a client or an account over a long period, not once
"I look after the partners who came in through the Asian office."
To roll out -- to start using a new system across a company, step by step, until everyone has it
"We rolled it out in four countries before we touched the head office."
Payroll -- the part of a company’s system that works out salaries and the taxes on them
"Payroll is the module they always ask about first."
Compliance -- doing exactly what the law requires, and being able to prove that you did
"They came to us for compliance, not for the reports."
A provider -- a company that supplies a service or a system to another company
"They had three providers and wanted one."
Headcount -- the number of people a company employs
"Their headcount doubled in two years and the old system stopped coping."
Straightforward -- simple and clear, with nothing complicated hidden inside it
"The contract is straightforward. The migration is not."
A pain point -- the exact problem that makes a client start looking for a new system
"Their pain point was not the price. It was the audit."
To walk somebody through something -- to explain something step by step, at the other person’s pace
"Let me walk you through the part that worries them most."
To go live -- the moment a new system stops being a test and starts being used for real
"We go live on the first of the month, never on a Friday."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At a software conference, somebody in the registration queue turns and asks the standard question. The commercial director could talk for an hour, so she gives the short version instead: she works for a company that builds tax and finance software, and she is the person who looks after the partners outside Brazil. Two ideas, one sentence, no pause.

The man in front of her is on the finance side. He says he has three providers and none of them talk to each other, which is the kind of sentence a salesperson waits all day to hear. She does not sell him anything. She asks what went wrong last year, because the pain point that brings a client to a new provider is almost never the price. It is the audit that nobody could answer.

Her clients are groups with a headcount over two thousand, which is the size at which payroll stops being straightforward and compliance becomes a full-time job. Last year her team rolled out the system in four countries. The person who signs the contract is never the person who uses it every morning, so she asks early which one she is talking to. And they never go live on a Friday, for a reason she is happy to walk anybody through: when the first run fails, the office has to be full.

1. "She works for a company that builds tax software." What does the word that do here?
A It joins two separate sentences that have nothing to do with each other.
B It hooks a second idea onto the word company, and says which company she means.
C It replaces the verb, so the sentence does not need one.
2. "She is the person who looks after the partners." Why who and not that?
A Because the word it points back to, person, is a human being.
B Because the sentence is about her, and who is always used for the speaker.
C Because that can only be used at the beginning of a sentence.
3. "The person who signs the contract is never the person who uses it." Why does who appear twice?
A For emphasis. One who would be enough.
B Because the sentence has two verbs, and every verb needs one.
C Because there are two different people in the sentence, and each one needs its own clause to say which person is meant.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Beyond Six Words -- The Story of a Company That Grew

How a company describes the years it has been growing, and the tense that carries them. Key words: to expand into, to build up, to land, a milestone, a track record, overseas, to scale, to take on, steady, a turning point, word of mouth, from scratch. Structure: present perfect simple.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To expand into -- to start doing business in a new country or a new market
"We expanded into Chile before we touched North America."
To build up -- to develop something slowly, over years, until it is solid
"It took a decade to build up a base that sells itself."
To land -- to succeed in getting a client or a contract you were competing for
"We landed the account after eleven months and three demos."
A milestone -- a moment that marks real progress, and that people remember afterwards
"The first contract abroad was the milestone. Everything after it was work."
A track record -- everything somebody has already delivered, used as proof of what they can do
"Our track record is twelve years old and it does not cross the border."
Overseas -- in or to another country, on the other side of the ocean
"Half of the pipeline is overseas now, and none of it was two years ago."
To scale -- to grow the business without the costs growing at the same rate
"Anyone can sell one licence. The question is whether it scales."
To take on -- to accept a new piece of work or a new responsibility
"I have taken on the Asian accounts as well as the Latin American ones."
Steady -- continuing at the same reliable rate, with no jumps and no drops
"Growth has been steady, which is less exciting and much easier to plan."
A turning point -- the moment when a situation changed direction and did not go back
"Saying yes to that trip was the turning point, although it felt like a bad week."
Word of mouth -- when people recommend you to other people, with no advertising involved
"Everything we have sold at home has come through word of mouth."
From scratch -- from the very beginning, with nothing ready and nothing to start from
"In a new country you build the reputation from scratch, every time."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The commercial director is asked the same question at every event: how long have you been selling outside Brazil? The short answer is two years. The long answer is the one worth giving. At home the company has built up a base since 2014, almost entirely through word of mouth, and it has never had to explain itself to anybody. That is the part that does not travel.

They have expanded into three markets so far. The first one chose them: a partner made an introduction and they landed the contract in 2024, before there was any plan at all. The second one they chose, and it has been slower. In a new country the track record counts for nothing, because nobody there has heard of the clients that made it, so the reputation has to be built from scratch. Growth has been steady rather than fast, which is harder to put on a slide and easier to live with.

The milestone everybody photographed was the first deal overseas. The real turning point came later and nobody photographed it: the week she took on the Asian accounts as well as the Latin American ones and stopped being able to do both alone. They have not opened an office abroad yet, and she is not sure they should. What they have learned is that the second market rewards being present, and presence does not scale the way software does.

1. "The company has built up a base since 2014." Why not "The company builds up a base since 2014"?
A Because building is a slow action, and slow actions always take have.
B Because the period opened in 2014 and is still open now, and English marks that with have plus the participle.
C Because 2014 is more than five years ago.
2. "They landed the contract in 2024." Why does this sentence not use have?
A Because 2024 is a closed year, and a named year closes the period.
B Because landing a contract happens in one second.
C Because the subject is they and not the company.
3. "They have not opened an office abroad yet." What does yet add to the sentence?
A That the office will never exist.
B That the office was opened and then closed again.
C That the period is still running, so it has not happened up to now and could still happen.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect Simple

GRAMMAR

One form, three jobs. It lets you talk about years of work without naming a single date.

JobFormExample

Past vs. Now -- Telling the Story With the Setback In It

How to tell a work story that has a bad month in it, and how the date decides the verb. Key words: a setback, a breakthrough, a pivot, a turnaround, an acquisition, a partnership agreement, to fall through, to follow up, to back out, eventually, to hit the ground running, to learn something the hard way. Structure: past simple vs present perfect.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A setback -- something that goes wrong and pushes the plan back, without ending it
"We had one serious setback in June and the year still closed well."
A breakthrough -- the moment a problem that had blocked everything finally opens
"The breakthrough came from a corridor conversation, not from the meeting."
A pivot -- a deliberate change of direction in what a company sells, or who it sells to
"The pivot to large groups was the decision that saved the product."
A turnaround -- a change from losing to winning, over a period rather than in one day
"It was a two-year turnaround and almost nobody outside noticed it happening."
An acquisition -- when one company buys another one
"I joined in the middle of an acquisition, which nobody should do on purpose."
A partnership agreement -- the signed document that sets out what two companies will do together
"We signed the partnership agreement in March and the work started in May."
To fall through -- when a deal that was almost done fails right at the end
"By June the whole thing had almost fallen through."
To follow up -- to come back to somebody after a first contact, to keep it moving
"We followed up every week, same day, for two months."
To back out -- to decide not to do something you had already agreed to do
"The new contact backed out of half of what we had agreed."
Eventually -- after a long time, in the end — and never with the meaning of sometimes
"Eventually somebody on their side said: let us start again."
To hit the ground running -- to start something new and be effective from the very first day
"I hit the ground running because there was no other option."
To learn something the hard way -- to learn something by making the mistake yourself, not by being told
"I learned that the hard way, and I would not have believed it before."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The version of the story that goes on the slide is short and clean: they signed the first partnership agreement in March and the market opened. The version worth telling is the other one. By June the whole thing had almost fallen through, because the people who agreed to it were not the people who had to live with it. The contact who signed left the company in May, and the person who replaced him backed out of half of what had been agreed.

It was a real setback. For about eight weeks the commercial director thought the year was lost. What she did about it was almost boring: her team followed up every week, on the same day, with the same short message and no pressure at all. Eventually somebody on the other side called and said that nobody had ever been that patient with them. That call was the breakthrough, and it had nothing to do with the product.

Since then they have signed three more agreements and have run every one of them the same way. She says she learned that the hard way, and that she would not have believed it if somebody had told her in March. The company has never opened an office abroad and, so far, it has not needed to. The pivot that made all of it possible happened years earlier, in 2019, and nobody photographed that one either.

1. "They signed the first partnership agreement in March." Why is this not "they have signed"?
A Because signing happens in one second.
B Because March is a named moment that is over, and a named moment forces the past simple.
C Because there was only one agreement.
2. "Since then they have signed three more agreements." Why does this sentence use have?
A Because "since then" opens a period that reaches the present, so it is not finished.
B Because three is more than one.
C Because the sentence is positive and positive sentences take have.
3. "Eventually somebody called." What does eventually mean here?
A From time to time, now and then.
B After a long wait, in the end.
C Possibly, if things go well.

Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

The Pitch That Opens Doors -- What Happens If They Say Yes

How a buyer actually decides, and the sentence that turns a feature into a promise. Key words: a proof of concept, to automate, an audit trail, data migration, onboarding, deployment, exposure, to plug into, interoperable, a fiscal year, to cut through the noise, the bottom line. Structure: first conditional, with the zero conditional for what is always true.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A proof of concept -- a small first version a client runs to test whether it really works
"We offered a proof of concept on one company before they committed to all of them."
To automate -- to make a machine do a job that a person used to do by hand
"If you automate the filing, your team will save two weeks a quarter."
An audit trail -- the record that shows who did what, and when, so an auditor can check it
"Their auditor rejected the old audit trail, and that is why they called us."
Data migration -- moving all the information from the old system into the new one
"The contract is straightforward. The data migration is where the risk is."
Onboarding -- the first weeks in which new users are trained and set up
"Tell them how long onboarding takes for four hundred users, before they ask."
Deployment -- the work of putting the system in place so that people can start using it
"If deployment slips by a month, their whole fiscal year moves."
Exposure -- how much risk you are open to if something goes wrong
"Reducing their exposure is worth more to them than another feature."
To plug into -- to connect to a system a company already has, without replacing it
"It plugs into whatever they are already running, which is half the objection gone."
Interoperable -- able to work together with another system, exchanging information both ways
"They had four systems and none of them were interoperable."
A fiscal year -- the twelve months a company uses for its accounts, which may not start in January
"If the migration starts in October, we will be live before their fiscal year ends."
To cut through the noise -- to be heard when everybody else is saying something similar
"The pitches that cut through the noise are rarely the most impressive ones."
The bottom line -- the one number, or the one fact, that decides the answer
"The bottom line for them is the penalty, not the licence."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The note goes round before every meeting and it always says the same three things. If you open with features, you will lose the room, because the people who decide were not in the demo. Open with what changes for them instead. If you automate the filing, their team will save about two weeks a quarter — that is a promise somebody can repeat to a boss, and a feature is not.

Then deal with the risk before anybody asks. Explain the data migration. Show the audit trail. Say out loud how long onboarding takes for four hundred users and how many people deployment needs from their side. If companies do not know what a project will cost them in hours, they assume the worst, and the worst is what they put in the budget. The pitches that cut through the noise are almost never the most impressive ones; they are the ones that reduce exposure.

Finally, be conservative with numbers. If you say eight weeks and it takes twelve, you will not lose four weeks — you will lose the reference, because buyers talk to each other and a bad proof of concept is discussed at three other companies within a month. If the system plugs into what they already have, say so early: half the objection disappears. And if they ask something you cannot answer, say that too. The bottom line is that being checkable is worth more than being impressive.

1. "If you open with features, you will lose the room." Why is there no will after if?
A Because the sentence is about the past.
B Because the half with if always uses the plain present, even when it is about the future.
C Because will is only used in questions.
2. "If companies do not know what a project will cost, they assume the worst." Why is there no will in the second half either?
A Because it is not a promise about one client. It is something that is always true, so both halves stay in the present.
B Because the writer forgot it.
C Because assume is a verb that cannot take will.
3. "If you say eight weeks and it takes twelve, you will lose the reference." What is the argument?
A That four weeks of delay is a very large amount of time.
B That clients never check what a supplier tells them.
C That the real cost is not the delay but the client who will no longer recommend you, because buyers talk to each other.

Vocabulary

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The note after the call is three lines long. The objection was the price, but the price is almost never the real one. The counterpart on the other side cannot defend the number to a board that was not in the meeting, which is a different problem with a different answer. There was also real pushback on the timeline, and that one is worth listening to.

What happens next depends almost entirely on the words. This will fix it and this might fix it are the same claim at two different temperatures, and only the second one survives being wrong. You must consider the audit risk and you may want to look at the audit risk before Thursday ask for exactly the same thing, and only the second one leaves the other person room to say no without losing face. Experienced negotiators use the weakest form that still says the thing, which sounds like weakness and is the opposite of it.

Then comes the arithmetic. We could move the date, but the scope would have to change with it. I would suggest one concession on the delivery date and none on the price, because the date is the only leverage left. Anything the other side claims, ask them to back it up with a number. And where the obstacle cannot be removed — an approval process, a liability clause the legal team will not touch — do not fight it: work around it. The people who hold their ground on everything win the meeting and lose the account; the ones who meet halfway on everything have nothing left by the second round.
