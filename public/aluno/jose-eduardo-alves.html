Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A room of strangers asks what you do, and the title in your head does not travel well. Here is the whole answer, in three shapes. The first one is the easy one: "I advise companies that buy and sell grain. Most of them are grain traders, and a few of them import diesel." Look at the verb. It is bare. In this tense, with I, you and we, the verb never changes shape at all.

The second shape is the one people get wrong. To say no, English does not touch the verb. It puts do not in front of it: "We do not go to a hearing every week. We write contracts, and we read the ones the other side sends." Never we not go, and never I am advise. In Portuguese you say that you are a lawyer; in English the verb does the work by itself.

The third shape is the question, and it is the one that keeps a conversation alive. English puts do in front again: "Do you work with contracts as well?" A question word simply goes before it: "Who do you work for?" And the short answer is short: "Yes, I do." One last use of this tense: it says how often something happens. "I travel to Miami twice a year, and I read every shipment file before the deadline."

1. "I advise companies that buy and sell grain." Why is there nothing in front of the verb?
A Because the sentence is short.
B Because a positive sentence with I, you or we needs only the bare verb.
C Because advise is a verb that never takes anything in front of it.
2. Why is it "we do not go to a hearing" and not "we not go to a hearing"?
A Because a negative sentence in this tense needs do not in front of the bare verb.
B Because go is an irregular verb.
C Because a hearing happens in front of a judge.
3. The text warns against "I am advise companies". What is the problem?
A Companies needs the word the in front of it.
B The verb does the work alone. There is no am, is or are in front of it.
C The sentence has to start with a question word.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In the last lesson every sentence was about you. This one is about somebody else, and English marks that with a single letter. Marina specializes in commodity contracts. Her associate handles the smaller deals. She reviews every difficult clause before anybody signs it. Look at the verbs. With he, she or it, the verb takes an -s, and only with those three.

Now the part that costs points. To say no, the -s leaves the verb and goes to does: "She does not go to court", never she does not goes. The same happens in a question: "Does he review every case?", never does he reviews. There is only one -s per sentence, and does takes it whenever does turns up.

Two verbs are irregular and you will use both every day: have becomes has, and do becomes does. "The firm has seven lawyers." Spelling has small rules too: verbs ending in -ch, -sh, -s or -x take -es (watch becomes watches), and a consonant before -y becomes -ies (study becomes studies). Finally, words of frequency go before the verb: "Her schedule rarely changes", "He always reviews the fee first."

1. "She reviews every difficult clause." Why does the verb carry an -s?
A Because the sentence is about the past.
B Because the subject is she, and he, she and it take the -s.
C Because review is an irregular verb.
2. Why is it "she does not go to court" and not "she does not goes to court"?
A Because does already carries the -s, and there is only one per sentence.
B Because go is irregular and never takes anything.
C Because the sentence is negative, and negatives have no verb.
3. Where do words like rarely and always go?
A At the end of the sentence, always.
B Before the verb: her schedule rarely changes.
C Between the verb and its object: she reviews rarely the clause.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Last week nothing went the way it was planned. On Monday the buyer sent the first draft, and it arrived with two clauses nobody expected. On Tuesday a colleague called the client. On Wednesday the other side did not answer at all. On Thursday the negotiation finally started, and the delivery was postponed. On Friday the client signed. Look at the verbs: most of them end in -ed, and that ending never changes, whoever the subject is.

Some verbs refuse the -ed and become a different word instead: send becomes sent, go becomes went, read stays read on paper but sounds like "red". There are about a hundred of them, and the ten you will use every week are enough to start.

Now the part that costs points. To say no, the past leaves the verb and goes to did: "They did not accept the clause", never did not accepted. The same happens in a question: "Did you attend the meeting?", never did you attended. One past per sentence, and did takes it whenever did turns up. The verb to be is the exception: it uses was and were, and it never asks did for help — "He was not happy", not he did not was happy.

Finally, the past almost always travels with a time expression: yesterday, last week, two days ago, on Monday, in March. If the time expression is there, the verb has to follow it into the past.

1. "They did not accept the clause." Why is there no -ed on accept?
A Because accept is an irregular verb.
B Because did already carries the past, and there is only one past per sentence.
C Because negative sentences have no tense.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "He did not was happy with the draft."
B "He was not happy with the draft."
C "He was not happied with the draft."
3. What does a time expression like "two days ago" tell you about the verb?
A Nothing. The verb can stay in the present.
B That the verb needs an -s.
C That the verb has to be in the past too.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Renata is a lawyer, and she attends seminars every Monday. That is her routine, and English uses one word for a routine. But this year she is currently living in Amsterdam, this week her supervisor is reading her proposal, and this month two colleagues are handling her cases. Those are not routines. They are true now and they will stop, and English uses two words for them: am, is or are, plus the verb with -ing.

The choice is not about the verb. It is about the time expression next to it. Every Friday, usually, always, on Mondays ask for one word. Right now, at the moment, this week, this semester ask for two. Put them together and the sentence breaks: I am usually reviewing contracts is wrong, because usually and -ing are fighting for the same job.

Negatives and questions do not need do here, and that is a relief. The negative puts not in the middle: "The firm is not waiting for her." The question moves am, is or are to the front: "Are you applying this year?", "What are you working on?" Never what do you working.

Two more details. Spelling: a final -e disappears (write becomes writing), and one vowel plus one consonant doubles the consonant (submit becomes submitting). And a small group of verbs refuses the -ing completely, because they describe a state and not an action: know, want, need, mean, believe. You say "I know the answer", never I am knowing.

1. What decides between one word and two words?
A The verb itself: some verbs take -ing and others do not.
B The time expression: routine takes one word, now or this period takes two.
C The subject: he and she take two words, I takes one.
2. Why is "I am usually reviewing contracts" wrong?
A Because usually says routine and -ing says now, and they cannot both be right.
B Because review never takes -ing.
C Because the sentence needs do in front of it.
3. How do you ask a question in this tense?
A With do or does at the front: "Do you working today?"
B By moving am, is or are to the front: "Are you applying this year?"
C By putting the verb at the end of the sentence.
