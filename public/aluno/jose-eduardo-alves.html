Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A room of strangers asks what you do, and the title in your head does not travel well. Here is the whole answer, in three shapes. The first one is the easy one: "I advise companies that buy and sell grain. Most of them are grain traders, and a few of them import diesel." Look at the verb. It is bare. In this tense, with I, you and we, the verb never changes shape at all.

The second shape is the one people get wrong. To say no, English does not touch the verb. It puts do not in front of it: "We do not go to a hearing every week. We write contracts, and we read the ones the other side sends." Never we not go, and never I am advise. In Portuguese you say that you are a lawyer; in English the verb does the work by itself.

The third shape is the question, and it is the one that keeps a conversation alive. English puts do in front again: "Do you work with contracts as well?" A question word simply goes before it: "Who do you work for?" And the short answer is short: "Yes, I do." One last use of this tense: it says how often something happens. "I travel to Miami twice a year, and I read every shipment file before the deadline."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In the last lesson every sentence was about you. This one is about somebody else, and English marks that with a single letter. Marina specializes in commodity contracts. Her associate handles the smaller deals. She reviews every difficult clause before anybody signs it. Look at the verbs. With he, she or it, the verb takes an -s, and only with those three.

Now the part that costs points. To say no, the -s leaves the verb and goes to does: "She does not go to court", never she does not goes. The same happens in a question: "Does he review every case?", never does he reviews. There is only one -s per sentence, and does takes it whenever does turns up.

Look at the verbs: most of them end in -ed, and that ending never changes, whoever the subject is.

Some verbs refuse the -ed and become a different word instead: send becomes sent, go becomes went, read stays read on paper but sounds like "red". There are about a hundred of them, and the ten you will use every week are enough to start.

Renata is a lawyer, and she attends seminars every Monday. That is her routine, and English uses one word for a routine. But this year she is currently living in Amsterdam, this week her supervisor is reading her proposal, and this month two colleagues are handling her cases. Those are not routines. Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Three small words carry almost all the advice in professional English, and the difference between them is not force. It is who decides. Should says what you think is best: "You should call the admissions office first." Could opens a door without recommending it: "You could consider the January intake." Might warns about something possible, not certain: "If we sign this clause, we might lose the choice of jurisdiction."

It is he should ask, never he should to ask and never he shoulds ask. The negative puts not straight after: "You should not ask a client for that letter." The question moves the modal to the front, and it never calls do: "Should I follow up after ten days?"

You must send me the document today is an order, and you do not give orders to a client or to a university. The same idea with should is just as clear and costs you nothing. English marks the distance between advising and commanding inside the verb, and Portuguese does not, so the hand reaches for the strong one without noticing.

Twelve minutes on the question behind this lesson: what happens to a person whose career changes direction in the middle, and why the culture treats that as a failure of focus rather than as an asset.

Tip: subtitles and a full transcript are on the page. Note every sentence where she contrasts what somebody used to be with what they are now.
