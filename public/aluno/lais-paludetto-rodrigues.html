Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The plane lands and the questions start immediately. Where are you from? How long are you staying? Do you have any brothers or sisters? What time does your school start in Brazil? Everybody asks the same things, and the officer at the desk asks them fast.

In the car, my host dad talks the whole way. He tells me about the house, about the rain, about his daughter. Then he stops and I understand that it is my turn. How far is the school from here? What do people eat here that we don't have in Brazil? Who else lives with you? Three questions, and suddenly it is a conversation and not an interview.

That is the whole trick of the first week. The person in front of you is doing small talk because they want to break the ice, not because they want information. If you only answer, the conversation dies at the third question. If you ask back, it does not.

Why does the text say the conversation in the car stopped being an interview?
A Because the host dad ran out of things to say.
B Because she started asking questions back instead of only answering.
C Because they changed the subject to Brazil.
Look at "What time does your school start in Brazil?" Why is it start and not starts?
A Because does already carries the -s, so the main verb goes back to its base form.
B Because school is plural.
C Because questions in English never use -s.
According to the text, what is small talk really for?
A Getting exact information about the other person.
B Testing how good your English is.
C Making people relax when they have just met.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- How an English Question Is Built

GRAMMAR

Four boxes, always in the same order. Learn the order once and every question in this lesson builds itself.

FormUseExample
Question word + helper + person + verbThe helper (do, does, are, is, can, have) always comes before the person.Where do you live?
What time does it start?
Yes / no questionsExactly the same, without the question word. You start with the helper.Do you have a locker?
Are you coming with us?
With the verb to beTo be is its own helper, so nothing extra is added.Where are you from?
Is she your sister?
The -s ruleWhen does is in the question, the main verb loses its -s: "What time does the bus leave?" — never leaves.
Who or what as subjectOne exception: when you ask about the person doing the action, no helper is needed. "Who lives here?", "What happened?"

Portuguese lets you ask a question with the word order of a statement. English does not. When you are not sure, say the helper out loud first and the rest follows.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Done, or Done When?

GRAMMAR

One question decides it. Is there a date in the sentence?

FormUseExample
Present perfect
have / has + participle		The result matters now. No date in the sentence, and none needed.I have sent the form.
They have confirmed the family.
Past simple
sent / signed / went		A finished moment with a known time: yesterday, last week, in June, on Monday.I sent it on Monday.
They confirmed it last week.
NegativePresent perfect uses have not / has not. Past simple uses did not plus the base verb.It hasn't arrived yet.
It didn't arrive on time.
QuestionHave / Has for the perfect, Did for the simple.Have you sent it?
Did you send it on Friday?
The four signalsalready, yet, just, never / ever all pull the sentence to the present perfect.
The trapA closed date and the present perfect never share a sentence. "I have sent it yesterday" does not exist. Pick one.

When you are not sure, ask yourself the only question that matters: is there a date? If yes, past simple. If no, present perfect.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nobody hands you a list. In my household in Burnaby the rules live on a small board next to the fridge, and half of them are not written anywhere. I have to be home by ten on school nights and by midnight on Fridays. I don't have to eat dinner with them every evening, but they sit down together at seven and it is the best half hour of the day. I mustn't use the washing machine after nine, because the wall is thin and Maya goes to bed early.

The chores took me one week to understand. Mine are the dishes on Tuesdays and the laundry on Sundays, and if I tidy up the kitchen without being asked, nobody says anything, but the whole evening is lighter. Am I allowed to invite friends over? Yes, but I have to say it in the morning, not when they are already standing at the door.

The thing nobody tells you is that the hard part is not the rules. It is bringing something up when it does not work for you. On Tuesdays my family in Sao Paulo calls at seven, exactly at dinner time. I did not say anything for two weeks. When I finally asked, Greg said: just tell us before, not after. Being considerate is not obeying everything. It is saying the thing early, while it is still small.

A It is not necessary, but she can if she wants.
B She usually forgets to use it.
C It is forbidden.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Free, Obliged or Forbidden?

GRAMMAR

Four small words decide three very different situations. Two of them look similar and mean the opposite.

FormUseExample
have to / has toObligation that comes from outside you: a rule, a school, a family.I have to be home by ten.
don't have toNo obligation at all. Optional, and nobody minds either way.You don't have to cook tonight.
mustn'tForbidden. The opposite of the line above, not a softer version of it.You mustn't use the machine after nine.
be allowed toPermission, seen from the person who receives it. Very common in questions.Am I allowed to have friends over?
can / could / would it be okay ifThree levels of politeness for the same request. The bigger the favour, the softer the form.
The trapdon't have to and mustn't are opposites. "You don't have to come" means you are free. "You mustn't come" means do not come.

After must and can the verb goes straight in, with no to. And English says I'm allowed to, never I have the permission.
