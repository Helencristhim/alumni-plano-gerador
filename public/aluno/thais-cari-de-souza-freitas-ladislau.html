Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A call of strangers asks what you do, and the title in your head does not travel well. Here is the whole answer, in three shapes. The first one is the easy one: "I write the credit policy for a digital bank. I also work on pricing and on the offer." Look at the verb. It is bare. In this tense, with I, you and we, the verb never changes shape at all.

The second shape is the one people get wrong. To say no, English does not touch the verb. It puts do not in front of it: "We do not have branches. We do not send the same offer to every customer." Never we not have, and never I am work. In Portuguese you say that you are a director; in English the verb does the work by itself.

The third shape is the question, and it is the one that keeps a conversation alive. English puts do in front again: "Do you work on risk as well?" A question word simply goes before it: "Who do you report to?" And the short answer is short: "Yes, I do." One last use of this tense: it says how often something happens. "We meet twice a month, and I read the portfolio every Monday."

1. "I write the credit policy for a digital bank." Why is there nothing in front of the verb?
A Because the sentence is short.
B Because a positive sentence with I, you or we needs only the bare verb.
C Because write is a verb that never takes anything in front of it.
2. Why is it "we do not have branches" and not "we not have branches"?
A Because a negative sentence in this tense needs do not in front of the bare verb.
B Because have is an irregular verb.
C Because a digital bank has an app instead.
3. The text warns against "I am work on pricing". What is the problem?
A Pricing needs the word the in front of it.
B The verb does the work alone. There is no am, is or are in front of it.
C The sentence has to start with a question word.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Yesterday you said what you do. Today the sentence is about somebody else, and one letter appears. "My analyst opens the dashboard at seven. The model approves most of the requests and it declines the rest." With he, she and it, the verb takes an -s. With I, you and we, it does not. That is the whole rule, and it is the letter Brazilians drop most.

Now say no. The -s does not disappear — it moves. It goes onto does, and the verb goes bare again: "The model does not write the rule, and it does not answer for a default." Never the model don’t write, and never the model does not writes. One -s per sentence, and does takes it.

The question works the same way. Does goes in front and the verb stays bare: "Does he review every case above ten thousand?" A question word simply goes before it: "How often does the team meet?" The short answer keeps the does: "Yes, he does." And watch the spelling: after ch, sh, s, x and o the ending is -es, and after a consonant plus y it becomes -ies. So she watches the target, and she studies the data before she reviews the limit.

1. "My analyst opens the dashboard at seven." Why does the verb end in -s?
A Because the sentence is about the morning.
B Because the subject is one person: he, she or it takes the -s.
C Because open is an irregular verb.
2. Why is it "the model does not write the rule" and not "the model does not writes the rule"?
A Because does already carries the -s, so the verb goes back to its bare form.
B Because write is a short verb.
C Because a model is not a person.
3. The text says "she studies the data". Why is it not "she studys"?
A Because study is irregular in every tense.
B Because data is a plural word.
C Because after a consonant plus y the ending becomes -ies.

In, On, At -- Your Week, Said Correctly

Three small words that decide whether your week makes sense. Key words: a time zone, a quarter, a deadline, an agenda, a slot, headquarters, to join, to reschedule, a follow-up, remote. Structure: prepositions of time and place — in, on and at.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A time zone -- the local time of one part of the world
"We are one time zone apart, so nine here is eight there."
A quarter -- three months of the business year
"We close the quarter in September and we present it in October."

"Can we reschedule the review to Thursday, at the same time?"

"I send a follow-up on the same day, never on the next one."
Remote -- working away from an office
"My team is remote, so the office is the calendar."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Three words carry your whole calendar, and the difference between them is size. The biggest box takes in: months, years, seasons and parts of the day. "We close the quarter in September." "I do my best work in the morning." A month is thirty days, and a morning is four hours — both are boxes, so both take in.

One day inside that box takes on. "The review is on the third Wednesday." "The deadline is on September the thirtieth." And a day plus a part of it still takes on: "on Monday morning". In American English the weekend goes the same way: "I never work on the weekend."

The smallest thing of all, a point on the clock, takes at. "The call starts at nine." "He joins at ten past nine, every time." Place works with the same idea. A country, a city or a room is a space you are inside, so it takes in: "Our headquarters is in Sao Paulo." A point you go to takes at: "at the office", "at home", "at headquarters". And one warning: before next, last, this and every, English uses no preposition at all. "We meet every Monday", never in every Monday.

1. Why is it "in September" but "on September the thirtieth"?
A Because the second sentence is longer.
B Because a month is a big box and takes in, and one day inside it takes on.
C Because September is a special month.
2. The text says "our headquarters is in Sao Paulo" but "at the office". What decides which word to use?
A A space you are inside takes in; a point you go to takes at.
B Cities always take in and buildings always take on.
C It depends on how far away the place is.
3. Why is "in every Monday" wrong?
A Because Monday needs on, so it should be "on every Monday".
B Because before every, next, last and this, English uses no preposition at all.
C Because Monday is a point on the clock, so it needs at.
