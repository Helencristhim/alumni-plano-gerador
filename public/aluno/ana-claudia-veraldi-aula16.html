Read for the Main Idea

The Waiter Who Did Not Smile

Reading the Clues, and Reading Them Wrong

We are all detectives. A neighbor carries a yoga mat, so she must do yoga. A man looks at his phone every minute, so he might be waiting for news. Most of the time these small deductions are useful, and most of the time we never check them.

The problem starts when we travel. A Brazilian in Paris orders a coffee, and the waiter does not smile. The obvious conclusion is he must be annoyed with me. But in France a serious face is simply professional, and a waiter who smiled all the time would look a little strange. The clue was real; the conclusion was Brazilian.

It works the other way too. A French visitor in Sao Paulo is puzzled when strangers smile and chat in the elevator. They can’t be this friendly with everybody, she thinks. They must want something. They do not. It is just how people behave there.

English gives us three small words for this: must when we are almost sure it is true, can’t when we are almost sure it is not, and might when we honestly do not know. In another culture, the safest word is often the smallest one. Before you say he must be angry, try he might just be French.

Chapter 4: The Code

How Sure Are You?

must · might · can’t — and must have, for the past

Grammar Discovery

Almost Sure, Not Sure, Almost Sure Not

"The lights are on. They must be home."

"The car is not there. They might be at the beach."

"It is four in the morning. They can't be having a party."

"The grass is wet. It must have rained last night."

None of these sentences is a fact — they are all guesses from a clue. Which one is the most sure? Which one is the least sure? And look at the last one: what changes when the guess is about the PAST?

FormWhat it doesExample
must + verbAlmost sure it IS true (from evidence).They must be home.
might / may / could + verbPossible. You do not know.They might be at the beach.
can’t + verbAlmost sure it is NOT true.They can’t be asleep.
must have + past participleAlmost sure about the PAST.It must have rained.
might have / can’t have + p.p.Possible / impossible in the past.She can’t have seen us.
never mustn’t hereMustn’t means it is forbidden. For a negative guess, use can’t.never: they mustn’t be home

In one line: must = almost sure yes, can’t = almost sure no, might = maybe — add have for the past.

  • Can’t: almost sure it is NOT true.

  • Somebody must have dropped a glass of water.
  • Must have + past participle: a guess about the past.
