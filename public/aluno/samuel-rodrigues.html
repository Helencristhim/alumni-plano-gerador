Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Present simple — your practice

What is always true: your role, your firm, what you normally do.

  • +  I handle corporate matters. / He handles them.
  • −  I do not handle litigation.
  • ?  What do you handle on your side?

Present continuous — your week

What is true around now, and will change: this matter, this month.

  • +  I am handling the Atlas matter.
  • −  We are not discussing price.
  • ?  What are you working on this week?

Signal words. usually, normally, every year → simple. this month, at the moment, right now → continuous. In a first call, use both, in this order: what you do, then what you are doing.

Vocabulary

Eight words of risk. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Provision -- a term written into a contract to cover a situation

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the internal note. The verbs in bold analyse a past that cannot be changed.

The force majeure provision was removed in the final round of comments, to simplify the text. Eighteen months later the regulator suspended the licence, the client could not take delivery, and the other side called it a breach.

If the provision had stayed in the contract, the client would have had a remedy. If the board had known the full liability, it might have asked for a cap. And if nobody had removed that paragraph, the termination notice would not have been valid.

None of that happened. What we do now is different: every supply contract gets a force majeure provision, a clear termination clause, and a cap on liability.

1. Did the provision stay in the contract?
A Yes — that is why there was a remedy.
B No — and that is why there was no remedy.
C The note does not say.
2. What does "the board might have asked for a cap" tell you?
A The board asked for a cap.
B The board did not ask, and it is only a possible past.
C The board will ask for a cap next time.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Third conditional — the past you cannot change

Two halves, both unreal, both in the past:

  • If + had + past participle  →  would have + past participle
  • "If the provision had been there, we would have had a remedy."
  • Softer: might have / could have — "the shareholders might have approved it."

The mistake to avoid

Never if we would have. The if half takes had, always.

Memo register: drop the "if"

"Had the provision been included, the notice would not have been valid." Same meaning, no if, had in front. This is how it goes into writing.

Vocabulary

Eight words from the shareholders agreement. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Deadlock -- a block where no side has the votes to decide

Veto right -- the power of one member to stop a decision alone

Fiduciary duty -- the duty to act in the company's interest before your own
"A director who blocks every exit may fail to fulfill a fiduciary duty."

B Because assuming it was the right thing to do.
C Because it is only a remote possibility.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Mixed conditional — a past cause, a present problem

The two halves sit in different times:

  • If + had + past participle  →  would / could / might + base verb
  • "If the founders had agreed on a tag-along right, he would not be locked in today."
  • Lesson 2 kept both halves in the past (would have been). Today the result lands in the present (would not be).

Modal perfect — your reading of a past decision

  • must have + past participle — you are almost sure. "They must have assumed it."
  • should have + past participle — it was the right thing, and it did not happen. "It should have been drafted."
  • might have + past participle — it is possible. "They might have underestimated the risk."

Say it slowly. In natural speech have is weak: should've, must've, might've. Rushed, it comes out as should of — and the sentence stops being a modal perfect.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Causative — have something done

You arrange it. Someone else does it. You answer for the result.

  • have + object + past participle
  • "We had the agreement reviewed by outside counsel."
  • In progress: "They are having the register updated."
  • Already done: "We have already had the property appraised."
  • After recommend, use the -ing form: "I'd recommend having it reviewed" — never to have.

Three registers, one fact

  • informal — "We checked the title."
  • formal, active — "Our team reviewed the agreement."
  • professional, causative — "We had the agreement reviewed by outside counsel."

get works the same way and is less formal: "we got it done". Keep have for the client, get for the corridor.

Say it slowly. The participle links to the object: had-it-reviewed, getting-it-done. Rushed, the participle disappears and the sentence goes back to being active.

Eight words a dispute clause decides. Listen to each one and read the example out loud.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Read the position note. The words in bold carry the emphasis to the front of the sentence.

The buyer's board wants out. Revenue is down eleven percent and they are asking whether that is a material adverse change. Our answer is that it is not: the clause carves out market-wide conditions, and the seller's representations were accurate at signing.

Never have I seen a carve-out drafted that broadly. Not only did the seller disclose the March claim in the data room, but they also flagged it in the April call. Under no circumstances should the condition precedent be waived, although signing can move by a week. If the dispute goes forward, the governing law clause sends it to arbitration in London.

In "Never have I seen", why does have come before I?
A Because it is a question.
B Because the negative word moved to the front, so the auxiliary moves before the subject.
C Because the sentence is in the past perfect.
Why would a lawyer choose this over "I have never seen..."?
A The emphasis lands first, in a register that fits a formal call.
B It is shorter.
C It is more polite.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Inversion — put the weight at the front

Move the negative or the adverb to the front, then the auxiliary comes before the subject, exactly like a question:

  • Never / Rarely / Seldom  →  "Never have I seen a carve-out drafted that broadly."
  • Not only... but also  →  "Not only did the seller fail to disclose it, but they also renewed it."
  • Under no circumstances / On no account  →  "Under no circumstances should this be waived."
  • Hardly... when  →  "Hardly had the agreement been signed when the dispute arose."
  • No auxiliary in the plain sentence? Use do / does / did.

One per point, never two in a row. Twice in the same minute it stops sounding formal and starts sounding theatrical.

Holding the floor

  • Interrupted? Do not restart and do not apologise.
  • Take it back in six words: "If I may just finish this point."
  • Finish, then hand it over: "and now to your point".
