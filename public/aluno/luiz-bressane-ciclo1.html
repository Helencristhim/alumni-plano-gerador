Lesson 09 — Before you read either one

1 · Two kinds of writing

Before you read

One of the two texts you will read was written by a person who was there. The other was written by someone who arrived afterwards and wrote down what he was told.

Mark the two statements that are true of a witness account and not of an incident report.

It is written by someone who saw what happened.
Yes — that is what makes it an account. It is one person’s version, and it is the only one of the two written from inside the event.
It is written to a fixed form, with headings and times.
That is the report. The form is what makes it complete and also what makes it leave things out.
It can say what the person felt or thought at the moment.
Yes. Only the witness can say this — and it is also why an account can be honest and still not match a record.
It is signed by the officer on duty.
The report. The signature says who wrote it down, not what was seen.
It is the same on every night of the year.
Neither, and it is not what makes them different: the report’s form repeats, its content does not.
An account is one person’s version. A report is a record made to a form — it can be complete and still leave out what only the witness could know.
Why this matters tonight
Both texts will disagree with each other tonight. Neither of them is lying: they were written by different people, for different purposes, at different hours.

2 · Key language

Vocabulary

Choose the better meaning for each expression. All six come up in the two texts in activity 3, and again in class.

an account
to claim
to state
to leave something out
consistent with
as far as I can tell
Two of these do the work
Two of these do the work of the whole lesson: claim and state. What a text states you can point to. What someone claims you can only attribute -- and that is a different sentence.

3 · Where it says so

Read the two texts below. Then, for each of the six statements, say where it comes from. Not every statement belongs to one text alone.

Witness account · Ms. Ferraz · Incident report · Officer on duty
Ferraz: I got home a little before eleven. There were four of them on the corner, and one of them called out to me first -- I did not speak until he did. I did not see anyone leave.

Report: Attendance recorded at 23:40. Three individuals present at the location. The complainant states that she addressed the group. No mention of any individual leaving the scene.
Someone in the group spoke before she did.
Her account says one of them called out to her first.
There were three people on the corner.
Three is the report’s number. The account says four.
Nobody is recorded as leaving the scene.
Both say it — and where two texts agree, you can say so without attributing it to one of them.
She was frightened by the group.
Neither text says this. It fits the scene, which is why it is easy to say by accident.
The time is close to eleven at night.
Both. A little before eleven and 23:40 are the same part of the night.
She spoke to the group first.
The report says she addressed the group. The account says the opposite — this is a real divergence.
“She was frightened” is the one to hold on to
Both texts put the hour near eleven — they do not disagree about when, only about how exactly. On who spoke first they say opposite things: that is a real divergence.

And she was frightened is in neither. It is the kind of thing a reader adds without noticing, because it fits. Saying it out loud as if it came from the account is the mistake this lesson is built to catch.

4 · What neither one tells you

Reading

Mark the two things you cannot get from either text.

Why the group was on that corner.
Neither text says it. That is the kind of gap you ask for — and closing a briefing on it is stronger than closing on a divergence.
How many people the report recorded.
The report says three. It is on the page, so it is not a gap.
What was said after the first words.
Both texts stop there: she went in, and the report registers no further exchange. A genuine gap.
What time the officer recorded.
23:40, written down. Not a gap.
Whether the witness spoke first.
Both texts answer this — and they answer it in opposite ways. That is a divergence, which is not the same thing as a gap.
Two texts that disagree are still two texts. What both of them leave out is not a divergence — it is a hole, and it is the thing worth asking for.
The hole is not a divergence
In class you will report the divergences. The strongest briefings end with the hole, not with the last divergence.

5 · Say where it came from

Each sentence reports something. Complete it so that the source is attached to the claim.

The report records three people;
She was not the first to speak —
On the time,
Neither text mentions why the group was there,
Reporting is not deciding
Notice what the rejected endings do: they turn a report into a verdict. Four is the correct number is not reading -- it is deciding, and nobody asked you to decide yet.

Lesson 10 — Before you hear any of it

1 · Before the room fills

Before you listen

You are at a conference in your field. The speaker is presenting results from a pilot program.

Mark the two things that make a question, asked out loud, land well in a room like that.

It points at something the speaker actually said.
Yes. Quoting her fixes what you are asking about, and she can answer without guessing.
It shows the room how much you know about the subject.
No. That is a speech with a question mark at the end, and it spends the time you have.
It names the part you are not sure about, and asks about that.
Yes. A question with one target gets one answer.
It is long enough that everyone can follow your reasoning.
No. Length is what makes a question unanswerable: by the end she is answering the beginning.
It waits until the speaker has finished the whole talk.
Not the point — waiting is a habit of the room, not a property of the question.
A question that quotes the speaker back to herself is doing two things at once: it proves you followed, and it gives her something exact to answer.
The one that ruins it
The long question is the one that goes wrong most often, and it goes wrong for a reason that has nothing to do with English: by the time you reach the question mark, the room has stopped listening and the speaker is answering the first thing she remembers. Short is not modest — it is what gets answered.

2 · Key language

Vocabulary

Choose the better meaning for each expression. All six are in the talk you will hear.

to hold
pre-selected
a figure
to open up
worth having
a different claim
Two of these carry the talk
Does that hold for…? is the whole question the man in the audience asks, and it is the most portable question in a conference room: it takes any claim and asks where its limit is.

And a different claim is the speaker's own point — not that the number is false, but that it answers a question nobody asked.

3 · What a question is doing

Six questions someone might ask after a talk. Say what each one is actually doing.

“Does that hold for the second year as well?”
It uses her claim and asks for its limit — the most portable question there is.
“Sorry — was that sixty or sixteen?”
A repair, not an argument. Perfectly fine, and it does not touch the claim.
“Do you have the same data for civil cases?”
It asks for material the talk did not cover: a request, not a challenge.
“You mentioned the cases were pre-selected — selected by whom?”
Her own word, given back with a question attached. That is what makes it answerable.
“Are you sure you are not choosing the numbers that suit you?”
The doubt is legitimate; the target is her. Aimed at the claim, the same doubt gets an answer instead of a defense.
“Could you go back to the slide before this one?”
A request to repeat. It buys you the detail you missed, and costs nothing.
“Are you choosing the numbers that suit you?” is the one to avoid
It may be exactly what you think. Said that way it stops being a question about the data and becomes a question about her — and the room hears the second one.

The same doubt, as a question about the claim: What would the figure be if the cases had not been pre-selected? Nobody has to defend themselves, and you still get your answer.

4 · What the number measures

Listening

Two lines from the talk. Claire makes the same point twice, in two different ways.

Speed
Speed

Mark the two statements that match what she is arguing.

The pilot program failed.
Not said. She is arguing about what a number proves, not about whether the work was any good.
The figure is real, but it answers a question nobody asked.
Her thesis, in one line.
The cases in the pilot were not typical of all cases.
Her evidence for the thesis: the sample is what makes the figure look like that.
Ninety percent is too high to be true.
She does not dispute the number anywhere. Disputing it for her leaves you defending arithmetic.
The second year produced a worse program.
The second year changes the figure, which is not the same as changing the program.
She separates two things that sound like one: the figure is right, and it is evidence of something narrower than people take it for. She says so twice, in different words — that repetition is the clue.
Why this distinction is the lesson
Attacking the number is the easy move and it is usually wrong — the number is normally correct. What is wrong is what it is offered as evidence of. That distinction is the difference between an argument that survives and one that gets dismissed as scepticism.

5 · Build the question

This is how Marcus asked his, in the room. Listen once before you build yours.

Notice the first three words
Every one of these begins by giving the speaker back something she said. That is not politeness — it is what makes the question answerable. A question that starts from your own reasoning asks her to reconstruct it first, and in a room with forty people she will not.

Lesson 11 — Before you argue any of it

1 · Arguing about what did not happen

Before you argue

You do this in Portuguese every week: say what would have been different if one fact had been another.

Mark the two things a hypothetical argument has to do to be worth making.

It has to change something that is still open.
Yes. A hypothetical that changes nothing is a curiosity; the test is what it does to the decision in front of you.
It has to prove the other side acted in bad faith.
No — and reaching for bad faith turns an argument about facts into an argument about people.
It has to name the one fact that would have been different.
Yes. One fact, named. Two or three at once and nobody can follow which one you are arguing from.
It has to be about the most serious point in the case.
No. It has to be about the point that is still open, which is often not the most serious one.
It has to be impossible to disagree with.
No. If it were impossible to disagree with, there would be nothing to argue — the skill is holding it when someone does.
A hypothetical that changes nothing still open is an observation, not an argument — and the person listening will say so.
The question your interlocutor will ask
What does the hypothetical buy us? is the objection you should expect, and it is a fair one. The answer is never “because it is true”. It is always what it changes about the decision in front of us.

That is why the form matters: if X had been…, we would not be… puts the consequence in the present, where the decision is.

2 · Key language

Vocabulary

Choose the better meaning for each expression. All six are in the objections you will hear.

to take a statement
to buy (us) something
even if
to hold up
the timing
to concede a point
“Even if” is the one that does the work
It lets you agree with something and lose nothing: Even if you are right about the timing, it would not change what the witness says today. The concession is real, and the argument survives it.

Refusing to concede anything is what makes an argument sound weak, not strong — and it is the habit hardest to break in a second language, because conceding requires a form you have to reach for.

3 · Which fact would have to change

Six statements about the same case. Say what each one is doing.

Case summary
The statement was not recorded on the night of the incident. It was taken eleven days later, after the witness had spoken to two other people present. The defense argues that the delay explains the inconsistencies; the prosecution argues that the inconsistencies are the point.
“The statement was taken eleven days later.”
A fact from the summary: quotable, and where the argument anchors.
“If it had been taken that night, the inconsistencies would not be there.”
The hypothetical — what the missing condition would have produced.
“Even if the delay explains some of it, it does not explain all of it.”
A concession, with the turn kept: grant part, hold the rest.
“And what would that change now?”
The question that follows a hypothetical, and the one you should expect. Answer it before it is asked.
“She had spoken to two other people first.”
Fact. It is what makes the third version explainable without accusing anyone.
“Had she been asked that night, we would have one version, not three.”
The same hypothetical in the inverted form — more formal, and one syllable shorter to say.
The two hypotheticals are built differently
If it had been taken… and Had she been asked… say the same thing. The second drops the if and inverts — it is more formal, and it is what you will read in written argument in English.

You do not have to use it. You do have to recognize it when the other side does.

4 · Conceding without losing

Grammar

Mark the two that concede something and still hold the argument.

“Even if that were true, it would not change the timeline.”
Concede the premise, keep the conclusion. Nothing is given away.
“You may be right about that. Let us leave it there.”
The concession alone. It sounds gracious and ends the argument against you.
“That is a fair point — the difference is that the witness had already spoken to two people.”
Concession, then the fact that changes what it means. This is the pair to practice.
“I disagree with everything you have said.”
No concession at all. It costs you the goodwill and it does not answer anything.
“Fine, if you insist.”
Surrender with irritation attached — the worst of both.
A concession that ends the sentence gives the point away. A concession followed by the difference is or it would not change keeps it.
Where it goes wrong in a second language
Conceding needs two moves in one breath: accept, then turn. In your own language you do it without noticing. In English the second move needs a form you have to reach for — and if it does not come, the sentence stops after the concession and sounds like surrender.

That is why this lesson practices the pair, never the concession alone.

5 · Build the hypothetical

Complete each one so that the consequence lands in the present, where the decision is.

If the statement had been taken that night,
Even if the delay explains part of it,
Had she been asked before she spoke to the others,
That is a fair point —
Look at what the wrong endings do
They are all grammatical. What they lack is a consequence that touches the decision in front of you. It would be better is true and buys nothing; I accept your point entirely concedes and stops.

The test is the objection from activity 1: what does it buy us?

6 · Three sentences, written out

Writing

Complete the paragraph. Watch the verb forms — there is no word bank here on purpose.

If the statement taken on the night, we discussing three versions now.

Even if the delay part of it, it does not explain why the third account appeared.

Had she asked before she spoke to the others, we one version.

Why there is no word bank in this one
In a vocabulary gap-fill the bank is required — without the candidates on screen, “complete the sentence” is not lexical recall, it is a memory test.

Here the gap asks for the FORM of the verb, and the bank would hand you the answer. This is the one exercise in the lesson that measures precision without conversational pressure — and it exists so that the lesson can tell the difference between not knowing the form and not reaching it in time.

Lesson 12 — Before you explain any of it

1 · What she does not know

Before you explain

Your interlocutor is a lawyer from another country. She is competent and she knows nothing about how your system works.

Mark the two things she needs before your argument can land.

Who decides, and what exactly they decide.
Yes. Without it she maps your case onto her own system and hears a different argument from the one you are making.
The full history of the institution.
No. It is the kind of context that feels responsible and costs you the whole three minutes.
That the defense is a public body, not a firm the family hired.
Yes. It changes what she assumes about choice, strategy and resources.
How many cases you handle in a year.
No — it is about you, not about what she has to understand to follow the case.
The name of the court where it happened.
No. A name she cannot place adds nothing she can use.
She needs whatever changes the meaning of what you are about to say. Everything else is background, and background spends the three minutes you have.
The test for what to explain
Not “is it interesting?” but does my argument mean something different if she does not know it?

If the defense being a public body changes how she hears your position — and it does — it goes in. If the name of the court does not, it stays out. Three minutes is short, and background is what eats it.

2 · Key language

Vocabulary

Choose the better meaning for each expression. You will need all six to explain a system to someone outside it.

in our system
which means that
to be assigned to
the burden of proof
to cut both ways
that is fair
“Which means that” is the one that does the explaining
Explaining a system to an outsider is not a list of facts — it is a chain of consequences. In our system the defense is a public body, which means that the family does not choose us, which means that…

Without it you produce facts and she has to assemble them herself, in a language that is not hers either.

3 · In or out of three minutes

Six things you could say to her. Decide what each one is for.

“In our system the jury decides guilt, not the sentence.”
She needs it: in her jurisdiction the two may travel together, and without this she hears a different argument.
“The court was built in 1942.”
Interesting, and it spends time you do not have.
“We are assigned to the case — the family does not choose us.”
She needs it, or she will assume a retained lawyer and a choice of strategy that did not exist.
“I have been doing jury work for eleven years.”
Later, if she asks. It is credentials, not the case.
“The statement was taken eleven days after the night.”
She needs it — the objection she is about to raise depends on it.
“Our caseload is roughly two hundred a year.”
Later. It describes the office, not the argument.
The middle column is the expensive one
Nothing in it is false, and none of it is useless in a longer conversation. In three minutes it is what pushes the argument off the end.

The third column is different: it is what she may well ask for, and answering it then is fine. What you cannot do is spend the three minutes on it before she has.

4 · When the objection cuts both ways

Reading

She will make one objection of substance: if the delay explains the inconsistencies, it explains them for both sides — so why does it help you?

Mark the two responses that answer it without starting again.

“That is fair — it does cut both ways. The difference is who had to prove something that night.”
Concede in four words, then give her the piece she is missing.
“Let me go back to the beginning so the sequence is clear.”
A restart dressed as clarity. She asked one question; this answers a different, longer one.
“It does, and that is exactly why the burden matters here.”
The objection is granted and turned into your own point — the strongest answer available.
“I disagree — the delay only affects one side.”
It is not true, and she knows it is not. Denying a correct objection costs more than the objection did.
“That is a different question from the one I was answering.”
It may be accurate, and it sounds like a dodge. Say what you grant first, then draw the distinction.
The objection is correct, and denying it costs more than granting it. What answers it is the asymmetry she does not know about yet — who carries the burden.
Going back to the beginning is the expensive mistake
It feels like clarity and it is a restart: three minutes become six, and she stops listening somewhere in the second one.

The move that works is to grant the objection in four words and then say the one thing she does not have. She is a lawyer — she will do the rest herself.

5 · Chain the consequence

Complete each one so that she gets the consequence, not only the fact.

In our system the defense is a public body,
The jury decides guilt and not the sentence,
That is fair — it does cut both ways.
The statement came eleven days late,
Every wrong ending stops at the fact
They are all true and none of them tells her what to do with it. Which is unusual, I know is even friendly — and it leaves her holding a fact with no consequence attached.

She is not slow. She simply does not have the system in her head, and assembling it is your job in these three minutes.

Attaching a claim to its source

Three things a sentence can do with a claim, and the words that do each one.

You can put a finger on the line. Nobody has to trust you.

Language Reference

Saying what did not happen, and what it changes

Three moves. In a real turn you need all three, and usually in this order.

The factThe statement was taken eleven days later.
Anchors you. Nobody argues with it, and it is where the hypothetical stands.
The hypotheticalIF it had been taken that night, we would not be arguing about it now.
The formal version drops the if and inverts: Had she been asked that night… You will read it more than you say it.
The concessionEven if you are right about the timing, it would not change what she says today.
Accept, then turn. A concession that ends the sentence gives the point away.
The objection to expect, and the only answer that works And what does that buy us? is a fair question, and the answer is never “because it is true”. It is always what it changes about the decision in front of you — which is why the consequence goes in the present: we would not be arguing about it now.

Language Reference

Explaining a system to someone outside it

Three moves, and the order matters more than the words.

Only what changes meaningIn our system the jury decides guilt, not the sentence.
The test is not “is it interesting” but does my argument mean something different if she does not know it?
Chain the consequenceThe defense is a public body, which means that the family does not choose us.
A list of facts asks her to assemble your system herself, in a language that is not hers either.
Grant, then supplyThat is fair — it does cut both ways. The difference is who had to prove it that night.
Four words of concession, then the one thing she does not have.
The move that costs you the three minutes Let me go back to the beginning so the sequence is clear. It feels like clarity and it is a restart — three minutes become six, and she stops listening somewhere in the second one. She is a lawyer: give her the missing piece and she will do the rest herself.
