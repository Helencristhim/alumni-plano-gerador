Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nobody has ever lost a job because of a preposition. But something smaller happens, and it happens every week. A finance analyst writes to a colleague in another country: “The file will be ready in Monday, at March.” The colleague understands, answers, and moves on — and then, without deciding to, reads the next three sentences a little more slowly. The information was all there. Only the confidence in it moved.

English uses three small words for time, and each one has its own size. At is a point: at nine, at half past four, and, for a reason nobody can explain, at night. On is a square on a calendar — a day or a date: on Monday, on the fifth working day, on 3 March. In is a box you are inside of, and it is the biggest of the three: in March, in 2026, in the morning. Place works exactly the same way. At is one building or one address: at the Morumbi office. In is a city, a country, a room you can walk into: in Sao Paulo, in the shared service center.

Two traps survive the rule. The first is the part of the day. Alone, it takes in: in the morning. With a day in front of it, the day wins and it takes on: on Monday morning. The second is a pair that looks identical and is not. In time means early enough for something to still work: the file arrived in time for the review. On time means exactly at the hour agreed: the call started on time. A close can finish on time and still not be in time, and anybody who has run a month-end knows the difference is not academic.

1. According to the text, what changes when the preposition is wrong?
A The colleague does not understand the message.
B The information still arrives, but the confidence in it drops.
C The message has to be written again from the beginning.
2. Why does the text say that in is the biggest of the three words?
A Because it has more letters than the other two.
B Because it is used more often in business English.
C Because it goes with a box you are inside of: a month, a year, a city.
3. What is the difference between in time and on time, in the text?
A In time means early enough for something to still work; on time means exactly at the hour agreed.
B They mean the same thing, and both are correct.
C On time is for people and in time is for documents.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a moment, in the middle of every explanation, where a listener decides whether to keep listening. It is not the first sentence. It is the second one — the one that has to say how it is connected to the first. A person who knows the subject perfectly can lose a room there, because the facts arrive as a list of separate things and the listener has to guess the order.

English has a small set of words for exactly this, and a process is the easiest place to see them working. First, then and after that only say the order: this one, and now this one. Once says something stronger — that the second step cannot start before the first is complete. Until marks the moment a situation stops being true: nothing can be reconciled until the last entry has been posted. As soon as means immediately after, with no gap at all. And by the time looks forward from a point that has not arrived yet: by the time the fifth day arrives, the report is already with the controller.

There is one trap, and it catches almost every learner whose first language marks the future twice. All of these words already carry the future inside them, so the verb after them does not need to. English says once the cut-off has passed, not once the cut-off will pass. The same happens with until, as soon as and by the time. Notice what this does to an explanation: the future stops being repeated in every clause, and the listener stops having to hold it. The steps line up, and the second sentence finally sounds like it belongs to the first.

1. According to the text, where does a listener decide whether to keep listening?
A In the first sentence, because that is where the subject is announced.
B In the second sentence, which has to say how it is connected to the first.
C At the end, when all the facts have been given.
2. What is the difference between then and once, in the text?
A There is none; they are two ways of saying the same thing.
B Then only gives the order; once says the second step cannot start before the first is complete.
C Then is used in writing and once is used in speaking.
3. Why does the text say the verb after these words does not need the future?
A Because the words already carry the future inside them, so repeating it is unnecessary.
B Because the future does not exist in English grammar.
C Because these words are only used to talk about the past.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Nobody in a review call is asking for a number. The number is already on the screen, in front of everybody, and it has been there since before the meeting started. What the room is waiting for is the half of the sentence that comes after it — the part that says why. A person who gives only the figure has answered a question nobody asked.

English marks that second half with a small set of words, and the first thing to notice is that they do not all sit in the same place. Because, since and as are followed by the reason, and the reason has to be a full sentence with its own verb: the cost went up because we signed two new contracts. So is the mirror image — it is followed by the result: we signed two new contracts, so the cost went up. Therefore does the same job as so in a more formal register, which is why it belongs in an e-mail and rarely in speech. And which is why attaches a result to the whole sentence that came before it: the item is a one-off, which is why the forecast does not change.

Two traps catch almost everybody. The first is due to and because of. They also introduce a reason, but they demand a noun, not a sentence: due to the late invoice, never due to the invoice arrived late. The second trap is doubling. Many languages allow both halves to be marked at once, and English does not: a sentence takes because or so, and choosing one of them is not a stylistic preference, it is the grammar. Notice what this does to an explanation under pressure: once the connector is chosen, the rest of the sentence has only one place to go.

A The number, because nobody has seen it yet.
B The half of the sentence that says why — the number is already on the screen.
C A written report to be sent after the meeting.
2. What is the difference between because and so, in the text?
A Because is followed by the reason; so is followed by the result.
B Because is formal and so is informal, but they take the same half.
C There is no difference; both introduce the reason.
3. Why is due to the invoice arrived late wrong?
A Because due to is too formal for spoken English.
B Because the reason has to come after the result.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Ask somebody how their year went and you can hear, in the first ten seconds, whether they will be listened to. The person who answers with a list of months has already lost the room. The person who answers with two sentences — one that closed and one that is still open — is the one whose words get repeated later, in a meeting they were not invited to.

English separates those two sentences with a change of shape, and the decision is made by the time expression, not by how recent the event feels. The past simple is for an action that is finished inside a period that is also finished: we rolled out the template in March, I signed it yesterday, the backlog grew last January. The present perfect — have or has plus the past participle — is for a period that is still running: year to date, not one report has been late, since March the team has streamlined eleven steps. It is also what English uses when the result is still true and the date does not matter at all: two people have been promoted, nobody has left.

Two traps close the question. The first is that a finished time word wins, always, however recent the event is: I have signed it yesterday is wrong for the same reason I have done it last month is wrong, and no amount of recency rescues either. The second is ago, which is finished by definition and therefore always takes the past simple: we rolled it out three months ago. Notice what these two shapes give you in a review. One sentence tells the room what you did. The other tells them what is still true because of it — and only the second one survives the meeting.

C Whether the sentence is spoken or written.
3. Why is I have signed it yesterday wrong?

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a common belief, in every finance team in the world, that the only two answers to a hard question are a confident one and a silent one. The confident one is dangerous, because it is remembered when it turns out to be wrong. The silent one is worse, because it is remembered every time. What almost nobody is taught is that English has a third answer, and it is not vague at all — it is precise about something else. It is precise about how sure you are.

The tools are four small verbs, and they sit on a scale. Must be is the top: almost certain, because no other explanation is left standing. It must be the new contract, because nothing else changed. Could be and may be sit in the middle: possible, and you have a reason to think so. It could be freight, because the fuel index moved in the same month. Might be is weaker still: possible, but you would have to check. And cannot be is the floor — not doubt, but the opposite of it: you have evidence against the idea. It cannot be a posting error, because every entry traced back correctly.

The shape is simple and there are two traps in it. First, these verbs are followed by a bare verb, with no to and no -s: it must be, never it must to be. To talk about the past, add have plus the participle: it must have been posted twice. Second, the opposite of must be is cannot be, not must not be — must not in English is an order, not a deduction. And one habit matters more than the grammar: every one of these sentences is followed by a reason. The verb says how sure you are; the because after it is the only part that makes anybody believe you.

1. Why does the text say the silent answer is worse than the confident one?
A Because it takes longer to give.
B Because the confident one is remembered when it is wrong, and the silent one is remembered every time.
C Because silence is considered rude in a review call.
2. What is the difference between could be and might be, in the text?
A Could be is possible with a reason to think so; might be is weaker — you would have to check.
B Could be is about the past and might be is about the future.
C They are the same, and either one is fine.
3. What does the text say is the only part that makes anybody believe you?
A Choosing the strongest verb on the scale.
B Speaking quickly and without pauses.
C The reason that comes after the verb.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A rate goes from four per cent to six per cent. How big is that? The honest answer is that there are three correct ways to say it, and they sound so different from each other that the choice can decide whether a room approves something or refuses it. It went up by two percentage points. It is fifty per cent higher than it was. It went from four per cent to six. All three are true. Only the last one is impossible to misread.

The trap has a name and it is worth learning it once, properly. A movement between two percentages is measured in percentage points, never in per cent. Saying it went up by two per cent makes a fifty per cent increase sound like almost nothing — and the person listening will make a decision on that. The rest of the machinery is smaller. Comparatives take than, never that: higher than last year. Multiples take as: twice as high as, half as expensive as. And the words that soften or sharpen a comparison — slightly, roughly, significantly — go in front of it, not after the number: slightly above budget, not above budget slightly.

What all of this is really protecting is not accuracy. The figures were accurate before. It is protecting the listener, who has about four seconds to build a picture and will build one whether or not you helped. Giving both numbers first, from and to, hands them the picture instead of a conclusion. It costs one extra second, and after it nobody in the room can quietly arrive at a different number from yours.
