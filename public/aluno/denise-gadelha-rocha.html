Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. You already manage every one of these in Portuguese.

Underwriting -- deciding which risks the company accepts, and at what price
"Better underwriting in the south is what turned the quarter around."

"Our exposure to climate risk has grown with the portfolio."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the report and answer the questions. Notice which verbs take have and which do not.

Denise opens the quarterly review with the headline figure. So far this year the region has grown eleven percent year on year, and the top line now stands at forty million in premium. Two things drove it. In March her team closed the largest account in the region, and since January tighter underwriting in the south has brought the loss ratio down four points, so the bottom line holds. The corporate portfolio carried almost all of the growth.

Then she flags the part nobody asked about. Climate exposure in the south has grown with the portfolio, and the outlook for the next two quarters depends on a decision the board has not taken yet. Last quarter the region also lost two brokers in Argentina. Her takeaway is one sentence: the growth is real, but it is not free.

1. The text says "so far this year the region has grown" but "in March her team closed". Why the difference?
A "This year" is still open, so it takes the present perfect. "In March" is a finished, named time, so it takes the past simple.
B "Grow" is irregular and "close" is regular.
C The present perfect is simply more formal in a report.
2. Why is it "since January tighter underwriting has brought the loss ratio down"?
A Because "since" can take either tense in a business report.
B Because "since" marks a period that runs from a past point up to now, and that always takes the present perfect.
C Because the loss ratio is a number and numbers take the present perfect.
3. "The board has not taken the decision yet." What does the tense tell the reader?
A The decision is still open and still expected — it matters right now.
B The decision was rejected at some point in the past.
C The decision belongs to a period that is already closed.
4. Why does "last quarter the region also lost two brokers" use the past simple?
A Because losing brokers is bad news, and bad news takes the past simple.
B Because "lose" has no past participle.
C Because "last quarter" is a finished period, and a finished named period blocks the present perfect.

GRAMMAR

Vocabulary

"I spearhead commercial operations across Latin America."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Third Conditional

GRAMMAR

The tense for a past that never happened — and the one senior people use to show judgement.

PartShapeExample
The conditionIf + had + past participleIf I had stayed in operations...
The resultwould / could / might + have + past participle...I would not have learned the commercial side.
What it meansNeither part happened. The past is closed — you are reading it, not regretting it.
Choosing the modalwould = you are sure · could = it was possible · might = perhaps, you are not sure
Either order worksThe result can come first, with no comma.I would never have built that network if I had stayed there.
In speechAlmost everything contracts: If I'd stayed, I wouldn't have learned. Hearing the contraction is half the listening battle.
The one trapNever If I would have known. "Would" never follows "if." The condition takes had; the result takes would have — one on each side.

Vocabulary

Rapport -- an easy, trusting connection between people

Common ground -- something two people share that makes talking easy

"Let us circle back to the climate exposure after the numbers."
To hit it off -- to like someone immediately, from the first minutes

GRAMMAR

The small talk is not wasted time. If they had not built that rapport before nine, the difficult conversation at eleven would be much harder now. Denise reads the room, notices that the Chief Risk Officer is sitting alone, and uses the last four minutes there instead. If she had not moved across the room, she would still be waiting for an introduction that was never going to come. When the topic gets too big for four minutes, she closes it cleanly: they will circle back at lunch. Later she admits she is swamped for the rest of the month, but that she and Diego hit it off immediately. If Milan had not been running late, none of it would have happened at all.

A In the present — that is why it is "would be" and not "would have been".
B In the past, at nine o'clock in the morning.
C In the future, at lunch.
2. "If Milan had not been running late, none of it would have happened." Why is this one different?

B Because both the cause and the consequence are in the past, so it takes "would have happened".
C Because it is a negative sentence.

A The consequence would be happening right now — "still" is a present marker and it pulls "would be".
B The waiting finished a long time ago.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Mixed Conditionals

GRAMMAR

The cause is in the past. The consequence is on your desk this morning.

TypeResult sideExample
Third conditionalwould have + past participle — result in the pastIf Milan had not run late, none of it would have happened.
Mixed conditionalwould + base verb — no have — result nowIf we had not built that rapport, this would be much harder now.

Owning the Numbers -- Presenting Quarterly Results with Precision

Reporting a quarter with precision, and a bad number without a defendant. Key words: variance, margin, one-off, offset, benchmark, run rate, like for like, underperform, break down, account for, forecast, materially. Structure: the advanced passive voice for impersonal reporting -- and the rule about when not to use it.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. You use every one of these in every management meeting, in Portuguese.

Variance -- the gap between what you planned and what actually happened
"The variance in Argentina was nine percent."
Margin -- what is left after costs, as a share of what came in
"Volume grew, but the margin did not follow it."
One-off -- something that happened only once and is not part of the pattern
"That claim was a one-off. Strip it out and the quarter looks different."
To offset -- to cancel out a loss with a gain from somewhere else
"The claims in the south were offset by the corporate renewals."
Benchmark -- a standard from outside that you measure yourself against
"Against the market benchmark, we are two points ahead."
Run rate -- the yearly figure implied by the pace you are running at now
"At the current run rate we close the year at forty-eight million."
Like for like -- comparing only what is genuinely comparable, with the extras stripped out
"Like for like, without the acquisition, growth was six percent."
To underperform -- to do worse than expected, or worse than the market around you
"Argentina underperformed, and the reason is on the next slide."
To break down -- to split a total into the parts it is made of
"Let me break the forty million down by market."
To account for -- to explain something, or to make up a share of a total
"Corporate accounts for two thirds of the growth."
Forecast -- the number you expect, stated before it happens
"The forecast was built in January, before the currency moved."
Materially -- enough to change the decision, not just enough to notice
"The currency moved, but not materially."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the note and answer the questions. Notice how often nobody is named.

Growth of eleven percent year on year was recorded across the region. Like for like, with the acquisition stripped out, the figure is six percent, against a market benchmark of four. The margin was held: the claims in the south were offset by the corporate renewals, and those renewals were driven by the commercial team in Brazil.

One variance requires explanation. In Argentina the forecast was missed by nine percent. The pipeline had not been updated since May, and two renewals were counted twice. Neither issue is expected to repeat: the reconciliation is being rebuilt this quarter, and a second review point has been added. It is expected that the run rate will be recovered by December. The currency moved, but not materially, and it does not account for the variance.

1. "The pipeline had not been updated since May." Why does the note not say who failed to update it?
A Because the person is not the information. Naming them would move attention from the problem to an individual.
B Because the writer does not know who it was.
C Because the passive voice is always more polite than the active.
2. "Those renewals were driven by the commercial team in Brazil." Why is the agent kept here?
A Because the sentence would be grammatically wrong without it.
B Because this is good news, and who did it is the information.
C Because Brazil is the largest market in the region.
3. "The reconciliation is being rebuilt this quarter." What does this form tell the reader?
A The work is in progress right now — not planned, not finished.
B The work was completed last quarter.
C The work will begin at some point next year.
4. Why does the note use "it is expected that" instead of "I expect"?
A Because the writer is not sure and wants to avoid responsibility.
B Because it presents the expectation as a shared institutional view rather than one person's opinion — the standard register of a written report.
C Because "I expect" is grammatically incorrect in business English.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Passive in a Report

GRAMMAR

Passive for the problem, active for the credit — and your own name for your own decisions.

FormShapeExample
Simple past passivewas / were + past participleThe forecast was missed by nine percent.
Past perfect passivehad been + past participleThe pipeline had not been updated since May.
Continuous passiveis / are being + past participleThe reconciliation is being rebuilt.

Vocabulary

Buy-in -- agreement that people actually act on afterwards

Champion -- someone who argues for your idea when you are not in the room
"I need one champion in Milan, not five polite nods."
Trade-off -- what you give up in order to get something else

"I socialised the proposal with three people before the board saw it."
To sound out -- to find out someone's view informally, before anything is committed
"Let me sound out Milan before we put it on an agenda."

"He has a vested interest in that market, and that is fine once you know it."
Momentum -- the sense that something is already moving and will not stop
"Two yes answers before the meeting and the proposal has momentum."

"I would rather escalate it now than discover in November that nobody could decide."
Mandate -- the authority you have actually been given to decide something
"Below the local limits this is inside my mandate, and I will say so."
To win over -- to change someone's position by persuading them, not by outranking them
"I did not win him over with the numbers. I won him over with the trade-off."
To get behind -- to support something actively, not just to stop objecting to it
"Colombia got behind it once the trade-off was on the table."

GRAMMAR

The decision was taken on Thursday, but it was won in the four days before it. Denise decided to socialise the limits proposal rather than putting it straight on an agenda. She started with the sceptic, because the objection she had not heard yet was the one that could kill it in the room.

Group Finance avoided committing to a number, but they agreed to look at it before Thursday. Henrik, whose own capacity would be rationed, had a vested interest and said so. He recommended waiting for the reserving page, and then he offered to speak on it if it came up while she was away. That is a champion, and it is not the same thing as agreement.

By Wednesday two markets had got behind it and the proposal had momentum. She remembered to send the pre-read on Tuesday night, so nobody arrived unprepared. 1. "They avoided committing to a number, but they agreed to look at it." Why does one verb take -ing and the other take to?
A Because the choice belongs to the first verb: "avoid" always takes -ing, "agree" always takes to. There is no wider rule.
B Because the first is negative and the second is positive.
C Because "commit" is irregular.
2. "The group is committed to reviewing the limits." Why "reviewing" and not "review"?
A Because the review has already started.
B Because here "to" is a preposition, not the start of an infinitive — and after a preposition the verb always takes -ing.
C Because "commit" and "review" cannot appear together in an infinitive.
3. "She remembered to send the pre-read on Tuesday night." What does this tell you?
A She did not forget, and the pre-read went out.
B She has a memory of having sent it at some point.
C She thought about sending it but did not.
4. Why does the text say a champion is "not the same thing as agreement"?
A Because a champion usually disagrees with the detail.
B Because agreeing costs nothing, while a champion offers to argue for it when you are not there.
C Because a champion has to be more senior than you.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Gerund and Infinitive

GRAMMAR

The first verb decides. There is no rule to derive — but there are only about twenty verbs you will ever need.

GroupThe verbs you will actually useExample
Verb + toagree · decide · offer · refuse · plan · hope · fail · manage · intendWe agreed to escalate it.
Verb + -ingavoid · recommend · suggest · consider · involve · risk · delay · justify · postponeThey avoided committing to a date.
Verb + person + topersuade · ask · advise · encourage · remind · expect · allowHe persuaded me to sound them out.
After any prepositionAlways -ing: interested in hearing, instead of escalating, before putting it on an agenda.
The trap inside the trapIn committed to, look forward to and object to, the "to" is a PREPOSITION: committed to reviewing, never committed to review.
Where the meaning changesI stopped to explain = I paused in order to explain · I stopped explaining = I explained and then gave up.
And againI remembered to send it = I did not forget · I remember sending it = I have the memory of having sent it. Both appear in minutes of meetings, and they are not interchangeable.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Future Perfect and Future Continuous

GRAMMAR

Two futures, two effects. One closes a commitment against a date. The other states an arrangement and asks without ordering.

FormWhat it does in an emailExample
will have + past participle (future perfect)stands at a future moment and looks back: at that point, this is doneBy Thursday we will have closed the Colombian renewal.
will have been + past participle (passive)same deadline, but the doer does not matter — the register of governanceBy the time you land, the wording will have been signed off.
will be + -ing (future continuous)states an arrangement already in place. No promise extracted, no favor askedNext week I will be presenting the figures in Milan.
Will you be + -ing?asks without giving an order. "Send me the numbers" commands; this one enquires about a planWill you be sending the Mexican numbers before Friday?
The deadline anchorthe future perfect almost always travels with one of theseby Wednesday evening · by the end of the quarter · by the time you land
Trap 1 — by, never untilby Thursday = at any point up to Thursday, and then it is done. until Thursday = the whole time continuing up to Thursday and stopping there. A deadline is always by.
Trap 2 — no will in the time clause"By the time you land..." / "When the broker replies..." — after when, by the time and as soon as, the verb stays in the present and the will lives in the other half.
Why it matters here"We will close it on Thursday" is a plan. "By Thursday we will have closed it" is a commitment with a date attached, and it is the difference between a note and an instruction the reader can schedule around.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Used To, Be Used To, Get Used To

GRAMMAR

Three words, three meanings. One test separates them, and it takes two seconds.

FormWhat it meansExample
used to + verbA habit or state in the past that is over now. Past only — there is no present form of this structure.I used to translate everything in my head.
be used to + -ingYou are accustomed to it. It is normal for you. Works in any tense: I am, I was, I will be.I am used to presenting in English.
get used to + -ingYou are becoming accustomed. The process, not the result.I am getting used to the pace.
The two-second testCan you say "I am used to it"? Then the "to" is a PREPOSITION and the verb after it takes -ing. Cannot? Then it is past habit, and the verb stays bare.
Negative and questionThe "d" moves to the auxiliary: I did not use to prepare them · Did you use to dread that call? Never did not used to.
The one to watchI am used to work means nothing at all. Either I used to work (past habit) or I am used to working (it is normal for me). Two different sentences, one letter apart.
Why it matters this weekThis is how a senior person describes their own history with a language without sounding defensive. "I used to translate everything in my head" is the sentence of somebody who has moved. "My English is not good" is not a sentence about progress at all.

Structure: modal perfects -- should have, must have, could have, might have, would have.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words a board uses when a quarter has to be explained.

Post-mortem -- the structured review held after something has finished, to establish what happened and why
"This is a post-mortem, not a confession. There is a difference and the board can hear it."
Hindsight -- the clarity you only have once the outcome is known, which the person deciding did not have
"In hindsight the signal looks enormous. At the time it was one line in a report of forty."
Blind spot -- the area you were in a position to see and did not, because of where you were standing
"The blind spot was assuming the renewal cycle would behave as it did last year."
Root cause -- the thing that, if it had gone differently, changes everything downstream of it
"The June signal was not the root cause. It was the first place the root cause became visible."
Warning sign -- an early signal that a problem was forming, visible before the loss appears
"The warning sign was there in June. We read it and we explained it away."
Near miss -- the loss that almost happened and did not, which is exactly why nobody studies it
"We treated it as a near miss repeating itself. It was not repeating. It was building."
Oversight -- something you failed to do without ever noticing that you had not done it
"That was a judgment call, not an oversight, and I would like the committee to hear the difference."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Hedging and Epistemic Distance

GRAMMAR

Three layers do the work, and they stack in a fixed order: the modal sets the likelihood, the adverb marks the degree, the impersonal structure moves the claim off you and onto the evidence.

LayerWhat it doesThe forms you will actually use
Modals of probabilityPut the claim on a scale of likelihoodwill (certain) · should (expected) · may / might (possible) · could (conceivable)
Adverbs of certaintyMark the degree inside the sentenceclearly · evidently · presumably · apparently · arguably · possibly · conceivably
Impersonal structuresMove the claim off you and onto the evidenceit appears that... · the data suggest that... · there are indications that... · what we are seeing is consistent with...
The distancing "would"One step further back, with the structure unchanged: it appears becomes it would appear, the data suggest becomes the data would suggest. Nothing about the fact changes — only your distance from it.
Where the adverb goesMid-position, after the auxiliary and before the main verb: "capacity is apparently tightening", "we can arguably hold the rate". Front position is for emphasis only: "Arguably, we can hold it."
One hedge, not twoStacking reads as evasion, not as precision: "it would possibly maybe harden" becomes "it may harden". One marker per claim, chosen on purpose.
After a modal, bare infinitiveNever an -s and never a "to": would suggests and would to suggest are both wrong. Only would suggest, would appear, may hold.
