Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Taking the Floor -- Opening a Results Presentation

The first ninety seconds of a quarterly review with your global head. Key words: underwriting, premium, loss ratio, portfolio, top line, bottom line, year on year, exposure, outlook, to flag, headline figure, takeaway. Structures: present perfect and past simple for reporting a period, and the four-move opening.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. You already manage every one of these in Portuguese.

Underwriting -- deciding which risks the company accepts, and at what price
"Better underwriting in the south is what turned the quarter around."
Premium -- the amount a client pays for a policy
"We wrote forty million in premium in the quarter."
Loss ratio -- the share of the premium that goes back out as claims
"The loss ratio has come down four points since January."
Portfolio -- the whole set of clients and policies you are responsible for
"The corporate portfolio carried the growth this quarter."
Top line -- the revenue, before any cost is taken out
"The top line is up, and that is the easy half of the story."
Bottom line -- the profit, after everything has been paid
"Growth that does not reach the bottom line is not growth."
Year on year -- compared with the same period twelve months earlier
"We have grown eleven percent year on year."
Exposure -- how much the company could lose if a risk actually happens
"Our exposure to climate risk has grown with the portfolio."
Outlook -- what you expect to happen in the months ahead
"The outlook for the fourth quarter is solid, with one caveat."
To flag -- to point something out early so nobody is surprised later
"I want to flag one risk before we go into the numbers."
Headline figure -- the one number that carries the whole story
"The headline figure is eleven percent. Everything else explains it."
Takeaway -- the single point you want the room to remember
"If there is one takeaway today, it is that the growth is real."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the report and answer the questions. Notice which verbs take have and which do not.

Denise opens the quarterly review with the headline figure. So far this year the region has grown eleven percent year on year, and the top line now stands at forty million in premium. Two things drove it. In March her team closed the largest account in the region, and since January tighter underwriting in the south has brought the loss ratio down four points, so the bottom line holds. The corporate portfolio carried almost all of the growth.

Then she flags the part nobody asked about. Climate exposure in the south has grown with the portfolio, and the outlook for the next two quarters depends on a decision the board has not taken yet. Last quarter the region also lost two brokers in Argentina. Her takeaway is one sentence: the growth is real, but it is not free.

1. The text says "so far this year the region has grown" but "in March her team closed". Why the difference?
A "This year" is still open, so it takes the present perfect. "In March" is a finished, named time, so it takes the past simple.
B "Grow" is irregular and "close" is regular.
C The present perfect is simply more formal in a report.
2. Why is it "since January tighter underwriting has brought the loss ratio down"?
A Because "since" can take either tense in a business report.
B Because "since" marks a period that runs from a past point up to now, and that always takes the present perfect.
C Because the loss ratio is a number and numbers take the present perfect.
3. "The board has not taken the decision yet." What does the tense tell the reader?
A The decision is still open and still expected — it matters right now.
B The decision was rejected at some point in the past.
C The decision belongs to a period that is already closed.
4. Why does "last quarter the region also lost two brokers" use the past simple?
A Because losing brokers is bad news, and bad news takes the past simple.
B Because "lose" has no past participle.
C Because "last quarter" is a finished period, and a finished named period blocks the present perfect.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect and Past Simple in a Report

GRAMMAR

One question decides it every time: is that period finished, or are you still inside it?

FormWhen you use itExample
have / has + past participleThe period is still open, or the result matters right now.We have grown eleven percent this year.
past simpleThe time is finished and named, or both sides know which time you mean.In March we closed the largest account.
have been + verb-ingStarted in the past and still going on today.I have been leading the region since the promotion.
Markers that pull havethis quarter · so far · since January · over the last six months · already · still · to date · not yet
Markers that block itlast quarter · in March · in 2024 · yesterday · two weeks ago · when I joined
Negative and questionhave not / has not + participle · Have you...?The board has not taken the decision yet. Have you seen the numbers?
Two trapsNever We have closed it in March — a named finished time takes the past simple. Never I am leading the region since 2024 — since takes have been leading.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

The Director's Voice -- Presenting Yourself and Your Role

Saying who you are and what you own, at the level the room expects. Key words: spearhead, oversee, accountability, scope, stakeholder, deliverable, alignment, cross-functional, counterpart, leverage, liaise with, drive. Structure: the third conditional, for telling a career decision as judgement rather than luck.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read the definition. These are the words that turn a job title into a scope.

To spearhead -- to lead something new from the front, before anyone else moves
"I spearhead commercial operations across Latin America."
To oversee -- to be in charge of work that other people carry out
"I oversee a cross-functional team of forty people."
Accountability -- being the one person who answers for a result
"The accountability for the region sits with me, not with the team."
Scope -- the limits of what a role or a project actually covers
"Argentina came into my scope when I took the directorship."
Stakeholder -- anyone whose interests are affected by what you decide
"Every stakeholder in the region reads that report."
Deliverable -- a concrete result someone is expecting from you, by a date
"There are three deliverables before the board meets."
Alignment -- agreement between people on direction and priorities
"Half my job is alignment between Milan and the regional teams."
Cross-functional -- involving people from several different areas of the company
"It was a cross-functional project: commercial, claims and finance."
Counterpart -- the person who holds your job in another country or company
"I speak to my counterparts in Colombia and Mexico every week."
To leverage -- to use something you already have to get more out of it
"We leveraged the broker network we built in Mexico."
To liaise with -- to keep contact and coordinate between two groups
"I liaise with Milan on everything above the local limits."
To drive -- to make something happen and keep it moving forward
"The corporate team drove the result, and I want that said out loud."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the profile and answer the questions. Notice which sentences describe things that never happened.

Denise spearheads commercial operations across Latin America and oversees a cross-functional team of forty. Her scope covers four markets. Above the local limits she liaises with the group head office in Milan; below them, the accountability is hers alone. She works with three counterparts in the region, and every stakeholder — brokers, regional teams, the global function — reads the same numbers differently.

She came up through the technical side of the business, which is unusual for the role. If she had stayed there, she would never have built the broker network that now drives the region. If the group had not leveraged that network last year, the corporate result would have been flat. She says the hardest part of the job is not the numbers but the alignment: agreeing on one set of deliverables across four markets takes longer than producing them. If someone had told her how much English the directorship would require, she might have started preparing a year earlier.

1. "If she had stayed there, she would never have built the broker network." What does this tell you?
A She did not stay, and the network exists because of that.
B She stayed, and the network never got built.
C She may still decide to stay in the future.
2. Why is the condition written as "if she had stayed" and not "if she would have stayed"?
A Both are correct; "had" is simply shorter.
B After "if" the verb takes the past perfect. "Would" belongs on the other side of the sentence, never after "if".
C Because the subject is "she" and not "I".
3. "She might have started preparing a year earlier." Why "might" and not "would"?
A "Might" leaves room for doubt — it is a possible outcome, not a certain one.
B "Might" is the polite form of "would" in professional writing.
C "Would" cannot be used with the verb "start".
4. Why does the text say the accountability "is hers alone" rather than "is shared with the team"?
A Because she does not trust her team.
B Because responsibility can be shared, but accountability sits with one person — the one who answers for the result.
C Because the team members are in other countries.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Third Conditional

GRAMMAR

The tense for a past that never happened — and the one senior people use to show judgement.

PartShapeExample
The conditionIf + had + past participleIf I had stayed in operations...
The resultwould / could / might + have + past participle...I would not have learned the commercial side.
What it meansNeither part happened. The past is closed — you are reading it, not regretting it.
Choosing the modalwould = you are sure · could = it was possible · might = perhaps, you are not sure
Either order worksThe result can come first, with no comma.I would never have built that network if I had stayed there.
In speechAlmost everything contracts: If I'd stayed, I wouldn't have learned. Hearing the contraction is half the listening battle.
The one trapNever If I would have known. "Would" never follows "if." The condition takes had; the result takes would have — one on each side.

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Reading the Room -- Strategic Small Talk and Executive Rapport

The twelve minutes before a meeting starts, and how to run them. Key words: rapport, common ground, break the ice, small talk, catch up, touch base, be swamped, jet lag, read the room, circle back, hit it off, run late. Structure: mixed conditionals -- a cause in the past with a consequence sitting on your desk today.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the definition. You do all of this already — in another language.

Rapport -- an easy, trusting connection between people
"We built rapport in ten minutes and the meeting ran itself."
Common ground -- something two people share that makes talking easy
"We found common ground in thirty seconds: both of us hate long decks."
To break the ice -- to make the first minutes less stiff
"He broke the ice by complaining about the coffee."
Small talk -- light conversation that opens the way to a real one
"The small talk before the call is where the deal actually moved."
To catch up -- to exchange news with someone after some time apart
"It has been months. Let us catch up properly after the session."
To touch base -- to make brief contact just to stay in sync
"I touch base with my regional leads every Monday, fifteen minutes."
To be swamped -- having far too much work at this exact moment
"I am completely swamped this week, but next Tuesday is open."
Jet lag -- the tiredness you feel after flying across time zones
"Give me one more coffee. The jet lag is winning."
To read the room -- to sense the mood of a group and adjust what you say
"He did not read the room. He opened with a joke about the results."
To circle back -- to come back to a topic later, on purpose
"Let us circle back to the climate exposure after the numbers."
To hit it off -- to like someone immediately, from the first minutes
"We hit it off straight away, which made the negotiation much easier."
To run late -- to be behind schedule, and to say so before anyone asks
"Milan is running late, so we have five extra minutes."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the account and answer the questions. Watch where the consequence lands: in the past, or today.

Denise arrives in Milan at six in the morning and the jet lag is already winning. She is in the room at a quarter to nine, twelve minutes before the first item. Diego, her counterpart from Mexico, is there too. They have spoken on calls for two years and never once had time to catch up. He breaks the ice by asking about the flight, and within a minute they have found common ground: neither of them can stand a fifty-slide deck.

The small talk is not wasted time. If they had not built that rapport before nine, the difficult conversation at eleven would be much harder now. Denise reads the room, notices that the Chief Risk Officer is sitting alone, and uses the last four minutes there instead. If she had not moved across the room, she would still be waiting for an introduction that was never going to come. When the topic gets too big for four minutes, she closes it cleanly: they will circle back at lunch. Later she admits she is swamped for the rest of the month, but that she and Diego hit it off immediately. If Milan had not been running late, none of it would have happened at all.

1. "If they had not built that rapport before nine, the difficult conversation would be much harder now." Where is the consequence?
A In the present — that is why it is "would be" and not "would have been".
B In the past, at nine o'clock in the morning.
C In the future, at lunch.
2. "If Milan had not been running late, none of it would have happened." Why is this one different?
A Because it is about a person and not about a meeting.
B Because both the cause and the consequence are in the past, so it takes "would have happened".
C Because it is a negative sentence.
3. "If she had not moved across the room, she would still be waiting." What does "still" tell you?
A The consequence would be happening right now — "still" is a present marker and it pulls "would be".
B The waiting finished a long time ago.
C "Still" is only there for emphasis and changes nothing.
4. Why does the text say the small talk was not wasted time?
A Because the meeting started late anyway.
B Because the rapport built before nine is what makes the difficult conversation at eleven possible.
C Because she had nothing else to do while waiting.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Mixed Conditionals

GRAMMAR

The cause is in the past. The consequence is on your desk this morning.

TypeResult sideExample
Third conditionalwould have + past participle — result in the pastIf Milan had not run late, none of it would have happened.
Mixed conditionalwould + base verb — no have — result nowIf we had not built that rapport, this would be much harder now.
The conditionIdentical in both: If + had + past participle. Only the result side moves in time.
Continuous resultwould + be + verb-ing, for something in progress nowShe would still be waiting for an introduction.
Words that give it awaynow · today · this week · still · at the moment on the result side always pull would be, never would have been.
Why it is usefulIt explains a situation today without blaming anyone: the cause is closed, the consequence is on the table.
The trap, againStill never If I would have known. "Would" never follows "if" — in any conditional at all.
