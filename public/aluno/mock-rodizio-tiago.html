The Update Nobody Read

Before class 1: The glossary

Vocabulary

Tap Listen and say each word out loud. Eight words — you will need all of them in class.

Update — short news about how something is going
"Here is the update: both batches left on Monday."
On track — going as planned, at the expected speed
"We're on track for the 12th."
Behind schedule — later than planned
"The second batch is two days behind schedule."
Blocker — the one thing that is stopping the work from moving
"The blocker is the tooling supplier."
Lead time — the time between the order and the delivery
"Their lead time went from three weeks to five."
Batch — a quantity of products made or sent together
"The first batch left the factory on Monday."
Shipment — goods sent from one place to another
"The shipment arrives on the 14th."
Sign-off — the formal approval you need before the next step
"We start production the day we get your sign-off."

Before class 2: The reading

Input

Read it once, without a dictionary. In class we will work on it — you only need the general idea now.

Four hundred words nobody read

Sofia coordinates production at a metal parts factory in Joinville. Every Friday afternoon, for two years, she wrote a status report for her American client. It was thorough. It had numbers, dates, the name of every supplier, and it was usually four hundred words long. She was proud of it, and she never missed one.

In March the client called her, upset. A shipment had slipped by ten days and he had found out from his own warehouse, not from her. Sofia opened her sent folder and found the report from three weeks earlier. The delay was there. It was in the fourth paragraph, in the middle of a long sentence about tooling.

So the information had been sent, and the information had not arrived. Those are two different things, and she had been measuring the wrong one.

She changed the format that same week. Not four hundred words on Friday, but sixty seconds on Monday, on the phone, always in the same order: where we are, what is stuck, what I need from you, when it closes. If nothing was stuck, she said so, and the call took twenty seconds.

Nobody ever thanked her for the long reports. Four months into the short calls, the client asked her to explain the format to his other two suppliers.

Before class 3: Did you get it?

Quiz

Two quick questions. This is not a test — it just tells you whether to read it once more.

Why did the client find out about the delay from his warehouse?
A Sofia decided not to report it.
B It was in her report, buried inside a long paragraph.
What does Sofia do now when nothing is stuck?
A She still calls, and the call is very short.
B She skips the call that week.

Before class 1: The questions you will need

Vocabulary

Eight lines you will use on any call where someone asks you for something. Tap Listen once for each. Do not memorise them — just let your ear meet them before class.

Before I confirm, can I check one thing? — buys you time without saying no
Use it the second someone asks for something you are not sure about.
Can I ask what's driving this? — asks for the reason behind the request
The single most useful question in this lesson.
What happens if we don't? — finds out how serious the request really is
The answer tells you whether it is a wish or a problem.
Just to be clear — checks one detail before you move on
"Just to be clear, the length stays the same?"
Do you mean X or Y? — turns a vague request into a choice
"Do you mean lighter, or physically smaller?"
So what you need is — says the requirement back in your own words
If you get this one wrong, they correct you — which is the point.
Would it help if — tests another way without promising it
"Would it help if we shipped fewer per box?"
I'll confirm by Thursday — closes with a date instead of a promise
A date is what turns "I'll check" into something real.

Before class 2: Until they are automatic

Speaking

This is the part that matters. In class you will say these to a customer who is not making it easy, and under pressure you only reach for what your mouth already knows. Record each one until it comes out without thinking.

Before I confirm, can I check one thing?
Can I ask what's driving this?
Just to be clear, the length stays the same?
So what you need is a lighter box, not a thinner part.
I'll confirm by Thursday.

Before class 3: Bring two real ones

Reflection

In class the conversation will be about your factory, not a textbook one. Come with the raw material.

Think of a request from a customer where what they asked for was NOT what they needed. Record 60 seconds: what they asked for, what it turned out to be, and how you found out. If none comes to mind, describe the request you get most often — and say what you think is really behind it.
Now the uncomfortable one. Think of a time you said yes too fast — you agreed, started the work, and only then understood what was really being asked. Record 40 seconds: what did agreeing cost, and which question would have saved you?

2Can I ask what's driving this?

4So what you need is a lighter box, not a thinner part.

Two Suppliers, One Slot

Priming 1: The two quotes

Input

Read it once. Do not decide anything yet — deciding is the task, and the task is in class.

One production slot, two ways to fill it

You have one free production slot in October and two quotes on your desk. Both suppliers can make the bracket. Only one of them can have the slot, and purchasing wants your recommendation by Friday.

Delta Metal has supplied you for six years. Their unit price is 4.20 dollars, with a minimum order of 5,000 pieces and a lead time of four weeks. They hold the ISO certification your American customer asks for, and in six years they have missed two dates, both by less than a week. Their quote ends with a line you have read many times: any delay above five days carries a penalty clause of 2% of the order value.

Krammer is new. Their unit price is 3.65 dollars, thirteen percent below Delta, with a minimum order of 12,000 pieces and a lead time of six weeks. Their capacity is larger and they say they can absorb a second order later in the year. Their quote says nothing about certification, and there is no penalty clause in it — in either direction.

You called two companies who use Krammer. The first said they are good and cheap and answer the phone. The second said the first six months were difficult, and then it settled. That is the track record you have.

The saving, if you go with Krammer, is about seven thousand dollars on this order. Your customer's audit is in March, and you do not yet know whether they will ask for the certificate of every supplier in the chain, or only of yours.

Priming 2: The words of a quote

Vocabulary

Ten words that live inside any supplier quote. Tap Listen once for each. You do not have to produce them yet.

Quote — a written offer with prices and conditions
"Two quotes on your desk, one slot."
Unit price — the price of one single piece
"Their unit price is 3.65 dollars."
Minimum order — the smallest quantity a supplier will accept
"A minimum order of twelve thousand pieces."
Capacity — how much a factory can produce in a period
"Their capacity is larger than ours."
Certification — an official document proving a standard is met
"They hold the ISO certification our customer asks for."
Penalty clause — the part of a contract that says what a late supplier pays
"A penalty clause of two percent of the order value."
Trade-off — what you give up to get something else
"The main trade-off is price against certainty."
Downside — the bad part of an option that is otherwise good
"The downside is six weeks instead of four."
Track record — what someone has actually done in the past
"Six years is a track record. Two phone calls is not."
To commit — to promise something in a way you cannot easily undo
"I don't want to commit before Monday."

Priming 3: Notice, do not answer

Noticing

There is nothing to submit here, and no right answer. Read the twelve lines below and go back to the text with them in your head. In class you will need them — nobody will hand them to you first.

• A is cheaper, but…

• The main trade-off is…

• What worries me is…

• If we go with Krammer, we…

• I'd go with Delta, and here is why.

• It should save around seven thousand.

• On paper it looks better than it is.

• The risk I can't cover is…

• That would only matter if…

• I'd rather pay more and sleep.

• We've never had a problem, which is not the same as never having a risk.

• I can live with the price if we fix the lead time.

One thing to notice while you read: three of them say a number, and none of them say it as a certainty. Ask yourself why.

Priming 4: Say them once

Speaking

Once each, no more. This is not drilling — it is letting your mouth meet the shape before you have to use it under pressure.

The main trade-off is price against certainty.
What worries me is the audit in March.
It should save around seven thousand dollars.
The risk I can't cover is the certificate.

Priming 5: Bring a decision of your own

Reflection

The task in class is about choosing under pressure. Come with a real one, or the second half of the lesson will be about a company that does not exist.

Think of a supplier, a machine or a process you had to choose between two options. Record 60 seconds: what the two options were, which one you picked, and what you gave up. Do not organize it — the shape is what we work on in class.
Now the other half. Record 40 seconds: who disagreed with that decision — or who would have, if they had known — and what they would say to you.

Survival Card — Lesson 3

1The main trade-off is price against certainty.
2What worries me is the audit in March.
3It should save around seven thousand dollars.
4The risk I can't cover is the certificate.
5I can live with the price if we fix the lead time.

Before It Becomes a Problem

0%
▼

Before class 1: The glossary

Vocabulary

Eight words for a problem that has not happened yet. Tap Listen and say each one out loud.

Heads-up — an early, informal warning
"Heads-up: the press has been running hot."
Risk — something bad that may happen, but has not happened yet
"There's a risk that we lose two days."
Impact — the effect a problem has, in days or in money
"The impact would be about four days."
Contingency — the plan you have ready in case it goes wrong
"The contingency is the older press in building two."
To escalate — to take a problem to someone with more authority
"I want to escalate this today, not next week."
To flag — to make something visible so it is not missed
"I'm flagging it now, while it is still small."
Bottleneck — the slowest point, which holds everything else back
"The press is our bottleneck right now."
Downtime — time when a machine is not producing
"Two days of downtime moves the delivery."

Before class 2: The four parts of a warning

Input

Read it once. In class you will hear a real warning that only has the first two — and you will fix it.

1. The heads-up — what you noticed, and when. "Heads-up: the press has been running hot since Tuesday."

2. The risk — what may happen, said as a possibility, not a fact. "There's a risk that we lose two days this month."

3. The condition — the thing that would change the outcome. "Unless we get maintenance in before Friday, it will stop."

4. The request, with a date — what you need from the other person, and by when. "I need a decision by Wednesday."

Most warnings at work stop after part two. That is why they feel like complaints, and why nothing happens after them.

Before class 3: Did you get it?

Quiz

Two quick questions. Not a test — it just tells you whether to read it again.

Why say "there's a risk that it stops" instead of "it will stop"?
A It can still be avoided — and if it holds, you keep your credibility.
B It is more polite, and politeness matters more than the facts.
What happens to a warning with no date in it?
A It gets handled first, because it sounds urgent.
B It becomes news, and news goes to the bottom of the pile.

Before class 4: Put them back

Practice

Write the missing word. Tap Listen to hear the full sentence.

"The press is our right now."
"Two days of moves the delivery."
"I'm it now, while it is still small."

Before class 5: Say it out loud

Speaking

The four parts, one at a time. Listen, then record yourself.

Heads-up: the press has been running hot since Tuesday.
There's a risk that we lose two days this month.
Unless we get maintenance in before Friday, it will stop.
I need a decision by Wednesday.

Before class 6: Bring one of your own

Reflection
Think of one risk in your factory right now — something that has NOT gone wrong yet, but could this month. Record 40 seconds: what you noticed, and what could happen. Do not worry about the four parts — that is the work we do in class.

Survival Card — Lesson 4

1Heads-up: the press has been running hot since Tuesday.
2There's a risk that we lose two days this month.
3Unless we get maintenance in before Friday, it will stop.
4The impact would be about four days on the customer's side.
5I need a decision by Wednesday.
