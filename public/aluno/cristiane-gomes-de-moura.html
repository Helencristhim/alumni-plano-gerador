Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Who Is Cristiane? -- Saying Who You Are, and Asking Back

Somebody you have just met asks who you are and what you do, and the answer stops after four words. Key words: a neighborhood, free time, a hobby, to walk the dog, to go out with friends, a problem solver, a team, a teenager, a play, to travel. Structure: present simple with I and you — am for who you are, the bare verb for what you do, and do for the negative and the question.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody you have just met asks who you are. The whole answer fits into four shapes, and you already have all four. The first one says who you are: "I am Cristiane, and I am forty-eight." Look at that little word. It is am, and it appears because the sentence is about your name and your age, not about an action. English never says I have forty-eight years. Age is something you are.

The second shape says what you do, and here the am disappears completely: "I live in a neighborhood called Tatuape. I lead a team of nine people. I walk the dog every morning." The verb stands alone, with nothing in front of it. This is the shape people get wrong most often, because in Portuguese you say that you are responsible for something. In English, the verb does the work by itself. Never I am work. Say I work.

The third shape says no, and English does not touch the verb. It puts do not in front of it: "I do not have much free time from Monday to Friday, and I do not work on Sundays." The fourth shape is the question, and it is the one that keeps a conversation alive: "Do you have a dog? What do you do in your free time?" And when you have finished answering, hand the question back with three words: what about you? That is how the other person keeps talking, and how you get a few free seconds to think.

1. "I am forty-eight." Why is there an am in this sentence?
A Because every English sentence about a person needs am.
B Because the sentence says who you are and how old you are, not what you do.
C Because forty-eight is a large number.
2. The text warns against "I am work with technology". What is the problem?
A Technology needs the word the in front of it.
B The sentence has to start with a question word.
C The sentence says what you do, so the verb stands alone and there is no am in front of it.
3. According to the text, what do the three words "what about you?" give you?
A The other person keeps talking, and you get a few free seconds to think.
B A polite way to end the conversation.
C A way to avoid saying your age.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Am, the Bare Verb, and Do

GRAMMAR

Am says who you are. The bare verb says what you do. The two never stand together.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

My Day, My World -- One Whole Day, and Everybody in It

Your day is easy to live and hard to say, and the moment you talk about somebody else a small letter appears out of nowhere. Key words: to wake up, a routine, to get ready, a lunch break, a meeting, to handle, busy, to get home, a weekday, to relax. Structure: present simple in the third person — the s on the verb, does for the negative and the question, and where always, usually, sometimes and never go.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To wake up -- to stop sleeping and open your eyes in the morning
"I wake up at six, and my son wakes up at half past seven."
A routine -- the things you do in the same order, almost every day
"My routine almost never changes, and that is why it works."
To get ready -- to do everything you need before you leave the house
"He takes forty minutes to get ready, and I take ten."
A lunch break -- the hour in the middle of the working day when you stop to eat
"I take my whole lunch break, and I never eat at my desk."
A meeting -- a time when people sit together, in a room or online, to decide something
"She usually has four meetings, and sometimes five."
To handle -- to take care of something difficult, and not let it fall apart
"She handles the difficult meetings herself."
Busy -- having a lot to do and very little time left over
"Monday is my busiest day, and Friday is quiet."
To get home -- to arrive at the place where you live, at the end of the day
"I get home at seven, and the dog is already at the door."
A weekday -- any day from Monday to Friday, when most people work
"On a weekday I do not go out. On Saturday I do."
To relax -- to stop working and let your body and your head rest
"I only really relax on the weekend, and everybody here knows it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Here is your own day, and here is the same day told by somebody watching you. "I wake up at six. I get ready in ten minutes. I handle the difficult things myself." Nothing strange yet: with I, the verb stands alone, exactly as you learned last week. Now watch what happens when the person telling the story is not you: "She wakes up at six. She gets ready in ten minutes. She handles the difficult things herself." A small s appears on every verb, and it appears only with he, she and it.

That s is greedy, and it never sits in two places at once. The moment a sentence says no or asks a question, does turns up and takes the s for itself, and the verb goes back to being bare: "She does not eat at her desk." "Does he get ready quickly?" "How often does she get home before eight?" Never she does not eats, and never does he gets. One s per sentence, and does always wins it.

The last piece is how often. Words like always, usually, often, sometimes and never go before the verb: "She never eats at her desk." "He usually gets home late." But they go after am, is and are: "She is always busy on a weekday." "I am never late for a meeting." And they never go at the end. Not she goes never. Say she never goes, and then say what she does instead: "She relaxes on the weekend."

1. "She wakes up at six." Why is there an s on the verb here, but not in "I wake up at six"?
A Because the second sentence is shorter.
B Because the s only appears with he, she and it — never with I, you, we or they.
C Because wake is an irregular verb.
2. Why is it "she does not eat at her desk" and not "she does not eats at her desk"?
A Because does already took the s, and there is only one s per sentence.
B Because eat cannot take an s in any sentence.
C Because the sentence is about a desk and not about a person.
3. According to the text, where does never go in a sentence?
A At the end of the sentence, always.
B Immediately after the person: she never, he never, and nowhere else.
C Before the verb, but after am, is and are.
