Asking for the Right Thing -- The First Call With a Buyer

A buyer you have never met is on the call: you have four questions to ask and three to answer. Key words: a supplier, a buyer, a quote, terms, lead time, to place an order, a discount, a trial order. Structure: can, could and would for requests and offers.

▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A supplier -- a company that sells goods or materials to another company
"We have been their supplier in Brazil for six years."
A buyer -- the person in a company who decides what the company purchases
"The buyer for the Midwest region called me this morning."
A quote -- a written price that a supplier offers for one specific order
"I will send you a quote before Friday."
Terms -- the conditions of a deal: price, payment and delivery together
"The price is fine. It is the payment terms I want to discuss."
Lead time -- the number of days between the order and the delivery
"Our lead time is thirty days to the port."
To place an order -- to formally ask a supplier to send you the goods
"If we place the order this month, we can ship in August."
A discount -- money taken off the normal price
"We can offer a discount of five percent on larger orders."
A trial order -- a small first order, to test a supplier before a big one
"Could we start with a trial order? It is safer for both of us."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Karen Doyle is a buyer. She works for a distributor in the United States, and this morning she is on a call with a new supplier in Brazil. She opens with a request, not with a speech: "Could you tell me" what you produce for private label?

The supplier answers, and then asks for something too. "Would you be able to" send me the specification today, so I "can prepare" a quote? Karen says of course, and asks the question every buyer asks: what is your lead time? Thirty days to the port, plus the ocean.

Then comes the difficult part. Karen wants a discount on a small quantity, and the supplier says no — politely. "I am afraid we cannot" give our best price on a small order, but we "can offer" better payment terms. He suggests a trial order instead. Last year, he says, we "could not" accept those conditions; this year we "can", and here is why. Karen agrees to read the quote on Friday. If the numbers work, she will place the order in July.

1. "Could you tell me what you produce?" Is Karen talking about the past?
A Yes, she is asking about something that happened before.
B No. Here could is politeness, not time. She is asking for something now.
C No, because could is only used for the future.
2. Look at "we can offer better payment terms". What form of the verb comes after can?
A To offer, with to before the verb.
B Offering, with -ing at the end.
C Offer, the naked verb: no to, no -s, no -ing.
3. "Last year we could not accept those conditions." What does could not mean here?
A It was not possible for them in the past, and now it is.
B They are refusing politely, right now, on this call.
C They will never accept those conditions.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Can, Could and Would

GRAMMAR

Three words that open a commercial conversation. After all of them, the verb stays naked.

Reading the Quarter -- The Numbers, and What Happened to Them

Three months, one page of numbers, and somebody asking what happened. Key words: revenue, a cost, a profit, a margin, growth, a target, a forecast, to break even, the bottom line. Structure: the past simple, regular and irregular.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Revenue -- all the money a company receives from its sales, before any cost
"Revenue grew eleven percent in the second quarter."
A cost -- the money a company spends to make or to sell something
"The cost of packaging rose again in April."
A profit -- what is left when you take the costs off the revenue
"We made a small profit, and nobody expected it."
A margin -- the part of the price that is profit, written as a percentage
"Our margin fell two points, and the reason was the dollar."
Growth -- the increase in a number, compared with the same period before
"Volume growth came from two regions only."
A target -- the number a company decides it wants to reach in a period
"We missed the target by two million."
A forecast -- the number you expect for a period that has not happened yet
"The forecast for the third quarter was too optimistic."
To break even -- to earn exactly what you spend: no profit and no loss
"The new line broke even in nine months."
The bottom line -- the final profit, on the last line of the report
"Sales looked great, and the bottom line did not move."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The second quarter closed on the thirtieth of June. On the first page it looked like the best three months in three years. Revenue grew eleven percent and volume grew eight. The new line, which the company launched in January, broke even in month five.

The second page told a slower story. The company set a target of forty-three million and did not reach it. The cost of packaging increased fourteen percent in April, and nobody changed the price list. The division absorbed all of it.

So the margin fell two points, and the bottom line did not move at all. The company sold more products to more customers and finished the quarter with the same profit it started with. The forecast for the third quarter is a different conversation.

1. "Revenue grew eleven percent." Why is it grew and not growed?
A Because the subject is singular.
B Because grow is an irregular verb: its past is a different word, not verb plus -ed.
C Because the sentence is a question.
2. "The company did not reach the target." Why is it reach and not reached?
A Because did already carries the past. After did, the verb goes back to its naked form.
B Because reach is an irregular verb.
C Because the sentence is negative, and negatives never use -ed anywhere.
3. The whole text is in the past. What tells you the period is finished?
A Nothing. The quarter could still be open.
B The word forecast, at the end.
C The closed time reference: the quarter closed on the thirtieth of June, and April is a month inside it.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past Simple

GRAMMAR

One tense for everything that is finished, measured and closed. It is the tense every results report is written in.

UseFormExample

Irregular verba different word, learned one by onegrow → grew · fall → fell · break → broke · sell → sold
Negativedid not + naked verbIt did not move. We did not reach it.
QuestionDid + subject + naked verb?Did revenue grow in the second quarter?
Time wordsa finished periodlast quarter, in April, two years ago, in 2025
Short answerYes, it did. / No, it did not.Did you reach the target? No, we did not.

Never: Revenue growed eleven percent · We did not reached the target · Did the line broke even? · The margin is fell · We have missed the target last quarter.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Two people are looking at the same page. Claire runs the category for an American distributor. She says consumption per person is flat, and asks Roberto what he expects. He answers with an opinion, and he marks it as one: "I think" the category "will grow" slowly, and the premium segment "will grow" fast.

1. "I think the category will grow slowly." Why will and not going to?

B Because will is always used with the word category.
C Because going to is only used for the past.
2. "We are going to enter the premium segment next year." What does that form tell the listener?
A That he hopes it happens.
B That the decision is already taken. The board approved it in March.
C That it is less certain than will.
3. "Demand is going to grow faster than one company can serve." Why not will here?
A Because demand is a plural word.
B Because will cannot be used with demand.
C Because he is pointing at evidence he can see right now, not giving an opinion.

From the Plant to the Port -- The Chain Behind One Export Order

Forty pallets have to leave a factory in Brazil and arrive in a warehouse in Ohio. Key words: a shipment, a container, customs, a pallet, a delay, a warehouse, a freight forwarder, to clear customs. Structure: the passive voice -- what was done, not who did it.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A shipment -- a quantity of goods sent together, at one time, to one place
"The shipment left the plant on a Tuesday."
A container -- the large metal box that goods travel in, on a ship
"Two containers were loaded in Santos on Friday."
Customs -- the government office that checks goods entering a country
"Customs opened one pallet and closed it again."
A pallet -- the wooden base that boxes are stacked on, so a machine can lift them
"Forty pallets were prepared, and thirty-eight were shipped."
A delay -- the time between when something should arrive and when it does
"The delay was nine days, and six of them were at the border."

"The goods were delivered to the wrong warehouse."
A freight forwarder -- a company that organizes the transport of your goods for you
"Our freight forwarder was changed in March."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Forty pallets were prepared at the plant on the third of May. Two containers were loaded in Santos on the fifth, and the ship left on schedule. Eighteen days later the shipment arrived at the port, exactly on the promised date.

Then the goods were held at customs for six days. The reason is printed on the release form: the product code was missing on the invoice. Nothing is cleared at that border without a code on every line. The container was not opened, nothing was inspected, and no product was rejected. A document was incomplete, and the shipment waited.

The customer received the goods nine days late. Two more days were lost after the release, because a truck was only assigned two days later, and the first delivery was made to the wrong warehouse. The transport is organized by our freight forwarder, and the invoice template was changed in March, when that forwarder was changed too. The template was corrected last week, and every invoice is checked against the order before a container is loaded.

1. "The goods were held at customs for six days." Who held them?
A The freight forwarder held them.
B The sentence does not say. That is the point of this structure: what was done matters, who did it does not.
C The customer held them.
2. "The transport is organized by our freight forwarder." What is the word by doing here?
A Putting the person back into the sentence, because here it matters who does it.
B Showing where the transport happens.
C Showing when the transport happens.
3. "Nothing is cleared without a code" and "the goods were held". Why is one is and the other were?
A Because nothing is singular and goods is plural, and that is the only reason.
B Because one is a question and the other is not.
C Because the first is a permanent rule, now, and the second is one finished event in the past.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Passive Voice

GRAMMAR

The structure every logistics report is written in. It says what was done and leaves out who did it, on purpose.

UseFormExample
Now, or alwaysam / is / are + past participleNothing is shipped without the invoice.
Finished, in the pastwas / were + past participleThe shipment was held at customs.
Naming who did it+ by + person or companyThe transport is organized by our forwarder.
Negativewas not / is not + participleTwo pallets were not loaded.
QuestionWas / Were + subject + participle?Was the order delivered on time?
Useful participlesirregular, learned one by onehold → held · send → sent · make → made · lose → lost

Never: The shipment was hold · The goods delivered late · Two containers was loaded · The order was delivered on time? (for a question, put Was first) · It was happened.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A visitor stops at the booth and says she has two minutes. Most exhibitors would start presenting. Roberto does not. He asks: "Where are you based, and what do you buy?" She is a distributor in Toronto, selling on to four hundred stores.

Then the question that matters: "Who do you buy this category from today?" Two suppliers in Europe, and their prices rose twice last year. She asks one back: "Did you exhibit here last year?" No, they did not; this is their first year.

He offers a sample and the catalog, and asks the last one: "Who handles distribution decisions on your side?" Note the shape of that question — there is no do in it, because who is already the subject. She answers "I do", up to a volume, and above that her director decides. Then she gives him her badge and one instruction: follow up on Monday. She is now a lead, and ninety seconds produced more than a brochure ever could.

1. "Where are you based?" Why is are before you?
A Because the sentence is in the past.
B Because in a question the helper jumps in front of the subject, and with be the verb itself is the helper.
C Because where always comes with are.
2. "Who handles distribution on your side?" Why is there no do in this question?
A Because who is the subject of the sentence, so there is nothing to jump in front of.
B Because questions with who never take a helper, in any case.
C Because handles already ends in -s.

The Launch That Worked -- A Campaign, Told From the Inside

Two products left the factory in the same month and only one is still on the shelf. Key words: a launch, a campaign, a brand, a target audience, awareness, shelf space, to roll out, packaging. Structure: past continuous with past simple -- the background and the cut.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In March the team was preparing a launch for the fifteenth of May. The target audience was families, the campaign was ready, and they were testing two packaging designs in two cities. While they were testing, the competitor announced almost the same product.

They moved the date forward by six weeks. The packaging test was still running when they chose design B, so nobody ever saw the result. The campaign then ran in nine states, and the stock was arriving in two.

People were walking into stores and asking for the product by name, and the shelf was empty. Awareness doubled in eight weeks. Sales in the first month came in forty percent under plan. The premium line, meanwhile, rolled out in two states only, and while everybody was watching the big brand, it quietly won shelf space in a third state.

1. "While they were testing, the competitor announced." Which action started first?
A The testing. It was already in progress when the announcement cut into it.
B The announcement. It always comes first with while.
C They started at exactly the same moment.
2. "The campaign ran in nine states, and the stock was arriving in two." Why is one form -ing and the other not?
A Because stock is a plural word and campaign is singular.
B Because the stock was still in progress, arriving step by step, while the campaign is treated as one finished fact.
C Because the stock line is a question.
3. "Awareness doubled and sales came in under plan." Why are both in the past simple?
A Because they happened at the same time.
B Because they are two finished results, not two actions in progress. Nothing is being interrupted here.
C Because numbers always take the past simple.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous With Past Simple

GRAMMAR

Two layers in one sentence: what was already running, and what cut through it. It is how every business story is told.

UseFormExample
The background, in progresswas / were + verb-ingWe were preparing the launch.
The event that cuts inpast simpleThe competitor announced the same product.
while+ the long actionWhile we were preparing, they announced.
when+ the short actionThe campaign was running when the shelves went empty.
Negative and questionwas not / were not + -ing · Were you...?Nobody was watching the price. Were you testing both?
Two finished resultspast simple in both halvesAwareness doubled and sales came in under plan.

Never: We was preparing · While we prepared the launch · When the shelves were going empty (for a sudden event) · Nobody watching the price · They was testing.
