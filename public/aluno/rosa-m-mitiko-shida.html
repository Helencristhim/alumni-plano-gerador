Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

Two tenses carry your whole professional description. One says who you are; the other says what is moving around you this year.

FormWhat it saysExample
Present simple
work · manage · report		Your role, your routine and what is generally true: every week, usually, twice a day, never."I work for an American airline."
"I manage thirty corporate accounts."
Present continuous
am rolling out · am preparing		In progress now, temporary and still changing: at the moment, this year, this quarter, still."We are rolling out a new structure."
"At the moment I am preparing the numbers."
Negative and questionThe simple borrows do / does; the continuous already has am / is / are and needs nothing else."I do not handle private travelers."
"Are you joining the call on Thursday?"
Third person
he · she · it		In the present simple the third person adds -s. It disappears the moment you use the continuous."She manages the Santiago office."
"She is managing two teams this quarter."
The trap: state verbsbe, have, know, belong, understand, mean describe a state, not an action. They stay simple even when you mean right now."I know most of these clients."
NOT "I am knowing most of these clients."

A quick test that never fails: if you can put every week in front of the sentence and it still makes sense, use the simple. If only right now or this year fits, use the continuous.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Present Perfect

GRAMMAR

The tense that connects what you did to who you are today. In Portuguese the same idea often takes the present. In English it never does.

FormWhat it saysExample
have / has + third form
worked · sent · met · taken		The past, connected to now. You do not say when it happened, because that is not the point."I have worked here for twelve years."
"She has taken over the Chile accounts."
for · sincefor + how long the period is. since + the point where it started. Both mean it is still true today."for twenty years" · "for two months"
"since 2014" · "since March"
just · already · yetjust = a moment ago. already = sooner than expected. yet = still missing, and it goes at the end of the sentence."They have just announced it."
"I have already sent it."
"I have not decided yet."
Negative and questionThe auxiliary is already in the sentence. For a question you move it to the front; for a negative you add not to it."I have not met them yet."
"Have you signed the contract?"
The trapPortuguese keeps the present with desde and há. English never does. And after have comes the third form, not the past: take → took → taken.NOT "I work here since 2014."
NOT "She has took over the accounts."

One question decides everything: is the period closed, or still open today? If it is still open, use have or has plus the third form.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Simple or Present Perfect?

GRAMMAR

Both talk about the past. Only one of them says when. That single difference is what your manager hears when he reads your update.

FormWhat it saysExample
Past simple
signed · sent · closed		The moment is closed, and you say it or everybody knows it: yesterday, last March, on Tuesday, in 2019, two weeks ago."We signed the renewal on Tuesday."
"They postponed it two weeks ago."
Present perfect
have sent · has not confirmed		The period is still open, or the moment does not matter: this year, this quarter, so far, already, yet, ever, never."I have already sent the draft."
"They have not confirmed it yet."
The pair that goes togetherOpen with the present perfect, then give the detail in the past simple. This is how a good status update is built."We have lost two clients this year. One left in March and one left in June."
Questions about a whole careerFor experience with no date, English asks have you ever. Did you ever sounds like you are asking about a period that is over."Have you ever attended the convention?"
NOT "Did you ever attend the convention?"
The trapA closed time expression and have can never share a sentence. If yesterday is in the sentence, have cannot be.NOT "I have sent it yesterday."
NOT "Last quarter we have launched two routes."

One test decides almost every case: does the sentence say when? If it does, use the past simple. If it does not, and the period is still running, use the present perfect.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

She has never enjoyed this part. The session ends, ninety people stand up, and there are twenty minutes of small talk before the next one. In Portuguese she does this without thinking. In English she takes a coffee, looks at her phone, and waits for somebody she already knows to walk past.

This year she decides to try what the column suggested. She looks for a name badge she does not recognize, and she uses the colour as an icebreaker: green means operations, and she has never worked with that team. That is not a weakness. It is a question she genuinely wants to ask.

The conversation is easier than she expected. She asks whether he has ever coordinated a route launch, and he talks for four minutes. She says she has never handled the operations side and that she has always wanted to understand it. He asks about her client base, and she answers with a number and two names. Somewhere in the middle they discover that they both represent the company at the same event in March.

Then the part that costs nothing and that almost nobody does. Before she walks away, she says what happens next. She has not met his director yet, and he offers to introduce her. She asks him to send the launch timeline, and he asks for her numbers. Key expressions: minutes, action item, to recap, agenda, takeaway, to align, off the record, to circle back, chair, attendee, to touch base, word for word. Structure: he said and she told me, with the tense one step back in time.

Minutes -- the written record of what was said and decided in a meeting
"The minutes said the second phase had been approved on Tuesday."
Action item -- a task given to a named person in a meeting
"There were four action items and three of them were mine."
To recap -- to summarize the main points again, briefly
"Let me recap the three things he announced."
Agenda -- the list of subjects for a meeting
"The expansion was not on the agenda, and he raised it anyway."
Takeaway -- the main point somebody should remember afterwards
"The takeaway was that the dates had moved, not the budget."
To align -- to make sure everybody understands and agrees on the same thing
"We need ten minutes to align before we talk to the client."
Off the record -- said in confidence, not to be repeated officially
"He told me off the record that the date would move."
To circle back -- to return to a subject later
"He said he would circle back on the format next week."
Chair -- the person who runs a meeting
"The chair told us the agenda had changed that morning."
Attendee -- a person who is present at a meeting
"There were eleven attendees and only four of them spoke."
To touch base -- to make brief contact with somebody to check something
"I will touch base with the Santiago team before Friday."
Word for word -- repeating exactly what somebody said, with no change
"I am giving you his sentence word for word, not my reading of it."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The most useful skill in a large company is not speaking well in the meeting. It is what happens in the twenty minutes afterwards, when somebody who was not an attendee calls you and asks what was decided.

Rosa missed the regional call, so a colleague gave her the three announcements. He said the second phase had been approved on Tuesday. He said the Lima office would open in June and not in March, which makes every date in her plan wrong. And he told her the director wanted one page from each account manager before the convention.

She asked the question that separates a professional from a rumour: did he say what should be on the page, or is that your reading of it? He had not said. When asked afterwards, the director told him that if he specified the format everybody would write the same thing. That sentence, repeated word for word, was more useful than the whole recap.

There are two rules here and they protect a reputation. First, separate what was said from what you concluded: she said the office would open in June is a fact, and this probably delays our targets is your opinion, which must be labelled as yours. Second, if you were not in the room, say so. Passing on half a sentence you heard off the record is the fastest way to become the person nobody tells anything to.

1. Why does the text say "he said the second phase had been approved" and not "has been"?
A Because the approval was cancelled.
B Because in reported speech the tense moves one step back.
C Because the approval happened twice.
2. What is the difference between said and told in this text?
A Told is followed by a person; said is not.
B Said is more formal than told.
C There is no difference.
3. What question does the text call the one that separates a professional from a rumour?
A When exactly did he say it?
B Did he say that, or is that your reading of it?
C Who else was in the room?
4. What does the text say you must do if you were not in the room?
A Say nothing at all.
B Repeat it anyway, since somebody has to.
C Say that you were not there and that you are repeating what you were told.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech -- Statements

GRAMMAR

How English moves a sentence from the room where it was said to the room where you are. The tense takes one step back, and the reporting verb decides whether a person can follow it.

FormWhat it saysExample
present simple → past simpleThe step back is grammatical, not factual. She has not stopped managing the accounts."I manage…" → "She said she managed…"
present perfect · past simple → past perfectBoth collapse into the same form."It has been approved." → "He said it had been approved."
will · can · may → would · could · mightThe modals each move one step back too."It will open." → "She said it would open."
say vs telltell needs a person straight after it; say never takes one. This is where the Portuguese me disse causes the most damage."He told me that…"
"He said that…"
NOT "He said me…"
time and placenow → then · today → that day · tomorrow → the next day · here → there"I will send it tomorrow." → "She said she would send it the next day."

That is optional and leaving it out sounds more natural in speech. And the professional rule matters as much as the grammar: separate what was said from what you concluded, and say where the information came from when you were not in the room.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

A colleague missed the call and a client has heard a rumour. Choose the best thing to say.

1. You want to report what the director announced. You say:
A He said me that the second phase had been approved.
B He told me that the second phase had been approved.
C He told that the second phase had been approved.
2. The original sentence was "the office will open in June". You report:
A She said the office will open in June.
B She said the office would open in June.
C She said the office opens in June.
3. The original was "I can attend the convention". You report:
A He said he can attend the convention.
B He told he could attend the convention.
C He said he could attend the convention.
4. You want to make clear that the conclusion is yours and not the director's. You say:
A That is my reading of it, not his words.
B He said that our targets will move.
C Everybody knows the targets are moving.
5. A client asks about something you heard second hand. The most professional answer is:
A The director said the dates have moved, definitely.
B I was not on the call, so I am repeating what I was told.
C I cannot talk about that.

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer, and nobody is scoring it.

Take a real meeting from your own week and record the debrief, in about ninety seconds, for a colleague who was not there. Report three things that were said, using said and told correctly and moving each tense one step back. Then say one thing that was not said, and finish with one conclusion of your own, clearly labelled as yours.
