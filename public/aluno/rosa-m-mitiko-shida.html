Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Who Is Rosa? -- The First Regional Call

Saying who you are and what you do, on the first call with the new South America team. Key expressions: account manager, client portfolio, to be in charge of, to report to, headquarters, commercial area, corporate client, route, to roll out, regional office, workload, to keep up with. Structure: present simple for your role and your routine, present continuous for what is changing this year.

Vocabulary

Account manager -- the person responsible for a group of clients and everything the company does with them
"I am the account manager for the corporate clients in this region."
Client portfolio -- the whole group of clients that one person is responsible for
"My client portfolio covers about thirty companies."
To be in charge of -- to have the responsibility for a task, a team or an area
"I am in charge of the corporate accounts in this region."
To report to -- to have somebody as your direct manager
"From September, four countries report to one regional director."
Headquarters -- the main office of a company, where the top leadership sits
"That decision came from headquarters, not from the local team."
Commercial area -- the part of a company that sells and promotes what it offers
"I work in the commercial area, which is sales and marketing together."
Corporate client -- a company that buys from you, not a private traveler
"Every corporate client has a contract and a negotiated price."
Route -- the connection an airline flies between two cities on a fixed schedule
"That route runs twice a day, morning and evening."

"This year we are rolling out the new regional structure."
Regional office -- one office that covers several countries instead of only one
"The regional office covers Brazil, Argentina, Chile and Colombia."
Workload -- the amount of work a person has to do in a given period
"My workload doubles in the weeks before a convention."
To keep up with -- to stay at the same speed as something that keeps moving

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

She works for an American airline, in the commercial area — which means sales and marketing together, not one or the other. Her title is account manager, and she is in charge of the corporate clients in her region: companies with a contract, not families booking a holiday.

Her client portfolio covers about thirty companies. She visits two or three of them every week, and she spends one week in the city and one week on the road. In the weeks before a convention her workload doubles. That is a normal year, and it has looked like that for a long time.

This year is not a normal year. The company is rolling out a new structure across four countries, and from September the commercial teams report to one regional office instead of four separate ones. Nobody is moving accounts, but everybody is learning new names. At the moment she is preparing a one-minute introduction for a call with eleven colleagues she has never met.

The decision came from headquarters, and the working language of the new office is English. She reads it every day without difficulty. What she is practising now is something else: finding the word while somebody waits, on a call where the speed does not slow down and she has to keep up with it.

1. Why does the text say "she visits two or three of them every week" and not "she is visiting"?
A Because it is a routine — something that happens again and again.
B Because it is happening at this exact moment.
C Because it happened once, in the past.
2. "The company is rolling out a new structure." What does the present continuous tell us here?
A That it happens every year at the same time.
B That it is in progress and not finished yet.
C That it was finished before September.
3. The text says her workload doubles before a convention. What does that mean?
A That the work becomes more difficult.
B That the amount of work she has becomes twice as big.
C That she works with a second team.
4. What changes from September, according to the text?
A The clients move to a different account manager.
B The commercial area becomes a marketing team.
C Four separate teams start reporting to one regional office.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

Two tenses carry your whole professional description. One says who you are; the other says what is moving around you this year.

FormWhat it saysExample
Present simple
work · manage · report		Your role, your routine and what is generally true: every week, usually, twice a day, never."I work for an American airline."
"I manage thirty corporate accounts."
Present continuous
am rolling out · am preparing		In progress now, temporary and still changing: at the moment, this year, this quarter, still."We are rolling out a new structure."
"At the moment I am preparing the numbers."
Negative and questionThe simple borrows do / does; the continuous already has am / is / are and needs nothing else."I do not handle private travelers."
"Are you joining the call on Thursday?"
Third person
he · she · it		In the present simple the third person adds -s. It disappears the moment you use the continuous."She manages the Santiago office."
"She is managing two teams this quarter."
The trap: state verbsbe, have, know, belong, understand, mean describe a state, not an action. They stay simple even when you mean right now."I know most of these clients."
NOT "I am knowing most of these clients."

A quick test that never fails: if you can put every week in front of the sentence and it still makes sense, use the simple. If only right now or this year fits, use the continuous.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are on the first regional call with colleagues from four countries. Choose the best thing to say.

The director asks you to introduce yourself. The best opening is:
A "I am working in the commercial area since many years."
B "I am Rosa, and I am in charge of the corporate accounts in this region."
C "I am the responsible of the corporate accounts."
A colleague asks what your week normally looks like. You answer:
A "I usually spend one week in the city and one week on the road."
B "I am usually spending one week in the city and one week on the road."
C "Usually I spending one week in the city."
You want to say that the new structure is not finished yet. You say:
A "We roll out the new structure at the moment."
B "We are still rolling out the new structure."
C "We are still roll out the new structure."
Somebody speaks very fast and you lose the last sentence. The most professional thing to say is:
A "Yes, yes." (and you write nothing down)
B "Sorry, could you say that again, a little more slowly?"
C "I am not understanding nothing."
A colleague asks whether the accounts are moving to the regional office. The clearest answer is:
A "No. Nobody is moving accounts this quarter — only the reporting line changes."
B "I think maybe, I am not sure, it depends." (and you stop there)
C "The accounts are not moving since this quarter."

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Experience Counts -- Twenty Years in Two Sentences

Saying what you have done, what the company has changed, and what has not happened yet. Key expressions: to expand, to announce, to attend, to be based in, to launch, colleague, deadline, to take over, track record, milestone, so far, to catch up on. Structure: the present perfect -- have or has plus the third form, with for, since, just, already and yet.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it. These twelve stay here in your material -- they do not disappear after the class.

To expand -- to make a business bigger, into new places or new markets
"The company has expanded into three more countries this year."
To announce -- to tell people officially about a decision or a plan
"They have just announced the dates of the convention."
To attend -- to go to a meeting, a training session or an event
"I have attended that convention every year since 2019."
To be based in -- to have your main office in a particular city or country
"The new regional director is based in Bogota."
To launch -- to start something new, such as a route, a product or a campaign
"We have launched two new routes inside South America."
Colleague -- a person you work with
"A colleague from the Santiago office has already called me."
Deadline -- the day or the hour by which something has to be finished
"The deadline for the proposal is Friday at noon."
To take over -- to start doing a job or a responsibility that somebody else had
"She has taken over the Chile accounts since March."
Track record -- everything a person or a company has done so far, seen as proof
"Our track record with this client is the reason they stayed."
Milestone -- an important point in a project or a career that you can look back on
"One hundred corporate contracts is a real milestone."
So far -- up to now, with more still to come
"We have signed eleven new contracts so far this year."
To catch up on -- to do something you did not have time to do earlier
"On Friday mornings I catch up on my email."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

She has worked in travel and aviation for more than twenty years, and she has been with this company since 2014. She did not plan any of it. She studied business administration, went into tourism because a friend called her about a job, and never left the industry again.

A lot has changed in those twenty years. Airlines have merged and disappeared. The corporate contract has moved from paper to a system that is renegotiated every quarter. And this year the company has announced the biggest change of all: four countries, one regional office.

Her own year has been full. She has attended two colleague training sessions in English, she has signed eleven new contracts so far, and last month she has just passed a milestone she never expected: one hundred active corporate accounts. Her track record is the reason two of her biggest clients stayed when the prices went up.

Some things are still open. She has not met the Bogota team face to face yet, and she has not decided which client she is going to talk about at the convention. The deadline for that answer is Friday. On Friday morning, before she does anything else, she is going to catch up on her email and reply.

1. Why does the text say "she has worked in aviation for more than twenty years" and not "she works"?
A Because the period started in the past and is still open today.
B Because she does not work in aviation any more.
C Because it is a routine that happens every week.
2. What is the difference between "for more than twenty years" and "since 2014"?
A There is none — the two words mean the same.
B "For" gives the length of the period; "since" gives the point where it started.
C "For" is used in questions and "since" in answers.
3. "She has not met the Bogota team yet." What does yet add?
A That it will never happen.
B That it has not happened up to now, and it is still expected.
C That it happened a long time ago.
4. The text says her track record is the reason two clients stayed. What is a track record?
A A promise about what she is going to do next year.
B The list of companies in her portfolio.
C Everything she has already done, seen as proof of what she can do.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Present Perfect

GRAMMAR

The tense that connects what you did to who you are today. In Portuguese the same idea often takes the present. In English it never does.

FormWhat it saysExample
have / has + third form
worked · sent · met · taken		The past, connected to now. You do not say when it happened, because that is not the point."I have worked here for twelve years."
"She has taken over the Chile accounts."
for · sincefor + how long the period is. since + the point where it started. Both mean it is still true today."for twenty years" · "for two months"
"since 2014" · "since March"
just · already · yetjust = a moment ago. already = sooner than expected. yet = still missing, and it goes at the end of the sentence."They have just announced it."
"I have already sent it."
"I have not decided yet."
Negative and questionThe auxiliary is already in the sentence. For a question you move it to the front; for a negative you add not to it."I have not met them yet."
"Have you signed the contract?"
The trapPortuguese keeps the present with desde and há. English never does. And after have comes the third form, not the past: take → took → taken.NOT "I work here since 2014."
NOT "She has took over the accounts."

One question decides everything: is the period closed, or still open today? If it is still open, use have or has plus the third form.
