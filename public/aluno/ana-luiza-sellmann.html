Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Always True or True Right Now

GRAMMAR

One question, two answers. That is the whole decision in this lesson.

FormUseExample
Present simple
I work / she works		Always true: facts about you, routines, things that do not change this week.I live in Sao Paulo.
She works from home.
Present continuous
I am working		True right now, or temporary around now: today, this week, this month, these days.This month I am working on a festival.
My mother is living in Shanghai.
NegativeThe simple uses don't / doesn't. The continuous uses am not / isn't / aren't.I don't commute.
I am not working today.
QuestionDo / Does for the simple, Am / Is / Are for the continuous.Do you work from home?
Are you working on something new?
Signal wordsalways, usually, every day, never point to the simple. Right now, today, this week, at the moment, these days point to the continuous. The sentence usually tells you before you have to decide.
The short listThese verbs stay simple even when you mean now: know, want, like, love, need, mean, understand. Say "I want a coffee", never "I am wanting a coffee".

When you are not sure, choose the simple and keep talking. A finished sentence with the wrong tense is worth far more than a perfect tense you never say out loud.

1. Why does the text say "they have lived in Shanghai since 2024" and not "they lived"?
A Because they are still there now: the situation started in 2024 and has not finished.

2. The text says "In January I went to New York" and not "I have gone". Why?
A Because the trip was short, and short trips take the past simple.
B Because the sentence names a finished time, January, and a closed date takes the past simple.
C Because you never use have or has with the verb to go.
3. What does "I have not visited my own family yet" tell you?
A That she visited them a long time ago and does not want to go back.
B That the visit is impossible now.
C That the visit has not happened up to now, and it is still expected.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Does the Date Matter?

GRAMMAR

One question, two answers. That is the whole decision in this lesson.

FormUseExample
Present perfect
have / has + participle		The past when the day does not matter, or when the situation is still true now.I have worked on festivals.
She has lived here since 2024.
Past simple
worked / went / saw		A finished moment, when the sentence names the time or the time is understood.I went to New York in January.
We launched it in March.
Negativehave not and has not for the perfect; did not for the past simple.I have not been to Asia.
I did not go last year.
QuestionHave or Has plus the participle for experience; Did plus the base verb for a finished moment.Have you ever been to Brazil?
Did you go in January?
The six little wordsever and never for a whole life, just for a minute ago, already for sooner than expected, yet for still missing, for and since for how long. All of them live with have and has.
Participles worth knowinggo becomes been or gone, see becomes seen, do becomes done, write becomes written, eat becomes eaten, take becomes taken. Regular verbs simply add -ed.

If you are not sure, ask yourself one thing: does the sentence say when? If it does, use the past simple. If it does not, you do not have to invent a date — that is exactly what this form is for.

A Because painting is a hobby, and hobbies never take been plus -ing.

3. Why is it "I have known him since university" and never "I have been knowing him"?
A Because university was a long time ago.
B Because they have not spoken for ages.
C Because know is on the short list of verbs that never take the -ing form here.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Is It Over, or Still Going?

GRAMMAR

One question, two answers. That is the whole decision in this lesson.

FormUseExample
Present perfect continuous
have / has been + -ing		An action that started in the past and is still running now. The time and the effort are the point.I have been working here since March.
We have been waiting all week.
Present perfect simple
have / has + participle		A finished result you can count or show.I have written three emails.
We have finished the deck.
Negativehave not been or has not been plus the -ing form.I have not been sleeping well.
She hasn't been answering.
QuestionThe most natural way to ask about somebody's life right now.What have you been up to?
How long have you been living here?
for and sincefor plus a length of time: for two years, for ages, for a week. Since plus a starting point: since March, since Tuesday, since university. Never "since two years".
The short listThese verbs stay simple even when the situation continues: know, be, have, like, love, want, understand. Say "I have known him for years", never "I have been knowing".

If you are not sure, ask one thing: has it stopped? If it has not, add been and -ing. And if you pick the other one by mistake, the person still understands you perfectly — this is a choice about naturalness, not about being right.

Making plans that might change, promising things with a condition and warning people in advance. Key words: a heads-up, to sort out, to be up for, otherwise, to make it, a backup plan, to approve, a budget. Structure: the first conditional (if plus the present simple, will plus the base verb), with might, can, should and unless.

A heads-up -- a warning you give before something happens

A budget -- the money available for a project

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Condition and the Consequence

GRAMMAR

One shape, two halves. That is the whole lesson.

HalfFormExample
The conditionif + present simple. Never will, even though the sentence is about the future.If it rains, ...
If the client approves, ...
The consequencewill + base verb. In speech, almost always the contraction: I'll, we'll, they'll...., we'll move to my place.
..., we'll start on Monday.
Either order"If you send the brief, I'll review it today." = "I'll review the brief today if you send it." With if first, use a comma. With if second, no comma.
NegativeEither half can be negative: "If you don't answer, I won't book the table."
Softer than willmight = maybe, can = it is possible, should = advice. "If they change the brief, we can adjust the plan."
unless and otherwiseUnless means "if not": "Unless it rains, we meet at the park." Otherwise starts the consequence of not doing it: "Text me, otherwise I'll book for six."

There is only one thing to remember here: will never lives in the if half. Everything else in this structure works the way you already expect.

"Present Simple and Present Continuous" -- BBC Learning English, 6 Minute Grammar

Six calm minutes on the only decision you had to make in this lesson: is it always true, or is it true right now? Connection to Lesson 1: this is the grammar behind "I work in advertising" and "this month I'm working on a festival".

Tip: after each example, pause and say two sentences about yourself out loud — one always true, one true only this week. Two sentences, then stop. Six minutes total.

Series
"Mystic Mountain" -- Bob Ross, The Joy of Painting (full episode)

Half an hour of a man painting a mountain and describing every single thing he does while he does it. For a beginner painter this is a class, and for a listener it is the clearest present continuous you will ever hear: "I'm just going to take a little bit of white and pull it across here." Connection to Lesson 1: the whole episode is the grammar of right now.

Tip: watch with no subtitles and count how many times he says "I'm going to" or "I'm just" plus a verb. Then paint something small yourself and describe three steps out loud in English while you do it.

Lesson 2 — Experience Without a Date — The Present Perfect

Grammar Video
"Present perfect and past simple" -- BBC Learning English, 6 Minute Grammar

Six minutes on the exact decision you practiced today: the date is in the sentence, or the date does not matter. Connection to Lesson 2: this is the difference between "I went to New York in January" and "I have been to New York twice".

Tip: after each example, pause and say two sentences about your own life out loud — one with a date, one without. Two sentences, then stop.

Watch on YouTube ↗
Podcast
"Airbnb's Joe Gebbia at the How I Built This Summit" -- NPR, with Guy Raz

A live conversation where a founder is asked, for almost an hour, one version of the same question: what have you built, and how. Slow American English, a real audience, and a speaker who tells stories instead of listing facts. Connection to Lesson 2: it is a whole hour of somebody framing a career with have and has, and then dropping into the past simple for each specific story.

Tip: listen for the moment he switches from "we have always been" to "then in 2008 we decided". Write down two examples of each and copy the pattern for your own career.

Lesson 3 — What Have You Been Up To? — The Story Still Running

Grammar Video
"How to use the present perfect continuous tense" -- BBC Learning English, 6 Minute Grammar

Six minutes on the exact form you practiced today: have been plus the -ing form, for things that started in the past and have not stopped. Connection to Lesson 3: this is what makes "I have been working on it since March" sound like a native speaker instead of a translation.

Tip: after each example, pause and say one true sentence about your own week with for or since. One sentence, then play again.

Watch on YouTube ↗
Podcast
"Making a Mark: Visual Identity with Tom Geismar" -- 99% Invisible

A designer who has been drawing logos for sixty years talks about the symbols you see every day without noticing them. Calm American English, one idea at a time, and a full transcript on the page if you want to read along. Connection to Lesson 3: a whole conversation about a career that has not stopped, told exactly the way you practiced today.

Tip: listen once with the transcript closed. Then open it and find three sentences with have been or has been. Read those three out loud.

Talk
"Your elusive creative genius" -- Elizabeth Gilbert, TED (19 minutes)

A writer talks about the pressure of having to produce good ideas on demand, and about what she has been telling herself for years to survive it. Clear, slow and funny. Connection to Lesson 3: it is about exhausting creative work that has been worth it, and it is the best possible listening for somebody who freezes because she wants everything to come out perfect.

Lesson 4 — If It Rains, We Move — Plans, Promises and Backup Plans

Grammar Video
"The first conditional" -- BBC Learning English, 6 Minute Grammar

Six minutes on the shape you practiced today: if plus the present, will plus the verb. It also covers the mistake that costs everybody the most, which is putting will inside the if half. Connection to Lesson 4: this is the sentence behind "If it rains, we'll move to my place".

Tip: pause after each example and finish the sentence yourself with something from your own Saturday. One sentence, then play again.

"Questlove" -- Song Exploder

Twenty minutes of one musician taking a single song apart and explaining every decision: what he tried, what he dropped, what would have happened if he had kept it. Short, calm, and the audio itself is half the story. Connection to Lesson 4: it is a whole episode of creative decisions with consequences, which is exactly the conversation you practiced with the budget.

Tip: listen once for the music and once for the language. On the second listen, write down two decisions he made and turn each one into your own conditional sentence.

"A powerful way to unleash your natural creativity" -- Tim Harford, TED (14 minutes)

An economist explains why the best creative work often comes from the plan that went wrong, using a famous studio session where the piano was broken. Clear British English, one story at a time. Connection to Lesson 4: the whole talk is about backup plans, and about what happens if the first plan fails.

Tip: watch with English subtitles. Then answer out loud in one minute: what will you do next time a plan of yours falls apart? Start the answer with "If".
