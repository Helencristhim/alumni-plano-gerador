Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Always True or True Right Now

GRAMMAR

One question, two answers. That is the whole decision in this lesson.

FormUseExample
Present simple
I work / she works		Always true: facts about you, routines, things that do not change this week.I live in Sao Paulo.
She works from home.
Present continuous
I am working		True right now, or temporary around now: today, this week, this month, these days.This month I am working on a festival.
My mother is living in Shanghai.
NegativeThe simple uses don't / doesn't. The continuous uses am not / isn't / aren't.I don't commute.
I am not working today.
QuestionDo / Does for the simple, Am / Is / Are for the continuous.Do you work from home?
Are you working on something new?
Signal wordsalways, usually, every day, never point to the simple. Right now, today, this week, at the moment, these days point to the continuous. The sentence usually tells you before you have to decide.

When you are not sure, choose the simple and keep talking. A finished sentence with the wrong tense is worth far more than a perfect tense you never say out loud.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Does the Date Matter?

GRAMMAR

One question, two answers. That is the whole decision in this lesson.

FormUseExample
Present perfect
have / has + participle		The past when the day does not matter, or when the situation is still true now.I have worked on festivals.
She has lived here since 2024.
Past simple
worked / went / saw		A finished moment, when the sentence names the time or the time is understood.I went to New York in January.
We launched it in March.
Negativehave not and has not for the perfect; did not for the past simple.I have not been to Asia.
I did not go last year.
QuestionHave or Has plus the participle for experience; Did plus the base verb for a finished moment.Have you ever been to Brazil?
Did you go in January?
The six little wordsever and never for a whole life, just for a minute ago, already for sooner than expected, yet for still missing, for and since for how long. All of them live with have and has.
Participles worth knowinggo becomes been or gone, see becomes seen, do becomes done, write becomes written, eat becomes eaten, take becomes taken. Regular verbs simply add -ed.

If you are not sure, ask yourself one thing: does the sentence say when? If it does, use the past simple. If it does not, you do not have to invent a date — that is exactly what this form is for.

One question, two answers. That is the whole decision in this lesson.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Condition and the Consequence

GRAMMAR

One shape, two halves. That is the whole lesson.

HalfFormExample
The conditionif + present simple. Never will, even though the sentence is about the future.If it rains, ...
If the client approves, ...
The consequencewill + base verb. In speech, almost always the contraction: I'll, we'll, they'll...., we'll move to my place.
..., we'll start on Monday.
Either order"If you send the brief, I'll review it today." = "I'll review the brief today if you send it." With if first, use a comma. With if second, no comma.
NegativeEither half can be negative: "If you don't answer, I won't book the table."
Softer than willmight = maybe, can = it is possible, should = advice. "If they change the brief, we can adjust the plan."
unless and otherwiseUnless means "if not": "Unless it rains, we meet at the park." Otherwise starts the consequence of not doing it: "Text me, otherwise I'll book for six."

There is only one thing to remember here: will never lives in the if half. Everything else in this structure works the way you already expect.
