Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Who Is Joyce, in Sixty Seconds? -- Your Career in Two Tenses

How to introduce yourself to people on a global call who have never met you, and tell twenty years of work without mixing up the tenses. Key words: a track record, a turning point, to oversee, to be responsible for, to report to, hands-on, to move up, a stakeholder, a first impression, my background. Structure: the past simple for the finished parts of a career, and have plus the third form for the parts that are still going on.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Priya is a finance manager in Mumbai, and last month she joined a call with a new director. This is how she introduced herself. “I started my career at an audit firm in 2008. I spent five years there, and it was hard work, but it gave me my background. In 2013 I moved up to a controller role at a bank.” Every verb in that part is in the past simple, because every one of those things is finished and has a date.

Then her story comes to the present, and the verbs change. “I have been with this company since 2019. I have led the tax team for three years, and I report to the regional director in London.” She is still with the company and still leads the team, so she uses have plus the third form, with since for the start and for for the length.

Her last sentence is the one the director remembered: “Right now I am responsible for the monthly close in four countries, and I am quite hands-on.” Finished part, open part, and today. That order made a good first impression in under a minute.

Why does Priya say "I started my career in 2008" and not "I have started my career in 2008"?
A Because 2008 was a difficult year.
B Because the start of her career is finished and has a date.
C Because she does not work in audit anymore.
Why does she say "I have been with this company since 2019"?
A Because she is still with the company today.
B Because the sentence is a question.
C Because she left the company in 2019.
What is the difference between "since 2019" and "for three years"?
A They are the same, and you can use either one.
B "Since" is only for companies.
C "Since" gives the starting point, and "for" gives the length of the time.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Explain It to Zurich -- A Complex System in Four Sentences

How to explain the Brazilian tax reform to people who have never paid a Brazilian tax: impact first, one comparison, no list of acronyms. Key words: the bottom line, to replace, a transition period, to phase out, a burden, to charge, straightforward, the impact, to boil down to, in a nutshell. Structure: the present simple for how a system works, and the small words that give a reason or a result: because, because of, due to, so, which means.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

The bottom line -- the most important point, the one the listener needs to decide
"The bottom line is that our costs go up during the transition."
To replace -- to take the place of something old with something new
"Two new taxes replace five old ones."
A transition period -- the years when the old system and the new system run at the same time
"The transition period runs until 2033."
To phase out -- to remove something slowly, in steps, over a long time
"The old state tax is phased out between 2029 and 2032."
A burden -- a heavy cost or a heavy amount of work that somebody has to carry
"Running two systems at once is a real burden for the tax team."
To charge -- to ask for an amount of money as a price or as a tax
"The new tax is charged where the product is consumed."
Straightforward -- simple and easy to understand, with no surprises
"The idea is straightforward. The transition is not."
The impact -- the effect that a change has on a person, a team or a business
"What is the impact on our prices next year?"
To boil down to -- to have one simple main point after all the details are removed
"The whole reform boils down to one idea: one tax, charged at the end."
In a nutshell -- said in very few words, the short version of something
"In a nutshell, Brazil is moving to a VAT."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Last month Ana, a tax manager in Brazil, had five minutes with her new American boss. He asked the question every foreign manager asks: “What is this reform, and why should I care?” This time Ana did not start with the history. She started with the bottom line. “In a nutshell, Brazil is moving to a VAT. Two new taxes replace five old ones.”

Then she gave him one comparison. “It works like the sales tax you know, with one difference: the tax is charged where the product is consumed, not where it is made.” Every verb here is in the present simple, because she is describing how a system works, not telling a story.

Last, she gave him the impact and the reason. “There is a long transition period, so we run two systems until 2033. Because of that, the team has a heavy burden for a few years, which means I need one more analyst.” Her boss said yes in the same meeting.

Why does Ana use the present simple in "It works like the sales tax you know"?
A Because she is describing how a system works.
B Because the story happened last month.
C Because her boss is American.
Ana says "because of that" and not "because that". Why?
A Because "because" is only used in questions.
B Because "that" here is a thing, and "because of" comes before a thing.
C Because it is more polite.
What does "which means" do in her last sentence?
A It connects a fact to its result: the heavy work leads to the request for an analyst.
B It asks the boss a question.
C It starts a new topic.

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Cut It in Half -- Headline First, Then Stop

How to summarize something long for somebody with no time: the headline first, one reason, what you need, and a voice that goes down at the end so they know you have finished. Key words: a headline, the gist, a key takeaway, concise, to leave out, to get to the point, to ramble, an overview, to wrap up, to sum up. Structure: short sentences with a full stop, and the words that tell the listener where you are.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A headline -- the main point of a message, said first and in one sentence
"The headline is that we closed the month on time."
The gist -- the general meaning of something, without the details
"I did not read the whole report, but I got the gist."
A key takeaway -- the one idea that people should remember after a meeting
"The key takeaway from today is that March is the risky month."
Concise -- short and clear, with no words that are not needed
"Her update was concise: three sentences and a number."
To leave out -- to decide not to include something
"I left out the history and kept the impact."
To get to the point -- to say the important thing without a long introduction
"Please get to the point. We have five minutes."
To ramble -- to talk for a long time without a clear direction
"When I am nervous, I ramble and forget the main point."
An overview -- a short description of the whole situation, without the details
"Can you give us a quick overview of the Brazil numbers?"
To wrap up -- to finish a meeting or a presentation
"Let me wrap up with one number."
To sum up -- to say the most important points again, very briefly, at the end
"To sum up, we are on time and on budget."

Lesson 04 -- Pre-class

When the Faces Go Blank -- Checking, Rephrasing, Clarifying

What to do when your listeners stop reacting: check with a specific question, say it another way instead of faster, and ask for clarification without losing authority. Key words: to rephrase, to clarify, to lose someone, to follow, a blank look, to nod, to go over, skeptical, to check in, confusing. Structure: the small words that go with the verb (explain to, depend on, discuss, join), the ones most speakers of Portuguese get wrong.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

To rephrase -- to say the same idea again with different words
"Let me rephrase that. The cost goes up only in the first year."
To clarify -- to make something easier to understand by explaining it better
"Could you clarify what you mean by the second option?"
To lose someone -- to explain in a way that the listener stops understanding
"I lost them when I started talking about the state tax."
To follow -- to understand an explanation while it is happening
"Sorry, I am not sure I follow. Can you give me an example?"
A blank look -- a face that shows no reaction and no understanding
"When I said split payment, I got a room full of blank looks."
To nod -- to move your head up and down to show that you agree or understand
"Americans nod all the time. Germans often just listen."
To go over -- to explain or check something again, in detail
"Which part should I go over again?"
Skeptical -- not sure that something is true or will work
"The director looked skeptical, so I showed him the number."
To check in -- to ask quickly how people are doing or if they understand
"I check in with the team every ten minutes on long calls."
Confusing -- difficult to understand, because it is not clear
"The old tax system is confusing even for Brazilians."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Marta is a controller in Lisbon who presents to her German headquarters every month. For a long time she made small mistakes she did not hear herself. She said “Let me explain you the numbers” and “It depends of the market”. Nobody corrected her, but her manager told her that her English sounded “almost right”, and almost right is what people remember.

So she made a list of the verbs she used every day and the small word that goes with each one. You explain something to somebody: “Let me explain the numbers to you.” Things depend on other things: “It depends on the market.” You discuss a topic with no small word at all: “Let us discuss the budget.” And you do not arrive in a call, you join it.

The second change was her check question. Instead of “Does that make sense?”, which everybody answers with yes, she started to ask “Which part should I go over again?” The first time she asked it, the German director said: the second slide. It was the first real question he had asked her in a year.

A She spoke too slowly.
B It sounded almost right, because of small mistakes.
C It was too formal.
Which sentence is correct?
A Let me explain you the numbers.
B Let me explain to you about the numbers.
C Let me explain the numbers to you.
Why did she stop asking "Does that make sense?"?
A Because everybody answers yes, and she learns nothing.
B Because it is too long.

Lesson 3 — Cut It in Half

TED Talk
Chris Anderson: “TED’s secret to great public speaking” (8 min)

The head of TED says a good talk carries one idea, not ten, and that the speaker’s job is to build that one idea in the listener’s head. The same rule works for a two-minute update on a call.

Tip: before you watch, write your next update in one sentence. After you watch, check: is that sentence one idea, or three?

Watch on TED ↗
TED Talk
Melissa Marshall: “Talk nerdy to me” (5 min)

A communication teacher who works with engineers explains how experts lose their audience with too much detail, and what to cut. Five minutes, very clear American English.

Tip: count the pieces of advice she gives. Then say each one again in your own words, in one sentence each.

Lesson 4 — When the Faces Go Blank

Talk
Erin Meyer: “How Cultural Differences Affect Business”

The author of The Culture Map on why the same silence, the same face and the same “yes” mean different things in Germany, India, France and the United States. Your five countries, explained.

Tip: while you watch, write the name of each country she mentions next to one colleague of yours. Is the face you see on calls really confusion?
