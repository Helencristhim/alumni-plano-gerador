Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every exchange interview starts with the same kind of question: tell us about yourself. Most candidates answer with a list of schools and dates, and the list sounds the same for everybody. The candidates who stand out tell a story instead, and English has a tense that was made for stories that are not finished yet.

When something started in the past and is still going on, you use have been + -ing: “I have been studying English since I was seven.” It tells the committee that the effort is still alive. When you want to show a result, a number or an achievement, you use have + past participle: “I have read forty novels in English this year.”

Two little words decide the time. Since goes with a starting point, and for goes with a length of time: since 2019, for eleven years. Some verbs, like live, know and be, describe a state, so they usually take the simple form: “My brother has lived in London for three years,” never “has been knowing”.

And there is one border you cannot cross. When the time is finished, like last year or in 2024, English uses the simple past: “I went to Scotland last year.” A candidate who says “I have gone to Scotland last year” is understood, but the committee notices. With a clear story and the right tense, a competitive interview becomes a conversation, and that is what gives you a head start.

1. According to the text, why do some candidates stand out?
A They list more schools and dates.
B They tell a story instead of a list.
C They have a perfect accent.
2. Which form does the text recommend for an activity that is still going on?
A have been + -ing, as in I have been studying.
B the simple present, as in I study since.
C the simple past, as in I studied.
3. Why is “I have gone to Scotland last year” wrong?
A Because Scotland needs the article the.
B Because gone is not a past participle.
C Because last year is a finished time, so English uses the simple past.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Legal English has a favorite structure, and you see it in every article of every treaty: the passive voice. Instead of saying who acts, the text puts the action first. “The treaty was signed in Geneva” tells you what happened and when. Who held the pen is not important, so it is not mentioned.

The passive is built with be + past participle, and the verb be carries the time. “The convention has been ratified by forty states” describes a result that is still true. “The case is being heard this week” describes something in progress. When the person who acts matters, it comes after by, never after from: “It was approved by the congress.”

Modal verbs work the same way. A reservation can be made at the moment of ratification, and the text must be approved before it becomes binding. The most common mistake is to forget be after the modal, as in “a reservation can make”, which means something completely different.

Finally, legal and academic texts often report a general belief without saying who holds it: “It is often said that international law has no teeth.” Once you recognize the passive, a long legal sentence becomes much easier to read, because you stop looking for a subject that was never there.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The day after a seminar, the most common question in any law school is simple: what did the professor say? Answering it well is harder than it looks, because you are not repeating the words. You are reporting them, and English changes the sentence when it moves from somebody’s mouth into your summary.

The first change is the verb. If the professor said “Precedents guide judges,” your report is “She said that precedents guided judges.” The tense moves one step back: is becomes was, have read becomes had read, and will becomes would. When the idea is still true, or when you report it right away, the change is optional.

The second change is who heard it. Say does not take a person directly, so “she said me” is always wrong. Use told: “She told me that the reading was short.” Questions become statements: “Is it binding?” turns into “He asked whether it was binding.”

Finally, a good summary does not use said for everything. A professor who gives reasons argues; one who shows a fact you missed points out; a classmate who admits the other side is right acknowledges it. The verb tells your listener what kind of statement it was, and a short summary with the right verbs gets your point across faster than a long one that trails off.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Legal writing loves long noun phrases, and most of them are held together by relative clauses. “The court which hears disputes between states” is not the same as “the court which judges individuals”, and the clause is the only thing that tells the reader which tribunal the text means.

A defining clause identifies the noun, so it has no commas: “Students who confuse the courts make the same mistake.” Without it, the sentence would say that all students make the mistake. Use who for people, which for things, and that for either, but only in this kind of clause.

A non-defining clause only adds information about a noun we already know, so it goes between commas: “The Inter-American Court, which is based in San José, hears human rights cases.” There is only one Inter-American Court, so the clause is extra. After a comma, English never uses that.

Two more words complete the set. Whose shows possession, and it replaces the possessive completely: “a citizen whose rights were violated”, never “whose his rights”. Where introduces a place: “The Hague is the city where both courts sit.” Once you see these clauses, a ruling that looked impossible to read becomes a list of simple facts about who, which and where.

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

You want to write a prohibition in legal English. You write:
A "Users may not share their account."
B "Users may to share their account."
C "Users are prohibited to share their account."
The order never arrived. You tell support:
A "I am entitled of a refund."
B "I must a refund."
C "I am entitled to a refund."
You do not want marketing emails. You say:
A "I want to opt out of marketing emails."
B "I want to waive marketing emails."
C "I want to terminate marketing emails."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Think of an app you use every week. In ninety seconds, explain to a friend what you probably agreed to in its terms of service: one thing you must do, one thing the company may do, one thing you may not do, and one right you are entitled to, even if the terms say otherwise.

Survival Card -- Lesson 8

1The fine print is not decoration; it is the contract.
2The user shall keep the password confidential, but may cancel at any time.
3You must not share your account, but you do not have to give your phone number.
4A clause that removes all liability can be void under consumer law.
5If the order never arrives, the customer is entitled to a refund.
