Vocabulary

Vessel -- any ship or large boat that carries people or goods at sea

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Passive of an Incident Report

GRAMMAR

Every marine casualty report is written in the passive. Reading one is a matter of finding the verb before you look for a person, because the person is usually not there at all.

FormWhat it doesExample
be + past participlethe standard passive: what happened matters more than who did itThe hull was breached.
had been + past participlepast perfect passive: it had already happened before the moment describedThe plan had not been amended.
is / are + past participlepresent passive: a standing procedure, true every dayInformation is passed between the pilot and the officer.
by + agentonly when the agent is the point. Most report sentences drop itThe clearance was described by the operator as adequate.
The reading trapan option that turns the passive into an active names somebody the text never named. Find the verb before you look for a person.

A report is written in the passive because it is about the ship, not about a person. That is exactly where the trap lives: swap the passive for an active and the option quietly appoints a guilty party the text never appointed.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Perfects -- How Sure the Report Is

GRAMMAR

A modal perfect is a verb that carries a percentage. It tells you how much of what follows is fact and how much is the writer's own deduction -- and that difference is what the wrong options are built on.

FormWhat it doesExample
must have + past participlenear certainty: it is the only explanation left standingThe figures must have been produced from another plan.
may / might have + past participleone possible explanation among others, not chosenThe pumps may have been running against a closed valve.
could have + past participleit was possible; the report is not saying it happenedFree surface could have made the list worse.
could not have + past participleruled out: the report says it is impossibleIt could not have started the list.
The reading trapmust have is the writer's deduction, not a witnessed fact. An option that says 'the text states that the crane operators changed the sequence' is claiming more than the page does -- the page says the writer concluded it.

A modal perfect is how a report declares its own confidence. In the options, the swap is nearly always between the text STATES and the text SUGGESTS -- and the modal is the only thing on the page that tells you which of the two you are looking at.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read it inside a real sentence. These are the words the exam text will use.

Collision -- an impact between two vessels, or between a vessel and a fixed object
"There was no collision and no damage."
Visibility -- how far a lookout can see, in the conditions of the moment
"Visibility at the time was reported as half a mile in fog."
Give-way vessel -- the vessel required by the rules to keep out of the way of another
"He expected her to keep clear, because she was the give-way vessel."
Stand-on vessel -- the vessel required to hold her course and speed while the other keeps clear
"In restricted visibility there is no stand-on vessel."
Restricted waters -- narrow or shallow areas where a ship has little room to manoeuvre
"The schedule was never amended after the vessel entered restricted waters."
Lookout -- the crew member posted to watch and listen for danger, by sight and by hearing
"The report concludes that a proper lookout was not maintained."
Sound signal -- a prescribed blast on the whistle that tells other vessels what you are doing
"No sound signal was made at the prescribed intervals."
Distress -- the state of a vessel in grave and imminent danger, needing immediate assistance
"No distress call was transmitted."
Anchor watch -- the duty of monitoring a ship's position while she lies at anchor
"The anchor watch schedule still in use on board had never been amended."
Traffic separation scheme -- a set of one-way lanes that keeps opposing streams of shipping apart
"The two vessels passed inside a traffic separation scheme."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reported Speech in an Investigation

GRAMMAR

Everything a person says arrives inside a reporting verb. The clause after that tells you what was said; the verb before it tells you what the writer thinks of it. Read the verb first.

FormWhat it doesExample
said / told + that + backshiftthe reporting verb is past, so the verb inside moves one step backHe said that the lookout had been stood down.
past perfect insidewhat is reported had already happened before he spokeHe told investigators that he had seen the echo.
states / requires + that, no backshifta regulation is true now, so the verb inside stays in the presentRule 19 states that a vessel shall determine...
shall, in a regulationnot the future. It is an obligation: read it as mustshe shall take avoiding action = she must take avoiding action
The reading trapthe reporting verb carries the writer's own position. Confirmed, stated, claimed, alleged and denied are not interchangeable: swap one for another and you change who the text believes. That swap is a whole question.

In an investigation everything a person says arrives inside a reporting verb. The clause after that tells you WHAT was said; the verb before it tells you what the writer thinks of it. Read the verb first.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Causative -- Have and Get Something Done

GRAMMAR

Commercial English needs a way of saying who is responsible without saying who is at the keyboard. That is the causative, and the giveaway is always the same: a noun sitting between the two verbs.

FormWhat it doesExample
have + object + past participlethe subject arranges it; somebody else performs itThe operator had the containers surveyed.
get + object + past participlethe same idea, less formal, often with effort or pressure behind itThe agent got the documents reissued.
the object is the keythe noun sits BETWEEN the two verbs. That is what separates the causative from a perfect tensehad the delay costed / had costed
with a modalshould, must, will + have + object + participleOperators should have the documents reconciled.
The reading traphad the delay costed is NOT the past perfect of cost. If there is a noun between had and the participle, the subject did not do the work -- and an option that says the charterer calculated the figure himself is adding a fact the text does not carry.

The causative is how commercial English says who is responsible without saying who is at the keyboard. In the options the swap is always the same: the arranger is turned into the performer, and everything else is left untouched.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Inversion After Negative and Limiting Adverbials

GRAMMAR

Formal prose puts the emphatic word first and pays for it by moving the auxiliary in front of the subject. Nothing about the facts changes -- but the degree does, and the degree is what the wrong option attacks.

FormWhat it doesExample
negative adverb firstrarely, seldom, never, little, at no time: the auxiliary jumps in front of the subjectRarely is a casualty caused by one failure.
hardly / scarcely / no sooner ... whenone thing had only just happened when the next didHardly had they finished when the data changed.
not only ... but alsothe inversion happens in the FIRST half only; the second half is ordinaryNot only does it increase fatigue, but it also removes...
only when / only after / not untilthe inversion lands on the MAIN clause, never inside the only-clauseOnly when the drill is real does the paperwork describe the ship.
The reading trapinversion is EMPHASIS, not negation. Rarely is a casualty caused by a single failure does not mean never. Turning rarely into never, or hardly into not at all, is the standard wrong option -- and it is wrong by degree, not by topic.

Inversion tells you the writer is leaning on something. It never tells you the thing is absolute. Every question built on an inverted sentence tests whether you read the degree or only the direction.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Clefts and Relative Clauses -- Controlling Where the Reader Looks

GRAMMAR

A cleft says look here and nowhere else. A relative clause says this one, not the other one. In a legal text those two instructions are not decoration: they are the meaning.

FormWhat it doesExample
It + be + X + who / thatthe it-cleft: it names X and excludes everything elseIt was not the surveyor who was at fault.
What + clause + be + Xthe wh-cleft: it delays X to the end, where the emphasis fallsWhat produced the detention was an amendment.
preposition + which / whom / whoseformal relative clause: the preposition moves to the front with the pronounthe society to which the owner had contracted the surveys
whose + nounpossession inside the clause; it can belong to a thing, not only to a personthe state whose flag she flies
The reading trapthe relative clause does NOT always attach to the nearest noun. The port authority in whose waters she lay was acting for a state which had ratified both -- which points at the state, not at the waters and not at the authority. Every wrong option re-attaches the clause to the wrong noun.

Both structures exist to control where the reader looks. The cleft says look here and nowhere else; the relative clause says this one, not the other one. In a legal text those two instructions are the whole meaning.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Gerund or Infinitive -- When the Form Is the Fact

GRAMMAR

Most verbs take one form or the other and nothing changes. Four of them change the EVENT, and all four appear in procedure writing. Read the second verb first: it is where the fact lives.

FormWhat it doesExample
stop doing / stop to docease the activity / pause in order to do something elseHe stopped monitoring it. / He stopped to read the code.
remember doing / remember to dorecall a past action / not forget a future obligationHe remembered signing it. / He must remember to sign it.
try doing / try to doexperiment with a method / attempt something difficultHe tried running it on the generator. / He tried to restart it.
regret doing / regret to informbe sorry about a past action / formal notification of bad newsHe regretted not reading the code. / We regret to inform you.
The reading trapthe two forms are not stylistic variants: they describe DIFFERENT EVENTS. An option that swaps stopped monitoring for stopped to monitor reverses the fact while keeping every word of the sentence, and that is the cleanest distractor in the whole paper.

In a procedure text these pairs are not grammar: they are the record of what was done. Read the second verb before you read the noun, because the second verb is the fact.
