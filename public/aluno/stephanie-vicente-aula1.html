1 · Lead-in

Ten minutes with your coordinator

Tomorrow
Your coordinator has ten minutes and has read neither document. She has to answer a parent this afternoon.
What she needs
What the course promises, what the plan actually does, and where the two do not line up.
The trap
If you say it without saying where it came from, she cannot use it. She has to quote something to a parent.
2 · Prediction

Before you read either one

Two documents about the same course, written four months apart. Where do they usually stop agreeing first?

AIn the topics — the plan covers different subjects
BIn the time — the plan needs more hours than the course has
CIn the level — the plan is written for a different level
B. Topics are what everyone checks, so they rarely drift. Time is what nobody recounts after the first version — and it is what breaks first, because every good activity takes longer than it looks on paper. If you chose C, you were thinking about a bigger failure than the usual one.
3 · Reading for gist

Read both once

Document A -- Course syllabus, published to families in February

School -- syllabus published to families

Intermediate Conversation, 30 hours. Each session runs for 90 minutes and covers one unit of the coursebook. Students complete the book in twenty sessions.

Every session ends with a short writing task, collected and returned with written feedback the following week. Speaking is assessed twice: once at session ten and once at the end.

The course assumes no work outside the classroom.

Document B -- Lesson plan for Unit 7, written in June

School -- lesson plan, Unit 7

Unit 7 -- Making Complaints. 90 minutes. Lead-in 10, vocabulary 15, listening 20, controlled practice 15, role-play 25, feedback 10.

Homework: students write the complaint email at home and bring it printed. The email is corrected in pairs at the start of the following session, not by the teacher.

Note for whoever teaches this: the role-play needs the full 25 minutes. If the listening runs over, cut the controlled practice, never the role-play.

Read both once. What is the overall relationship between them?

AThe plan follows the syllabus closely
BThe plan is a different course from the one the syllabus promised
CThe plan is the same course, but it quietly changed three of the promises
C. The unit, the level and the 90 minutes all match — this is not a different course. But three promises moved: the total time, who corrects the writing, and whether there is homework. B overstates it and A misses it, which is exactly the pair of mistakes a coordinator cannot afford in front of a parent.

5 · Guided discovery

Why the third column is the dangerous one

Why the third column is the dangerous one

Read each, decide whether you would say it to the coordinator, then click.

“Whoever wrote the plan had not reread the syllabus.”
Probably true, and unusable: no line says it. If she repeats it to a parent she is repeating YOUR guess with the school's authority behind it.
“The role-play matters more to the writer than the controlled practice.”
This one is nearly quotable — the note says cut the practice, never the role-play. Say it that way and it moves to column one.
Conditional
“The plan needs five minutes it does not have.”
Column two: neither document says it. You got it by adding six numbers — and adding is fine, as long as you say you added.
5 · Guided discovery

The rule you are actually using

This is the whole lesson. The English for it is on the next screen.

1. QUOTE it — the line exists. 'The plan gives the writing to the students.'
2. DERIVE it — you compared or counted. Say so: 'Adding up the plan, it comes to 95.'
3. OWN it — no line supports it. Say it is yours: 'My reading is that…' — or do not say it.
4. The coordinator can defend the first two in front of a parent. The third she can only defend as YOUR opinion, so she has to know it is one.
