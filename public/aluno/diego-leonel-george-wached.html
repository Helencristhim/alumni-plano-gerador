Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Mrs. Parker sits down in room four. Her chief complaint is a rash on both hands. It started three weeks ago on one hand, and this week it is spreading to the other. The doctor asks two questions that sound similar and are not: does it itch at night? and is it itching right now? The first one asks about the pattern. The second one asks about this exact moment.

She answers that it is very itchy at night and that it is tender when she presses it. She tried an over-the-counter cream for ten days and it did nothing. Nobody in the clinic is treating her at the moment, so she is not taking anything for it today.

The doctor takes her medical history. Skin problems run in the family: her mother had eczema, and it used to flare up in winter and then settle down again. The rash comes and goes, which is why she waited so long. Before she leaves, the doctor books a follow-up in six weeks and writes a referral in case the treatment does not work.

1. "It is spreading to the other hand this week." Why not "It spreads"?
A Because spreading is a slow action and slow actions always take -ing.
B Because it describes a temporary change happening now, not a habit that repeats.
C Because the rash is on two hands, and two hands take the plural form.
2. "Does it itch at night?" and "Is it itching right now?" What is the difference?
A The first asks about the usual pattern. The second asks about this exact moment.
B The first is polite and the second is direct, but they mean the same thing.
C The first is for a doctor and the second is for a nurse.
3. "Skin problems run in the family." Why is this not "are running in the family"?
A Because run is an irregular verb and irregular verbs never take -ing.
B Because the family is plural and plurals take the simple form.
C Because it is a permanent fact about the family, not something happening this week.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An abstract always tells the same story in the same order, and almost always in the same voice. First the question. Then the method: two hundred and forty adults were enrolled at four centers, and half of them were treated once a day. Notice that nobody is named. We are not told who signed the patients up, because it does not matter.

Then the numbers. Severity was assessed at baseline, at week six and at week twelve, and it was assessed by two dermatologists who did not know which schedule each patient had been given. Here the doer does matter, so the sentence names it, at the end, after the word by. Adherence was measured by weighing the tubes. Nine patients reported burning in the first week — and this one is active, because the patients themselves did the reporting.

Then the result. The difference between the two groups was small and it was not significant. Better adherence was associated with a better outcome. And then the last paragraph, where the authors conclude what it all means and admit one limitation: twelve weeks is short. In a well-written paper, the strong claim never appears in the passive to hide who is making it. It appears in the active, with the authors’ name on it.

1. "Two hundred and forty adults were enrolled at four centers." Why does the sentence not say who enrolled them?
A Because the authors want to hide a mistake.
B Because the doer does not matter here, and the passive lets the sentence start with the people who were studied.
C Because English never names the doer in the past.
2. "Severity was assessed by two dermatologists." Why is by used here and not in the sentence about enrolment?
A Because who did the assessing is part of the evidence: two independent dermatologists who did not know the schedule.
B Because by is always used with people and never with places.
C Because the verb assess cannot be used without by.
3. "Nine patients reported burning in the first week." Why is this one active?
A Because the number nine is small.
B Because report has no passive form in English.
C Because the patients themselves did the reporting, and the sentence is about them doing it.

GRAMMAR

One decision: does the reader need to know who did it? If not, put the receiver first and build the verb with be.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The doctor never begins with how long have you had it. He begins with a question that asks for a date: when did it start? And patients remember. This one said the onset was six weeks ago, in the week she changed jobs. Two patches appeared on her elbows first, and a third one came later, on the knee.

Then the second question, the one patients never answer without being asked: did anything change in the week before it started? She changed jobs in March and she did not sleep for about a month. Stress, she says, is her trigger. The patches are dry and scaly, and when she scratches them small pieces come off. Last week the skin around one of them was swollen.

And then the question residents forget: did you use anything on it? She used a cream her sister gave her. It did not help and it burned, which may be a side effect or may be the reason it looks worse today. Before the doctor prescribes anything, he wants to examine the whole body surface, and he wants one test, only to rule out an infection.

1. "When did it start?" Why not "When it started?"
A Because start is an irregular verb.
B Because an English past question needs the auxiliary did, and the main verb goes back to its base form.
C Because when is a question word and question words never take -ed.
2. "She did not sleep for about a month." Why not "She did not slept"?
A Because did already carries the past. The past moves once, never twice.
B Because sleep has no past form in English.
C Because a month is a long period and long periods take the base form.
3. "A third one came later." Why is it came and not comed?
A Because it is a question and questions change the verb.
B Because the subject is singular.
C Because come is irregular: it has its own past form, and -ed is never added to it.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Every over-the-counter box sold in the United States carries the same label, with the same seven headings, in the same order. That is why a customer who learns to read one box can read every box. And the whole thing is written in four little verbs.

Under Warnings, the strongest ones live. You must not use a hydrocortisone cream on broken skin or on the face: that is a ban, and it does not leave you a choice. You should stop and ask a doctor if the skin becomes more irritated, or if the symptoms last more than seven days: that is advice, and it leaves the decision with you. The two feel similar in translation and they are not the same instruction.

Under Directions come the practical ones. Adults can apply a thin layer three or four times a day: permission. Under two years old, you have to ask a doctor first: an obligation that comes from outside you. And the sentence that traps everybody: you do not have to cover the area with a bandage. That is not a ban. It is a free choice.

The rest is practical vocabulary. The dosage is on the box, the expiration date is on the tube, the generic is on the same shelf for half the price, the copay is what you pay at the till, and the pharmacist is the person you ask when you ran out of your prescription on a Sunday.

1. The label says you do not have to cover the area with a bandage. What does that mean?
A It is forbidden to use a bandage.
B You may use a bandage or not -- it is your choice.
C You should always use a bandage.
2. Which of these is a ban, and not advice?
A You should stop if the skin becomes more irritated.
B You must not use it on the face.
C You can apply it three times a day.
3. Why does have to appear instead of must in "under two years old, you have to ask a doctor first"?
A Because must is impolite in English.
B Because have to has no negative form.
C Because the obligation comes from outside -- a rule of the product, not the speaker's own decision.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A hypothetical needs its own grammar, and English marks it. If you describe an option nobody has chosen using the grammar of something already decided, you have decided for the patient -- and in a consent conversation that is a clinical problem, not a stylistic one.

Compare the two. If you start the cream on Monday, I will see you in six weeks -- that is a plan, and it is going ahead. If you chose the cream, it would take about twelve weeks -- nothing has been chosen. The verb after if looks past, and nothing here happened in the past: the past form is simply the marker of hypothesis. And the rule with no exceptions: never would after if.

Alongside it sit the modals of possibility. The tablets might work faster; they could affect the liver. Both leave the outcome open, which is exactly what an honest option does. And the sentence that hands the decision back is what would you like to do? -- not what do you want.

The rest is the shape of the conversation. Name the trade-off. Say what is reversible and what is not. Offer to hold off, because waiting is a real third option and a patient who is not offered it cannot choose it. Give your own view -- in your position I would probably... -- and then say plainly that it is your reasoning, not a decision. Ask whether there is a concern that has not been mentioned. Tell them to talk it through at home. That is what informed consent means: the conversation, not the signature.
