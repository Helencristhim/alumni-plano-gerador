Lesson 1 · The Opening Question

What You Actually Run

Somebody at a trade fair asks what your company does. You have about two minutes, and every door after that one depends on them.

Chapter 1: First Words

So, What Does Your Company Do?

Five words, and everybody asks them. Nobody in the room has ever heard of a group of five companies in the interior of São Paulo, so the answer has to build the picture from nothing.

Answer it now, out loud, the way you would answer it at a stand in Chicago. Where did you run out of words?

Today’s Goal

Two Minutes, Three Moves

1. The Words

a group of companies, a manufacturing plant, point-of-sale material, to supply, lead time...

2. One Structure

what the company has delivered, and what it has been doing all along

3. Your Answer

the group, the scale, the client list — two minutes

You already read this language every day. What is missing is the frame that carries it in speech: how much you have delivered and how long you have been at it — two different sentences, and English keeps them apart.

One Question

And How Long Have You Been Doing It?

Answer it out loud, right now, in one sentence.

Then answer the second one, which is the one that actually sells the company: and what has the group delivered in that time?

Chapter 3: The Code

How Much You Have Done, and How Long You Have Been At It

One perfect, two jobs

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Share?

"We have supplied forty-seven of the fifty largest chains in the country."

"We have been supplying them since 2016."

"This year we have opened a second plant."

"I have been running the group for ten years."

Two of the red verbs carry been and an -ing, and two do not. Look at the blue part: it only appears on one of the two sides, and that is the clue.

JobFormExample
How many, how muchhave / has + past participleWe have supplied forty-seven chains.
How long, still goinghave / has + been + verb-ingWe have been supplying them since 2016.
A result inside an unfinished periodthe simple form, with this year / this monthThis year we have opened a second plant.
Since and forsince + a point in time · for + a length of timesince 2016 / for ten years
State verbsbe, have, know, own: simple form even for durationI have been a director for ten years.
QuestionHow many have you...? vs How long have you been...?How long have you been working with them?
The trapnever the present tense with since / forNot I am here since ten years. Say I have been here for ten years.

In one line: the simple form counts what is finished, the -ing form measures what is still running.

Dialogue

A Trade Fair Stand, Tuesday Morning

D
Dana Whitfield, category manager. I do not think we have met. So, what does your company do?
F
Fernando da Cunha. We are a group of companies in Brazil, and four of them make point-of-sale material — the price tags and the shelf labels you read in the aisle.
D
Right. And who do you work with down there?
F
We supply forty-seven of the fifty largest supermarket chains in the country, and eight of the ten largest drugstore chains. We have been working with most of them for over a decade.
D
That is a serious list. Do you make it yourselves, or do you buy it in?
F
We make it. We have two manufacturing plants, and the design is in-house as well. We only source the raw material abroad.
D
Then the question everybody here is going to ask you. What is your lead time?
F
Fifteen days from the order, and that is the reason the big chains stay with us. In a group this size you wear several hats, so I sign the contract and I also answer for the delivery. And you — how many stores are you buying for?
Comprehension

About Dana

1. How does Dana open the conversation, and what does she say about the two of them?
She gives her name and her job, category manager, and says she does not think they have met.
2. What does Dana want to know about how the product is made, and how does she phrase it?
She wants to know whether the company manufactures it or buys it from somebody else. She asks it as "Do you make it yourselves, or do you buy it in?"
3. Which question does Dana say everybody at the fair is going to ask, and why does that matter to a buyer?
The lead time. For a buyer it is the number that decides whether a supplier can keep the shelves working, so it comes before the price.

Listening 1 · A Supplier

The First Morning of the Fair

A packaging supplier introducing his company on day one. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does he say his company makes, and how long has it been doing it?
Shelf labels and price tags, which he calls the boring half of the store. The company has been doing it for thirty-one years.
2. What number does he give about the stores, and why does he say it first?
Four thousand stores across eleven states. He says it first because it is the only thing a buyer can actually do something with.
3. What does he say is the mistake other exhibitors make at the fair?
They open with the price. He says the price is the fourth question, and nobody has ever bought from him because of the first number he said.

Listening 2 · The Buyer

What We Listen For in the First Two Minutes

A retail buyer on a podcast, saying what she is actually listening for. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What mistake does she say most suppliers make in the first two minutes?
They describe the product in detail before she knows who else buys it, and she says she cannot judge a product she has no reference point for.
2. What three things does she say a good opening contains?
Who you already supply, how fast you deliver, and one thing you have changed in the last year.
3. What does she say about suppliers whose English is not perfect?
That it has never cost anybody a contract. She says a slow clear answer is worth more than a fast one she has to guess at.

Listening 3 · Business Radio

The Price Tag That Changes Itself

A business radio report on electronic shelf labels. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What figure does the reporter give about the paper labels, and what does he say it hides?
That a large supermarket changes around forty thousand paper labels a week. It hides how many hours of staff time that is, which is where the real cost sits.
2. According to the report, why has the change been slower than everybody expected?
Because the screens are the cheap part. Fitting them across every aisle of a chain takes years, and most chains have been running the two systems side by side.
3. What does the report say the printing suppliers have done in response?
They have moved into promotional signage, which changes weekly and is still printed, instead of competing on the shelf edge.

A group of five companies, four of them in retail and one in transport. The transport arm started as an internal need and became a business of its own two years ago. The group sources part of its raw material abroad and exports within South America.
1. Two lines on this card measure time, and they do it in opposite ways. Which are they, and what is the difference?
"Lead time: 15 days from order" measures how long one delivery takes. "Since: supplying the sector for over twenty years" measures a period that is still open today.
2. The card says the design is in-house and the raw material is sourced abroad. Why would a buyer care about that difference?
Because in-house design means changes can be made without a third company, while sourced material means part of the lead time depends on somebody else.
3. According to the paragraph, how did the transport company start, and what is it now?
It started as an internal need of the group, and two years ago it became a business of its own.
