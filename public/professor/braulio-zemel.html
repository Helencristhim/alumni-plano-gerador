Programa de 46 aulas. A geração alterna os dois formatos por número de aula: aula ímpar gira em torno de diálogo e role-play, aula par em torno de um texto autêntico com gist e true/false — atendendo ao pedido do aluno por um texto por aula sem transformar o curso inteiro em leitura. Cada aula acrescenta 12 expressões novas, e nenhuma expressão volta como conteúdo novo (REGRA 22). Os blocos seguintes são detalhados ao final de cada bloco de 12.

Metodologia

Simplificar, não ampliar

Este aluno erra por saber demais: constrói períodos longos e trava no meio. O alvo de produção é frase curta e correta. Quando ele complicar, corte com shorter — eight words.

O fio entre as aulas

Todo warm-up retoma o vocabulário da aula anterior antes de abrir a nova. Foi a ausência disso que o fez abandonar o curso em grupo — e o motivo nº 1 da reclamação.

Listening em duas velocidades

0.75x na primeira escuta sem ele pedir, 1x na segunda, quantas repetições ele quiser. Pergunta ANTES do play, sempre (REGRA 2.1). Repetir não é fracasso: é o treino.

Texto como âncora

Canal dominante dele é leitura. Aula PAR gira em torno de um texto autêntico; aula ÍMPAR em torno de diálogo e role-play. O Stage 1.3 do Pre-class é texto em toda aula.

Adulto com o que dizer

66 anos, jornal todo dia, opinião formada. Nada de tom condescendente e nada de tópico decorativo. Ele não precisa de ideias — precisa da língua para as ideias que já tem.

Silêncio é permitido

Ele pondera antes de falar: espere até dez segundos sem intervir. E lembre que ele sai estressado de conversa em inglês — baixar a tensão é parte do método, não cortesia.

Estrutura sugerida da aula (60 min)

  • 5 min — O fio: callback do vocabulário da aula anterior + warm-up sem saudação scriptada
  • 12 min — As doze expressões: reveal cards + pronúncia + gap-fill com banco de palavras
  • 10 min — Language focus: uma estrutura, sempre dentro de fala de jantar ou de notícia
  • 15 min — Input autêntico: diálogo line-by-line ou texto, mais os dois listenings em 0.75x e 1x

CCQs da Aula 1 (Concept Checking Questions)

  • Small talk — Do we use it with an old friend, or with someone we have just met? (Someone we have just met.)
  • Common ground — If we disagree about everything, do we have common ground? (No — it is what we SHARE.)
  • To chime in — Do I start the conversation when I chime in? (No — it is already going, and I add my voice to it.)
  • To lose the thread — Do I stop HEARING, or do I stop knowing WHERE we are? (I stop knowing where we are.)
  • To follow up on — Is it a new subject, or the same one one step deeper? (The same one, deeper.)
  • To get by — Do I speak perfectly when I get by? (No — well enough for the situation.)
  • “I met your wife in Lisbon” → “Did you?” — Why did and not do? (Because “met” is past, so the echo borrows the past auxiliary.)
  • “She reads three papers a day” → ? — There is no auxiliary here. What do I borrow? (Does she?)
  • “The election is next month, isn't it?” — Am I asking because I have no idea, or inviting the table to agree? (Inviting them to agree.)
  • “You are from Denmark, isn't it?” — Is this correct? (No — the tag copies the subject: aren't you?)

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

For more than thirty years the week never changed. He left the house before seven and he came back at ten at night, and Saturday was the only day that belonged to him. It was a demanding job and, for most of those years, he liked that about it. Looking back, the word for it is relentless.

Then, this year, he retired. It did not happen overnight — he had two years to wind down — but the year itself was a turning point, and he knew it while it was happening.

Now the week is his. He reads three newspapers every morning, before anybody else is awake, because he is an avid follower of current affairs and always was. Twice a week he goes to the club, and on Wednesday evenings he has a cooking class. He helps with his grandchildren, which is the most fulfilling part of the week and also the most tiring.

And some of it is still unfinished. He is still settling into a week with nothing fixed in it. On a quiet Tuesday afternoon he is completely at a loose end, and he is looking for something new to take up. He says he has thirty years of Sundays to make up for, and he is in no hurry at all.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Three Tenses, One Story

GRAMMAR

The three forms that tell anybody who you are, in under a minute.

FormWhat it saysExample
Past simple

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

For about fifteen years he was the quiet one at dinner. His wife's English was better than his had ever been, so at every table abroad she talked and he ate. People thought he was serious. He was not serious. He was tongue-tied.

What changed was not a course. It was one evening in Lisbon, when a man across the table picked up on something he had said about the price of housing and asked him a direct question. He answered in five words. The man asked another. Twenty minutes later they were still going. If that man had not asked the second question, he would have gone home the same man he arrived as.

Since then he has watched how it works, and it is less impressive than people think. Somebody breaks the ice with something small and slightly boring. Somebody else brings up a subject with a little weight in it. Two people find common ground and hit it off, and the rest of the table relaxes. Small talk is not the opposite of real conversation. It is the corridor you walk down to reach it.

The part he got wrong for fifteen years was thinking he had to be fluent to join in. He did not. He had to be curious out loud. If he had understood that at forty, he would have had fifteen more years of dinners instead of fifteen years of nodding politely at his plate.

The question arrives anyway, usually twenty minutes in. And this is where the three short forms earn their place. I would rather talk about what the job did to my weekends than about what it paid. I would rather they did not ask, but they do, so there is no point being surprised by it. You had better have an answer ready, because inventing one at the table always sounds like an evasion, and an evasion sounds worse than a number.

Notice the shape of those three. After would rather, had better and may as well, the verb comes bare, with no to in front of it. And when the preference is about somebody ELSE, the verb steps into the past: I would rather they did not ask, never do not ask. That step backwards is the same one English makes after wish, and it means the same thing: this is not real, it is what I would prefer.

The rest is vocabulary you already half own from reading. A windfall is money nobody planned for and it is spent badly in every country. A bubble is obvious afterwards and invisible from inside. And if you cannot explain where a yield is coming from, you had better assume you are the yield. Those three sentences will hold a table for ten minutes, and not one of them is about you.

1. "I would rather they did not ask." Why the past tense?
A Because it happened yesterday.
B Because the preference is about somebody else, and English steps the verb backwards.
C Because ask has no present form after rather.
2. What do would rather, had better and may as well have in common?
A The verb after them is bare -- no to.
B They are all polite ways of giving advice.
C They all take a verb ending in -ing.
3. According to the text, why should you have an answer ready?

B Because inventing one at the table sounds like an evasion, and that sounds worse than a number.

A Money nobody planned for -- and it is spent badly in every country.
B A price that everybody agrees on until they do not.
C What an investment pays you back as a percentage.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Preference, Warning and Resignation

GRAMMAR

Three short forms that let you take a position without a long sentence. All three take a bare verb, and one of them is stronger than it looks.

FormWhat it saysExample
would rather + bare verbYour own preference, right now. Never to after it."I would rather talk about the policy."
would rather + subject + PASTA preference about what somebody ELSE does. The past tense here is not about the past."I would rather they did not ask."
had better + bare verbStrong advice with a consequence behind it. It is not politeness, and it is not it would be better."You had better check that figure."
may as well + bare verbThere is no better option, so we might as well do this. Resignation, said lightly."We may as well walk."
The trapsNo to after any of the three. And had better is heavier than it sounds -- use it about facts, not about people you have just met.NOT "I would rather to talk."
NOT "You had better to check."
NOT "I would rather that they do not ask."

A practical note. All three are short on purpose, and shortness is what makes them sound certain. In a conversation about the economy the person who convinces the table is almost never the one with the longest sentence.
