Quem é o Dejair

Farmacologista, formado em farmácia bioquímica, pesquisador e professor de ensino superior. Sua linha de pesquisa é dor neuropática associada à hanseníase. Lê artigo científico em inglês sem dificuldade nenhuma — o inglês escrito e técnico está consolidado há anos.

Idade não confirmada. O valor no cadastro é provisório; trate como adulto de 31 a 50 até o gestor confirmar. Isso muda o ritmo, não o conteúdo.

O prazo que ordena o curso inteiro

Congresso internacional sobre dor, em Bangkok, 26 de outubro de 2026. Ele vai assistir e participar de um workshop. Nas palavras dele: "às vezes, você é obrigado a falar também um pouquinho do que você está vendo. Se também você quer perguntar, as pessoas perguntam, você quer responder, dar a sua opinião."

São 24 aulas, três por semana. O curso é uma contagem regressiva — cada aula tem de deixá-lo mais perto de conseguir abrir a boca naquele workshop.

O gargalo real: ouvir, não ler

Palavras dele: "A maior dificuldade, na verdade, é ouvir." E o custo disso, também nas palavras dele: "a gente sempre sai um pouco no prejuízo, porque falta alguma coisa da fluência, a gente acaba perdendo um pouco da interação entre os pares."

Não é vergonha nem medo de errar — é perda concreta de conexão científica. Isso muda como se corrige: o problema dele não é autoestima, é ferramenta.

Sotaque é conteúdo, não detalhe

Ele trava especificamente com indiano, russo e turco — não com americano ou britânico. Recebeu na própria instituição 18 alunos ingleses, 6 russos e 2 turcos, e descreveu a dificuldade de "contactar com eles". A diretora executiva prometeu na consultoria treino de listening com sotaques variados, e essa promessa está no material a partir desta aula: o Listening 1 é com voz indiana, de propósito.

Evite montar aula só com áudio de nativo americano ou britânico. O desafio de Bangkok é inglês como língua franca — falado por quem também aprendeu.

O que NÃO fazer com ele

  • Não gastar aula lendo texto científico. Ele já domina esse canal; usar tempo síncrono nisso é desperdício.
  • Não trazer atividade-surpresa sem pre-class. Ele valoriza a preparação em voz alta: "ajuda bastante do que você chegar na sala de aula e pegar a surpresa do que é daquele dia."
  • Não usar como tema, por enquanto, a aula que ele deu para os 18 alunos ingleses. Ele descreveu como "muito sofrível" — o episódio ainda dói. Volte a ele lá pela aula 8 ou 9, quando já houver confiança para transformá-lo em vitória.

Como conduzir esta aula 1

Esta aula é diagnóstica. Seu trabalho não é corrigir — é medir. Deixe-o falar sem interrupção no warm-up e anote: quanto tempo leva para começar, onde para para escolher palavra, o que ele evita dizer. As correções vêm no fim de cada bloco, nunca no meio da frase.

O Listening 1 (voz indiana) também é diagnóstico: anote onde exatamente ele perde o fio — se é velocidade, se é vocabulário, se é o som. Essa informação define as próximas três aulas.

Gramática desta aula

Present simple para fatos e rotina. É a base de qualquer apresentação pessoal: I work on…, My main focus is…, I'm based at…. Ele já usa isso na leitura; aqui a meta é usar falando, sem pausa para pensar.

Logística

Segundas e quartas às 20h (em casa, conexão estável) e terças às 9h (no trabalho, onde a internet cai — ele avisa quando acontecer). Aulas de 60 minutos, três por semana: formato escolhido por ele na consultoria depois de descartar 1h30 duas vezes por semana.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Questions and Negatives

GRAMMAR

In the present simple, a question or a negative needs a helper: do or does. The helper carries the tense and the -s, so the main verb goes back to its plain form.

question — I / you / we / theydo + subject + verbDo you collaborate with hospitals?
question — he / she / itdoes + subject + verbDoes your group publish in English?
negative — I / you / we / theydo not + verbWe do not develop new drugs.
negative — he / she / itdoes not + verbShe does not work with children.
wh- questionwh- + do / does + subject + verbWhere does she collect the data?

One -s per sentence. "Does she publishes?" is wrong — the -s is already inside "does".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Going to and Will

GRAMMAR

Both are the future. The difference is when you decided. Use going to for a plan you already had before you spoke. Use will (usually I'll) for a decision, an offer or a promise made at the moment of speaking.

plan you already hadam / is / are going to + verbI am going to take part in the Wednesday workshop.
negative planam / is / are not going to + verbWe are not going to stay for the closing dinner.
question about a planam / is / are + subject + going toAre you going to the dinner on Wednesday?
decision, offer or promise nowwill / I'll + verb"I do not have your paper." -- "I'll send it tonight."
fixed programme timepresent simpleThe congress starts on October 26th.

After look forward to comes a noun or an -ING verb: "I am looking forward to meeting you", never "to meet you".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

Use the present continuous (am / is / are + verb + -ing) for what is happening right now, and for what is true only for a while: this week, this year, until December. Keep the present simple for what is always true.

happening right nowam / is / are + -ingShe is presenting in room two right now.

Verbs of state -- know, want, need, understand, believe -- do not take -ing. "I am not knowing that group" is wrong.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Count the syllables. Short adjectives (one syllable, or two ending in -y) take -er and the -est. Longer adjectives take more and the most. Never both at once.

short adjectiveadjective + -er thanHer explanation was clearer than mine.
short adjective, topthe + adjective + -estIt was the smallest room in the building.
long adjectivemore + adjective + thanThis design is more complex than the other one.
long adjective, topthe most + adjectiveIt was the most useful session of the week.
irregulargood / bad / farbetter, the best -- worse, the worst -- further, the furthest

"More bigger" is wrong: English chooses one of the two forms, never both. And the second half of a comparison is than, not that.

When Things Go Wrong -- Staying in the Conversation

What to say in the three seconds after you did not understand, while the room is waiting. Key words: to catch, to follow, to clarify, to rephrase, to misunderstand, to double-check, in other words, to interrupt. Structure: modal verbs could, should and might for repair and polite requests.

▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example out loud. None of these is about English. All of them are about staying in the room.

To catch -- to hear and understand a word at the moment it is said
"Sorry, I did not catch the last part of your question."
To follow -- to understand an idea while somebody is explaining it
"I am not following. Could you go back one step?"
To clarify -- to make an idea clearer, with different words
"Could you clarify what you mean by severe cases?"
To rephrase -- to say the same thing again, in different words
"Let me rephrase that. My question is about the control group."
To misunderstand -- to think somebody said one thing when they said another
"I think I misunderstood your question. May I try again?"
To double-check -- to confirm something you think you already understood
"Let me double-check: you mean the second group, not the first?"
In other words -- the phrase you use before you say it a simpler way
"In other words, you are asking whether it is age or severity."
To interrupt -- to speak while somebody else is still speaking
"Sorry to interrupt, but could you say that number again?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Could, Should and Might

GRAMMAR

These are modals. After every one of them comes the plain verb, with no to and no -s. What changes is not the meaning of the request -- it is how careful it sounds.

direct, among colleaguescan + verbCan you repeat that?
careful, in front of the roomcould + verbCould you repeat that, please?
asking about the right momentshould + verbShould I ask now or wait until the end?
taking the blame yourselfmight have + past participleI might have misunderstood -- did you say the second group?
advice to somebody senioryou might want to + verbYou might want to check the follow-up in that paper.

No to after a modal: "Could you to repeat" is wrong. And the adverb is slowly, not slow: "speak more slowly".

"How important is politeness?" -- 6 Minute English, BBC Learning English

Six minutes on why the same request sounds different depending on the words around it. That is exactly the difference between can and could that you worked on today.

Tip: listen once with your eyes closed. Then listen again and write down every request you hear. Mark which ones use could or would, and which ones are direct. Notice who they are talking to in each case.

Conference Talk
"Don't insist on English!" -- Patricia Ryan, TED

An English teacher of forty years argues that good ideas get lost when people are judged by their English instead of by their thinking. This is the talk to watch on a day when your English feels like the problem.

Tip: watch it once, without subtitles, and see how much you follow. Then watch again with subtitles and count what you missed. That number is your listening measure -- write it down and repeat the test in four weeks.

Listening
"How do you learn to speak a language?" -- 6 Minute English, BBC Learning English

Six minutes about what actually makes speaking improve, and what does not. Useful now, when you are eight lessons in and starting to hear your own progress.

Tip: listen for what they say about making mistakes. Then say out loud, in English, one thing you do today that you could not do eight lessons ago. One sentence, and be specific.
