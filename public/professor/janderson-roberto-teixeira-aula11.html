Lesson 11 · The Rules

Rules and Deadlines

What is obligatory, what is forbidden, and what is simply not necessary. English keeps those three apart with two small verbs, and one of them is a trap.

Chapter 1: The Rules

One Rule You Have to Follow at Work

One thing you have to do at the bank every week, and one thing everybody assumes is obligatory and is not.

Where You Start

Obligatory, Forbidden, Unnecessary

You have to

"You have to sign it before Friday." -- there is no choice

You must not

"You must not send it before it is approved." -- it is forbidden

You do not have to

"You do not have to come in on Friday." -- you can if you want

The trap is in the middle two. Must not and do not have to look like the same negative and mean opposite things: one forbids, the other frees. Saying the wrong one to a client or a colleague does not sound like a small grammar slip — it changes what they are going to do next.

Chapter 3: The Code

Have To, Must Not, Do Not Have To

Two negatives that look identical and mean opposite things

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"You have to sign it before Friday."

"You must not send it before it is approved."

"You do not have to come in on Friday."

"Do I have to fill in the form today?"

Two of these sentences are negative. One of them forbids something and the other one frees you from it. Which is which, and how would you know if nobody told you?

FormWhat it saysExample
have to + plain verbObligation, usually from outside you: a rule, a boss, a policy, a law."You have to sign it before Friday."
"He has to approve it first."
must + plain verbObligation that feels internal, or a written rule. Common on signs and in documents."I must call him today."
"Visitors must report to reception."
must notFORBIDDEN. There is a rule against it."You must not send it before it is approved."
do not have toNOT NECESSARY. You may do it if you want. This is the opposite of must not."You do not have to come in on Friday."
Questions and other tensesHave to borrows do, like a normal verb, and it works in every tense. Must does neither."Do I have to fill it in?"
"I had to sign it twice."
NOT "I musted"

In a meeting, have to is safer than must almost every time: it sounds like a fact about the rules rather than an order from you. Keep must not for things that are genuinely forbidden.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"You must not come in on Friday, it is optional."
"You do not have to come in on Friday."
"Do you must sign this today?"
"Do you have to sign this today?"
"Yesterday I must sign it twice."
"Yesterday I had to sign it twice."

Must not forbids and do not have to frees: they are opposites, not variations. Must has no question form of its own and no past, so English borrows have to for both — do you have to, I had to. Read the correct versions aloud twice.

Free Practice

The Rules of Your Own Job

No keywords. The conversation is yours.

The Habit

Three Rules, Out Loud

Before the next class, say three rules of your own week out loud each day: one with have to, one with must not, one with do not have to. Real rules, from your real diary.

The two negatives are opposites. The only way to stop confusing them is to say both, out loud, until one of them feels wrong in the wrong place.
