Two Museums, One Monument

Two Museums, One Monument

Few objects in any museum carry as much argument as the sculptures that once decorated the Parthenon in Athens. Carved in the fifth century BCE, the frieze, the metopes and the figures from the pediments were designed as parts of a single building dedicated to Athena. Today roughly half of what survives is displayed in the Acropolis Museum in Athens, and a large proportion of the rest is in the British Museum in London, where it has been for more than two centuries. It is arguably the most famous restitution case in the world, and it has become a test for how museums everywhere think about ownership.

The sculptures were removed between 1801 and 1812 by agents working for Thomas Bruce, the seventh Earl of Elgin, who was then the British ambassador to the Ottoman Empire, which ruled Greece at the time. Elgin maintained that he had acted with official permission, and the document he relied upon ostensibly allowed his team to draw, measure and take away certain pieces. The difficulty is that the original has never been found. What survives is purportedly a translation into Italian, and scholars continue to disagree about how much it actually authorized. In 1816 the British government bought the collection from Elgin, and it passed into the care of the museum.

The case for keeping the sculptures in London rests on several arguments that deserve to be taken seriously. The museum presents itself as a custodian of world culture rather than a national treasury, a place where visitors can see ancient Athens alongside Egypt, Assyria and China, free of charge. Its supporters point out that the sculptures were protected in London during periods when conditions in Athens were unstable, and that British law makes it very difficult for the museum to deaccession objects from its collection. To be sure, stewardship on this scale has real value, and millions of visitors have encountered the sculptures who would never otherwise have seen them.

The case for reunification, however, is not simply a matter of national pride. Its strongest form is architectural. The sculptures were not made as independent works of art; they were made for a particular building, which still stands a short walk from the museum built to receive them. When the Acropolis Museum opened in 2009, its top floor was designed to display the surviving frieze in the same orientation as the temple, in sight of the Parthenon itself, with casts marking the pieces that remain abroad. For many Greeks, the sculptures are not only national patrimony; they are fragments of a single monument that has been separated for two hundred years.

Both positions have become deeply entrenched, and the vocabulary of the debate reflects it. Supporters of the museum speak of legality and access; supporters of return speak of legitimacy and integrity. Granted, the legal question may never be settled to everyone's satisfaction. Yet the fact remains that law and legitimacy are not the same thing, and a museum that relies only on the first may find it increasingly difficult to defend the second.

It would be premature to predict the outcome. Discussions about loans and partnerships have been reported at various points, and neither side has shown much appetite for an irrevocable concession. What seems clear is that the question has shifted. It is no longer only who owns the sculptures, but what a museum is for, and on that question the evidence suggests that public opinion is moving faster than the law.

Chapter 4: The Style Code

Confidence, Calibrated

Hedging and concession: the grammar of a trustworthy argument

Discover the Style

Why Does Careful Sound Stronger?

"While the museum has cared for the sculptures, their removal remains contested."

"It is arguably the most famous restitution case in the world."

"Granted, the sculptures survived in London; that alone does not settle who should own them."

"The evidence suggests that the original permit was far narrower than Elgin claimed."

Remove each colored expression. Does the argument become stronger, or only louder?

FormWhat it doesExample
hedging adverbslower certainty to what the evidence supportsarguably, ostensibly, purportedly
hedging framesattribute a claim to evidence or to othersThe evidence suggests that... It has been argued that...
concession + contrastadmits the other side before answering itWhile X, Y. Although X, the fact remains that Y.
concession markerssignal a point granted, and prepare a replyGranted, X; yet Y. To be sure, X. However, Y.
The C2 traphedging everything. A writer who writes "it could perhaps possibly be argued" has no position at all. Hedge the claim that is uncertain, and state the one you can defend.

Thank you for inviting me. Let me begin with what an encyclopedic museum is for, because I think it is often misunderstood. Our purpose is to let visitors see the cultures of the world side by side, so that no single culture is seen in isolation, and so that a teenager from any city can stand in one building and understand that human history is shared. That idea still matters to me enormously. I also want to be honest about something. Some objects came into museums like mine in ways that would not be acceptable today, and that history has to be faced openly rather than defended by habit. I do not think every claim can be answered in the same way, and I am wary of anyone who says that it can. What I would propose, instead of permanent returns decided case by case under pressure, is a different model: long-term loans, shared research and genuine partnerships that keep objects moving between institutions. That may not satisfy everyone. It is, arguably, the approach that serves the largest number of people.

I would like to start by agreeing with my colleague on one point. The sculptures were cared for in London, and an enormous number of people have seen them there. I don't dispute that for a moment. But I want to explain why these sculptures are different from a painting or a coin. A painting can hang in any room. These were carved as parts of a single building, a building that still stands on the hill above the city, and their meaning depends on being seen together with it. When the new museum in Athens was designed, its top floor was built to display the surviving sculptures in sight of the Parthenon, in the same order in which they decorated the temple, with empty spaces and casts left for the pieces that are missing. Every visitor who stands there sees the gaps. So the question, for me, is not really whether London has been a good custodian. It is whether a monument that still exists should remain divided when the place that made it has prepared a home for it.

Chapter 6: Your Turn

Argue Both Sides

From hedged sentences to a debate you cannot choose

Quick Fire

Calibrate the Claim

1 / 6

Calibrate: "This is the most famous restitution case ever."

  • This is arguably the most famous restitution case in the world.
  • Arguably admits that someone could name another case.

Calibrate: "The permit was fake."

  • The permit, purportedly issued in 1801, has never been found.
  • State what is known, hedge what is claimed.

Concede and answer: "They were safe in London, so they should stay."

  • Granted, they were safe in London; safety alone does not settle ownership.
  • Granted + the point + semicolon + your reply.

Concede and answer: "Returning them will empty every museum."

  • While one return might encourage others, it does not necessarily follow that museums would be emptied.
  • It does not necessarily follow that: a polite way to block a slippery slope.

Remove the excess: "It could perhaps possibly be argued that the claim is maybe weak."

  • It could be argued that the claim is weak.
  • One hedge per claim. More than one is not caution, it is fog.

State it with evidence: "Everyone agrees opinion is changing."

  • The evidence suggests that public opinion is moving faster than the law.
  • Attribute the claim to evidence, not to everyone.
