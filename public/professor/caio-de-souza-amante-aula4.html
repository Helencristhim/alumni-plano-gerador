Reading

The Number That Did Not Matter

The Number That Did Not Matter

Paulo Ferraz sold sixty percent of his logistics software company in 2021, and he says the number people ask about is the only number that did not matter. “Everybody wants to know the headline price,” he says. “The headline price was agreed in about forty minutes. We spent the next eleven weeks on everything else, and everything else is where the deal was actually won and lost.”

What took eleven weeks was a sequence of conditions. Roughly a third of the price was an earnout — money that would only arrive if the company hit agreed numbers over the following three years. The buyer wanted the threshold set at growth of twenty percent a year. Paulo wanted it at twelve. “If you set it at twenty, I will spend three years running the company for your spreadsheet instead of for the business,” he told them, and he says that sentence was the most useful thing he said in the whole process. They settled at fourteen, with a clause that excluded any quarter affected by a currency move over a certain size.

The second fight was about people. The buyer wanted the four senior engineers locked in for three years; Paulo refused to sign anything that made a payment contingent on individuals staying, on the grounds that he could not honestly promise it. Instead they wrote a retention pool that the engineers themselves would receive, and the payment attached to the team as a whole rather than to any one name. “If you tie money to four specific people, you have just told those four people exactly what they are worth,” he says. “That is not a retention plan. That is an auction.”

He is blunt about the part founders underestimate. The term sheet was not binding — almost none of them are, apart from the confidentiality section and the non-disclosure agreement signed weeks before anything else. “People treat the term sheet like a contract and it is not. It is a description of what both sides currently believe. What makes it hold is that neither side wants to reopen it.” Closing was delayed twice, both times for reasons that had nothing to do with either company.

Asked what he would tell a founder starting the same process, he does not talk about price. “Decide, before the first meeting, what you would walk away from. Write it down. If you have not decided that, every condition they propose sounds reasonable, because you have nothing to measure it against. I had three things on my list. I gave up two of them. But I knew I was giving them up, and that is a completely different thing from finding out afterwards.”

Chapter 4: If You Move

The Sentence That Gives Without Giving Away

The shape a negotiator uses more than any other

Grammar Discovery

Which One Is Not an Offer?

"If you move on the threshold, we will move on the timetable."

"If closing slips past June, the whole timetable will change."

"If revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays in full."

"We will not sign anything unless the clause protects the team."

Look at the red half of every sentence: not one of them contains will, even though all four are about the future. The green half is where will lives. And the orange one is not an offer at all — it is a rule that fires by itself.

JobFormExample
An offer, a trade, a condition you proposeIf + present simple, will + bare verbIf you move on price, we will move on timing.
A risk you are flaggingsame shape, and the result is the consequenceIf closing slips, the timetable will change.
An automatic rule, not a negotiationIf + present, + presentIf revenue passes the threshold, the earnout pays.
A line you will not crossunless = if notWe will not sign unless the clause protects the team.
Softer, more formalwould instead of will in the resultIf you moved on the threshold, we would consider it.
The trapwill never follows ifNot if you will accept. Say if you accept.
The permanent targetafter if, he/she/it still takes the -sNot if the buyer accept. Say if the buyer accepts.

In one line: the condition stays in the present. Only the result gets a future.

Listening 1 · A Deal Lawyer

How to Say No Without Saying No

A deal lawyer on the three sentences that keep a negotiation alive. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does she say happens when a founder simply says no?
The other side stops proposing, and once they stop proposing, the founder has lost the only information he was getting.
2. What is the first sentence she recommends, and what does it do?
If you move on that, we can move on this — it refuses and offers at the same time, so nothing closes.
3. What does she say about the word unless?
That it is the politest hard line in English, because it names the condition instead of naming the refusal.

What an Earnout Really Costs

A European fund partner on the condition founders accept too quickly. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Why does she say founders accept an earnout too quickly?
Because it raises the headline number, and the headline number is the one they will repeat to everybody afterwards.
2. What does she say an earnout really is?
Three more years of working for somebody else, on numbers somebody else chose.
3. What is her advice about the threshold?
To negotiate the threshold harder than the price, because if the threshold is wrong the price is theoretical.
