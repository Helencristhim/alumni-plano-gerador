The Recording He Never Played Back

On the first evening of the program he recorded three minutes about himself and never played the file back. It was, by every account including his own, painful: long silences, a sentence started three times, and a vocabulary that ran out exactly where the interesting part began. Had anyone told him that evening that he would one day chair a quarterly call in English, he would have laughed. What he did not understand at the time was that the recording was not a verdict on him. It was a baseline.

Sixteen sessions later the same man opened the same file and could not finish it. Not because it was bad, but because it was somebody else. He has since said that the strangest part was hearing how hard he once had to work for sentences that are now second nature. If he had kept a diary, he says, he would be able to point at the week it turned; but nothing turned in a week. The gain was incremental, which is precisely why he could not feel it while it was happening. Nobody notices the moment a plateau ends.

His teacher had him record the same three minutes again on the last evening — same prompt, same clock, no notes. He was told to speak off the cuff, and he did. There were still mistakes; there always are. But he held his own for three minutes, he asked two questions back, and he closed on a number. His blind spot, he now admits, was never grammar. It was the half-second of silence he used to fill with an apology, and which he now fills with a line he can fall back on: give me a second, let me put that another way.

He would be the first to say that a course does not finish anything. By this time next year he will have been leading in English for eighteen months, and by the time the next audit committee meets he will have presented four sets of results in a language that was, not long ago, the reason he stayed quiet. That is not fluency arriving one morning. It is a benchmark moving — slowly, and then all at once — because somebody was willing to be recorded badly on a Tuesday and to keep the file.

Chapter 4: The Code

Standing in the Future, Looking Back

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

'By the time the board meets, we will have closed the quarter.'

'By December I will have presented four sets of results in English.'

'By the fourteenth I will have sent you the deck.'

'By this time next year I will have been leading in English for eighteen months.'

All four sentences stand at a point in the future. What has to appear in every one of them?

FormUseExample
will have + past participlefinished before a future pointBy Friday I will have signed it.
will have been + -ingstill running at that point, seen as a lengthBy June I will have been leading the team for two years.
by + a future pointthe deadline the sentence looks back fromby then · by 2027 · by the fourteenth
by the time + PRESENT tensethe deadline clause never takes willBy the time they ask, I will have prepared it.
present perfect vs future perfectlooking back from now vs from laterI have led it for six months. / By June I will have led it for a year.

Two questions decide the form. Is it finished at that point, or still running? Finished takes will have done; still running takes will have been doing. And where is the deadline? If the deadline is a clause, it stays in the present — by the time they ask, never by the time they will ask.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"By the time they will ask, I will have prepared it."
"By the time they ask, I will have prepared it."
"Until Friday I will have signed the contract."
"By Friday I will have signed the contract."
"By June I will work here for two years."
"By June I will have been working here for two years."

Until is the whole stretch of time before a moment; by is the deadline itself. And a clause after by the time stays in the present: the future is already carried by the main verb.

Dialogue

A Call You Could Not Have Taken

S
Felipe, Sarah Whitmore. A year ago I called you about a CFO role and you took the call in English for the first time. I want to put you in front of a board next month. Before I do — how would you describe where you are now?
F
Honestly? I'd say I've come a long way, though I'd stop short of calling it finished. Twelve months ago I could follow everything and produce almost nothing. Now I chair the quarterly call myself.
S
That is a real shift. What made the difference, do you think?
F
Repetition, mostly. It is muscle memory more than knowledge — the phrases arrive before I choose them. And I stopped apologizing for the pause. Now I fall back on one line: let me put that another way.
S
Some candidates tell me they are fluent and then freeze on the first difficult question. Where would you say your blind spot still is?
F
On a bad line, with three people talking at once. I hold my own in a meeting; a crowded call is still work. I would rather be candid about that than oversell it.
S
That answer is exactly why I am calling. One more thing — the board meets on the fourteenth. Will you be ready?
F
By the fourteenth I will have presented these numbers four times, so yes. And by then I will have been running this function in English for a full year. It will not be my first difficult room.
S
Good. I will send you the brief tonight. You have changed since that first call, haven't you?
F
I have. Same numbers — different voice.

0:000:00

Not grammar — speed. You still understand everything, you just take a second longer to produce it.

Quick Fire

Which Tool Does This Moment Need?

1 / 6

A board member asks about a decision that went wrong three years ago.

  • If we had seen the pricing issue earlier, we would not have taken the write-off.
  • The regret sits entirely in the past: third conditional.

You have fifteen minutes and four strangers, and nobody in the room knows where you are taking them.

  • I will take you through three things: where we are, what changed, and what I need from you.
  • Signposting. Name the map before the journey, not during it.

You disagree with the chief executive in front of his own board.

  • I take your point, and I would want to flag one risk before we commit to that.
  • Hedge first, then land the objection. Never the reverse.

A recruiter asks you to describe a time you turned something around.

  • The division was losing money; the pricing model was the root cause; I rebuilt it in a quarter; margin went from minus three to plus six.
  • STAR, and finish on the number.

Someone asks whether you will be ready for the board meeting on the fourteenth.

  • By the fourteenth I will have presented these numbers four times.
  • Future perfect: stand at the deadline and look back at what is already done.

The line is bad and you have lost the last sentence completely.

  • Sorry, I lost you there. Could you give me that last part again?
  • Ask cleanly. Apologize for the line, never for your English.
