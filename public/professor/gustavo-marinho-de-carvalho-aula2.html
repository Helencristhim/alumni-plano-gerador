A British story that was read everywhere, and understood differently everywhere

Reading

Read for the Main Idea

The Six-Month Experiment

In 2022 a group of British companies agreed to try something unusual. For six months their staff worked four days a week instead of five, for the same pay. The companies came from very different places: software, marketing, finance, and a fish and chip shop. Researchers followed them from the first week to the last.

The rule was simple. Nobody was asked to work longer hours on the four days, and nobody lost money. In exchange, the teams had to find a way to keep the work going. Most of them started with meetings, because meetings were the easiest thing to cut. Some shortened them, some limited who had to attend, and a few removed whole categories of meeting from the calendar.

When the six months ended, most of the companies that had taken part decided to keep the shorter week. That result travelled around the world in a single headline, and the coverage in each country asked a different question about it. In Britain a lot of the reporting asked whether the trial had been designed well enough. Elsewhere the same story was often reported as a decision that had already been made, and the only question was when it would arrive.

This is worth noticing, because it happens with almost every international story. The event does not change when it crosses a border. The question does. If you have ever tried to talk about the news with somebody from another country and felt that the two of you were discussing different things, this is usually why.

Grammar Discovery

What Do These Four Sentences Do?

"The vote took place last Tuesday, and two newspapers reported it the same morning."

"Prices have gone up since January."

"I have read three articles about it this week, and I still do not know the outcome."

"She pointed out the problem yesterday, and nothing has changed since then."

The red sentences name a finished moment: last Tuesday, the same morning. The blue ones name no moment at all — they tell you what is different now. The orange sentence does both, in that order, and that is what a real answer sounds like.

In one line: the question is not when it happened. It is whether the sentence names the moment.

Listening 1 · Business Radio

Two Countries, Two Questions

A short radio piece on why the same story becomes two conversations. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. According to the speaker, what did NOT change between the two countries?
The event itself. Nobody lied and nobody invented anything.
2. What were the two different questions each country asked?
One asked how much it will cost, and the other asked who decided it.
3. What single question does she recommend, and why does it work?
What did people talk about where you are. It works because you stop trying to agree about the event and start asking about the question, and it works even when you have only read the headline.

Listening 2 · A Voice Note

Leave the Verdict at Home

A voice note about talking politics with a client abroad. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What did he use to believe, and who changed his mind?
He used to think the safe move was to say nothing at all. A colleague told him the opposite, and he says she was right.
2. He says the risk is not the subject. What is it?
The verdict. You can bring up almost any story if you bring it as a question instead of as a conclusion.
3. What happens, according to him, if you announce what you think first?
The room gets quiet, and it stays quiet for the rest of the call.
Real Document

The Briefing in Your Inbox

The Morning Briefing — Thursday
Three things to know before nine · 4 min read

1. The four-day week is back in the headlines. A second group of companies has announced a trial for next year. The first one took place in 2022, and most of the companies that took part kept the shorter week afterwards.

2. Shipping costs have risen again. Prices have gone up every month since January, and the increase is now widespread across Europe and South America. No single source explains it.

3. The vote nobody expected. It took place on Tuesday and it got three days of coverage everywhere. Nobody knows the outcome yet, because the result has not been confirmed.

Sample briefing written for this lesson. The items are examples, not real reports.
1. In item 1, which sentence names a finished moment, and how do you know?
"The first one took place in 2022." The year 2022 is finished, so the verb is the past simple. The announcement of the second trial has no date, so it uses has announced.
2. In item 2, why is it "have gone up" and not "went up"?
Because "since January" means it started before and is still happening. There is no finished moment in the sentence.
3. Take item 3. Say in two sentences what happened and what has changed.
A possible answer: The vote took place on Tuesday and it got three days of coverage. Since then nothing has been confirmed, so nobody knows the outcome.
