Metodologia — 5 Abordagens

1. Aula conversada, com intenção

Ele pediu "muito mais conversada do que assim, ah, ok, a gente vai fazer esse exercício". Cada slide é ponto de partida para conversa sobre o trabalho real dele. Quando ele trouxer um problema do GRI, siga o problema — a estrutura da aula volta depois.

2. Inteligibilidade, não sotaque

O objetivo não é soar americano. É que o investidor de Xangai ache a palavra que importa. Toda aula trabalha acento tônico nas palavras longas do setor, ritmo e pausa antes da ideia principal.

3. Feedback medido

Ele quer saber "como é que está" depois de 10 anos. No fim de cada role-play livre, dê números: quantas frases com a palavra-chave no fim, quantas palavras novas usadas, e uma observação de acento. A aula 8 repete o benchmark da aula 1.

4. Estrutura por descoberta, em registro executivo

A gramática de C1 aparece em frases do setor de infraestrutura, é nomeada só depois e é cobrada no palco: cleft, inversão, condicional invertida, reported speech. Nada de regra isolada.

5. Correção direta, em bloco

A tolerância dele ao erro é altíssima, então a correção pode ser franca — mas no fim de cada atividade, nunca no meio da frase, para não quebrar o fluxo de uma fala que ele já sustenta sozinho.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Most executives believe the problem at a dinner table is vocabulary. It rarely is. The problem is where the important word lands. In a room where English is everyone’s second language, people listen for the end of the sentence, because that is where the voice falls. If the key idea is buried in the middle, it disappears.

English has a family of structures that solve this. The first is the wh-cleft: instead of “Investors want stability,” you say “What investors want is stability.” The sentence takes longer, and that is the point: the room waits for the last word. A variation signals that you have thought about it: “What I have come to realize is that the pipeline is rarely the problem.” Notice the word that. It cannot disappear.

When you want to give a personal view without sounding weak, use “The way I see it, greenfield is still where the risk sits.” Never “the way how I see it” — that is Portuguese in English clothes. When you want to make a request smaller and harder to refuse, use All: “All we need from the regulatory framework is a date.”

Finally, when the action is the news, put it in focus with do: “What the ministry did was postpone the auction for the concession.” None of these structures is decoration. Each one decides which word the investor from Abu Dhabi will remember when the dinner is over.

Who Gets Paid First
Adapted from a comment essay on infrastructure capital · ~650 words

Ask an engineer what an infrastructure project is and you will hear about geology, spans and traffic forecasts. Ask an investor and you will hear about something considerably less photogenic: the order of a queue. Beneath the concrete, every large scheme is a hierarchy of promises, and almost all of the intelligence in financing one goes into deciding who stands where in that hierarchy. 1 The answer sets the price of every dollar that goes in, and it explains behaviour that, from the outside, looks merely timid.

At the front stand the senior lenders. They advance the bulk of the money, they are repaid before anyone else, and they are famously unimaginative about risk. 2 Behind them sits subordinated debt, repaid only once the senior banks have been made whole, and charging handsomely for the privilege of waiting. At the very back, collecting whatever survives, sits equity, which is to say the party that is paid last and blamed first.

This layering explains a great deal that would otherwise look irrational. A pension fund that turns down a road promising fifteen percent in favour of one offering nine is not being cowardly; it is reading the queue. 3 What it is buying, in the second case, is not a return at all but a position: near the front, protected, and dull enough to survive a committee.

The arrangement has consequences for what actually gets built. The institutions with the deepest pockets are also the ones with the strictest mandates, and their money therefore flows towards assets that are already operating and already earning. 4 The effect shows up first in the price. Too much capital pursuing too few operating assets has compressed yields across the sector, so that the safe road now pays less than it did a decade ago, while the unbuilt one still cannot find a lender at any price.

Hence the instruments designed to rearrange the queue itself. A development bank that agrees to take the first loss on a solar cluster is not being generous; it is buying private money, and it knows roughly what it is paying. Guarantees, first-loss tranches, political risk insurance and concessional lending all perform the same operation by different means: they move a specific danger from the party that cannot carry it to the party that can. 5 Practitioners call the result blended finance, and they judge it not by how much public money is spent but by how much private money arrives behind it.

There is a subtler reason these structures matter, and it concerns the first investor rather than the largest. Funds are institutions, but they are staffed by people, and people are reluctant to be the only name on a page. 6 After that the queue fills with surprising speed, and the same committees that hesitated for two years will complain that their allocation was too small.

All of which the language of the sector is beautifully designed to obscure. Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Speakers who open conferences for a living use one structure that most people only ever meet in writing. It is called inversion, and it works like a spotlight: it takes a limiting word, puts it at the front of the sentence, and forces the room to wait for the rest.

Compare “Capital has not been this cautious since 2008” with “Not since 2008 has capital been this cautious.” The facts are the same. The second version marks an inflection point. Notice what happens after the limiting phrase: the auxiliary comes before the subject, exactly as in a question. “Rarely do we see every stakeholder at the same table.” “Only by fixing the contracts can we move the money.”

If the verb has no auxiliary, English lends it one: do, does or did. “Not only did the auction close, but it closed early.” Forgetting the auxiliary is the most common mistake: “Rarely we see” sounds like a sentence that stopped halfway.

The last rule is about taste, not grammar. One inversion sets the tone. Two in a row sound like a sermon. Use it once, in the sentence you want remembered, pause after it, and move on to a show of hands, the rhetorical pivot or the moment you hand over to the first panelist.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

An agreement made on stage is a hope. It becomes a commitment when it has three things: a name, a date and a public way to check it. The language of a good closing address is built to add those three things, and English has two future forms that do most of the work.

The first is the future perfect: will have + past participle. It describes something that will be finished before a moment in the future: “By the end of March, the ministry will have published its draft.” Put a name and a date in the sentence and it becomes an action item. Notice the time word: by March, not until March, and by the time we meet, not by the time we will meet.

The second is the future continuous: will be + -ing. It places the listener inside a moment in the future: “This time next year, we will be discussing projects, not frameworks.” A host who says this is not predicting. He is creating a meeting that everyone in the room now has a reason to attend.

Together with a one-sentence recap of the through line and a single call to action, these two forms turn applause into a roadmap. And the public scorecard at the next roundtable will show who decided to follow through on what they said.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Future Perfect and Future Continuous

GRAMMAR

Will have done closes a promise before a date; will be doing puts the room inside the future.

Three short tasks on material you already read for work. No new sources, no screen time you were not going to spend anyway.
