A Beam of Light in a Tax Collector's Room

A Beam of Light in a Tax Collector's Room

In the summer of 1600, a young painter from Lombardy unveiled two large canvases in a side chapel of the church of San Luigi dei Francesi in Rome. It was his first major public commission, and it was not, strictly speaking, an altarpiece: the paintings hang on the side walls of the chapel, facing each other across a narrow space that is, for most of the day, almost completely dark. One of them, The Calling of Saint Matthew, would change the way European painters thought about light for the next half century.

The subject is simple enough. According to the Gospel, Christ passed a tax collector named Matthew at his table and said, 'Follow me', and Matthew rose and followed. Earlier painters had usually set the scene in an idealized landscape or a dignified hall. Caravaggio set it in a room that looks unmistakably like a Roman tavern. Five men sit around a table counting coins, two of them in the feathered hats and slashed sleeves of fashionable young men. Christ and Peter enter from the right, almost lost in shadow, and Christ extends a hand whose limp, foreshortened gesture seems to reach into our own space.

What makes the painting unforgettable, however, is not its cast but its light. A single beam of raking light cuts diagonally across the wall above Christ's head and falls on the faces at the table. Everything else sinks into darkness. The contrast is so stark that the figures in the penumbra seem to emerge from the canvas rather than sit within it. This extreme form of chiaroscuro, in which most of the picture is plunged into darkness and only a few areas are violently lit, is usually called tenebrism, and it was Caravaggio, more than anyone, who made it a language.

It is tempting to read that beam as a purely theatrical device, a spotlight aimed at the moment of choice. Yet the light may be more literal than it looks. Visitors to the chapel have long noted that its direction seems to correspond to the real window high above the altar, as if the daylight of the church itself were entering the scene. If that reading is right, the painting does not merely depict an event from the past; it stages the calling in the viewer's present, in the very room where the viewer stands.

Nor is it settled which man is Matthew. For centuries the bearded figure pointing at his own chest was assumed to be the saint, asking in disbelief whether he could possibly be the one being called. Some historians now argue that the bearded man is pointing instead at the young man hunched at the end of the table, who refuses to lift his eyes from the coins. The disagreement is not a footnote. It is the ambiguity of the gesture that keeps the painting alive, because the viewer has to decide, as Matthew did, what the moment means.

Some contemporaries found this unflinching naturalism provocative. A sacred story acted out by ordinary faces in a smoky room seemed to some viewers undignified, even vulgar, and several of Caravaggio's later religious commissions were criticized or turned down. It is easy, knowing the violent and chaotic life he went on to lead, to explain the paintings through the man. That explanation is less illuminating than it appears. What distinguishes the Calling is not the biography behind it but a set of decisions visible on the canvas: the tavern, the heightened contrast, the hand that does not quite point, the light that seems to come from the viewer's world.

Those decisions proved pivotal. Within a generation, painters across Europe had absorbed his lessons, and the darkness he invented became, in their hands, a new way of telling stories.

Chapter 4: The Style Code

The Sentence That Points

Cleft sentences: how critics tell you where to look

Discover the Style

Where Is the Writer Pointing?

"It is the light, not the gesture, that decides the meaning of the scene."

"What distinguishes tenebrism from ordinary shadow is the violence of the contrast."

"It was not beauty that Caravaggio pursued, but a kind of truth."

"All he needed was a window, a table and a handful of ordinary faces."

Rewrite each sentence without the colored frame. What exactly does the frame add?

FormWhat it doesExample
It is / was + focus + that / whoputs one element under a spotlight, often in contrast with anotherIt is the light that calls Matthew.
What + clause + is / was + focusbuilds suspense and delivers the focus at the endWhat unsettled his critics was the dirty feet.
It is not X but Y thatcorrects an expectation the reader probably hasIt was not piety but naturalism that provoked them.
All / The only thing + clause + isemphasizes how little was neededAll he needed was a single window.
The C2 trappointing at nothing. A cleft on a vague focus ("What I think is that art is important") is filler with good posture. Use it once or twice per paragraph, on the claim that matters.

Last week we looked at Caravaggio himself. Today I want to talk about what happened after him, because his story has a strange shape. His influence spread astonishingly fast. Within twenty years painters from Naples to Utrecht were using his dramatic light, his ordinary models and his dark backgrounds. And yet, after his death, his own reputation faded for a very long time, and for centuries many writers treated him as a talented but vulgar painter. Among the artists who absorbed his lessons, three are worth remembering today. Artemisia Gentileschi used the same violent contrasts, but she told stories, often about women, from a perspective that very few painters had given them. Georges de La Tour, in France, turned the beam of light into a single candle and made darkness feel quiet rather than violent. And the young Rembrandt, who never went to Italy, learned the language of shadow from painters who had. Art historians often call these followers the Caravaggisti. The term is useful, but I find it slightly misleading, because it suggests copying. The best of them borrowed his light in order to say something entirely their own.

Chapter 6: Your Turn

Point at What Matters

From cleft sentences to a spoken formal analysis

Quick Fire

Make It Point

1 / 6

Make it point: "The light calls Matthew."

  • It is the light that calls Matthew.
  • It is + focus + that. Stress the focus when you say it.

Make it point: "The setting shocked viewers."

  • What shocked viewers was the setting.
  • What + clause + was + focus puts the answer at the end.

Correct the expectation: "People think the gesture matters most, but the light matters most."

  • It is not the gesture but the light that matters most.
  • Not X but Y: correct the reader in one sentence.

Make it point: "He only needed one window."

  • All he needed was a single window.
  • All + clause + was emphasizes how little was required.

Make it point about a person: "The young man refuses the call."

  • It is the young man who refuses the call.
  • Who for people is more precise in formal writing.

Upgrade with a cleft and a term from today: "There is a lot of dark in the painting."

  • What dominates the canvas is darkness, broken by a single beam of raking light.
  • Combine the cleft with a precise term instead of a lot of.
