Read for the Main Idea

The Same Favor, Three Ways

How Much Politeness Is Too Much?

Imagine you need directions in a new city. In Oslo, Where is the station? is a perfectly normal question. Norwegians are famously straightforward: they get to the point, and a long, careful question can even sound strange to them, as if you were hiding something.

Ask the same thing in London and it may come across as a little blunt. A British speaker is more likely to say Sorry, could you tell me where the station is? or even I was just wondering if you knew where the station was. The British tend to hedge, especially when they feel they are imposing on somebody.

Americans usually sit somewhere in the middle. They are friendly and quick: Hi! Do you know where the station is? is courteous enough almost everywhere in the United States. Too much hedging can even sound awkward, as if the question were a bigger favor than it is.

Notice what happens to the grammar as the question becomes more polite. Where is the station? becomes Could you tell me where the station is? The question moves to the front, and the second half goes back to the normal order of a statement. So the polite version is not only longer. It is built differently. And the skill at this level is not knowing one polite form, but choosing the right one for the person in front of you.

Chapter 4: The Code

The Question Moves to the Front

direct · polite · very polite — and the word order that changes

Grammar Discovery

From Direct to Very Polite

"Where is the station?"

"Could you tell me where the station is?"

"Do you happen to know if the kitchen closes at nine?"

"I was wondering if you could help me with my bags."

These four questions go from the most direct to the most polite. Look at the colored words. In the first one, the verb comes BEFORE the subject. What happens to the order in the other three? And where did the word do go?

FormWhat it doesExample
direct questionVerb before the subject. Fine with friends, and in Norway.Where is the station?
Could you tell me + wh-wordPolite. The second half has statement order.Could you tell me where the station is?
Do you know + if / whetherFor yes/no questions. No do in the second half.Do you know if the kitchen closes at nine?
Would you mind + -ingA polite request. The answer no means yes, I will.Would you mind opening the window?
I was wondering if + couldVery polite, typical in the UK. Past forms make it softer.I was wondering if you could help me.
choose the levelThe more you impose, and the more British the listener, the higher you go.a coffee in Oslo ≠ a favor in London

In one line: the polite part goes to the front, and the question itself goes back to normal order.

Answer on the Spot

Read each situation. Answer out loud, then tap Tips for support language.

1 / 6

You are in London and you need to find a pharmacy. Ask a stranger.

  • Sorry, could you tell me where the nearest pharmacy is?
  • Sorry + could you tell me + statement order.

In Oslo, you want to know the price of a sandwich. Ask the Norwegian way.

  • How much is this sandwich?
  • Direct is fine in Norway. Too much hedging sounds strange.

You want to know if the museum is open on Mondays. Ask politely.

  • Do you know if the museum is open on Mondays?
  • A yes/no question inside: if or whether.

You need your British host to turn down the music. Ask without sounding pushy.

  • I was wondering if you could turn the music down a little.
  • I was wondering if + could: very polite, very British.

On a train, ask the person next to you to move their bag.

  • Would you mind moving your bag, please?
  • Would you mind + -ing. If the answer is no, it means yes.

An American friend says: just ask, do not beat around the bush. Ask for a ride to the airport.

  • Could you give me a ride to the airport on Friday?
  • Could you is enough. No need for I was wondering here.
