Jornada de Transformação

De: Advogada que lê contratos master internacionais em inglês sem nenhuma dificuldade, mas trava na hora de explicar oralmente o que encontrou — e que descreveu a aula em grupo como "uma tortura" pela dinâmica competitiva.

Para: Profissional que passa o feedback jurídico ao exterior com clareza, vocabulário técnico preciso e uma estrutura pronta na cabeça — sabendo exatamente o que está fazendo e por quê.

Promessa do Programa

"De 'parecia uma tortura' a conseguir explicar o problema do contrato com clareza — sem travar, sem dar branco, com as palavras certas na hora certa."

Forças

  • Vocabulário receptivo jurídico já consolidado — lê contratos internacionais complexos sem dificuldade
  • Raciocínio analítico e estruturado: exige lógica explícita e a usa como alavanca de aprendizagem
  • Autonomia real de estudo — fazia o material do formato em grupo sozinha, e bem
  • Metacognição alta: nomeia com precisão cirúrgica onde trava ("é na hora de explicar o que eu entendi")
  • Motivação concreta e recorrente: reuniões de feedback com gestores no exterior, mais duas viagens a trabalho em novembro e dezembro

Pontos de Melhoria

  • Produção oral espontânea em inglês técnico-jurídico — o gargalo declarado do programa
  • Repertório ativo limitado: reconhece a palavra na página, não a recupera na fala
  • Tempo de processamento PT→EN alto — pensa em português e traduz, e aí trava
  • Ausência de estrutura pronta para abrir uma explicação técnica: começa do zero toda vez
  • Dinâmica de grupo competitiva a paralisa — precisa de espaço um-a-um, sem disputa por turno de fala

A Jornada — narrativa do programa

Ana Paula é advogada e analisa contratos master internacionais — contratos guarda-chuva que atendem vários países ao mesmo tempo. A análise ela faz em português, sob as regras brasileiras, mas o contrato está em inglês e o feedback vai para o exterior. O inglês dela não está ausente: ela lê esses documentos todos os dias e o vocabulário jurídico já é familiar. O que colapsa é a PRODUÇÃO — nas palavras dela, "o problema é na hora de passar o feedback, é na hora de explicar o que eu entendi do problema que tem no contrato". Essa frase é o eixo do programa inteiro.

Ela vem do formato em grupo (chegou ao Essential 4) e fazia as aulas individuais com material que não era dela — a competitividade do grupo a travava e ela mesma nomeou a aula como "uma tortura". Por isso o programa não começa com diagnóstico: ela já é aluna, a consultoria já mapeou o gargalo, e gastar a primeira aula remapeando seria desperdiçar exatamente o tempo que ela mais quer. A Aula 1 entra direto no cenário crítico — dizer em voz alta o que está errado numa cláusula — e entrega já na primeira sessão a estrutura de três linhas (problema → consequência → recomendação) que ela vai usar na reunião real da semana. Da Aula 2 em diante o programa constrói camada por camada dentro do universo contratual dela: reportar o que o contrato diz, estruturar um parecer falado, comunicar risco com condicionais, sustentar uma recomendação sob objeção. Cada aula abre nomeando o foco linguístico do dia — ela disse que "se não tiver lógica, não dá jeito, não consigo passar de etapa".

Metodologia — 5 Abordagens

1. Converter leitura em fala

O inglês da Ana já existe em modo receptivo — ela lê contratos master sem esforço. O programa não ensina esse vocabulário: ele o move da página para a boca, sempre dentro do universo contratual dela.

2. Estrutura antes de vocabulário

Ela trava porque começa do zero toda vez, não por falta de palavras. Cada aula entrega um molde pronto — a estrutura de 3 linhas da Aula 1 é o primeiro — para que a fala tenha por onde começar.

3. Gramática com lógica explícita

"Se não tiver lógica, não dá jeito." A regra nunca vem primeiro — exemplos, percepção, sistematização —, mas o foco linguístico do dia é anunciado na abertura. Saber onde está a orienta e reduz a ansiedade.

4. Um-a-um, sem disputa de turno

A aula em grupo a paralisava pela competitividade — ela chamou de "tortura". Aqui o silêncio é dela: ela processa antes de falar (9/10 em reflexividade) e o professor não preenche a pausa.

5. Material real dela

Cenário classificado como crítico no perfil: ela pode trazer um contrato ou uma reunião real e a aula se reorganiza em torno disso. O programa é a trilha, não a prisão.

Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Flagging the Problem -- Saying What Is Wrong With a Clause

Turning the contract language you already read into the sentences you say out loud. Key words: clause, provision, liability, breach, termination, indemnity, ambiguous, governing law, amendment, scope, enforceable, waiver. Structures: modals of obligation and recommendation -- must and have to for what the contract or the law requires, should for what you recommend, and the difference between must not and do not have to.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the definition. You already recognise these on the page -- the goal now is to say them.

Clause -- a numbered section of a contract that sets one rule
"Clause fourteen is the one that worries me."
Provision -- a specific condition or requirement written into a contract
"The provision on data is too broad."
Liability -- legal responsibility for a loss, a cost or a damage
"This clause puts all the liability on our side."
Breach -- when one side does not do what the contract requires
"A late payment is already a breach of contract."
Termination -- the end of a contract, by agreement or by one side alone
"Termination requires ninety days of notice."
Indemnity -- a promise to cover the loss or the cost of the other side
"The indemnity has no limit, and that is the risk."
Ambiguous -- written in a way that allows more than one meaning
"The wording here is ambiguous, and that is dangerous."
Governing law -- the country whose law decides how a contract is read
"The governing law is English law, not Brazilian law."
Amendment -- an official change made to a contract after signature
"We need a short amendment, not a new contract."
Scope -- the limits of what a contract actually covers
"That service is outside the scope of the agreement."
Enforceable -- valid enough that a court can force a company to obey it
"That penalty is simply not enforceable here."
Waiver -- giving up a right that the contract gives you
"Signing this is a waiver of our right to claim."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Ana reviews a master agreement and finds a problem in clause fourteen. Under that clause, the supplier must cover every claim against the client, and the indemnity has no limit. In Brazil, a company has to follow local rules on liability, so part of that provision is probably not enforceable anyway. The governing law, however, is English law. Her recommendation is short: the client should cap the amount and add local wording in a simple amendment. The parties must not sign before that change. The rest of the document is fine, so the client does not have to renegotiate the whole agreement.

1. Why does the text say "the supplier must cover" and not "should cover"?
A It is an obligation written in the contract itself, not an opinion.
B It is only Ana's personal recommendation.
C It is something that already happened.
2. "A company has to follow local rules" uses "has to" because:
A The obligation comes from this particular contract.
B The obligation comes from outside -- the law, not the parties.
C It is a suggestion the client can ignore.
3. The text says the client "should cap the amount". This is:
A A prohibition -- the client is not allowed to do it.
B An obligation under English law.
C Ana's professional recommendation -- the client can still say no.
4. What is the difference between "must not sign" and "does not have to renegotiate"?
A "Must not" means it is forbidden; "does not have to" means it is not necessary.
B They mean exactly the same thing.
C "Must not" is polite and "does not have to" is direct.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modals of Obligation and Recommendation

GRAMMAR

How to separate what the contract requires, what the law requires, and what you recommend.

FormUseExample
must + verbAn obligation written in the contract itself.The supplier must notify us within ten days.
have to / has to + verbAn obligation that comes from outside: the law, the regulator, the court.The company has to keep the records for five years.
should + verbYour professional recommendation. The client can still say no.We should replace this wording before signature.
must not / cannotProhibition -- it is forbidden.The parties must not disclose the commercial terms.
do not have toNo obligation -- it is optional. This is NOT the same as "must not".You do not have to answer today; the deadline is Friday.
QuestionDo / Does + subject + have to · Should + subject + verbDoes the supplier have to notify us? / Should we cap the indemnity?
Two trapsNever to after must or should: "must sign", not "must to sign". Never -s in the third person: "she should review", not "she shoulds review".

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Reporting What the Contract Says -- Quoting a Clause Without Reading It

Nobody can follow legal drafting by ear, so you never read the clause out loud -- you report it. Key words: party, obligation, deadline, notice, confidentiality, warranty, penalty, renewal, draft, wording, term, counterparty. Structures: reporting verbs for a document in force (states, requires, provides, specifies) and reported speech for what a person said, with the tense one step back -- plus say versus tell.

Vocabulary

Counterparty -- the other company in the deal

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Ana has to summarise a call for a manager who was not there. She does not read the document out loud. She reports it. The new draft states that the term is three years, and it requires ninety days of notice before renewal. Clause twelve also provides that confidentiality survives the end of the agreement. Then she moves to the people. The lawyer for the counterparty said that his client wanted a longer term, and he told her they could accept sixty days if the penalty came down. That second part is not an obligation yet: it is not in the wording. One column is the document, in the present. The other column is a conversation, one step back.

1. Why is "the draft states that the term is three years" in the present tense?
A The document is in force, so it still says that today.
B Because somebody said it during the call.
C Because the term has already finished.
2. The text says the lawyer "said that his client wanted a longer term". Why "wanted" and not "wants"?
A Because the client changed his mind afterwards.
B Because we are reporting what a person said, so the verb moves one step back.
C Because "want" has no present form after "said".
3. The text uses "he told her" and "he said that". What is the difference?
A "Tell" takes a person after it; "say" does not.
B "Tell" is formal and "say" is informal.
C They are interchangeable in every sentence.
4. Why does Ana say the sixty days is "not in the wording"?
A Because the counterparty never mentioned it.
B Because the manager refused it on the call.
C Because somebody said it in a conversation, but the document still requires ninety.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Reporting a Document and Reporting a Person

GRAMMAR

Two columns that must never be mixed: what the contract says, and what somebody said.

FormUseExample
states / provides / specifies + thatA document in force. It stays in the present, because it still says it today.Clause five states that the term renews automatically.
requires / allows + object + toThe same idea with an obligation or a permission attached to a party.The agreement requires us to give thirty days notice.
said (that) + verb one step backA person, in a conversation that already happened. is → was · wants → wanted · can → could · will → would.He said the deadline was Friday.
told + person + (that)Tell needs a person straight after it. Say never takes one.She told me they would send a new draft.
QuestionWhat does clause X say? · What did they say?What does the draft state about the term? / What did their lawyer say?
Three trapsNever "he said me" -- it is "he told me". Never leave a person's words in the present ("he said the deadline is Friday") -- that reads like an agreed fact. And never put a contract in force in the past ("clause five said that") -- a living document speaks in the present.

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

Making Your Case -- Structuring a Legal Opinion in Spoken English

A spoken opinion cannot build up to the answer: it has to start with it. Key words: finding, assessment, exposure, precedent, compliance, jurisdiction, remedy, dispute, arbitration, due diligence, caveat, material. Structures: the connectors that carry an argument -- in short for the position, first and second for the reasons, therefore for the consequence, however for the caveat -- and the four-slot shape that holds them together.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the definition. These are the words of an opinion, not of a contract.

Finding -- something you discovered while reviewing the document
"I have three findings, and only one of them is serious."
Assessment -- your professional judgement of the situation
"My assessment is that we should not sign this version."
Exposure -- how much the company can lose if things go wrong
"Our exposure under this clause is the whole contract value."
Precedent -- an earlier decision that is used as a rule for later cases
"There is no precedent for that wording in our group."
Compliance -- following the rules that apply to the company
"The compliance annex is missing from this draft."
Jurisdiction -- the country whose courts can decide the case
"That clause sends every dispute to a jurisdiction we do not know."
Remedy -- what the injured side can ask for when a rule is broken
"The only remedy in this draft is a written apology."
Dispute -- a formal disagreement between the two sides
"Every dispute goes abroad under this clause."
Arbitration -- settling a disagreement with a private judge instead of a court
"Arbitration in Singapore is not a neutral choice for us."
Due diligence -- the checking a company must do before it commits
"Without the annex we cannot prove due diligence."
Caveat -- a warning that limits what you have just said
"One caveat: this changes if they accept our wording."
Material -- important enough to change the decision
"It is a defect, but it is not material."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

A manager asks Ana one question: can the group sign on Friday? She does not start with the history of the document. In short, her assessment is that they should not sign this version. Then come the reasons, and there are two. First, the arbitration clause sends every dispute to a jurisdiction where the group has no local lawyer, so the exposure there is real and the only remedy would be slow. Second, the compliance annex is missing, and therefore the group cannot prove due diligence if a regulator asks. Then, and only then, the caveat. However, neither finding is material if the other side accepts the standard wording, and there is precedent for that in two other countries. So, to come back to the question: not this version, but possibly Friday.

1. Why does the text put "In short, her assessment is..." at the beginning?
A Because a spoken opinion has to give the answer first, before the reasons.
B Because "in short" is a polite way to open any conversation.
C Because the manager has already read the analysis.
2. What is the job of "First" and "Second" in the text?
A They show which reason is more serious.
B They order the reasons, so the listener knows how many are coming.
C They introduce a contrast between two opposite ideas.
3. "The annex is missing, and therefore the group cannot prove due diligence." What does "therefore" introduce?
A The cause of the problem.
B The consequence of the fact just mentioned.
C An exception to the rule.
4. Why does the caveat come after the reasons and not before them?
A Because a caveat placed early makes the whole opinion sound uncertain.
B Because the caveat is less important than the vocabulary.
C Because "however" can only appear at the end of a text.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Connectors of Argument and Sequence

GRAMMAR

Four slots, always in the same order: position, two reasons, one caveat, position again.

ConnectorJobExample
In short
The short answer is		Opens with your position. The answer comes first, never last.In short, we should not sign this version.
First ... Second ...Orders your reasons and tells the listener how many are coming. Two is enough.First, the arbitration clause... Second, the annex...
therefore
as a result
which means		Links a fact to its consequence. It comes before the consequence — because comes before the cause.The annex is missing, and therefore we cannot prove due diligence.
However
That said
One caveat		Introduces the caveat, after the reasons and never in the middle of them.However, none of this is material if they accept our wording.
So, to come back to your questionCloses by repeating the position in the same words you used to open.So, to come back to your question: not this version.
Three trapsNever stack connectors: "But however", "Also moreover". It is on the other hand, not "in the other hand". And a spoken opinion never opens with "It depends" — the dependency belongs in the caveat, with a name.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the best answer for each situation.

The manager has four minutes and asks: "Can we sign on Friday?" You start with:
A "The short answer is no, not this version. Two reasons, and one caveat."
B "The agreement was signed in 2019 and amended twice, so let me explain the history."
C "It depends a little on the jurisdiction, there are arguments in both directions."
You found six problems, but only two would change the decision. On the call you present:
A All six, in the order you found them.
B The two material ones, and mention that the rest are minor.
C None of them, until the manager asks a direct question.
You want to link a missing annex to what it prevents. Which is correct?
A "Because the annex is missing, therefore we cannot prove due diligence."
B "The annex is missing, and therefore we cannot prove due diligence."
C "Therefore the annex is missing, we cannot prove due diligence."
There is real uncertainty about one point. The best place for it is:
A Spread across every sentence, with "maybe" and "I think".
B In one named caveat after the reasons, with a size and a condition.
C Left out completely, so the opinion sounds stronger.
The manager interrupts your second reason to ask if the problem is real. You say:
A "It is material, and here is why. Let me finish this one and I will come back to your question."
B "Sorry, sorry, my English is not good, let me start again from the beginning."
C "But however, in the other hand, it depends."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. There is no right or wrong answer.

A manager abroad has four minutes and one question: can we sign this contract? Answer in the four slots, in order -- your position, two reasons (with First and Second, and one therefore), one named caveat, and then your position again. Take your time to think before you start, and try to keep the whole answer under thirty seconds.

Suits -- Season 1, Episode 1 (Pilot)

Tip: watch with English subtitles and write down three sentences where a lawyer flags a problem. Rewrite each one using your own clause and your own client.

Tip: pick one argument you hear and try to say it back in three lines -- the claim, the consequence, the recommendation. Record yourself.

Lesson 2 — Reporting What the Contract Says -- Quoting a Clause Without Reading It

Series
The Diplomat -- Season 1, Episode 1 ("The Cinderella Thing")

An American ambassador spends every scene doing exactly what you did today: telling someone who was not in the room what a document says and what a person said, and keeping those two things apart. The stakes are absurd, the language habit is real.

Tip: watch with English subtitles and write down three sentences that start with a reporting verb. Mark which ones report a text and which report a person.

Podcast
Planet Money (NPR) -- "Why terms of service got so frustratingly awful"

Journalists explain contract law to an audience of non-lawyers, in clear American English, without a single clause read out loud. Listen to how they report what a contract does instead of quoting it -- that is the skill of this lesson, done by professionals who have to hold an audience.

Tip: there is a full transcript on the page. Listen first with the transcript closed, then open it and check the reporting verbs they used.

YouTube
BBC Learning English -- "How to use reported speech" (6 Minute Grammar)

Six minutes on the exact structure you practised today: the tense that moves one step back when you report what somebody said, and the difference between say and tell. British accent, slow and clear -- useful ear training, since half your calls are not with Americans.

Tip: after the video, take three sentences from your last work call and report them out loud. Then check: did the verb move back?

Lesson 3 — Making Your Case -- Structuring a Legal Opinion in Spoken English

Film
The Trial of the Chicago 7 (2020)

Two hours of people making a case out loud, in front of an audience that will not wait. Watch the closing arguments and notice the shape: the position first, the reasons in order, and the concession made on purpose instead of hidden. Written by Aaron Sorkin, so every sentence is built to be heard, not read.

Tip: pick one speech and write down its first sentence. Does it announce the position, or does it build up to it? Then rewrite your last opinion the same way.

Watch on Netflix ↗
Podcast
Lawyer 2 Lawyer (Legal Talk Network) -- any recent episode

Practising American lawyers debating a live legal question for half an hour. What is useful for you is not the law, it is the register: they take a position in the first minute, defend it with two or three reasons, and name the counter-argument instead of pretending it does not exist.

Tip: stop the episode after the first two minutes and say out loud, in one sentence, what each speaker's position is. If you cannot, listen again -- and notice what made it hard.

Listen on Legal Talk Network ↗
Talk
TED -- Julian Treasure, "How to speak so that people want to listen"

Ten minutes on why some people are believed and others are not, and almost none of it is about vocabulary. Pace, pauses and register do most of the work -- which is good news when you are working in a second language and thinking before you speak.

Tip: the talk has an English transcript on the same page. Watch it once without the transcript and once with it, and copy one habit into your next call.
