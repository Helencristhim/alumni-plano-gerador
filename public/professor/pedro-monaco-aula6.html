Chapter 3: The Code

How Much More?

Grading a comparison instead of just making one

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"Rent is far higher there than the salary suggests."

"A ten per cent pay rise buys you slightly less than it sounds."

"Food is nowhere near as expensive as housing."

"There are far fewer flats under that price than there were."

All four sentences compare two things. Three of them also tell you HOW BIG the difference is, and one of them tells you it is small. Which words are doing that work?

FormWhat it doesExample
far · much · a lot · considerablyA BIG difference. These go in front of the comparative, and very never does — that is the single commonest error a Portuguese speaker makes here."far higher" · "much more expensive"
not "very more expensive"
slightly · a bit · a little · marginallyA SMALL difference. Same position, opposite message. Useful when you want to be accurate rather than dramatic."slightly less" · "a bit further out"
not nearly as · nowhere near asA big difference stated the other way round. Nowhere near as is stronger and belongs to speech; not nearly as works everywhere."Food is nowhere near as expensive as housing."
fewer · lessFewer for things you can count, less for things you cannot. And both take far, much or slightly in front of them."far fewer flats"
"far less space"
the ... the ...Two comparatives locked together: change one and the other changes with it. This is the structure the whole reading is built on."The further out you live, the longer you commute."

In Portuguese the same little word grades the plain adjective AND the comparative, so the wrong version arrives whole and at full speed. English splits them: very goes with the plain adjective, and far, much and a lot go with the comparative. Nothing else about this is difficult, and almost everybody gets it wrong under pressure.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"Rent in London is very more expensive than in Sao Paulo."
"Rent in London is far more expensive than in Sao Paulo."
"There are far less flats under that price than there were."
"There are far fewer flats under that price than there were."
"The cost of living is more high than the salary suggests."
"The cost of living is much higher than the salary suggests."

The first is the Portuguese pattern arriving whole: very belongs to the plain adjective, and the comparative takes far, much or a lot. The second is countable against uncountable — flats can be counted, so it is fewer. The third builds the comparative with more on a short adjective that already has its own form: higher, never more high.

Reading

What the Offer Is Worth

What the Offer Is Worth

Every year a few thousand people move to London on a salary that looked generous on the offer letter and turns out to be slightly less than the city needs. The number itself is not the problem. The problem is that almost nothing in London costs what the equivalent thing costs anywhere else, and the differences are not evenly spread: some things are far more expensive than people expect, a few are considerably cheaper, and one thing — housing — is so much more expensive than everything else that it quietly decides the rest of your life there.

Start with the deposit. A tenancy usually asks for five or six weeks of rent up front, held until you leave, plus the first month in advance. That is a great deal of money to find in the same week as a flight, and it is the moment most people discover that their take-home pay is a good deal smaller than the salary they agreed to. Income tax and national insurance come off before anything else, and the disposable income left at the end is the only number that ever mattered.

Then there is the trade-off nobody explains in advance, which is space against time. The further out you live, the more room you get and the longer you commute, and the two do not move at the same speed: twenty minutes further out buys you slightly more space, while forty minutes further out buys you a great deal more. Nearly everybody underestimates how much of their week the journey takes, and hardly anybody moves back in once they have somewhere to put their books. Utilities are higher than in Brazil, food is roughly comparable, and public transport is expensive but so much better than driving that the comparison is not really a comparison.

So is it worth it? Most people who stay say yes, and most of them are not paying for a family. That is the honest version of the answer. The professional opportunity is genuinely larger, the salaries in law and finance are far higher than almost anywhere in Europe, and there is considerably more of everything to do than you will ever get through. It is also a city where a great many people scrape by on incomes that sounded enormous when they accepted them, and where whole neighbourhoods have been priced out in a single decade. Both things are true at once, and anybody who tells you only one of them is selling something.
