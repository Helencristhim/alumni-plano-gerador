Read for the Main Idea

The Question the Demo Did Not Answer

Nobody Buys the Demo

If you ask a vendor why a deal was won, you will usually hear about the product. If you ask the buyer, you will hear about something else entirely. In enterprise software the decision is almost never made in the room where the demo happens. It is made afterwards, by three or four people who were not in that room, and who are asking a question the demo did not answer: what happens to us if this goes wrong.

That is why the pitches that cut through the noise are rarely the most impressive ones. If a supplier only shows what the system does when everything works, the buyer has to imagine the rest, and what people imagine is always worse than the truth. The pitches that win tend to do the opposite. They start from the risk: they explain the data migration before anybody asks, they show the audit trail, they say out loud how long onboarding takes and how many people it needs. If companies do not know what deployment will cost them in hours, they assume the worst, and the worst is what they budget for.

There is a second thing buyers do that vendors consistently underestimate. They talk to each other. A proof of concept that goes badly at one company is discussed at three others within a month, and no amount of marketing survives that conversation. Which is the argument for being conservative about what you promise: if you say the integration will take eight weeks and it takes twelve, you have not lost eight weeks. You have lost the reference. The bottom line is that reducing the buyer's exposure is worth more than adding a feature, and it is a great deal cheaper to do.

Chapter 4: If, Then

The Sentence You Can Be Held To

Two halves, and only one of them takes will

Grammar Discovery

Which Half Takes Will?

"If you automate the filing, your team will save two weeks a quarter."

"If the migration starts in October, we will be live before your fiscal year ends."

"If you have any concerns about deployment, I will walk you through it now."

"If companies do not keep an audit trail, they cannot prove anything afterwards."

The red half never changes shape: it is the plain present, every time. Three blue halves promise a result. One green half does not promise anything — it states what is always true.

JobFormExample
A real, probable promiseIf + present, ... will + verbIf you automate it, your team will save two weeks.
Something always trueIf + present, ... presentIf companies do not keep a trail, they cannot prove it.
The half with ifNEVER takes willnot if you will automate
Order does not mattercomma only when if comes firstYour team will save two weeks if you automate it.
Softer than willmay / can / should in the result halfIf it slips, we can move the go-live date.
Same meaning as ifwhen (you are sure it happens)When the migration starts, we will send two people.
The traptwo verbs in the future is one too manynot if we will close it, we will integrate

In one line: the future goes in one half only. That is what makes the sentence a promise instead of a hope.

Turn It Around

A Feature, and the Same Thing as a Promise

Feature — a fact about your product

"The system has automated tax filing."

Promise — a fact about their next quarter

"If you automate the filing, your team will save about two weeks a quarter."

Feature

"It is interoperable with most ERP systems."

Promise

"If you keep the system you already have, this plugs into it, so the data migration is smaller."

Feature

"We provide a full audit trail."

Promise

"If the auditor asks who changed that number, you will have the answer in one click instead of three days."

Forty Pitches, One Question

A finance director on what he is actually listening for. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What is he thinking about while the vendor demonstrates the system working perfectly?
The two weeks in January when nothing works and his team is doing it by hand.
2. What makes his posture change, in his words?
If a supplier opens with the migration, and tells him before he asks how long onboarding really takes with four hundred users. He says that is the one that gets the second meeting.
3. What does he say he is not looking for, and what is he looking for?
He is not looking for the best product — he says his current one is perfectly adequate. He is looking for the one that will not become his problem in February.

Listening 2 · A Sales Director

The Deal We Lost on One Number

A sales director on the promise that cost her the contract. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What had gone well before the last meeting?
Everything: the proof of concept worked and the numbers were good.
2. What did the colleague say, and why does she insist it was not a lie?
He said the deployment would take eight weeks. She says it was not a lie, it was optimistic.
3. Why was the number a problem, and what rule does she give at the end?
The buyer had already called two of their clients and both said about four months, so they had said something he could check and it did not match. Her rule: if you are unsure, give the longer number.

The Buyer Who Already Has a System

Scenario: A finance director who is not unhappy with what he has asks you three things: what actually changes if he buys, how bad the migration will be, and what happens if it goes wrong. Answer each one with a promise he could hold you to, and finish by asking what went wrong for him last year.

Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

The Same Sentence, Off Duty

Scenario: Dinner the night before the meeting. Your guest has never been to Brazil and has one free day. Offer three things using the same structure — if you do this, I will do that — and then ask two questions about what they would like to see.

Keywords:

if you have time on Sunday... I will pick you up if it rains, we can... what would you like to see?
Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Two Minutes, No Notes

Scenario: You have two minutes with the three people who were not in the demo. Do not describe the product. Tell them what will be different in their next quarter if they say yes, what the risk is, what you would do first to make that risk small, and one thing you are not promising.

No keywords, no notes, two minutes.
