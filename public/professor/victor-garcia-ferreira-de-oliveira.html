Metodologia — 5 Abordagens

1. Toda aula termina num turno longo

O gap foi nomeado três vezes na call: defender um ponto de vista por dois minutos. Então nenhuma aula fecha numa troca curta. O último bloco é sempre produção livre cronometrada, sem anotação e sem interrupção — e o professor sai dela com três números anotados.

2. Um domínio por aula, escolhido antes de escrever

Futebol e esporte, tecnologia e programação, vida universitária no Canadá, processos seletivos. Cada aula recebe um desses eixos. Argumentação sobre assunto que ele não domina não treina argumentação — treina invenção.

3. A estrutura sai por descoberta, dentro do assunto

Nenhuma aula abre com regra. A estrutura aparece em quatro frases sobre a vida real dele, é nomeada só depois e é cobrada na fala. Regra isolada e exercício descontextualizado esvaziam a aula para um aluno de 18 anos com atenção disputada.

4. Meaning em inglês, nunca tradução

B1 = zero português na tela do aluno. Definição em inglês simples, enunciado em inglês, hint em inglês. O português continua onde ele não vê: nesta aba e no ícone T de cada slide.

5. A aula tem de valer por si

A consistência extraclasse dele é frágil e todo mundo na call disse isso. O Pre-class da plataforma é a preparação inteira, e o homework é uma tarefa curta por aula, dita oralmente. Nada que dependa de duas horas de estudo em casa entra no desenho.

Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

Say It and Back It Up -- Holding One Position for Two Minutes

Anybody can say what they think in five seconds. The hard part is holding the same idea for two minutes and making it stand up. Key words: a point of view, evidence, a drawback, a trade-off, commitment, to back something up, to make a case for something, in the long run, to have an impact on something, a counter-argument. Structure: clauses of reason and result.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

A point of view -- the position you take on a question, together with the reasons behind it
"My point of view is that a season abroad teaches you more than a year of classes."
Evidence -- the facts and examples that show a statement is true
"The evidence is simple: I arrived in August and by December nobody translated for me."
A drawback -- the part of a good decision that still costs you something
"The main drawback of four training sessions a week is that you study late at night."
A trade-off -- what you give up in order to get something else you want more
"It is a trade-off: less free time, but a level of English you cannot buy in a classroom."
Commitment -- the time and effort you agree to give to something, week after week
"A club team is a real commitment. You cannot show up only when you feel like it."

"Do not just say the team helped you. Back it up with one thing that happened."
To make a case for something -- to give the reasons why something should be done or should continue
"I can make a case for sport at school in about ninety seconds."
In the long run -- over a long period, when the final result is what counts
"In the long run, the language you use every day is the one that stays with you."
To have an impact on something -- to change something in a way that people can actually see
"Two games a week had a bigger impact on my listening than any app I ever used."
A counter-argument -- the strongest thing the other side could say against your position
"The obvious counter-argument is that my grades dropped, and I have to answer that."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A student is asked whether a season with a club team abroad is worth the hours it takes. He does not start with it depends. He starts with a point of view: five months on a team teach you more than a year of classes. Then he gives the reason, and the word that opens a reason is because. He improved fast because he spoke English at training, on the bus and at home. Since nobody on the team spoke his language, there was no easy way out. Notice that since does the same job as because, only it comes first, before the claim.

The second half of an argument does the opposite job. It does not explain why; it says what followed. Training took four evenings a week, so he studied after ten at night. The whole season ran in English, which is why he stopped translating in his head. In a written paragraph the same idea is often carried by as a result. All three point forward, to the consequence, while because and since point backward, to the cause.

Two traps catch almost every Portuguese speaker here. The first is because of, which takes a noun and never a full sentence: because of the championship, he missed two weeks of school. The second is the phrase the reason is because, which sounds right and is not. Say the reason is that. Get those two right and the rest is repetition: a claim, a reason, a piece of evidence, one honest drawback, and an answer to the counter-argument before anybody has to ask for it.

1. "He improved fast because he spoke English at training." What does the word "because" open here?
A The consequence of the first idea.
B The cause, which is the reason behind the claim.
C An example that backs the claim up.
2. According to the text, what is the difference between "since" and "because"?
A They do the same job, but "since" usually comes first, before the claim.
B "Since" opens a consequence and "because" opens a cause.
C "Since" is only used for time and never for reason.
3. The text warns against "the reason is because". What should you say instead?
A The reason is because of.
B The reason is so.
C The reason is that.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Clauses of Reason and Result

GRAMMAR

Two jobs, two sets of words: one opens the cause, the other opens the consequence.

JobFormExample
Reason, after the claimclaim + because + subject + verbI improved fast because I trained in English.
Reason, before the claimSince / As + subject + verb, + claimSince the coach spoke only English, I had to listen.
Reason as a nounbecause of / due to + noun phraseBecause of the championship, I missed two weeks.
Result, in speechreason + so + resultThe games were on Saturdays, so I studied on Sunday.
Result, more formalreason. As a result, / Therefore, + resultThe season ran in English. As a result, my listening changed.
Result as a comment..., which is why + subject + verbNobody translated for me, which is why I stopped.
Question about a reasonWhy...? / What is the reason that...?What is the reason that you left the team?

Never: because of I trained every day · Since the season was long, so I missed classes · the main reason is because the language was everywhere · I joined the club for practice my listening.

Five months in Canada are already yours. Saying them in a way that lands is a different skill, and English keeps two tenses apart to do it. Key words: an achievement, a recommendation, to adapt, significant, to qualify for something, competitive, to stand out from, to gain experience, to build confidence, to rise to the challenge. Vocabulary

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody reads an application file in about seven minutes, and in those seven minutes two kinds of sentence do two different jobs. The first kind closes a box. He studied in Canada for five months. The team qualified for the final round last November. He received two recommendations before he came home. Every one of those sentences carries a time that is over, and English answers a closed time with the past simple. Note the trap: last November, in 2025 and two months ago all close the box, so none of them can take the present perfect.

The second kind of sentence points at today, and that is where the present perfect lives. He has played competitive soccer since he was nine, and he still plays. He has not submitted the language certificate yet, so the file is still open. His listening has changed in a way he calls significant, and it is still changing. Nothing here is finished, so the verb takes have or has plus the past participle.

There is a third use, and it is the one that opens interviews: experience with no date at all. I have lived abroad. Have you ever studied in another country? Nobody is asking when; they are asking whether it is in you. One warning for a Portuguese speaker, and it is the most common mistake of all: never use the present tense with for or since. Not I am back in Brazil since June, but I have been back in Brazil since June. Get that one right and half the file writes itself.

1. "The team qualified for the final round last November." Why the past simple and not the present perfect?
A Because qualify is a verb that never takes the present perfect.
B Because "last November" is a closed time, and a closed time takes the past simple.
C Because the sentence is about a team and not about a person.
2. According to the text, what do "has played since he was nine" and "has not submitted it yet" have in common?
A Neither of them is finished, so both point at today.
B Both of them describe events that are completely over.
C Both of them need a date to be correct.
3. The text calls one mistake the most common of all. Which is it?
A Asking a question with "ever" instead of a date.
B Using the past simple for an experience with no date.
C Using the present tense with "for" or "since", as in "I am back in Brazil since June".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

If the time is closed, use the past simple. If the time is open or absent, use the present perfect.

Stage 2: Put the Answer in Order

Order

Somebody has just asked you to tell them about the five months. Put your answer in a logical order, from what happened to what is still true.

?"I studied in Canada for five months and I played in a competitive league."
?"The team qualified for the final round in November, which nobody expected."
?"I came back with two recommendations from teachers who watched me every day."
?"I have played competitive soccer since I was nine, and I have never stopped."
?"Since I came back, I have stopped preparing sentences in my head before I speak."
?"I have not submitted the language certificate yet, and that is the last item."

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer a real speaker would give.

Somebody asks what you did in Canada. The season ended in June. You say:
A "I studied there for five months and I played in a competitive league."
B "I have studied there for five months last year."
C "I am studying there since five months."
The same person then asks how long you have been playing. You still play. You say:
A "I play soccer since I have nine years."
B "I have played competitive soccer since I was nine."
C "I played competitive soccer since nine years old."
You want to say that one item is still missing from your application. You say:
A "I did not submit the certificate yet."
B "I have not submitted the language certificate yet."
C "The certificate is not submitted since now."
You want to ask somebody about their experience, with no date in mind. You say:
A "Did you ever live in another country?"
B "Do you ever lived in another country?"
C "Have you ever lived in another country?"

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Think, then record your answer. There is no wrong answer here.

Somebody who has read your record asks the open question: tell me about the five months. Give them what happened, in the past simple, and what has changed and is still true, in the present perfect. Keep the two apart, and finish with the one thing about you that is still going on today. Speak for two minutes without stopping.

A world debate champion explaining what actually happens when two people argue well. It is the whole point of this lesson said by somebody who wins arguments for a living: a position, a reason, and a way of disagreeing that keeps the other person talking.

Tip: watch it once with English subtitles. Then write down the three questions she says a good disagreement needs, and try them on the person at home who disagrees with you most.
