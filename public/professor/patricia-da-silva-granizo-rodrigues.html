Quem é a Patrícia

Diretora de Recursos Humanos da JEM System, empresa americana de alarme de incêndio (distribuição de peças e serviços de engenharia). Ela responde também pela comunicação interna. É formada em Psicologia e já passou pela L'Oreal.

Nível B2. Ficou cerca de um ano e meio sem contato ativo com o idioma e sente que perdeu, principalmente listening — nas palavras dela, o americano fala muito rápido e fala comido. A gramática ela tem: erra por falta de prática e percebe o próprio erro depois de falar.

O pedido dela — e por que este material é diferente

Ela disse três vezes na consultoria, e está registrado também na ficha comercial: a aula é conversação. Palavras dela:

"Eu quero usar a aula pra que o tempo inteiro eu fale e essa pessoa me corrija. Fazer exercício de gramática eu faço depois, porque eu tenho disciplina."

Ela já teve professores particulares em que a aula de conversação virou leitura de exercício junto ou explicação de phrasal verb. Não pode acontecer aqui. A decisão de desenho, portanto:

  • O Pre-class carrega o peso mecânico. Vocabulário, matching, grammar in context, grammar tip, fill-in-the-blank, pronúncia gravada: tudo isso ela faz sozinha em casa. Ela reservou meia hora por dia, quatro dias por semana — e disse que faz porque quer absorver, não para cumprir tarefa. O material é dimensionado para caber nesses 30 minutos.
  • O IN CLASS é fala. Os componentes obrigatórios continuam todos lá (vocab reveal, grammar discovery, diálogo, listening, spot the error, quick fire), mas enxutos e com uma instrução explícita no ícone T de cada um: conduzir oralmente, rápido, corrigindo na fala. O centro da aula são os slides de discussão e os três role-plays.
  • Correção em tempo real. Ela pediu nominalmente: "essa pronúncia não tá boa, essa frase não tá boa, corrija minha pronúncia". Corrija na hora, não acumule para o fim da aula.

A armadilha que ela mesma denunciou

"Não sei se é porque eu sou psicóloga, mas eu começo a conversar e quando eu vejo o professor está falando mais que eu. Ele fala 70 por cento da aula e eu falo 30. Eu preciso de alguém que não caia nessa."

Meta objetiva: 70 a 80 por cento do tempo de fala é dela. Se você se pegar explicando, pare e devolva a pergunta. Se ela devolver uma pergunta para você, responda em UMA frase e devolva. Silêncio é ferramenta: não preencha a pausa em que ela está montando a frase.

Os temas — todos vieram dela

Recursos Humanos em primeiro lugar: retenção, desenvolvimento, remuneração, benefícios, avaliação de desempenho, plano de sucessão. Depois comunicação interna, que também é dela. Em segundo plano, finanças no nível de diretoria (orçamento, lucros, investimento) — ela mesma disse que não é o ponto principal.

Expansão para os EUA: a empresa vai abrir novas localidades no Texas e na Califórnia. Ela vai precisar falar de contratação e de leis trabalhistas locais, que nos EUA mudam de estado e às vezes de cidade.

Atlanta: há uma programação de viagem à matriz, talvez em setembro, sem data definida. Ela disse que lá vai mais conversar com as pessoas do que discutir negócios, e citou hotel e cotidiano. Isso entra no fim do bloco, não no começo.

O TOEFL — decisão em aberto, não assumir

O CEO colocou como meta que os diretores atinjam C1/C2 no TOEFL até o fim do ano, meta ligada a bônus. Ela se desencantou da meta e disse que o objetivo dela é falar, não a prova. A consultora recomendou deixar preparatório para as últimas 5 a 10 aulas do pacote, ou para uma contratação posterior. Nada disso está fechado — não transforme aula em simulado sem pedido explícito dela.

O formato das aulas

As aulas ímpares giram em torno de um diálogo line-by-line (a conversa que ela vai ter). As pares giram em torno de um texto curto que serve de trampolim para a discussão — nunca como exercício de compreensão em si (REGRA 29.2). Nos dois formatos, os três role-plays e os dois slides de discussão aberta continuam sendo o centro.

Todo listening é em velocidade natural americana, sem pedir desculpa pela velocidade. O player tem 0.5x a 1.25x: use 0.75x na segunda escuta, nunca na primeira.

Extras

Ela gosta de séries e Netflix ("adoro assistir"), gosta de listening e usa um aplicativo de resumo de livros. Não há nada que ela rejeite. Um aviso dela que muda a escolha: a concentração em áudio no trânsito dura dois ou três minutos, e ela disse que isso acontece igual em português. Por isso as indicações são curtas e a dica sempre diz o que fazer com três minutos.

Logística

Aulas online, 60 minutos, 1x por semana, segunda-feira às 19h (ela aceita 18h ou 19h; segunda é a prioridade dela). 30 encontros contratados. Plataforma Zoom. Professor alocado: Vinícius. Ela avisou que, se a agenda abrir, pode querer adiantar aulas ou comprar mais.

Lesson 01 -- Pre-class

The Two Minutes That Introduce You

The ten words your American counterparts use without explaining them, the two verb forms that decide whether your job sounds permanent or temporary, and the two sentences that open every conversation in Atlanta. Key words: remit, headcount, onboarding, retention, workforce, stakeholder, rollout, to report into, cross-functional, to oversee. Structure: present simple for what you run, present continuous for what you are working on.

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read it inside a real sentence. These are the words Atlanta uses without stopping to explain them.

Remit -- the area of work a person or a department is officially responsible for
"Internal communication sits inside my remit as well as HR."
Headcount -- the total number of people a company or a team employs
"Our headcount in Brazil is just under two hundred."
Onboarding -- the process of settling a new employee into the company
"We redesigned onboarding so a new hire is useful in three weeks."
Retention -- keeping the people you already employ instead of losing them
"Retention is the first number the American team asks about."
Workforce -- everybody who works for a company, taken together
"Half of our workforce is in the field, not in an office."
Stakeholder -- a person or group with an interest in the decision you are making
"Every stakeholder saw the policy before it went out."
Rollout -- the launch of a new policy or system across the company
"The rollout starts in Brazil and reaches the US sites in October."
To report into -- to have somebody as your direct manager
"The plant managers report into operations, not into me."
Cross-functional -- involving people from more than one department
"It is a cross-functional project, so finance is in the room too."
To oversee -- to be in charge of work that other people carry out
"I oversee four teams and I run none of them day to day."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- What You Run and What You Are Working On

GRAMMAR

Two forms, two different questions. An American counterpart asks both in the first minute, and answering them with the wrong form changes what they hear about your position.

FormWhat it doesExample
present simpleyour remit: what is true every week, with or without a projectI run HR for JEM System.
present continuouswhat is on your desk right now, and what will endAt the moment I am rolling out a new cycle.
at the moment, this quarter, right nowthe time words that force the continuousThis quarter we are hiring for two new sites.
usually, every week, alwaysthe time words that force the simpleI take the Atlanta call every Monday.
the mistake that costs youI am running HR tells an American your job is temporary. Say what you run first, then switch to the continuous for the project.

Say the permanent sentence first and the current one second. Reversed, the introduction sounds like a project description instead of a job.

Lesson 02 -- Pre-class

Running the Room

The ten expressions that steer a meeting, the four modal verbs that make you direct without sounding like an order, and the written rules of the monthly people call. Key words: agenda, to chair, action item, takeaway, to weigh in, to loop in, to circle back, to park, follow-up, bandwidth. Structure: modal verbs for chairing and softening.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each expression and read it inside a real sentence. All ten do the same job: they move a meeting without ordering anybody around.

Agenda -- the written list of what a meeting will cover, in order
"She sent the agenda the night before, with a time next to each item."
To chair -- to lead a meeting and decide who speaks and when
"Greg chairs the people call, so he opens it and he closes it."
Action item -- a task that comes out of a meeting, with an owner and a date
"Every action item has a name next to it before we hang up."
Takeaway -- the one thing people should remember when the meeting ends
"If there is one takeaway today, it is that attrition is not a pay problem."
To weigh in -- to give your opinion on something the group is discussing
"I would like HR to weigh in before we commit to a date."
To loop in -- to bring somebody into a conversation or an email thread
"Let me loop in legal before we answer that one."
To circle back -- to return to a point later, when there is time or information
"We can circle back to the bonus question at the end."
To park -- to leave a question aside on purpose so the meeting can move on
"I would rather park that until we have the legal answer."
Follow-up -- the message or the task that comes after a meeting
"The follow-up goes out the same afternoon, never two days later."
Bandwidth -- the time and capacity a person or a team has for more work
"My team does not have the bandwidth for a second rollout this quarter."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Modals That Steer a Meeting

GRAMMAR

Four small words carry most of the steering in an American meeting. They do not make you sound weak: they make you sound like somebody who expects to be answered rather than obeyed.

FormWhat it doesExample
Shall we ... ?proposes the next move and asks the room at the same timeShall we start with retention?
Could you ... ?a request that sounds like a choice, even when it is notCould you walk us through the numbers?
I would rather ...states your preference without overruling anybodyI would rather park that until Monday.
might / mayturns a judgement into a possibility, so nobody has to defend a positionThat might be a question for finance.
the trapCan you... and We must... are correct English and land as orders. Use could and would when you are not the most senior person in the room.

Can you... and We must... are perfectly correct English and land as orders. Keep them for your own team, not for a room of peers.

The Hard Conversation

The ten words a feedback conversation is made of, the conditional that shows a consequence without accusing anybody, and a real twenty-minute conversation with a manager whose reports are late. Key words: underperformance, improvement plan, to address, accountability, to set expectations, blind spot, candid, to document, turnaround, to follow through. Structure: second conditional in feedback.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each term and read it inside a real sentence. These are the words the conversation is made of, in the register American HR uses.

Underperformance -- work that is consistently below the standard that was agreed
"Nobody had named the underperformance out loud in eight months."
Improvement plan -- a written plan with goals and dates for somebody whose work is below standard
"An improvement plan is a chance to recover, not a step towards the exit."
To address -- to deal with a problem instead of leaving it alone
"We are going to address the reporting delay today, not in December."
Accountability -- being answerable for a result, not only for the effort you put in
"Accountability starts the moment a date has a name next to it."
To set expectations -- to say clearly what good work looks like, before it is judged
"Half of our performance problems are expectations nobody set."
Blind spot -- something about your own behaviour that everybody sees except you
"His blind spot is that he answers a question with a longer question."
Candid -- direct and honest, even when the message is uncomfortable
"I would rather be candid in March than sympathetic in December."
To document -- to write down what was said and agreed, so that it exists later
"If it is not documented, the conversation did not happen."
Turnaround -- a change from poor results to good ones
"That team is the clearest turnaround we have had in three years."
To follow through -- to do the thing you said you would do, all the way to the end
"He agrees in the room every time and then does not follow through."

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Second Conditional in Feedback

GRAMMAR

A past form used for something that has not happened. In a difficult conversation it does one job better than anything else: it turns an accusation into a picture that both of you can look at.

FormWhat it doesExample
If + past simple, would + verban imagined situation and its result: the message becomes a picture instead of an accusationIf you sent the numbers on Friday, I would have time to check them.
could / might instead of wouldsoftens the result even further, so nobody has to defend itIf we changed the deadline, that might solve half of it.
the past form is not past timeit marks distance from reality, not something that happenedIf I were in your position, I would ask earlier.
were, not wasthe careful form after if with I, he and sheIf I were you, I would document it today.
why it works in feedbackthe person can picture the behaviour without being accused of it. Instead of you never send it on time, say if you sent it on Friday, I would have time to check it.

Compare the two openings out loud: you are always late and if it arrived on Friday, I would have time to check it. The first asks for a defence. The second asks for a solution.

Vocabulary

Attrition -- the rate at which people leave the company over a given period

English puts its weight at the end of the sentence. These structures exist to move the important part there and make the room wait for it. It is not more words: it is the same words, reordered.

why this reads as presenceit is not more words. It is the same words, ordered so that the point lands LAST -- the structure tells the room where to put its attention before the content arrives.

"The succession plan has one name against four of the nine roles."
High potential -- an employee identified as able to grow two levels or more
"Being a high potential is a bet the company makes, not a reward it gives."
Talent pipeline -- the group of people being prepared for future roles
"Our talent pipeline is thin above supervisor level."
Stretch assignment -- a task deliberately above somebody current level, given to develop them
"A stretch assignment is the cheapest development there is."
Upskilling -- teaching the people you already employ new skills instead of hiring
"Upskilling the night shift cost less than one agency hire."
Readiness -- how close somebody is to being able to do the next role
"We grade readiness as now, one year, or two years -- never as maybe."
Bench strength -- how many capable replacements a company has for its key roles
"Bench strength is the number nobody looks at until somebody resigns."
Career path -- the sequence of roles somebody can move through inside a company
"If there is no career path, the best people write their own somewhere else."
To groom -- to prepare somebody deliberately for a particular role
"He was groomed for that job for three years before anybody told him."
To backfill -- to replace somebody who has left or moved, in the same role
"Every promotion creates a role to backfill, and we forget that every time."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about how it is written.

Ana has been with the company for eleven years and has been running the São Paulo site since March. In that time she has rebuilt the onboarding process and has taken two people from supervisor to shift manager. Her readiness for the next level is graded at one year.

We have been building the pipeline above supervisor level for two years and it has not worked. We have trained forty people and we have promoted four. What we have not been doing is giving anybody a job slightly too big for them, which is the only development that has ever moved anybody in this company.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Two Present Perfects

GRAMMAR

One of these counts results and one describes work in progress. Both connect the past to now, which is why neither of them can be replaced by the present simple -- the mistake that gives away almost every Portuguese speaker at B2.

FormWhat it doesExample
have / has + past participlea finished action whose result matters nowThree of the five have moved into bigger roles.
for / sincefor gives the length, since gives the starting pointShe has been here for eleven years.
have been + -ingthe activity itself, still going on, with its duration in viewWe have been running the programme since March.
the difference that mattersthe simple counts RESULTS; the continuous describes the WORKWe have trained forty people. We have been training all year.
the trapthe present simple for duration -- I work here for eleven years. This is the single most common B2 mistake in Portuguese speakers. Say I have worked here for eleven years.

I work here for eleven years is not a small error: to an American ear it means you are leaving in eleven years. Say I have worked here for eleven years.
