A Fair and the Sky

The Fair That Happens Rain or Shine

On the second Saturday of every month, the square in Serra Verde fills up with old furniture, seedlings and coffee. Nobody sells anything expensive. People bring what they have restored during the month, and everything depends on one thing: the sky. "If the forecast is good on Friday, we put out forty tables," says Dona Marta, who has organized the fair for eleven years. "If it looks bad, we put out twenty and we keep the rest in the shed."

The rule is painted on a board next to the church door, and it has not changed since 2014. If it rains before eight in the morning, everything moves into the old school gym. If the rain starts after that, the sellers cover their tables and carry on. The fair is never called off and it is never postponed. "People drive an hour to get here," Dona Marta says. "If we cancel one Saturday, half of them will not come back for three months. That is the part nobody thinks about."

Last March the town found out how well the plan works. A downpour started at twenty past seven, and by eight o'clock the gym was full of tables, seedlings and wet dogs. Not a single seller went home. "You cannot count on the weather out here, so you count on the backup plan instead," says Tiago, who restores chairs and has had a table since the first year. "If you wait for a perfect Saturday, you will wait forever. Chances are it will rain on the good ones too."

Chapter 4: The Code

Two Halves, One Future

if + present simple · will — and the half where will is banned

Grammar Discovery

Which Half Gets Will?

"If it rains before eight, we will move into the gym."

"If we cancel one Saturday, half of them will not come back."

"If you wait for a perfect Saturday, you will wait forever."

"I will call you tonight if the forecast changes."

The blue half is the condition, and it looks like the present. The green half is the result, and that is the only place the future is allowed to appear.

FormIts jobExample
If + present simple, will + verbA real possibility in the future.If it rains, we will move inside.
The trapwill is banned after if.If it will rain → If it rains
Either orderThe comma appears only when if comes first.We will move inside if it rains.
Less certainwill becomes might or may.If the forecast changes, I might stay home.
unlessIt already means if not, so never add a second negative.Unless it rains, we will set up outside.
as long asA condition you insist on.I will go as long as the road is fine.
when vs ifwhen = you are sure. if = you are not.When I get there / If I get there

In one line: the future lives in one half of the sentence only, and it is never the half with if.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"If it will rain on Saturday, we will move inside."
"If it rains on Saturday, we will move inside."
"Unless it does not rain, the fair will go ahead."
"Unless it rains, the fair will go ahead."
"If the forecast change, I will call you tonight."
"If the forecast changes, I will call you tonight."
"I will help you on Sunday if I will have time."
"I will help you on Sunday if I have time."

The first and the last one are the same error wearing different clothes: will crossed into the half where it is banned, once at the start of the sentence and once at the end. The half with if always looks like the present, wherever it sits.

Check Your Work

The Whole Rule on One Screen

Try everything first. Open the key only to compare.

+Reveal the rule in one page
This is the whole lesson on one screen. Photograph it and use it during the week.
  1. First conditional = a real possibility in the future. If + present simple, then will + verb: "If it rains, we will move inside."
  2. The future lives in ONE half of the sentence only. Never put will after if: "If it will rain" is wrong.
  3. You can start with either half. The comma appears only when the if half comes first: "We will move inside if it rains."
  4. will can become might or may when you are less sure: "If the forecast changes, I might stay home."
  5. unless already means if not, so it never takes a second negative: "Unless it rains", never "unless it does not rain".
  6. as long as and provided that add a condition you insist on: "I will go as long as the road is fine."
  7. when = you are sure it happens; if = you are not: "When I get there" against "If I get there".
  8. Expressions: a forecast, a downpour, to call off, to postpone, a backup plan, to fall through, to squeeze in, on short notice, to count on, to run out of, chances are, to be up to you.
