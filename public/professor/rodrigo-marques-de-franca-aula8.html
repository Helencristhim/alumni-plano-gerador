Read for the Main Idea

Both Things Are True at Once

The Argument That Concedes First

Every profession that sells judgement is now being told that judgement is about to be automated, and lawyers are hearing it more loudly than most. The honest place to begin is by acknowledging what the optimists have got right, because on the tasks where the output can be checked against a known answer the machines are already faster and, arguably, more consistent than the people they are compared with. Anybody who has watched a first-year associate spend a weekend on a document review knows that this is not a small claim. It would be easy, and dishonest, to open with the objection.

The counterargument is not that the technology is weak. It is that the tasks being measured are the ones that were always easiest to measure, which is a familiar problem in any industry, and the conclusion drawn from them is far broader than the evidence supports. Notwithstanding the results, almost none of the published tests examine the part of professional work that consumes the most hours: deciding which question is actually being asked. A client does not arrive with a question. He arrives with a situation and a mood, and the first hour is spent finding out what he is really worried about. Whereas a document review has a right answer, that hour does not, and nothing yet measures it.

On balance, then, the sensible position is neither of the two on offer. The tools are compelling and they are being sold with claims that do not hold water, and both of those things are true at once. Admittedly, saying so satisfies nobody: it is read as complacency by one side and as hype by the other. The blind spot is shared, though, and it is this. Everybody is arguing about whether the work can be done faster, while nobody is arguing about who is responsible when it is done wrong, and that second question is the one that will decide how any of this is used. Until it is answered, every prediction should be qualified, however confidently it is delivered.

Chapter 4: The Turn in the Sentence

Hand Them the Fact, Keep the Conclusion

Four ways to turn, and they do not take the same grammar

Grammar Discovery

What Comes After the Red Word?

Although the results are real, the tasks were the easiest ones to measure."

Despite the numbers, the conclusion is broader than the evidence supports."

Whereas a document review has a right answer, that first hour does not."

"The tools are compelling. The claims, however, do not hold water."

All four hand something to the other side and keep the conclusion anyway. Look at what follows each red word: sometimes a whole sentence, sometimes only a noun. That is the entire difference, and it is where most of the mistakes live.

JobFormExample
Concede a factalthough / even though + subject + verbAlthough the results are real, ...
Concede a thingdespite / in spite of / notwithstanding + nounDespite the numbers, ...
Contrast two caseswhereas / while + subject + verbWhereas a review has an answer, ...
Turn between sentenceshowever · nevertheless · that said... . The claims, however, do not.
Concede a whole clausedespite the fact that + subject + verbDespite the fact that the results are real
However + adjectiveno matter howHowever confidently it is delivered
The trapsno of after despite · never although and but togetherNot despite of. Not although X, but Y.

In one line: although and whereas take a sentence; despite and notwithstanding take a noun; however takes a full stop before it.

1. "The results are real. The tasks were the easiest ones." (although)

2. Same idea, starting from the numbers. (despite)
"Despite the numbers, the tasks were the easiest ones to measure."
3. "A document review has a right answer. That first hour does not." (whereas)
"Whereas a document review has a right answer, that first hour does not."
4. "The tools are compelling. The claims do not hold water." (however)

Listening 1 · An Essayist

I Write the Objection First

Somebody explaining how he actually builds an argument. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What exactly does he write first, and how does he write it?
The objection: the single strongest thing somebody could say against him, in one paragraph, put as well as they would put it.
2. What are his two reasons, and which one is he not proud of?
The honourable one is finding out on a Tuesday instead of in public. The tactical one, which he is not proud of, is that a reader who sees his own objection stated well stops looking for the trap.
3. What does he say happens surprisingly often after twenty years?
The objection turns out to be right, and the piece he meant to write turns into a different piece entirely.

Listening 2 · Four Seconds of Silence

I Changed My Mind in Public

Somebody describing the worst four seconds of a panel discussion. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What happened on the panel, and what did she notice as it happened?
Somebody made an argument she had never heard. She felt herself reaching for the answer she had prepared, and the answer did not fit.
2. What did she actually say, and how does she describe it?
That she would have to think about it. She says it sounds weak and was the only true thing available.
3. What lesson does she take, and how is it narrower than people expect?
Not that you should change your mind, but that you should notice out loud when an argument has landed, because everybody in the room already noticed.

Role-Play 2 of 3 — Semi-free

Argue the Side You Do Not Hold

Scenario: Take the position you disagree with most in your own field and argue it for two minutes, as well as its strongest defender would. No signalling that you disagree, no irony. Then, and only then, say where it breaks.

Keywords:

Role-Play 3 of 3 — Free

Three Minutes, Objection First

Scenario: Choose something you actually believe about your own profession. Build it the way the essayist does: the strongest objection first, stated fairly and in full, then your answer, then one limit on your own conclusion. Finish by naming what both sides are failing to see.

No keywords, no notes, three minutes.
