Meaning

One Fact, Four Shapes

The same information, with a different focus each time

The court decided the case in 1954.

Active and past simple: who did it, and when.

The case was decided in 1954.

Passive: the case matters more than the court.

It was in 1954 that the case was decided.

Cleft: the date is the point of the sentence.

Decided in 1954, the case is still taught today.

Participle clause: two facts in one sentence.

Two Traditions, One Negotiating Table

Two Traditions, One Negotiating Table

If you have ever worked on a contract between a Brazilian company and an American one, you have probably noticed that the two legal teams do not think in the same way. It is not only a question of language. Most of the world's legal systems belong to one of two great traditions, common law and civil law, and each of them has trained its lawyers to ask a different first question.

The common law was born in England after the Norman Conquest of 1066, when royal judges began to travel around the country deciding disputes. Because there was no single written code, what the judges relied on was their own earlier decisions. Over the centuries, this practice became a principle known as precedent: a court is expected to follow what higher courts have already decided in similar cases. It is often said that common law is made by judges, and to some extent this is true. Faced with a new problem, an American or English lawyer will usually look for a case first.

The civil law tradition, which is followed in Brazil, France, Germany and most of Latin America, has its roots in Roman law. Its modern form owes a great deal to the French Civil Code of 1804, written under Napoleon, which was copied or adapted across Europe and far beyond it. In this tradition, the starting point is the code itself: a written, organized body of rules approved by the legislature. A Brazilian lawyer facing the same problem would probably open the Civil Code before reading any judgment. The judge's role, at least in theory, is to apply the law, not to create it.

In practice, however, the two traditions have been moving closer for decades. Common law countries now have enormous bodies of written statutes, and in Brazil, certain decisions of the Federal Supreme Court have been binding on lower courts since a constitutional reform in 2004. Had the two systems stayed as far apart as they were two centuries ago, international contracts would be much harder to negotiate today. For a lawyer who works across borders, the lesson is clear: the important thing is not to decide which tradition is better, but to understand which question the other side is going to ask first.

Test-Style Questions

Four Kinds of Question

Rule out one option out loud, choose, then click to compare

1. VOCABULARY IN CONTEXT. In paragraph 2, the phrase relied on is closest in meaning to: (A) depended on (B) argued about (C) wrote down
(A) depended on. Read the sentence again with the new word inside it: it still works.
2. INFERENCE. What can be inferred from paragraph 3 about civil law judges? (A) They often ignore the Civil Code. (B) In theory, they have less room to create law than common law judges. (C) They are trained in England.
(B). The text says their role is to apply the law, not to create it. (A) contradicts the text; (C) is not mentioned.
3. PURPOSE. Why does the writer mention the constitutional reform of 2004? (A) To show that Brazilian law has moved toward precedent. (B) To criticize the Brazilian Supreme Court. (C) To give the date of the Brazilian Civil Code.
(A). It is evidence for the idea that the two traditions are converging.
4. SUMMARY. Which sentence best summarizes the article? (A) Common law comes from England, and civil law comes from Rome. (B) The traditions begin from cases and from codes, but they are converging, and lawyers must understand both.
(B). (A) is true, but it is only a detail; a summary needs the main idea and the conclusion.

To Stand by Things Decided

Part of a university lecture on precedent. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What does stare decisis mean, according to the lecturer?
More or less, to stand by things decided.
2. Why does precedent matter so much?
Because of predictability: if similar cases are decided in similar ways, people and companies can plan.
3. What example does he give of a court departing from an earlier decision?
Brown versus Board of Education, in 1954, which rejected the idea of separate but equal from Plessy versus Ferguson.
