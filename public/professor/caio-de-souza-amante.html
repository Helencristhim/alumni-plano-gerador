Jornada de Transformação

De: CEO que trava em reunião com nativo, fica calado e deixa outras pessoas falarem por ele. Ele nomeia com precisão: "quando eu entro numa reunião assim, cara, normalmente eu fico travado, não falo nada e deixo outras pessoas falarem". E carrega um custo com valor exato: perdeu uma negociação de R$ 110 milhões e atribui a perda diretamente ao inglês.

Para: Founder que apresenta a Dataside, conduz objeções e fecha acordos internacionais com a mesma confiança que tem em português — sem travar, sem pedir para repetir três vezes, e sem precisar de tradutor na mesa.

Promessa do Programa

"De CEO sem voz no mercado global a founder que conduz a própria negociação de M&A em inglês — sem travar, sem pedir para repetir, sem perder mais R$ 110 milhões por causa disso."

Forças

  • Autoconsciência pedagógica excepcional. Listou os próprios erros fossilizados com precisão de professor: "eu falo she like, he like, she have... são coisas que eu sei que são erradas, mas que na hora que eu estou falando, eu acabo falando errado". Isso encurta meses de diagnóstico — o alvo já está nomeado.
  • Stake financeiro concreto. Não estuda por obrigação: estuda porque não saber já custou nove dígitos. A motivação não precisa ser construída nem mantida.
  • Hiperfoco quando engaja. Declarou: "quando eu foco, eu foco demais". Construída uma empresa de 300 pessoas em 15 anos, a capacidade de execução está provada. O trabalho do professor é conseguir o engajamento; depois dele, o aluno se conduz.
  • Vocabulário técnico de negócios já presente passivamente. Usou naturalmente main goals, business english, merge de companhias, private equity. Há base léxica de M&A para ativar, não para ensinar do zero.
  • Hábito de estudo autônomo consolidado. "Eu estudo uns 20, 30 minutinhos sozinho... tento ler um trecho de alguma coisa, escutar alguma coisa." Leitura diária sobre negócios já é rotina — os Complementares vão ser consumidos.
  • Clareza absoluta sobre o inglês que precisa. Não quer inglês genérico. Sabe o registro, o contexto e o interlocutor. Isso permite personalizar cada aula sem adivinhar.

Pontos de Melhoria

  • Listening de nativo é o gargalo operacional número um. Quando não entende, ele trava e se cala — e a reunião segue sem ele. Por isso toda aula deste pacote traz no mínimo dois listenings em velocidade real, e o survival card sempre carrega a frase de pedir repetição.
  • Terceira pessoa do singular fossilizada. she like, he like, she have. Ele sabe a regra e erra mesmo assim, o que significa que o trabalho é de automatização, não de explicação. Por isso o -s é a segunda linha gramatical recorrente de todas as aulas do bloco 1, ao lado do ponto novo de cada aula.
  • Transferência negativa do português. "Penso em português, aí eu crio uma frase que é totalmente em português, só que em inglês não faz sentido nenhum." O antídoto é frase-modelo pronta e produção cronometrada, nunca tradução assistida.
  • Dispersão auditiva (TDAH não medicado). "Quando tem um áudio no meio da aula, às vezes eu me perco." Por isso o áudio nunca vem sem tarefa antes, nunca passa de um minuto e meio, e a pergunta está sempre na tela antes do play.
  • Tendência a dominar a dinâmica da aula. "O professor começa a se adaptar demais a mim. E a hora que eu vejo, eu estou quase dando aula." Este material existe em grande parte por causa disto: roteiro fechado, minutagem por bloco, e o professor conduzindo do slide 1 ao 31.
  • Zero tempo fora da aula. "O tempo não existe. Não existe." O Pre-class tem de caber em 20 minutos e ser 100% ativo.
  • Dificuldade em perceber progresso gradual. "Se eu pegar um assunto pra estudar, daqui duas semanas eu converso com você sobre aquilo. O inglês não é assim." Por isso toda aula termina com dois números ditos em voz alta: frases completas produzidas e verbos que perderam o -s.

A Jornada — narrativa do programa

Caio tem 48 anos, mora em São José dos Campos e é fundador e CEO da Dataside, uma consultoria de dados e inteligência artificial que ele construiu em 15 anos até quase 300 colaboradores. Em português ele é um comunicador poderoso: vendeu, contratou, levantou capital e conduziu a empresa inteira com a própria voz. Em inglês, essa voz desaparece.

O custo não é hipotético e tem valor de balanço: uma negociação de R$ 110 milhões perdida, e ele atribui a perda diretamente ao inglês — o comprador não sentiu confiança na comunicação. A expansão para EUA e Europa e as conversas de M&A estão travadas no mesmo ponto. A dor não é gramatical. É o silêncio constrangedor na reunião quando ele não entende o nativo falar, e a sensação de ser um CEO que perdeu a voz no palco mais importante.

O gargalo operacional é listening, e ele nomeou antes de qualquer pergunta. Quando perde o fio, trava e deixa os outros conduzirem. O segundo gargalo é a produção: sabe as palavras, monta a frase em português e traduz, e o resultado não faz sentido em inglês. O terceiro, que ele mesmo diagnosticou com nome e sobrenome, é a terceira pessoa do singular — she like, he like, she have. Ele sabe que está errado no momento em que fala. Isso não se resolve explicando: resolve-se repetindo até virar automático, e por isso o -s aparece como segunda linha gramatical em todas as aulas deste bloco, atrás do ponto novo de cada uma.

Três coisas mudam o modo de conduzir a aula, e todas vieram da consultoria. Primeira: ele tem TDAH não medicado e dispersa com áudio no meio da aula — por isso o listening aqui é sempre curto, sempre com a tarefa na tela antes do play. Segunda: ele pediu "tratamento de choque", com essas palavras. Quer o erro corrigido, não tolerado, e reclama de professor que se adapta demais — ao ponto de ele acabar dando a própria aula. Este material é a resposta a isso: roteiro fechado, minutagem por bloco, zero improviso. Terceira: ele não consegue enxergar progresso gradual em idioma, e isso é o que mais o frustra. Por isso toda aula termina com dois números ditos em voz alta — quantas frases completas ele produziu e quantos verbos perderam o -s — e esses dois números são comparados na aula seguinte.

Fora do trabalho: corrida, musculação e performance física, e leitura sobre produtividade, estratégia, finanças e comportamento humano. Baixo interesse por música e cultura pop, e pouca paciência para série longa — filme, sim. Os Complementares deste pacote seguem isso: nada de temporada de 10 episódios, e sim palestra curta, episódio único e vídeo de negócios em velocidade nativa. Aula ímpar segue o modelo de fala (diálogo line-by-line + três role-plays); aula par, o modelo de leitura (texto central + gist e true/false). Cada aula faz callback do vocabulário da anterior no warm-up.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A buyer in New York asks a founder what his company does, and the founder answers well — the numbers are right, the story is clear, the strategy holds. And still, something in the room moves half a step back. Later nobody can say exactly what it was. It was one letter. Dataside specialize in data, he said. The buyer want a faster close. Three or four times in ten minutes, and each time the sentence arrived a little less finished than the thought behind it.

English marks one person in the present simple, and only one. When the subject is he, she or it, the verb carries an -s: she wants, he runs, it costs. Everybody else takes the bare verb: I run, we specialize, they want. The trap for a Portuguese speaker is not the rule — the rule is simple and most speakers can recite it. The trap is the subject that does not look like a person. Dataside has three hundred people, because a company is an it. Revenue comes from consulting, because revenue is an it too. The private equity firm wants three years of numbers. Every one of those subjects is a single thing, and every one of them takes the -s.

In the negative and in questions the -s moves house. It leaves the main verb and goes to do: he does not move on price, and does the buyer need more time? Saying she does not wants puts it in both places, which is the second most common version of the same mistake. And there is a habit that travels from Portuguese with it: depends of instead of it depends on. Neither one changes what a founder means. Both change how finished the founder sounds, and in a first meeting that is the only thing a buyer has to go on.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a moment in every first meeting when a buyer stops asking what the company does and starts asking what it has done. The two questions sound similar and they are not. The first one can be answered by anybody with a website. The second one can only be answered by a company with a history, and English keeps a separate shape for it. Dataside has been operating for fifteen years. We have delivered more than four hundred projects. We have never lost a client to a competitor. None of those three sentences carries a date, and that is the whole point.

The shape is have or has plus the past participle, and it does one job: it says the count is still running. Four hundred projects today, four hundred and one next month. Compare that with we opened our second office in 2018. The year is named, the sentence closes, and nothing about it can change. That is the past simple, and it is the right shape whenever the time is on the table. The rule underneath both is short enough to carry into a meeting: name the time and the sentence closes; leave the time out and you are still counting. With for and since the count is explicit — for takes a length of time, since takes a starting point — and both of them require the present perfect, always.

Two traps cost a Portuguese speaker the most, and they are both traps of translation rather than of grammar. The first is since with the simple present. In Portuguese the equivalent sentence sits comfortably in the present tense, so the direct translation arrives as I am CEO since 2009. English will not take it: it has to be I have been CEO since 2009. The second trap is a named time glued to a present perfect — Last year we have opened an office — and the fix is to drop back to the past simple: Last year we opened an office. And underneath both, the permanent one: a company is an it, so it takes has. Not Dataside have grown. Dataside has grown.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a question a serious buyer asks that a curious one never does. Not what do you do, and not even what have you done, but how long has it been running. The reason is not politeness. A result can be a lucky quarter; a process that has not stopped for two years is much harder to fake, and much harder to lose. So the shape English uses for duration is the shape that fills a due diligence call: we have been building the engine since 2022, the pipeline has been growing every quarter since March, they have been running diligence for six weeks.

The form is have or has plus been plus the -ing verb, and the test that chooses it is one question: can you answer how many, or only how long? We have signed four contracts answers how many, so it is the ordinary present perfect — a counted result. We have been signing contracts since March answers how long, so it is the continuous — an activity still under way. Both are true about the same three months. They simply point at different things, and a buyer listens for which one you reach for first.

Two things trip a Portuguese speaker here. The first is that Portuguese covers this ground with the simple present tense, so the direct translation arrives as we are doing this since six weeks, which is wrong twice over — wrong tense and wrong preposition. It has to be we have been doing this for six weeks, with for before a length and since before a starting point. The second is that some verbs refuse the -ing entirely because they describe a state rather than an activity: be, have in the sense of owning, know, need, want, mean. Not I have been knowing him for ten years. I have known him for ten years. And underneath all of it, the permanent one: the pipeline, the company and the market are each an it, so they take has, never have.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways to report the same fact to a board. My team missed the forecast puts a person in the sentence, and the room immediately starts deciding whether somebody should be blamed. The forecast was missed by nine percent takes the person out, and the room starts deciding what to do next. The facts are identical. The conversation is not, and in a negotiation the difference between those two conversations is worth money.

The form is two verbs, always: a form of be, then the past participle. The forecast was missed. Churn is measured on client count. The accounts were audited in March. The first verb does all the work you can hear: it carries the tense and it agrees with the subject. The second one never changes. This is the piece a Portuguese speaker most often loses, because the same idea is carried in that language by a single short word attached to the verb, and the direct translation produces a sentence with only one verb in it. The forecast missed by nine percent is not English.

When the actor matters after all, it comes back on the end with by: the accounts were audited by Vieira and Marsh. Use it when the name changes what the sentence is worth — an auditor's name does, and a colleague's name usually does not. And when nothing is being done to the subject, none of this applies: two deals moved into the next quarter stays as it is, because nobody moved them.

One warning that has nothing to do with grammar and everything to do with the room. This form can be used to hide, and every experienced listener knows it. A mistake was made tells a board that somebody is not saying who, and you have spent your credibility to save one word. So the rule that goes with the form is short: take the person out when the person is not the point, and never when the person is you.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

In a negotiation, what was agreed is not what was said in the room. It is what was written afterwards. Somebody leaves the meeting, sends an email, and three weeks later everybody argues from that email and nobody remembers the call. So the person who can repeat other people accurately, in English, controls the record — and the person who cannot has handed that job to the other side.

The mechanism is one step backwards. Everything the person said moves one tense further into the past. We are ready becomes he said they were ready. The pool was agreed becomes she said the pool had been agreed, because it was already past when she spoke. The modals move with it: can becomes could, will becomes would, must becomes had to. The pronouns move too, and so does the time: tomorrow becomes the next day.

A reported question stops being a question. It loses the auxiliary, it loses the question order and it loses the question mark: can you hold the price? becomes he asked whether we could hold the price. Keeping the original order — he asked what did we want — is the single most common mistake in this structure, and it is audible immediately. The other one is smaller and just as visible in writing: tell needs a person after it and say does not. He told me. He said that. Never he said me.

There is one exception worth knowing, because it belongs to your world. When the thing reported is still true at the moment you are speaking, the step back becomes optional: he said the threshold is fourteen percent is correct if it still is. And the permanent target survives here too: in the present, the reporting verb keeps its ending. She says, not she say. Finally, one rule that is not grammar at all. Report the sentence, not your feeling about the sentence. If the buyer said the margin was too thin, say he said the margin was too thin. You are allowed to disagree in the next sentence; you are not allowed to edit his on the way home.

Tip: listen once without the transcript, then read it and mark every sentence where the host reports what a researcher said. That is the whole grammar of this lesson, used for real.

Celeste Headlee — “10 Ways to Have a Better Conversation” (TED)

A radio interviewer on listening as a skill rather than a courtesy, including the rule this lesson depends on: if you are building your answer while the other person is talking, you did not hear the sentence you are about to report. Eleven minutes, and she speaks fast.

Tip: watch it once with subtitles and once without, and note which of her ten rules you break in English but not in Portuguese. That difference is nerves, not language.
