Objetivo do Programa

Ponto de partida: o inglês já é a língua do corredor. Ela entende quase tudo — "eu entendo bem, talvez por escutar tanto agora" — e responde de um jeito que funciona, mas curto: "consigo responder de forma muito simples". O gargalo não é compreensão nem coragem, é repertório e estrutura da resposta.

Ponto de chegada: responder na altura do cargo. Explicar um procedimento, defender um número, sustentar uma posição técnica e conversar antes e depois da reunião — em inglês, com frases longas e vocabulário próprio da área jurídica, ambiental e regulatória.

Promessa do Programa

De responder de forma simples nos corredores a estar com o "inglês de lindo" na COP — em 80 aulas. As palavras são dela.

Forças e pontos de melhoria

Forças

  • Compreensão auditiva sólida: exposição diária intensa no trabalho acelerou o receptivo
  • Autoconsciência linguística precisa: sabe exatamente o que falta e consegue nomear o gap
  • Motivação ancorada em evento real com data: a COP de novembro de 2027
  • Imersão profissional involuntária: o inglês é a língua dos corredores da empresa atual
  • Histórico de melhora real por conversação — o método certo já funcionou para ela antes
  • Domínio total do conteúdo: contratos, contencioso, consultivo e regulatório já são rotina em português

Pontos de Melhoria

  • Produção oral elaborada: responde de forma funcional, mas simples demais para o cargo
  • Acesso lexical durante a fala: "às vezes esqueço algumas palavras"
  • Vocabulário ambiental e regulatório: área nova, terminologia ainda não dominada
  • Zero contato com inglês fora do trabalho — sem exposição complementar
  • Leitura em inglês inexistente: canal potente de expansão vocabular desligado
  • Conversa social: o foco atual é quase 100% profissional

Como cada aula funciona

Pre-class: antes da aula

Vocabulário com áudio, matching, um texto de contexto com quiz, a estrutura da aula explicada em inglês, aplicação, ordenação, pronúncia gravada e produção livre. Tudo em inglês: B1 não leva português na tela.

In class: com o professor

Slides em Zoom, sete capítulos, descoberta da estrutura antes da regra, diálogo linha a linha, dois listenings, um artefato real e três role-plays — do guiado ao livre, com segunda rodada obrigatória.

Compromisso de uso

Dois itens da aula usados no trabalho em até 48 horas, e a frase real trazida na aula seguinte. É o que move o inglês do reconhecido para o usado.

Complementares

Três recomendações por aula (série, podcast e vídeo), sempre com link do episódio exato. Objetivo declarado na consultoria: abrir o canal de leitura e escuta fora do trabalho, que hoje é zero.

Drilling gramatical descontextualizado e sensação de mesmice. Ela abandonou um curso anterior porque "parecia que eu estava repetindo o verbo to be toda vez" e "eu não mudava de página". Cada aula precisa ter conteúdo novo visível.

Informação sensível

Nunca entra. Funções no lugar de nomes, percentuais no lugar de valores, dados fictícios nos artefatos. Nome de empresa real e caso real ficam fora do material.

Stage 3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

A company can buy its law from outside, or it can keep it inside. A law firm has many clients and sells hours. An in-house lawyer has one client, the company, and sells judgement: the answer has to arrive before the business moves, not after.

The team that sits inside is the legal department, and its work falls into three groups. In a large group the in-house team rarely goes to court itself: it chooses the firm that represents the company, and it controls the strategy and the cost.

The third group is advisory work. Here nobody is in court yet. A manager asks a question, the lawyer gives an opinion, and the business decides. Much of that opinion is about compliance: the law, the rules of the sector and the internal policies of the group, which are often stricter than the law itself.

The industry changes the work. In agribusiness, a lawyer who reads a supply contract is also reading the weather, the harvest and the port. A late ship is a legal problem, and so is a dry season. And a department that handles everything from the field to the buyer still has limits: in most groups, employment law goes to a specialist firm outside.

Stage 4: Grammar Tip -- who, which and that

GRAMMAR

Two short sentences say less than one long one. A relative clause joins them: it adds information about a noun without starting a new sentence. This is the fastest way to stop answering in four words.

WordWhat it points toExample
whoa personthe lawyer who reviews the contract
whicha thing, a company, an ideathe clause which protects the seller
thata person or a thing; the natural choice when you speakthe group that exports grain
Do not repeat the subjectthe relative word already IS the subject: "the lawyer who reviews", never "the lawyer who she reviews".

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

Every lawyer is asked the same question at some point: so, tell me about yourself. And most answers fail in the same way. They are a list. The person says a job, then another job, then a third one, and the listener learns nothing except the order.

A story is not a list. It has a shape, and in English that shape is carried by two tenses. Look at what happens when a lawyer tells it well. "I started in a law firm in two thousand and thirteen, and I stayed for three years." Those years are closed: they have a beginning, an end and a date, so the verbs sit in the past simple. "Then I moved in-house, and I have never gone back." That sentence is different. It has no date, and it is still true today.

The same split runs through the whole story. "I have been the legal manager since two thousand and twenty-one" is a period that reaches this morning. "I have worked in agribusiness for eleven years" is a length of time that is still growing. But "the buyer stopped paying in two thousand and twenty-two" is finished, and so is the case that followed it.

The most useful move in a career story is the one that uses both in a row. First the closed event, then what it left behind. "The jurisdiction clause sent us abroad, and the case was slow and expensive. Since then, I have read that clause first, in every contract." The past simple gives the evidence. The present perfect gives the person listening the thing they actually want: what you do now because of it. That is the difference between a track record and a list of employers.

GRAMMAR

Most of a contract negotiation is not about the law. It is about consequences, said out loud, by somebody who can see them earlier than everybody else in the room. And in English, consequences have two shapes.

The first shape is for what is on the table. "If they sign on Friday, the first shipment will leave in three weeks." Nothing imaginary here: signing is likely, and the sentence is a plan. The verb after if stays in the present, and the will waits for the other half. That is the whole form, and the one mistake worth remembering is putting will in both halves.

The second shape is for what you are imagining in order to show what is wrong with it. "If we capped our liability at one shipment, a serious quality problem would cost us five times what the buyer could claim." Nobody is capping anything. The past form does not point to yesterday: it marks the sentence as hypothetical, and the would carries the consequence.

This second shape is worth more than its grammar. It is how a lawyer says no without closing the door. You are not refusing the person in front of you; you are showing the room what happens if the idea goes through. "If I gave you London and the cap on the same day, I would have nothing left to take to my director" is a refusal, and it still sounds like cooperation.

The same shape opens doors. At a deadlock, "if we parked this clause and came back to it tomorrow, we would both think more clearly" is a proposal that nobody has to lose in order to accept. And when the talk really is over, the first shape comes back, plain and calm: "if that is your final position, we will have to walk away."

Must not and cannot carry prohibition. Existing forest must not be cleared, and no softer word will do, because a prohibition said softly is heard as advice. And here is the trap that matters most in a legal sentence: do not have to is not must not. "You do not have to send it monthly" means there is no obligation. May and can carry permission. The project may appoint any accredited verification body; the rule allows it and the choice is yours. And should carries recommendation: you should wait for the measurement. Nobody breaks a rule by ignoring a should — which is exactly why the word has to be used honestly, and never to dress a prohibition in polite clothes.

Stage 3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the passage, then answer the questions about what it says.

Every professional who changes direction has to tell the same story twice a week for about a year, and in English that story needs three expressions that look almost identical and mean completely different things.

The first is used to plus an infinitive, and it describes a habit or a state that is over. "I used to spend two days a week on supply contracts." The sentence carries its own contrast: if the habit still existed, you would not say it this way. Two details are worth remembering. In the question and the negative, the d disappears: "Did you use to work with land law?" and "I did not use to work with it." And there is no present version of this expression: for a habit you still have, the present simple does the job.

The second is be used to, followed by a noun or an -ing form, and it means something is already familiar. "I am used to long documents." There is no effort left in it. It is not about the past at all: it is about how the thing feels to you now.

The third is get used to, and it is the process of the second one happening. "I am getting used to reading technical annexes." Not comfortable yet, but moving. This is the one that describes a steep learning curve honestly, without pretending the work is easy and without sounding lost.

Put together, they tell the whole arc. What you used to do, what you are used to, and what you are getting used to. Add the words for the mechanics of the change — to take on, to phase out, to hand over, previously, at the time — and a year of professional life fits into two minutes.
