Pontos de Melhoria

  • Produção oral muito abaixo da compreensão. É o gap clássico do autodidata: muito input, pouco output supervisionado. Não é construção do zero — é desbloqueio
  • Angústia de precisão gramatical. “Às vezes eu tenho algumas dúvidas gramaticais... eu estava certo, mas sempre fica aquela dúvida.” Ele nomeia isso como “um pouco psicológico”. Trate com REGRA EXPLÍCITA logo após a descoberta: ele precisa da confirmação escrita, não só do “soou bem”
  • Vocabulário ativo da própria área, em inglês, ainda fechado. Ele LÊ os termos todo dia na interface, mas não os usa para DESCREVER o que faz. Toda aula de trabalho abre esse estoque
  • Não consegue separar trabalho e projeto próprio nem em português. “Acaba se tornando trabalho, então não consigo separar muito bem.” Em inglês isso vira uma resposta longa e confusa — e a aula 1 existe para resolver exatamente isso
  • Não consegue isolar uma prioridade. Perguntado o que resolveria primeiro: “é um pouquinho de tudo... tem essa coisa do meio do caminho andado”. Quem prioriza somos nós; o pacote entrega a prioridade pronta, em blocos
  • Ampliação lexical pedida por ele. Quer vocabulário para o curso de composição de letras — e já tem o hábito de folhear dicionário. Alimente isso com collocação e nuance, nunca com lista solta

A Jornada — narrativa do programa

O Pedro tem 30 anos e trabalha no meio de campo entre produção audiovisual e produção musical, na Endemol de São Paulo, em programas musicais como The Voice e Masked Singer. É um cargo que não cabe num título, e ele sabe: quando perguntado o que faz, a resposta sai longa e sem contorno até em português. Por padrão da área, ele usa toda a interface de software em inglês há anos, e foi assim, mais tutorial e série britânica, que construiu o inglês que tem. O contato internacional do trabalho é com uma equipe argentina — ou seja, o inglês dele não vive no escritório.

O que motiva o programa é um alvo que ele mesmo nomeou: um curso da Berklee Online, escola de música de Boston, daqui a um ano a um ano e meio, com turma nova a cada três meses. Não é um curso técnico de assistir e gravar — são os cursos com “discussão literária de fato em inglês”, em que cada aluno apresenta o próprio trabalho e comenta o dos outros. Ele quer entrar lá preparado. As palavras dele: “eu quero fazer um curso em inglês e eu quero ter a liberdade desse curso”.

A tensão central deste perfil é uma assimetria, e ela é produtiva. Ele entende muito mais do que produz. O listening está à frente, o vocabulário passivo está à frente, e a fala trava. Some a isso uma segunda dor que ele descreve com precisão: a angústia de não ter certeza se a frase estava correta, que não passa nem quando ele pesquisa e descobre que estava. Isso define duas decisões do material: (a) a gramática sai por DESCOBERTA, porque é como ele aprende, mas sempre termina em regra escrita e explícita, porque é disso que a angústia dele precisa; (b) a produção oral é MEDIDA em número, aula a aula, porque evidência é o que convence um autodidata.

O eixo anti-repetição é estrutural: cada aula tem um domínio próprio — quem ele é e o que faz (1), há quanto tempo faz e o que entregou (2), dizer o que acha (3), dentro de uma letra (4), vender uma ideia (5) — e assim até a simulação final do seminário. Aula ímpar segue o modelo de fala (diálogo line-by-line + três role-plays); aula par, o modelo de leitura (texto central + gist e true/false), que é o ensaio direto da discussão de texto que ele vai encontrar na Berklee. Cada aula faz callback do vocabulário da anterior no warm-up.

Metodologia — 5 Abordagens

1. Descoberta primeiro, REGRA ESCRITA depois — sempre as duas

Nenhuma aula abre com regra: ele largou curso na infância por ser “quadradinho” e aprende procurando padrão. Mas a descoberta nunca termina sem a tabela: a dor declarada dele é a “angústia de não ter a certeza”, e só a regra escrita fecha essa porta. Deixar implícito, com este aluno, é deixar a angústia no lugar.

2. Input por cima do nível, produção por dentro

O receptivo dele está acima do B1: ele assiste série britânica e lê interface técnica todo dia. Então listening e leitura vao em velocidade e densidade reais, sem simplificação. O que se calibra para o B1 é a PRODUÇÃO exigida, nunca o texto oferecido. Subestimar o input entedia e perde o aluno.

3. Progresso se mostra em número, aula a aula

Autodidata confia em evidência. No bloco de fala livre, conte e diga os números: quantas frases ele sustentou sem parar e quantas vezes escolheu certo a estrutura da aula. É a linguagem de progresso que funciona com ele — e é o antídoto direto do “meio do caminho andado”.

4. Meaning em inglês, nunca tradução

B1 = zero português na tela do aluno. Definição em inglês simples, enunciado em inglês, hint em inglês. O português continua onde ele não vê: nesta aba e no ícone T de cada slide.

5. Corrija na hora, e diga o porquê

Ele pediu: “eu gosto de errar e que me falem”. Correção direta não fere este aluno — guardar a correção, sim, porque alimenta a dúvida. A única exceção é o bloco de fala livre, onde se anota e se devolve no fim, inteiro.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody asks what you do, and the honest answer has two halves. The first half is the part that is true every season, and English puts it in the base form of the verb. He works between audiovisual production and music production. He mixes at night. He books the studio two weeks in advance. His team produces music shows. Nothing there is happening right now as he speaks; it is simply the shape of the job. Only the third person is different, and it costs one letter: his colleague runs the picture side, the singer performs over a backing track.

The second half is the part he is in the middle of, and there the verb changes shape completely. Right now he is building the backing tracks for a new season. This week he is sitting in rehearsals with a notebook. Today he is arguing with the picture team about a cue that runs four seconds long. These are temporary. They started, they have not finished, and in three weeks they will be somebody else's problem. The clue is usually in the sentence: right now, this week, at the moment, today.

The same two halves organize everything else he could say. He produces his own projects on the side, which is permanent. He is pitching a new format for the auditions, which is not. He always keeps the third take; he is comparing three of them this afternoon. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: a few verbs almost never take the continuous form, even when the moment is now. Not I am knowing the answer, but I know the answer. The same goes for want, need, believe and understand.

1. "He mixes at night." Why the base form with -s, and not "he is mixing at night"?
A Because "mix" is a verb that has no continuous form.
B Because it describes the general shape of his work, not something he is in the middle of.
C Because the sentence has no object after the verb.
2. "This week he is sitting in rehearsals." What does the continuous form add here?
A That it is temporary: it started, it is not finished, and it will stop.
B That it happens every week of the year.
C That it is more polite than the base form.
3. The text warns against "I am knowing the answer". What is the problem?
A "Know" can only be used in questions.
B The sentence needs a time expression such as "right now".
C "Know" describes a state, not an action in progress, so English keeps it in the base form.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

One decision: is this the shape of the job, or the middle of this week?

JobFormExample
True every seasonthe base verbI work on music shows. We book the studio in advance.
True every season, he / she / itbase verb + -sMy colleague runs the picture side. The singer performs over a backing track.
In the middle of it nowam / is / are + verb-ingRight now I am building the backing tracks. This week we are rehearsing.
Negative, every seasondo not / does not + base verbI do not work with the camera team. He does not mix live shows.
Negative, right nowam not / is not / are not + verb-ingI am not mixing this week. We are not recording today.
QuestionDo / Does ... ? · Am / Is / Are ... ?Do you mix your own tracks? · What are you working on at the moment?
The trapstate verbs stay in the base formNot I am knowing that song. Say I know that song. Same for want, need, believe, understand.

Never: I am work on music shows · my colleague run the picture side · right now I build the backing tracks · what you are working on? · I am wanting a longer cue

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There is a paragraph that follows you everywhere: under your photo, at the top of an email to a stranger, next to the link to your showreel. Every sentence in it does the same job, and English gives that job one shape: have or has plus the past participle. He has worked in television. He has specialized in live music shows. He has run the music side of a show for four seasons. All of it started in the past, and all of it is still true today, which is exactly why the past simple does not fit here.

Then the sentence usually needs a measurement, and there are only two. Since points at the beginning: since 2016, since January, since he left college. For measures the distance: for eight years, for six months. The work has been steady since 2018. The projects he does on the side have turned into half of his income. He has been building backing tracks for eight years — and that longer form, have been plus -ing, says the activity is still running right now.

Some sentences carry no date at all, and they are the strongest ones. He has delivered more than a hundred live shows. He has been nominated twice. He is self-taught, and almost every job he has had has arrived by word of mouth. Each of those is a milestone or a fact, not a story, so it needs one line and no year. And the gap? There has been one, in 2020, and he names it in six words. Two warnings for a Portuguese speaker: never the present simple with since (not I work here since 2016), and never the continuous with for (not I am working here for ten years).

1. "He has worked in television since 2016." Why not "He worked in television since 2016"?
A Because "work" has no past simple form.
B Because it started in the past and is still true today, and the past simple would close it.
C Because "since" can only be used with "have".
2. What is the difference between "since 2016" and "for eight years"?
A "Since" points at the moment it began; "for" measures how long it has lasted.
B "Since" is formal and "for" is informal.
C "Since" is for work and "for" is for study.
3. The text says "He has delivered more than a hundred live shows" is one of the strongest sentences. Why?
A Because it uses a longer verb form than the others.
B Because it explains how he got the job.
C Because it is a result that needs no date at all, so one line is enough.

Blunt -- saying the true thing directly, with no soft edges

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Somebody plays you two minutes of their own work and then goes quiet. Every sentence you are about to say sits on one decision, and the decision is not about politeness. It is about whether the sentence names a finished time. "I have listened to it three times" carries no date, and that is the point: the fact matters, the moment does not. "I listened to it last night" names a time that is over, and the verb goes straight to the past form. Same event, two different sentences, and the only thing that changed is last night.

In a feedback round the two forms usually arrive together, in this order. The question opens with the present perfect: "Have you heard the new version?" The answer explains with the past simple: "Yes, I heard it yesterday." The same pattern covers experience. "Have you ever worked with a live band?" asks about a whole life up to now, so there is no date. "I have never mixed an orchestra" says the same thing from the other side. Three irregular participles do most of the work in this room: write becomes written, hear becomes heard, send becomes sent.

Then there is the content of the note itself. The hook lands or it does not. The bass line stands out. The transition is clunky. The strings are overdone and the voice disappears under them. One change is subtle and already works; one problem would go away if you tighten the middle; one thing is only a nitpick and you say so out loud, so the writer knows how much it weighs. And if you cannot describe the sound you want, send a reference instead of an adjective. One warning for a Portuguese speaker: never put a finished time next to the present perfect. Not I have heard it yesterday.

1. Why is it "I listened to it last night" and not "I have listened to it last night"?
A Because "listen" is an irregular verb.
B Because the sentence names a finished time, and a finished time sends the verb to the past form.
C Because the present perfect cannot be used with the verb "listen".
2. According to the text, what order do the two forms usually arrive in during a feedback round?
A The question opens with the present perfect, and the answer explains with the past simple.
B The past simple always comes first, because it is shorter.
C They never appear in the same conversation.
3. The text says to send a reference instead of an adjective. Why?
A Because adjectives are informal in a feedback round.
B Because a reference is faster to send than a sentence.
C Because an adjective like "warm" means six different things, and another track shows exactly what you mean.
