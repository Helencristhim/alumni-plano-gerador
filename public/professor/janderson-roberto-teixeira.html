Leia isto antes de tudo: o que já fez este aluno desistir

Janderson tem uma história de recomeços. Estudou desde criança por obrigação, fez intercâmbio no Canadá, quase dois anos de Berlitz presencial, e comprou um pacote de 100 aulas online das quais fez 2. Este programa existe para não repetir os erros conhecidos:

  • 1. Texto denso de economia mata este aluno. Ele fez quase um ano de um curso só de inglês para finanças, aos sábados, três horas. O professor levava textos gigantescos de economia e ele passava a aula, nas palavras dele, “fingindo que eu tô entendendo”. Financças neste programa entra como cenário de role-play e vocabulário aplicado, nunca como texto técnico longo.
  • 2. Autonomia de agenda já falhou duas vezes. “Se ninguém ficar no meu pé, eu vou fazer tudo o que você imaginar, menos estudar o inglês.” Dia e horário fixos e cobrança ativa não são confortáveis: são condição de funcionamento. Se ele remarcar duas vezes seguidas, alguém fala com ele.
  • 3. O gargalo é a REUNIÃO AO VIVO, não o jargão. “Na reunião mesmo, aí que mora realmente o perigo.” Com tempo e tradutor ele monta uma apresentação. O que falta é “aquela base, aquela abertura” — improviso. Por isso toda aula termina em produção livre, sem pistas.
  • 4. A direção apontou o alvo real na consultoria: em venda de investimento, “80% da conversa é um ice breaking”, e a parte técnica de negociação é a fácil de trazer para o inglês. O programa investe onde dói: small talk, opinião, viagem, conversa de corredor.
  • 5. Ele quer material que FICA. Vocabulário no Pre-class e no Survival Card, para sempre. Ele não precisa anotar nada.

Calibragem: A2 real, com receptivo maior que o produtivo

O nivelamento deu A2 e a consultoria confirma. Na call inteira ele produziu duas expressões espontâneas em inglês (so far, so good e bye bye) e descreveu tudo em português. Ao mesmo tempo, lê material do banco em inglês todos os dias e tem MBA. Receptivo alto, produtivo travado.

(a) O erro dele não é de vocabulário, é de complicação. Ele constrói um período longo em português na cabeça, tenta traduzir e trava no meio. Quando isso acontecer, interrompa com shorter — eight words. Frase curta e correta vale mais que período elegante abandonado.

(b) Silêncio é permitido. Ele pondera antes de falar. Espere até dez segundos sem intervir. E lembre que ele carrega vergonha de trinta anos: “acho que eu sou o único que enrola ali”. Baixar a tensão é parte do método.

(c) ZERO português na tela do aluno (REGRA 13). A2 não leva tradução: leva definição em inglês simples. O português vive aqui, no Planejamento, e no ícone T de cada slide.

Personagem-Jornada

De: “Todo mundo fala inglês, acho que eu sou o único que enrola ali.” Um banker sênior de 48 anos, com MBA e pós, que cuida de investimentos offshore num banco onde a língua franca é o inglês — e que hoje declara “confiança absoluta zero”. Consegue preparar uma apresentação com tradutor e tempo; trava no improviso. Tem uma filha de oito anos que estuda inglês em três escolas e já o ultrapassa.

Para: “É agora ou nunca.” Um homem que abre a conversa em inglês no corredor do banco, sustenta quatro minutos com um cliente estrangeiro, viaja sozinho sem depender de ninguém, e diz I don't understand, could you say that again? sem sentir que fracassou.

Metodologia

Encurtar, não ampliar

Ele trava porque tenta traduzir um período longo do português. Quando isso acontecer, corte com shorter — eight words. Duas frases curtas e uma pergunta de volta é o formato do programa inteiro.

O fio entre as aulas

Todo warm-up retoma o vocabulário da aula anterior antes de abrir o novo. É o que ele nunca teve nas escolas onde recomeçava sempre do básico.

Finanças como cenário

O banco, o cliente offshore e a reunião aparecem como CONTEXTO de fala. NUNCA como texto denso de economia — foi exatamente isso que o fez abandonar um curso de quase um ano.

Cobrança com leveza

Ele PEDIU estrutura e cobrança. Comece toda aula perguntando se ele fez o Pre-class e diga o percentual em voz alta. Uma frase, sem sermão, toda aula.

Errar em voz alta

Ele passou três horas por semana, por um ano, fingindo que entendia. Aqui vale o contrário: I don't understand, could you say that again? entra no Survival Card da aula 1 de propósito.

Aula às 20h30

Ele chega direto do banco, cansado. Energia dinâmica e variada, blocos curtos, nada de exposição longa. Se um bloco não render, siga — ele volta no Pre-class.

Estrutura sugerida da aula (60 min)

  • 5 min — O fio: callback do vocabulário da aula anterior + warm-up sem saudação scriptada
  • 12 min — As dez expressões: reveal cards + pronúncia + gap-fill com banco de palavras
  • 10 min — Language focus: uma estrutura, sempre descoberta pelos exemplos antes da regra
  • 15 min — Input: diálogo line-by-line ou texto, mais os dois listenings em 0.75x e 1x
  • 15 min — Simulação: role-play guiado > semi-livre > livre. Aqui ele fala mais do que você
  • 3 min — Wrap-up: survival card, checklist e os complementares mencionados ORALMENTE

CCQs da Aula 1 (Concept Checking Questions)

  • To be based in — Is it where I am today, or where I usually work? (Where I usually work or live.)
  • A colleague — A friend, or somebody from work? (From work. It can become a friend, but the word means work.)
  • To take care of — Do I just help, or am I responsible? (I am responsible.)
  • At the moment — Always, or now? (Now — and it will change.)
  • To deal with — Only people, or also problems? (Both. It is the work you handle.)
  • A client — Does the client pay me, or do I pay the client? (The client pays.)
  • To cover for — Is that person here? (No — they are away and I do their work.)
  • Straight away — Today, or immediately? (Immediately.)
  • “I work at a bank” — Only today, or every week? (Every week — it is permanent, so present simple.)
  • “This week I am covering for a colleague” — Permanent or temporary? (Temporary — so present continuous.)
  • “He deal with clients” — Is this correct? (No — he, she and it take an S: he deals.)
  • “What you do here?” — What is missing? (Do. A present simple question borrows do or does.)
  • “I am not understanding” — Why is this wrong? (Understand is not an action, so it never takes -ing: I don't understand.)

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Janderson works at a bank in Sao Paulo. He is based in the same building every day, on the eighth floor, and he takes care of investments for about forty families. He deals with clients who put money outside Brazil, and if one of them writes to him, he answers straight away. That is his job, and it does not change from one month to the next.

This week, however, three things are different. He is covering for a colleague who is on holiday, so he is dealing with two desks instead of one. He is also starting an English course, twice a week, at half past eight in the evening. And on Thursday he is taking the whole afternoon off, which almost never happens.

On Tuesday he is the most busy, so he never books anything after six. The team takes a break at eleven and everybody walks to the coffee machine at the end of the corridor. He does not drink coffee in the afternoon. At the moment he is drinking tea instead, and his colleagues find this very funny.

If you meet him by the coffee machine and ask him what he does, he has about ninety seconds to answer. Two sentences about the job that never changes, one sentence about the week that will be over on Friday, and one question back. That is the whole first minute.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

The question is never "when?" It is always "permanent or temporary?" Answer that first and the verb chooses itself.

FormWhat it saysExample
Present simple — positivePermanent. Your job, your routine, the facts about you."I work at a bank."
"She takes care of the visitors."
Present simple — negative and questionThe sentence borrows do or does, and the main verb loses its S."I don't work on Saturdays."
"Does she deal with clients?"
Present continuous — positiveTemporary. True this week, this month, this project, and it will stop."This week I am covering for a colleague."
Present continuous — negative and questionNothing is borrowed. Am / is / are simply moves."I am not covering for anybody."
"Are you working from home?"
The words that give it awayEvery day, usually, never, on Tuesdays → simple. At the moment, this week, right now, today → continuous."I usually answer straight away."
"At the moment I am reading it."
The trapA small group of verbs about the mind never takes the -ing form: understand, know, want, need, mean, remember. They are not actions.NOT "I am not understanding"
"I don't understand."

In a first conversation you will almost always need both, one after the other: what you do, and what you happen to be doing this week. That second sentence is what makes you sound like a person and not a job title.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past Simple

GRAMMAR

One tense covers everything that already finished. The whole difficulty is one question: is the past inside the verb, or inside did? It is never inside both.

FormWhat it saysExample
Regular verbs: verb + EDMost verbs simply take -ed, and the form is the same for every subject. No S anywhere."We booked a table."
"He walked away."
Irregular verbs: a new wordAbout a hundred common verbs change completely. The ten most common cover half of what you say every day.go → went · take → took · have → had
find → found · eat → ate · sit → sat
Negative: did not + plain verbDid carries the past, so the main verb loses it."I did not go anywhere."
NOT "I did not went"
Question: did + subject + plain verbSame idea, and English needs that did. A rising voice is not enough, as it is in Portuguese."Did you take the day off?"
NOT "You took the day off?"
The exception: to beWas and were work alone. They never borrow did."The restaurant was not open."
"Were you on your own?"
The words that give it awayYesterday, last weekend, two hours ago, in twenty nineteen, when I was twenty — all of them close the door on the present."Last weekend I took a day off."

You already recognise every one of these forms when you read them. The work now is producing them at speed, in a corridor, before you have had time to think.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

The past continuous is not about time. It is a frame: it paints the scene, and the past simple is what walks into it.

FormWhat it saysExample
was / were + verb-INGThe long action. It was already running when the story starts."I was driving to the office."
Past simpleThe interruption. It happened once, quickly, and it finished."… when the road closed."
when and whileWhen usually introduces the short action; while the long one. Both orders mean the same."I was reading when he called."
"While I was reading, he called."
Two long actionsIf both were happening at the same time, both take -ing."While I was waiting, she was working from home."
Negative and questionMove was or were, or add not after it. Nothing is borrowed — there is no did here."I was not driving."
"Were you waiting long?"
The trapVerbs about the mind still refuse the -ing form, exactly as in the present.NOT "I was not knowing"
"I did not know."

One practical use of this structure at work: it explains a delay without sounding like an excuse. I was on my way when the road closed gives the reason and the timing in six words.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Comparatives and Superlatives

GRAMMAR

Three shapes cover every comparison in English, and the only technical decision is the length of the adjective.

FormWhat it saysExample
Short adjective + ER + thanOne or two syllables, comparing exactly two things.cheap → "cheaper than"
big → "bigger than"
easy → "easier than"
MORE + long adjective + thanThree syllables or more. The adjective itself never changes."more convenient than"
"more reliable than"
THE + adjective + EST / THE MOSTThree or more things. The word the is obligatory."the cheapest of the three"
"the most expensive of the three"
AS + adjective + ASEqual. With not, the first one is less."as fast as the train"
"not as reliable as the train"
The three irregularsGood, bad and far refuse both rules and you simply learn them.good → better → the best
bad → worse → the worst
far → further → the furthest
Making it stronger or softerOne word in front does all the work. Never use two comparative forms together.much cheaper · far more reliable
slightly more expensive · by far the best
NOT "more cheaper"

The word after a comparison is always than — never that, and never of. That single word is the most common comparison mistake made by Portuguese speakers, and it is a one-second fix.
