O gargalo: pergunta fechada ele responde, pergunta aberta ele trava

Pedro descreveu o próprio problema com uma precisão rara: “se viesse uma pergunta diferente, eu não saberia muito bem. Uma pergunta mais aberta, mais profunda, eu teria muita dificuldade.” Nas duas entrevistas de emprego em inglês ele se saiu bem — porque sabia as perguntas de antemão. Num congresso em Londres, quando um desconhecido perguntou o que mais tinha chamado a atenção dele nas palestras, ele travou. O inglês dele dá conta; o que falta é ter uma FORMA pronta para a resposta longa. Consequência prática: toda aula deste bloco termina numa pergunta aberta cronometrada, e o role-play 3 nunca traz keywords. Compreensão pode ser B2 cheio (áudio em ritmo natural, texto autêntico); a exigência de produção sobe de resposta de três frases (aulas 1-6) para minuto e meio sustentado (aulas 13-20).

Riscos: rotina saturada e dependência de IA

Último semestre de faculdade pela manhã e trabalho à tarde e noite: não há folga na semana dele. Ele já negociou os horários com a chefe, o que é um bom sinal, mas o Pre-class precisa ser curto de fazer e claramente útil — material que parece dever de casa não sobrevive a essa agenda. Segundo risco, e o mais estratégico: ele redige contratos em inglês com IA e disse com todas as letras que quer parar de depender disso. Se as aulas só treinarem fala, a escrita jurídica continua terceirizada. Por isso a leitura de documento real entra desde a aula 2, e a produção escrita entra como tarefa oral-escrita a partir da aula 8.

Personagem-Jornada

De: “É muito no superficial, entendeu? Eu não tenho uma conversa assim mais profunda.” Entende quase tudo, se vira em pergunta e resposta curta, e a conversa morre ali. Redige contrato em inglês com IA ao lado. Travou na pergunta aberta de um congresso em Londres.

Para: “Eu gostaria de ter uma fluência para não ser totalmente dependente da IA.” Advogado que conduz reunião com o time americano, sustenta uma conversa de uma hora sobre qualquer assunto, redige e negocia cláusula em inglês por conta própria — e faz o TOEFL sabendo o que vai encontrar.

Promessa Transformadora

Em 80 aulas, Pedro sai do inglês que serve para se virar e chega ao inglês que sustenta a carreira dele: C1 como meta declarada, com o TOEFL trabalhado num bloco próprio e não de raspão. A âncora do programa é a frase dele mesmo: “eu vou pegar pesado”. O material foi desenhado para aproveitar isso — densidade alta, tema adulto, zero infantilização — e para caber numa semana que já está cheia.

Diretriz do programa (definida pela escola)

  • General English: repertório ativo e conversa sustentada — o eixo que leva do B2 ao C1.
  • Business: reunião, negociação, apresentação, entrevista, e-mail e a rotina administrativa que cerca o jurídico.
  • Legal: terminologia de contrato, compliance e leitura de documento em inglês — sem virar aula de Direito.
  • Travel: Londres e Reino Unido, que é onde ele quer estar e o contexto cultural que mais o engaja.
  • TOEFL (~20 aulas): bloco próprio, aulas 41-60, com as quatro seções (Reading, Listening, Speaking, Writing) e mock tests completos — nunca diluído nas outras aulas.

Nas aulas 1-20 o TOEFL é HORIZONTE, não conteúdo. O bloco de exame exige repertório e resistência de produção que se constroem antes. O que este primeiro bloco entrega é a matéria-prima do TOEFL: responder pergunta aberta com estrutura, ler texto denso sem tradutor, sustentar posição e resumir o que ouviu.

Programa de 80 aulas em 4 blocos de 20. Bloco 1 (1-20): base de fluência — sair da resposta rasa, ler sem tradutor e cobrir o terreno jurídico, corporativo e de viagem. Bloco 2 (21-40): profundidade profissional — negociação longa, contrato inteiro, compliance internacional e escrita autônoma (o fim da dependência de IA). Bloco 3 (41-60): TOEFL — as quatro seções, técnica de prova e mock tests completos, num bloco próprio de ~20 horas. Bloco 4 (61-80): consolidação de C1 — debate, registro acadêmico e profissional avançado, e conversa de uma hora sobre qualquer assunto. As aulas ímpares são de FALA (diálogo e role-play) e as pares de LEITURA (texto central, gist e true/false), alternadas para que nenhuma habilidade fique para trás.

Metodologia

A pergunta aberta em toda aula

O bloco final de cada aula é sempre uma pergunta sem roteiro, cronometrada. É o exercício-alvo do programa e não se pula, mesmo quando a aula atrasa.

Correção diferida

Nada de interromper a fala. O professor anota e devolve no fim, em bloco. Interromper este aluno faz a resposta encolher — que é exatamente o problema que viemos resolver.

Ler e escrever fixa nele

Ele mesmo descreveu como aprende: lê, depois escreve ou pratica. O Pre-class explora isso — texto e produção escrita curta antes da aula — e a aula usa a fala em cima do que ele já leu.

Vocabulário ativo, não reconhecido

12-15 expressões novas por aula, sempre cobradas na produção do mesmo dia. Palavra que ele só reconhece não conta — o eixo B2 para C1 é ativação, não exposição.

Tema adulto, densidade alta

Direito, negócios, atualidade, Londres, esporte. Zero infantilização e zero português na tela (REGRA 13): em B2 a tela do aluno é 100% inglês.

Saída programada da IA

A dependência de IA na redação é tratada como meta explícita: da aula 8 em diante, toda aula de leitura pede um parágrafo escrito por ele, sem ferramenta, discutido na aula seguinte.

Estrutura sugerida da aula

  • 10% — Warm-up: pergunta aberta de entrada, sem correção
  • 20% — Vocabulário: reveal cards, pronúncia e uso em contexto
  • 15% — Language focus: descoberta da estrutura, erro comum e drill
  • 25% — Input autêntico: diálogo line-by-line (ímpar) ou texto central (par) + dois listenings
  • 25% — Produção: role-play guiado > semi-livre > livre, terminando na pergunta aberta cronometrada
  • 5% — Wrap-up: survival card, checklist e tarefa dita ORALMENTE

CCQs da Aula 1 (Concept Checking Questions)

  • “I have worked here since 2023” — Do I still work here? (Sim — o present perfect com since mantém a situação aberta.)
  • “I worked there in 2023” — Do I still work there? (Não — o past simple fecha.)
  • “Have you been to London?” — Am I asking WHEN? (Não — só SE, alguma vez na vida.)
  • “She has gone to London” — Is she here now? (Não, ela está lá. Com been, ela já voltou.)
  • In-house counsel — Does this lawyer work for a law firm or for the company? (Para a empresa — é o cargo dele daqui a seis meses.)
  • To draft — Is a draft the final version? (Não — a primeira.)
  • Compliance — Is it only about the law? (Não — qualquer regra que se aplique à empresa, inclusive as internas.)
  • To touch on — Deep or brief? (Breve, de passagem — o oposto de to unpack.)
  • A counterpart — Does my counterpart work with me, or in another company? (Em outra empresa ou país, no mesmo cargo.)
  • Off the top of my head — Exact or approximate? (Aproximado — e é a fórmula que salva quem foi pego de surpresa.)

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Last June I went to my first international conference. I had a badge with my name on it, a lanyard, and no idea what to do with either. I have been in the legal team of a media company since 2023, and by then I had drafted a fair number of contracts in English — with a lot of help from a machine, which is a separate problem. So I could follow the sessions. What I could not do was talk about them.

The keynote that morning was excellent and far too short. The speaker touched on cross-border compliance for about four minutes and moved on, and four minutes was exactly the part of my job that nobody ever explains. My one takeaway from the whole day was that question: how do you build a rulebook that works in two countries at once. If somebody had unpacked that on stage, I would have paid for the ticket twice.

Then, in the coffee break, a man I had never met turned to me and asked what had struck me most in the talks. It is not a difficult question. It is not even a legal question. And I went completely blank — not because I did not have an answer, but because I had never practised giving one. I said something about the venue. He said something polite. That was the end of it.

I have thought about that thirty seconds many times since then. The lesson was not about vocabulary. Anybody who can draft a contract has the words. The lesson was that an open question needs a shape, decided in advance: one thing, why it stayed with you, what changes on Monday. Thirty seconds. I have never been caught by that question again, and I have not forgotten the name of the man who asked it, either — he is now a counterpart of mine in London, and we have worked on two deals together.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

Portuguese has one past where English has two. That is the whole difficulty: the choice is not a translation, it is a decision about the timeline. One question settles almost every case — does the sentence name a finished time?

FormWhat it doesExample
have / has
+ past participle		Something that started in the past and is still true now, or an experience with no time attached. The moment is irrelevant — the result is not."I have worked here since 2023."
"I have drafted contracts in English."
past simpleA finished action at a finished time. If the sentence says when — yesterday, last year, in June, two hours ago — it is past simple, always."I drafted it last year."
"The keynote finished twenty minutes ago."
for · sinceFor + a length of time. Since + the point where it started. Both belong to the present perfect. "I work here since 2023" is the single commonest error a Portuguese speaker makes in English."for two years"
"since March 2023"
just · already
yet · ever · never		Recent, earlier than expected, still expected, or at any point in a life. Ever and never are the words of the experience question — and they cannot be built with did."We have just opened an office."
"Have you ever worked with an American client?"
been x goneOne letter apart, opposite meanings. Been = went and came back. Gone = went and is still there."She has been to London." (she is back)
"She has gone to London." (she is there)

One habit worth building this week: every time you say since or for about your own work, stop and check that the verb in front of it is have or has. Nine times out of ten the sentence is about you, it is true right now, and the present perfect is the only tense that says so.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Read any corporate document in English and you will notice the same thing: almost nobody does anything. The subsidiary was incorporated. The filings were made. The account is subject to review. Somebody made every one of those decisions, and the sentences are built so that you do not have to say who. That is the passive voice, and in business and legal English it is not decoration. It is the default.

There are three honest reasons for it. Sometimes the person is unknown — nobody can say which officer looked at the form. Sometimes the person is obvious, and naming them wastes the reader's time. And very often the ACTION is what matters: what a contract cares about is that notice shall be given in writing, not about the individual who prints the letter. Add by only when the agent is new information: "all filings are reviewed by outside counsel" is worth writing, and "the letter was signed by a person" is not.

The dishonest reason is worth knowing too, because you will meet it. "Mistakes were made" is the most famous sentence in corporate English precisely because it is a confession with the confessor removed. When a report tells you that a decision was taken and never says by whom, the grammar is doing work that the writer did not want to do in the open.

The form itself is simple, and it is where the errors live. Only be carries the tense: is signed, was signed, has been signed, will be signed, can be signed. The participle never changes. After by, English uses the object pronoun — by us, never by we. And a company with no track record in a market is treated differently from one that has been there for ten years, which is a sentence you will read in some form every week for the rest of this expansion.

That second sentence is a defining relative clause, and it takes no commas. Take the commas seriously, because they change what you are saying. The clauses that protect us are rewritten in plain English means some clauses protect us, and those are the ones being rewritten. The clauses, which protect us, are rewritten in plain English means all of them protect us and all of them are being rewritten. The comma is not decoration. It is the difference between some and all, and in a contract that difference is the whole argument.

Non-defining clauses are the ones you could delete without damage. They always take commas, and they never take that. Our head of product, who is not a lawyer, has to understand the clause in one sentence. Delaware, where the subsidiary was incorporated, has its own company law. A partner whose filings are always late is telling you something before the due diligence gets a chance to.

Two habits will make you sound like somebody who speaks English rather than somebody who writes it. First, drop the pronoun when it is the object of its own clause: the safeguard we rely on, not the safeguard which we rely on. Second, do not name the object twice. GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are four words in an English contract that decide what your company is committed to, and every one of them is short. Shall and must create an obligation: this has to happen. May creates a permission: this is allowed, and nobody is forcing it. Shall not is a prohibition. And is entitled to states a right that belongs to one party whether the other side is happy about it or not.

The dangerous one is may. In Portuguese, pode carries permission and possibility at the same time, so a clause that GRANTS something reads like a clause that REQUIRES it. Either party may terminate the agreement on thirty days notice means nobody is locked in. Translate that as an obligation and you have written a contract that forces both sides out of a relationship they wanted to keep. That mistake is usually found two years later, by which time it is somebody else's problem.

The form is simple and it is where the errors live. After shall, must and may the verb is bare: shall indemnify, never shall to indemnify. There is no third person -s: may not accept, never may not accepts. And the passive is everywhere, because the obligation matters more than the person: Notice must be given in writing, payment is to be made within thirty days.

One last habit worth building. When you read a provision, say out loud which of the three things it does — obliges, permits or prohibits. If you cannot say which, the clause is either badly drafted or you have not finished reading it. That single question is what separates reading a contract from looking at one, and it takes about four seconds.

FormWhat it doesExample

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

There are two ways to ask the same question in English, and the polite one is not simply longer. Where are we on the indemnity? is direct and perfectly correct. Could you tell me where we are on the indemnity? is the version that opens a conversation instead of demanding an answer — and the moment you add that opening, something has to move inside the sentence.

What moves is the word order. Once the question has already been asked by Could you tell me, the second half goes back to being a normal statement: subject, then verb. where we are, not where are we. The auxiliary goes too: Do you know what he wants?, never what does he want. The tense is not lost — it moves onto the main verb, which is doing the work all along.

For a yes/no question there is nothing to move, so English adds a word instead: if or whether. I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed. Not that, which is the commonest mistake here, because Portuguese uses que in both places and nothing warns you. Whether is safer in writing and obligatory before or not; if is what everybody says out loud.

The reason any of this matters is speed. In a meeting you have about two seconds to take the turn, and Sorry, can I jump in? and Could you tell me... are prefabricated: you start speaking before the sentence is finished in your head, and the grammar buys you the rest. That is the real function of these openers. They are not manners. They are time.

"Would you mind telling me on your side? (Who signs this?)"
"I am not sure . (When was the filing sent?)"

Stage 2: Put the Sequence in Order

Order

Listen to the summary of how a good call is closed, then put the steps in order.

?Somebody says at the start that they have a hard stop, so the decisions come first.
?A point is raised that affects only two people, and it is taken offline.
?Somebody pushes back on a deadline instead of agreeing to it in silence.
?The finance team is looped in on the item nobody in the room can answer.
?Before anybody leaves, one person says out loud what was decided, by whom and by when.

Stage 3: Pronunciation

Speaking

Listen, then record yourself. You will see which words came through clearly. The speed buttons at the top of the page slow the audio down.

Sorry, can I jump in for a second?
Could you tell me where we are on the indemnity?
I was wondering whether we could circle back to that at the end.
Before we close, can I check that we are on the same page?
Do you know what he wants from us before Friday?

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

You are on a call with the American side. Choose the version that works.

You want to ask where a point stands, politely:
A "Could you tell me where are we on the indemnity?"
B "Could you tell me where we are on the indemnity?"
C "Could you tell me where do we are on the indemnity?"
You want to ask what the other side wants:
A "Do you know what does he want from us?"
B "Do you know what he wants from us?"
C "Do you know what he want from us?"
You want to ask a yes/no question indirectly:
A "I was wondering that the deadline is fixed."
B "I was wondering whether the deadline is fixed."
C "I was wondering is the deadline fixed."
The call is moving on and the item matters to you:
A Say nothing and send an email afterwards.
B "Sorry, can I jump in? This one is a blocker on our side."
C "Excuse me, you are speaking too fast for me."
You are asked for a number you do not have:
A "I do not know."
B "I would rather not guess on the call. Can I run a figure by the board and come back to you?"
C "Maybe around one million, more or less, I am not sure."

Stage 5: Free Production

Reflection

Record yourself answering the question below. Nobody is scoring it.

Think of a real call from this week. Say out loud, in about ninety seconds, what was decided, who is doing what and by when, and what is still open. Then add the one question you did not ask during that call — as an indirect question, the way you would say it now.
