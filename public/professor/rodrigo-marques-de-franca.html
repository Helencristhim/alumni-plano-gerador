O Rodrigo é advogado empresarial e corporativo em São Paulo, baiano, 49 anos. O inglês entra no trabalho dele por três portas: revisão de peças, arbitragens e leitura de contratos e documentos — e, eventualmente, algumas calls. Metodologia — 5 Abordagens

1. Um domínio por aula, declarado antes de escrever

O universo dele é largo — a prática, a leitura semanal, a call, o contrato, a viagem, o cinema, a mesa de jantar. Cada aula recebe um desses eixos, com vocabulário e gênero próprios, escolhido antes de a aula ser escrita. É o que impede que dez aulas virem dez variações da mesma conversa sobre direito.

2. Produção desde o primeiro minuto

Ele não precisa ser aquecido afetivamente: declarou "zero" medo de errar. O tempo de fala dele é alto desde a aula 1, e a correção vem no fim do bloco, não no meio da frase. O risco com este aluno é entediar, nunca constranger.

3. Estrutura por dentro de assunto adulto

A base entra dentro do tema denso: a mesma frase que descreve a carreira é a que treina o present perfect. A primeira escola dele foi "muito mais voltada para a estrutura gramatical do que para a conversação" — o antídoto não é fugir da gramática, é chegar nela por descoberta e sair dela falando.

4. Meaning em inglês, nunca tradução

B2 significa zero português na tela: definição em inglês simples, exemplo em inglês, hint em inglês. O português segue apenas onde o aluno não vê — nesta aba e nas instruções do ícone T.

5. Formato muda a cada dez ou quinze minutos

Ele descreveu o próprio estilo: "a interação entre ver e ouvir, escrever, etc., para mim funciona". Cada aula alterna reveal, descoberta, áudio, documento real e role-play. Bloco expositivo longo é o que perde este aluno — e foi por sobrecarga, não por desinteresse, que ele parou da última vez.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

At a seminar in Lisbon, somebody turns to the lawyer beside her and asks the standard question. He has worked in corporate law for twenty years, so the answer could take an hour, and he gives it in two sentences instead. He studied in a small city in the nineties, when the work there did not need English at all, and he moved south in 2005. That was the turning point.

Since then he has been at the same firm, and his caseload has grown every year. He is still hands-on: he reads the contracts himself instead of only signing off on them, and on a bad week he is juggling four matters that all became urgent on the same afternoon. It is a demanding practice. He has taken on two new matters this year and he has not turned down anything yet, which he admits is a problem.

She asks whether he has ever argued a case in English. He has, once. He argued it in 2019, and he says it was the longest afternoon of his professional life. He stopped studying the language for a year when a stint on an impossible file took over his calendar, and this month he is back on track. His track record speaks for itself; what he wants now is for his English to speak as clearly as it does.

1. "He has worked in corporate law for twenty years." Why not "He worked"?
A Because twenty years is a very long time.
B Because the period is still open: he started twenty years ago and he is still there.
C Because corporate law is a permanent profession.
2. "He moved south in 2005." Why is this not "He has moved south in 2005"?
A Because 2005 is a closed year, and a closed time takes the past simple.
B Because moving is a short action and short actions never take have.
C Because he moved only once.
3. "She asks whether he has ever argued a case in English. He has, once. He argued it in 2019." Why does the tense change in the third sentence?
A Because the second speaker is different from the first.
B Because ever and once cannot appear in the same paragraph.
C Because the date arrives. Experience with no date takes have; as soon as the year is named, the sentence moves to the past simple.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

It is often said that critics agree about the great productions and only argue about the mediocre ones. The current revival is a useful test, because it has been reviewed twice by the same newspaper and the two verdicts barely touch. The first review, written after the opening night, called the staging atmospheric and the lead role understated to the point of invisibility. The second, three weeks later, described the same performance as the most gripping thing on a London stage this year.

Nothing on stage had been changed. What had changed was the audience. A production that is built as a slow burn is punished by an opening night, where the room is looking for an effect in the first ten minutes. Three weeks later the theatre is full of people who paid and who arrive prepared to give it time. It is now widely believed in the industry that opening-night reviews measure the room as much as the work, and several papers have quietly moved their reviewers to the second week.

None of which means reviews are worthless. A review that says a production is uneven, and then says exactly which twenty minutes fall flat, is worth more than any number of stars. A play can be panned and still be worth your evening; a play that is thought to be a masterpiece can be, on the night you go, overrated and far too long. The production is set in a single room over one night, it ends with a standing ovation most evenings, and none of that tells you whether you will like it. The useful question is never whether the critic liked it. It is what the critic was looking at.

1. "It is now widely believed in the industry that opening-night reviews measure the room." Who believes this?
A The writer of the article, and only the writer.
B Nobody is named. The structure reports an opinion held generally, without the writer having to adopt it or attribute it to anybody.
C The newspaper that published the two reviews.
2. "Nothing on stage had been changed." Why the passive rather than "nobody had changed anything on stage"?
A Because who might have changed it is irrelevant. The passive puts the stage at the front, which is what the sentence is about.
B Because the passive is always more formal, and formal is always better.
C Because the writer does not know who was responsible.
3. "A play that is thought to be a masterpiece can be overrated." What is this structure doing?
A Stating that the play is definitely a masterpiece.
B Stating that the play is definitely not a masterpiece.
C Reporting the general opinion and then disagreeing with it in the same sentence. The writer never has to say who thinks so.
