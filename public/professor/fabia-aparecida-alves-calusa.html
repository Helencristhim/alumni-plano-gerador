Um prospect chegou pelo site e pediu a reunião em inglês. Fábia entrou com o sócio (ex-Price, morou em Londres). CCQs da Aula 1 (REGRA 23)

"Outsourcing" — Does the client keep doing the work with you, or do they stop? (they stop — you take it over)
"We handle payroll" — Is this only this month, or every month? (every month — that is why it is not "we are handling")
"Stakeholder" vs "decision-maker" — Ten people care about the deal. How many can sign it? (maybe one — the rest are stakeholders)
"My team handles" — Why the s here, and not in "we handle"? (my team = it, like she)
"Who handles payroll for you?" — Where is the do in this question? (there is none — "who" is the subject)
"Let me make sure I understood you correctly" — Are you saying you were wrong? (no — you are buying time and checking, and it sounds senior)

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

A short email from a prospect. Read it once, then answer.

Dear Fábia,

Thank you for your time yesterday. As I mentioned, we are opening a branch in São Paulo in January, and we do not want to build a finance team from scratch. We specialise in logistics software — accounting is not what we do well.

David handles operations and I look after finance, so the numbers come to me. Our head office in London signs off on any contract above fifty thousand, which means Ralph is the decision-maker, not us.

Could you send a short agenda before Tuesday? Ten people are joining and I would rather nobody talked over anybody.

Best,
Karen
Who signs off on the contract?
A Karen, because the numbers come to her.
B Ralph, at head office in London.
Why does Karen write "we specialise in logistics software" and not "we are specialising"?
A Because it is what the company always does, not something temporary.
B Because the sentence is in the past.
Why does she want an agenda?
A Because the meeting is short.
B Because ten people are joining and she does not want them talking over each other.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

What a firm does vs. what is happening

This one distinction changes how solid your firm sounds on a call.

Always true

We handle payroll.
We specialise in outsourcing.
My team takes care of tax.

Happening now

We are opening a branch.
Ten people are joining.
I am reviewing the numbers.

If you say "we are handling payroll for two hundred companies", the client hears just for now. Say "we handle" — that is thirty years of firm, not this month.

+ We handle the whole back office.
− We do not handle consulting. (short: don’t)
? Who handles payroll for you today? (asking about the subject — no do needed)

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

A short company profile. Read it once, then answer.

Meridian was established in 2003 and is headquartered in Lisbon. The firm has been serving international companies for more than twenty years.

Since 2019 its workforce has doubled, and its headcount now stands at eighty. Meridian serves a hundred and forty clients, and most of them have been with the firm for over five years.

Compliance sits at the centre of the business: every process has been audited every year since 2015. The firm set up its Brazilian desk in 2021.
Is Meridian still serving international companies today?
A Yes — "has been serving" means it started before and has not stopped.
B No — it served them for twenty years and then stopped.
Why is it "for more than twenty years" but "since 2019"?
A Because one is about the firm and the other about the staff.
B Because for measures how long, and since marks when it started.
Why is "set up its Brazilian desk in 2021" in the past simple?
A Because it happened once, on a date, and finished.
B Because the Brazilian desk has already closed.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Still true → have been. Finished → past simple.

On a call, this is not a grammar point. It is whether the client thinks you are still in business.

for + how long

for thirty years
for over two decades
for six months

since + the starting point

since 1994
since we opened
since the pandemic

Say "we worked in payroll for thirty years" and a prospect hears that you closed that division. Say "we have worked" and thirty years becomes an argument instead of an obituary.

+ We have been in this market for over thirty years.
− We have not had a single payroll error this year.
? How long have you been in this market? (not "How long are you")
⚠ One finished moment with a date: We set up the BPO division in 2018. (past simple, not "have set up")

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

A message from a prospect after the proposal. Read it once, then answer.

Hi Fábia,

I’ve already been through the proposal twice. The scope of work looks right and the fee structure makes sense — more than the other two we’ve seen.

Two problems. You’ve put six weeks to onboard us and we open in four. And head office hasn’t approved the budget yet, so I can’t sign this week.

Have you ever done a four-week onboarding? I’ve just forwarded your credentials to London, so they know who you are.

Has David read the proposal?
A Yes — twice, and sooner than you would expect.
B No — he is waiting for head office first.
Why "hasn’t approved the budget yet" and not "already"?
A Because head office will never approve it.
B Because it has not happened up to now, but it is still expected.
What does "I’ve just forwarded your credentials" tell you about when it happened?
A A moment ago — almost certainly today.
B Some time last month.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Four small words, four different messages

On a call, these are not grammar. They are tone.

already — sooner than expected. Goes between have and the verb.
"We’ve already onboarded twelve international clients." → this is proof.
just — a moment ago. Same position.
"I’ve just sent you our credentials." → this is momentum.
yet — not until now, but still expected. Goes at the end, and only in negatives and questions.
"We haven’t defined the scope yet." → this is a door left open.
ever — at any point in your life. Only in questions.
"Have you ever outsourced payroll before?" → this is discovery.

⚠ "Did you ever work with...?" is not wrong in American English, but on a first call "Have you ever worked with...?" keeps the door open to now. That is the one to use.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

A reply to a client who asked for something outside the scope. Read it once, then answer.

Hi David,

Two extra reports a month is not in the current scope, but I am not going to say no.

If we move you to a monthly retainer, both reports will sit inside it and you stop worrying about what is included. If your team sends the data by the tenth, the reports will be ready in the same month rather than the following one.

On the timeline: if they hand over clean data in week one, we will finish the onboarding in four weeks instead of six.

And a thought. If we handled your whole back office, you would not need three people on this internally — you would keep one and move the other two. Something to consider.

If that works for you, I will follow up with a revised proposal today.

Fábia
Does Fábia refuse the two extra reports?
A Yes — she says they are not in the scope.
B No — she accepts them, on the condition that the contract changes to a retainer.
Why "if we handled... you would not need" and not "if we handle... you will not need"?
A Because it is a picture she is painting, not an offer on the table yet.
B Because it already happened in the past.
What is missing from every "if" half of these sentences?
A A subject.
B The word will — it never goes inside the if half.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

A real offer, or a picture you paint

In a negotiation these are not two tenses. They are two different levels of commitment.

If + present, will

If you sign this week, we will start on Monday.
If they hand over the data, we will finish in four weeks.

You are committing. Only say it if you can do it.

If + past, would

If we handled everything, you would cut two roles.
If you had a partner here, what would change?

You are exploring. Nothing is promised yet.

The one mistake: "If you will sign this week..." The if half never takes will. The will lives in the other half of the sentence.

Openers worth memorising: "If that works for you..." · "In that case, we would..." · "Let’s say, hypothetically..."

B Notifying the client within 24 hours of a breach.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Four strengths, four different messages

On a compliance call, the modal you choose is the answer.

must — no choice. Law, regulation, or firm policy.
"We must notify you within 24 hours."
should — we recommend it. Good practice, not obligation.
"You should keep a copy on your side."
can — we are able to. The door is open if they want it.
"We can give your auditors read-only access."
may / might — it is possible. Honest about uncertainty.
"Onboarding may take an extra week at that volume."

Two mistakes to avoid: never must to (the verb after a modal stays bare: must keep), and modals never take an s: musts, cans.

Softening without weakening: could and would are the polite versions of can and will. "Could you send the data by Friday?" is more senior than "Can you send the data?". But never soften an obligation: "we should notify you in a breach" turns a law into a suggestion, and a client hears that as risk.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

The fact stays. The person steps back.

That is the whole job of the passive in a business email.

Active

I sent the proposal.
You have not approved it.
We will issue the invoice.

Passive — be + past participle

The proposal was sent.
It has not been approved yet.
The invoice will be issued.

Passive is not always better. When the credit is yours, the active voice sells: "We delivered it two days early", never "it was delivered". Passive for the problem, active for the achievement.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

A short stretch of a call, with the checks left in. Read it once, then answer.

David: We are looking at forty-eight employees, live on the first of March.
You: Forty-eight? The file we have says forty-two.
David: Forty-eight. The old spreadsheet is out of date.
You: Let me note that down. And Ralph approves the budget, doesn’t he?
David: He does, from London. Nothing moves until he has seen it.
You: Sorry, I didn’t catch the date for the file.
David: The tenth. Not the twentieth — the tenth.
You: Just to confirm: the tenth, and it goes to you, is that right?
David: To me. And copy Ralph.
You: Let me summarize: forty-eight people, file to you by the tenth, Ralph in copy, go live on the first of March.
Why "Forty-eight?" instead of "Sorry, could you repeat everything?"
A It is shorter, and short is always more polite.
B It asks about one thing only, so the call keeps moving instead of starting again.
C It hides the fact that you did not understand.
What is the job of the last line, the summary?
A To show David that you were paying attention.
B To catch anything still wrong while he is still on the call to correct it.
C To fill the last minute politely before saying goodbye.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Checking has three sizes. Pick the smallest one that works.

1. Echo the fact. Repeat the word you are unsure about, with your voice going up. "The tenth?" · "Ralph, in London?" · "Forty-eight?" This is the cheapest check there is: it asks about one thing and costs the other person two seconds.

2. Add a tag when you want a clear yes or no. Positive sentence, negative tag: "You said Tuesday, didn’t you?" · "Ralph approves it, doesn’t he?" If the tag is hard in the moment, "is that right?" works after anything and never changes shape.

3. Ask for the whole thing again only when you really lost the sentence: "Sorry, I didn’t catch that last part." · "Could you rephrase that?"

Most people jump straight to 3, or say nothing at all. The echo is what fluent professionals actually use, and it is the easiest of the three to say.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

Grammar

Three minutes of a monthly review call. Read it once, then answer.

Let me take the four lines in order.

Revenue closed at one point four eight million, which is nine percent higher than the second quarter, and about eighty thousand ahead of forecast.

The margin is where it gets interesting: twenty-two percent, three points lower than a year ago — and that is on higher revenue, which is unusual.

Why does she call the margin line "interesting" rather than "bad"?
A Because twenty-two percent is a good margin in any sector.
B Because it fell while revenue rose, and that combination points at a specific line.
C Because the client had already seen the figure before the call.
Every figure in this report arrives with something next to it. What is it, and why?
A A comparison — without one, a number tells the client nothing about whether it is good or bad.
B A percentage, because percentages sound more professional than absolute figures.
C A date, so that the client can find the figure in the system afterwards.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip

Grammar

Two things, or all of them.

Two things → comparative. Short word, add -er: higher, lower, thinner, stronger. Long word, use more: more profitable, more consistent. Then than: "revenue is higher than last quarter".

All of them → superlative. the highest, the lowest, the strongest · the most profitable. Almost always followed by a limit: "the highest since 2023", "the strongest quarter of the year".

Never both. "more higher" does not exist. To intensify, use much or slightly: "much higher", "slightly better than average".

And the one that is not grammar: the decimal separator. On the page it is 12.5; out loud it is "twelve point five". Never "comma".
