Why I Stayed in the Room

Why I Stayed in the Room

I was fifteen, and bored, the first time a painting refused to let me leave. My mother had brought me to a museum on a gray Sunday in November, and I had developed a meticulous strategy for surviving such mundane afternoons: walk briskly, read one label per room, and locate the coffee shop within the hour.

The strategy collapsed in a small gallery at the end of a corridor. The room was nearly empty, and the light had been kept deliberately low. On the far wall hung a canvas by Mark Rothko, two dark red rectangles floating on a field of deeper maroon. There was nothing to identify, no figure, no story, no object that a label could explain. I remember thinking, with the unearned confidence of a teenager, that a child could have painted it. I also remember that I did not move for twenty minutes.

What happened in those twenty minutes remains, in some sense, ineffable. The edges of the rectangles seemed to breathe. The red was not a single color but a disquieting negotiation between several, and the longer I looked, the less stable it became. The experience was visceral before it was intellectual: my chest tightened, and I felt, absurdly, as if I were being addressed. I left the museum profoundly unsettled and entirely unable to say why.

It would be tidy to report that I decided to become an art historian that afternoon. I did not. What the painting gave me was not an answer but a question, and the question proved catalytic, even galvanizing. How could something so deceptively simple produce a response so out of proportion to its means? I began to read, first clumsily and then compulsively. I learned that Rothko had wanted viewers to stand close to his canvases, that he had grown acutely aware of how lighting and hanging altered them, and that critics had described his late work as everything from sublime to empty.

None of that reading dissolved the original experience; if anything, it sharpened it. That, I have come to believe, is the quietly revolutionary promise of the discipline. Art history does not explain feelings away. It gives them a vocabulary precise enough to be tested, argued with and shared. A formative encounter becomes a research question, and a private shiver becomes a claim that other people can examine, challenge and, occasionally, share.

Years later, with a degree in art history and a career I had not planned, I found myself reading application essays for a living. I can usually tell within a paragraph which kind of writer I am meeting. The overwrought essay announces that art has always been the writer's passion and that one painting changed everything. The compelling one is more modest and far more incisive. It describes what the writer actually saw, admits what the writer did not understand, and follows the question that the confusion produced. Occasionally such an essay is riveting from its first line.

Candor, in other words, is more persuasive than certainty. The seminal moment in most intellectual lives is rarely revelatory in the way we imagine. It is usually a small, stubborn discomfort that refuses to go away, and there is something poignant about watching a young writer admit to one. The essays that stay with me are the ones in which the writer had the courage to stay in the room.

Chapter 4: The Style Code

Praise That Is Precise, Not Loud

The adverb that calibrates the adjective

Discover the Style

What Is the Adverb Doing?

"I left the museum profoundly unsettled and entirely unable to say why."

"How could something so deceptively simple produce such a response?"

"He had grown acutely aware of how lighting altered his canvases."

"That is the quietly revolutionary promise of the discipline."

Remove each colored adverb. What does the sentence lose, and why is very never a good replacement?

FormWhat it doesExample
degree adverb + adjectivemeasures the intensity exactly instead of turning up the volumeprofoundly unsettled, acutely aware
contrastive adverb + adjectivebuilds a tension between how something looks and what it isdeceptively simple, quietly revolutionary
viewpoint adverb + adjectivenames the dimension in which the adjective is trueintellectually demanding, emotionally honest
fixed collocationssome adverbs only sit naturally with certain adjectivesbitterly disappointed, acutely aware, deeply moving
The C2 trapwrong partners and stacked intensifiers. "Highly aware" and "utterly interesting" sound translated; "really extremely important" sounds panicked. One precise pair beats three loud words.

The First Meeting with the College Counselor

D
So, Stella. Art history. Let me guess: art has always been your passion.
S
I was going to say exactly that, which is probably a bad sign.
D
It is the sentence I read more than any other. Every reader has seen it hundreds of times, so it tells them nothing about you.
S
Then what should I say? It is true, even if it is a cliche.
D
Tell me about one specific moment instead. Not your whole life with art. One afternoon.
S
Last year I saw a very small painting in a museum that was deceptively simple, and I could not stop thinking about it for weeks.
D
Good. Now, what did you actually see? Leave out the adjectives for a second.
S
A woman at a window, mostly in gray. The light came from a place I could not find in the picture.
D
That sentence is worth ten passions. When I applied to college, I wrote two pages about loving history and never mentioned a single document I had read.
S
So the essay is not about proving that I love art. It is about showing that I look.

What Admissions Readers Remember

An admissions officer talks to a room of applicants. Sound first, no text.

0:000:00
1. How much experience does the speaker have, and how long does she say she spends on a first read of an essay?
She has read applications for eleven years, and she spends only a few minutes on a first read.
2. What does she say about essays that open with a famous quotation or a dictionary definition?
They borrow someone else's voice at the exact moment she wants to hear the applicant's, so she usually forgets them.
3. According to her, what makes an essay memorable, and what example does she give?
Specific, honest detail that only that applicant could write, like the student who wrote about reorganizing her grandmother's spice cabinet.

Hi everyone, I'm Rachel, and I have read college applications for eleven years, so let me tell you honestly what I remember. During the busiest weeks, a first read of an essay takes me only a few minutes. That is not because I don't care. It is because I have to read a great many of them, and the ones that survive are the ones that give me something I have never read before. Essays that open with a famous quotation or a dictionary definition almost never survive. They borrow someone else's voice at the exact moment I want to hear yours. What I remember is specific, honest detail that only one person could have written. Years ago a student wrote about reorganizing her grandmother's spice cabinet, and by the end I understood how she thinks, what she notices and what she is afraid of losing. She never once told me she was curious or passionate. She didn't need to. So when you write, don't try to impress me. Try to be the only person who could have written your essay.

Hey, Maya, it's Marcus. Mom said you're stuck on your college essay, so I'm recording this while I walk to the library. When I got here two years ago I was going to study economics. Quick Fire

Calibrate the Praise

1 / 6

Calibrate: "The museum trip was very important to me."

  • The museum trip proved quietly formative.
  • Name the kind of importance, not the volume.

Calibrate: "The documentary was really interesting."

  • The documentary was genuinely compelling.
  • Compelling says it convinced you; interesting says almost nothing.

Calibrate: "The ending made me super sad."

  • The ending was quietly poignant.
  • Poignant already contains the sadness. No intensifier needed.

Calibrate: "The painting is kind of weird and makes me uncomfortable."

  • The painting is faintly disquieting.
  • Faintly calibrates down, and that makes it more believable.

Calibrate: "I could not stop reading, it was so amazing."

  • The book was riveting.
  • Riveting describes the effect you just explained.

Calibrate: "That class changed my whole life."

  • That class proved catalytic.
  • Catalytic claims less than your whole life, so a reader believes more.
