A Jornada — narrativa do programa

Thiago tem uma viagem marcada para o começo de dezembro e uma carreira dentro de uma consultoria americana. O programa segue exatamente essa ordem. As seis primeiras aulas são a viagem acontecendo em tempo real: o avião pousa, o hotel recebe, o restaurante serve, a cidade confunde, algo dá errado, a conta chega. Da aula 7 à 12 ele deixa de sobreviver e passa a conversar — small talk, rotina, Fórmula 1, cultura, histórias, planos. Da 13 em diante o inglês entra no trabalho dele: explicar o que faz, ler documento técnico, conduzir call, defender prazo, reportar problema de obra, discutir contrato de concessão, apresentar número e recomendar solução. Cada aula tem UM ponto de estrutura, sempre novo, porque o gap declarado é de estruturação — e cada complementar traz uma missão ESCRITA, porque escrever é a habilidade que ele não treina.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

The line at passport control moves slowly. When your turn comes, the officer asks four short questions, always in the same shape. "What is the purpose of your trip?" "How long are you staying?" "Where are you staying?" "Do you have anything to declare?" Notice what happens in every one of them: the little word is, are or do jumps in front of the subject. In Portuguese it stays behind, and that is exactly why a question in English often comes out sounding like a statement.

1. In "Where are you staying?", what comes immediately after the question word?
A The subject: "Where you are staying?"
B The auxiliary: "Where are you staying?"
C The main verb: "Where staying you are?"
2. Which question is correct?
A "How long you stay in the country?"
B "How long stay you in the country?"
C "How long are you staying in the country?"
3. Which word makes "You have anything to declare" become a real question?
A Do, at the front: "Do you have anything to declare?"
B Nothing. A question mark is enough.
C Is, at the front: "Is you have anything to declare?"

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The order of a question

GRAMMAR

A question in English always puts the auxiliary in front of the subject.

Question wordAuxiliary / beSubjectRest
Whereareyoustaying?
How longareyoustaying?
—Doyouhave anything to declare?
Whatisthe purposeof your trip?

With be (is, are, was) the verb itself moves to the front. With any other verb you need do, does or did, and the main verb stays in its base form: "Did you check a bag?", never "Did you checked a bag?".

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- There is / there are and could

GRAMMAR

One structure says what exists in a place. The other asks for what you want.

StructureUseExample
There is + singularOne thing existsThere is a safe in the room.
There are + pluralTwo or more things existThere are two beds and one sofa.
Is there / Are thereAsk whether it existsIs there a pool on the roof?
Could I / Could you + base verbAsk politelyCould I have a late check-out?

Portuguese uses one verb for both ideas, and that is the trap. In English, have is only for what somebody owns ("I have a reservation"). For what exists in a place you always need there is or there are.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

A server hears the same three sentences all evening. "I would like a table for two." "I would like some water." "I would like to see the menu." Look at what changes and what does not. Would like never changes: it is the polite way to say what you want, and it works for every person. What changes is the word right after it. A table is one thing you can count, so it takes a. Water is not something you count, so it takes some. And when what you want is an action, not a thing, you need to before the verb. Say "I want" instead and the sentence is still correct English, but it sounds like an order, not a request.

1. Why do we say "some water" and not "a water"?
A Because water is always plural in English.
B Because water is uncountable, so it cannot take a or an.
C Because water is a drink and drinks never take a.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "I would like see the menu, please."
B "I would like to see the menu, please."
C "I would like to a menu, please."
3. According to the text, what is the difference between "I want" and "I would like"?
A "I want" is wrong English in a restaurant.
B They mean different things.
C Both are correct, but "I want" sounds like an order and "I would like" sounds like a request.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Would like + countable and uncountable

GRAMMAR

The word you put after would like depends on whether you can count what you are asking for.

You can count itYou cannot count itAn action
I would like a table.I would like some water.I would like to see the menu.
I would like an appetizer.I would like some rice.I would like to order now.
I would like two main courses.I would like some bread.I would like to pay by card.

Questions use the same shape: "Would you like a side dish?" The short answers are "Yes, please" and "No, thank you". And if you really need a number for something uncountable, count the container: "two glasses of water", "three cups of coffee".

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Directions in English are built from very small words, and each one belongs to one of two families. The first family tells you where something is: the station is on the corner, the crosswalk is between the bank and the hotel, your friend is waiting at the entrance. Nothing moves in those sentences. The second family tells you where you go: walk across the park, go along the avenue, get off at the second stop. Something is travelling from one point to another. When you are not sure which word to use, ask yourself one question: is anything moving here? If the answer is no, you need a word from the first family.

1. In "The station is on the corner", why do we use a place preposition?
A Because corners always take on.
B Because nothing is moving: the sentence says where the station is.
C Because the station is a building and buildings take on.
2. Which sentence uses a movement preposition?
A "My office is between two banks."
B "Walk across the park to the museum."
C "She is waiting at the entrance."
3. According to the text, what is the quickest way to choose the right preposition?
A Learn a list of nouns and the preposition each one takes.
B Ask whether anything is moving in the sentence.
C Use in when you are not sure.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Place and movement

GRAMMAR

Two families of prepositions, and one question that tells them apart: is anything moving?

FamilyWordsExample
Placein, on, at, between, opposite, next toThe station is on the corner.
Movementto, into, across, along, through, up, downWalk across the park.
Public transportget on / get offGet off at the second stop.
Cars and taxisget in / get out ofGet into the taxi at the door.

Two more traps for a Brazilian speaker: you arrive at a station, an airport or an office, and in only for a city or a country. And along, not in, is the word for following a street: "walk along this avenue", never "walk in this avenue".

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text and answer the questions.

Two different decisions hide in every sentence about quantity. The first is made by the noun. People, plates and guests can be counted, so they take many: "how many people are coming?" Food, bread and sugar cannot, so they take much: "how much food should I bring?" The second decision is made by the sentence. A positive sentence takes some: "we have some bread." A negative or a question takes any: "we do not have any bread." Portuguese uses one word for the first decision and does not change anything for the second, which is why how many food and not have some bread come out so easily. When you hesitate, a lot of is safe in a positive sentence with both kinds of noun.

1. Why is it "how much food" and not "how many food"?
A Because food is always singular in English.
B Because food cannot be counted, and uncountable nouns take much.
C Because how many is only used with people.
2. Which sentence is correct?
A "We do not have some salad."
B "We do not have any salad."
C "We do not have much salads."
3. According to the text, what is safe when you hesitate?
A Using much for everything.
B Using a lot of in a positive sentence, with both kinds of noun.
C Leaving the quantifier out.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Some, any, much and many

GRAMMAR

The noun chooses between much and many. The sentence chooses between some and any.

WordGoes withExample
somepositive sentences, both kindsWe have some bread.
anynegatives and questionsWe do not have any bread.
much / how muchuncountable: food, bread, timeHow much food?
many / how manycountable: people, plates, guestsHow many people?

Three more that follow the same logic: a few goes with countable nouns (a few guests), a little with uncountable ones (a little sugar), and a lot of works with both. One exception worth remembering: the polite offer keeps some even though it is a question — "Would you like some coffee?"
