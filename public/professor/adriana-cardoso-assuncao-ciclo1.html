Aula 04 · Reading / R1 — Lesson 04 — Before you read the article

1 · What a CEO needs from a news briefing

In your lesson you tell a CEO what a news article really says.

Mark the three things a CEO usually needs from that kind of briefing.

What happened, in one or two sentences
Without the story, the details have nothing to hang on. One or two sentences is usually enough.
Your opinion, before any of the facts
Your reading is useful at the end, marked as yours. Said first, it sounds like one of the facts.
Every number in the article, in the order it appears
A CEO needs the information that changes a decision. The rest can wait until someone asks.
Who says each important piece of information
A figure from the company and a figure from a union or a consultant are not worth the same, and the CEO cannot see the article.
The article read out word for word
Reading it out takes longer than the CEO has, and it leaves the choosing to them.
Which parts have already happened, and which are only expected or possible
A forecast repeated as a fact can end up in someone else's decision as a fact.
The story, who says each important part, and how sure it is. The rest the CEO can ask for.

Mark the two sentences that sum up the article.

She gives a range, between 2 and 3 million bags, and says it depends on the rain.
The harvest was late after a dry winter, and production may fall.

The cooperative hired more seasonal workers than last year.
The article says it hired about 1,500, fewer than last year.
The cooperative has published what it will pay next season.
The last sentence says the opposite: it has not said how much it will pay.
There is a forecast about the weather and an estimate about production, from two different people.
The meteorologist expects El Niño to last into March; the economist estimates the fall in production.

5 · How the numbers are said

The harvest started

Production could fall by

The cooperative has not said

4 · Cash, or lost value?

A CFO often asks which part of a cost is money the company actually paid. Is each item cash the company paid, or value it lost?

Work that did not get done while a position was empty
No payment leaves the company. The output that did not happen is the cost.
An agency fee to find a replacement
An invoice arrives and someone pays it.
A background check for a new hire
A service the company pays for, per person.
A manager’s hours spent interviewing candidates
The manager is paid anyway. What is lost is the work they did not do during those hours.
Overtime paid to cover for the people who left
It shows up on the payroll.
A new hire who is still slower than the person who left
The salary is the same; the output is lower for a while.
