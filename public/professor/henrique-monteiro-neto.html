CCQs da Aula 1 (Concept Checking Questions)

  • To be well-informed — Is it about reading a lot, or about knowing what matters? (Knowing what matters — quantity alone is not it.)
  • A vested interest — Do I gain something if this goes my way? (Yes — that is exactly what makes it vested.)
  • To keep abreast of — Once, or continuously? (Continuously — it is a habit, not an event.)
  • A blind spot — Do I know it is there? (No — if I could see it, it would not be blind.)
  • To be hard-pressed to — Is it impossible, or just very difficult? (Very difficult — and I probably will not manage.)
  • “It may well have been a mistake” — Am I sure? (No — but I think it is quite likely. Stronger than “may”, weaker than “must”.)
  • “It must have been deliberate” — Do I know, or am I concluding? (Concluding — from evidence, with confidence.)
  • “They can’t have known” — Is this the negative of must have? (Yes — English uses can’t have, never mustn’t have.)
  • “I would go so far as to say” — Am I softening, or committing? (Committing — I am announcing a strong claim before I make it.)
  • “That said” — Does it agree with what I just said, or push against it? (Push against — it opens the counter-argument politely.)

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

He is well-informed in the way that takes thirty years and no shortcuts. Four outlets every morning, two of which he chose precisely because they argue against him, and a habit of reading the ones he distrusts first. He would tell you it is not virtue. It is that he has been caught out too often by hearsay that arrived looking like a fact.

Last month a small regional paper broke a story about three public contracts. By Thursday the national coverage was everywhere; by Friday a minister had gone. He read all of it and took almost none of it at face value. The documents must have been sitting there since March, he said, so somebody can't have been reading them. And the officials involved may well have known for months.

What interests him is not the scandal but the spin. Every side has a vested interest, and the same three numbers can be arranged into two opposite stories. He is hard-pressed to remember a single week when that was not true. So he follows the story closely for a fortnight, and only then does he weigh in on it — usually at a dinner, usually when somebody makes a sweeping statement he cannot let stand.

He is honest about the rest. Africa is a blind spot, and he knows it. Once or twice a year he is swayed by an argument he sat down intending to demolish. And there are decisions that, in hindsight, he should have seen coming and did not. He takes his own certainty with a grain of salt too. It is the only way to keep abreast of a world that keeps rearranging itself.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Modal Perfects — the Scale of Certainty About the Past

GRAMMAR

Five ways to talk about something that already happened when you were not there to see it. The auxiliary is what tells your listener how sure you are, so choosing it is not decoration.

FormWhat it doesExample
must have
+ past participle		A confident conclusion drawn from evidence. You did not see it; you are inferring it, and you are prepared to say so out loud."The leak must have come from inside."
may / might / could haveA real possibility and nothing stronger. May well have pushes it towards likely without ever claiming proof."They may well have known for months."
can't / couldn't haveThe negative of must have. This is the one Portuguese speakers get wrong, because mustn't have feels symmetrical — and does not exist in this meaning."She can't have expected that."
should / ought to haveRetrospective judgement. The right thing was not done, and hindsight makes that visible."We should have seen it coming."
would have
+ past participle		The unreal past: what was going to be true under a condition that never came about."A week earlier, it would have changed the vote."

One structure underneath all five: modal + have + past participle. The verb after have never changes form, however far back the event is. And in speech the have reduces to 've — must've come, should've seen — which is why it disappears from the ear long before it disappears from the grammar.

I do understand why people dislike it. They arrive in August, when the old town is teeming with visitors; they spend both days inside a square that is overrated and photographed from one angle; and they leave convinced that the place is soulless. The very fact that the place photographs badly is what has protected it. It is precisely because the northern quarter is run-down that anybody under thirty can still afford to live in it. That is not romanticism about poverty; it is arithmetic. Gentrify it, as three comparable districts have been gentrified, and within a decade it will be clean, safe, expensive and every bit as dull as the developments people fly past on the way in.

What I would tell a visitor is short. The market at seven, while it is bustling and before it becomes a photograph; ten minutes at the cathedral and no more; then a walkable hour in a residential street forty minutes out, where nothing whatever is happening. It is nothing like as impressive as the guidebook version, and it is the only version that captures the essence of the place. It grows on you. Two days is not enough, and it is worth the detour anyway.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Emphasis — Putting Weight on a Claim

GRAMMAR

Written English emphasises by moving words around. Spoken English does it with a small set of markers and with stress. Neither adds information, and both change how much of what you said somebody remembers.

FormWhat it doesExample
do / does / did
+ BASE form		The emphatic auxiliary. It answers a doubt nobody has spoken aloud. Affirmative only, never with another auxiliary, and the verb after it does not carry the tense."I do think so." (not do thought)
"She did warn us."
the very + nounExactly this one and no other. The very fact that is the most useful member of the family: it turns a detail into the whole argument."The very fact that nobody goes there is the point."
"That is the very street."
It is precisely /
exactly / only because …
that …		Isolates ONE cause and excludes the rest. Especially useful just before a counter-intuitive claim, because it warns the listener to pay attention."It is precisely because it is run-down that it survives."
every bit as · far and away
by some margin · nothing like as		Comparative intensifiers. Note the trap: nothing like as good means MUCH WORSE, not similar. Portuguese speakers read it the wrong way round at first."Far and away the best square."
"Nothing like as bad as they say."
FrontingMoving the complement to the front of the sentence. Emphatic and slightly literary; in speech it is a strong move and should be used once, not four times."Beautiful, it is not."
"That I do not accept."

A word of calibration, which matters more at your level than the forms themselves. Emphasis with a stranger reads as insistence; with somebody you know it reads as conviction. The C1 skill is not producing these structures — it is choosing how many to use, and with whom.
