Jornada de Transformação

De: Aluna com dois certificados Cambridge no currículo e um ano e meio sem nenhuma aula, que descreve a si mesma em uma palavra: "enferrujada". As palavras que ela sabia não vêm mais na hora — "eu vou pesquisar e eu falo: nossa, óbvio que era isso" — e montar uma frase demora. No estágio, os protocolos dos experimentos estão em inglês e ela ainda tropeça para ler direto.

Para: Isabel que lê o protocolo de bancada e o abstract de um artigo na velocidade de leitura, sem tradução mental, e que fala do próprio trabalho — e da própria vida — com o repertório de volta e sem a pausa que hoje precede cada frase.

Promessa do Programa

"De enferrujada a fluente no que importa: o protocolo do experimento lido direto, o artigo destrinchado em minutos, e a fala com a velocidade e o repertório de quem nunca parou."

Forças

  • Base certificada e real. Inglês no colégio desde os 9 anos, 2x por semana, com equipe e prédio próprios; Cambridge B1 em 2022 e B2 em 2024. Não é aluna que começa do zero — é aluna que precisa reabrir o que já tem
  • Compreensão oral preservada, e ela sabe disso: "entender, eu acho que eu ainda entendo tudo como quando eu fazia aula". Dá para começar a aula em velocidade real desde a primeira
  • Speaking foi a nota mais alta dela no Cambridge B2. A fala está enferrujada, não ausente — a musculatura existe
  • Autodiagnóstico preciso e sem drama. Ela nomeia o próprio gargalo com exatidão: "o meu repertório de palavras está menor, eu demoro mais para falar uma frase"
  • Alta tolerância ao erro. Fala das próprias falhas com humor ("passei com um cheiro") — dá para corrigir sem rodeio e sem custo emocional
  • Motivação concreta e semanal: os protocolos do estágio estão em inglês. Cada palavra ensinada tem uso na quarta-feira seguinte

Pontos de Melhoria

  • Latência na fala — o problema não é erro, é o tempo entre a intenção e a frase. Só se resolve com tempo de fala medido, nunca com explicação
  • Repertório lexical encolhido pelo desuso, sobretudo o técnico da área dela
  • Escrita — ela relata trocar a ordem das letras dentro da palavra, coisa que não fazia antes. Sinal de erosão ortográfica por desuso, não de dificuldade
  • Leitura técnica ainda custosa: "tenho entendido, ok, mas ainda estou com um pouquinho de dificuldade para ler e entender direto"
  • Nomenclatura gramatical não funciona com ela — "eu nunca fui boa em saber o que é past perfect, porque nem em português eu sei direito essas coisas". Gramática só entra pelo USO
  • Exposição fora da aula é quase nula. Ela consome mídia brasileira e está tentando mudar. Complementar que pareça tarefa não será feito

A Jornada — narrativa do programa

A Isabel tem 18 anos e uma semana que não cabe em si: estágio de manhã, projeto de iniciação científica em casa, aula na faculdade até as 23h e extensão na segunda à tarde. O inglês entra nessa agenda como respiro ou não entra — se virar mais uma disciplina da USP, morre.

O pedido dela tem duas metades, e as duas são literais. A primeira é repertório de ciências biológicas: ler artigos e, muito objetivamente, entender as receitas dos experimentos do estágio, que são em inglês. A segunda é a fala, que ela mesma elegeu como incômodo número um: as palavras que sabia não vêm, e cada frase custa uma pausa.

Por isso o programa não começa no laboratório. A Aula 1 começa no teatro — que ela faz desde pequena, trancou duas vezes e quer retomar. É o único assunto em que ela tem história para contar, opinião formada e vontade de falar; é o melhor lugar do mundo para atacar a queixa número um sem que a aula pareça exercício. O laboratório entra na Aula 2, pela porta da leitura.

Daí em diante o pacote alterna dois formatos e seis mundos. Aula ímpar é de FALA (diálogo line-by-line + três role-plays); aula par é de LEITURA (texto central + gist e true/false). E o assunto gira entre o gênero do artigo científico, o protocolo de bancada, a vida universitária e a escolha de carreira, o palco, a rotina impossível, e o inglês social das viagens e das festas. Nenhum fato biográfico dela sustenta mais de uma aula.

Duas travas pedagógicas valem para as 48 aulas. A primeira: nunca diga o nome do tempo verbal — mostre o que ele faz, peça a regra por descoberta, e siga. A segunda: meça o tempo de fala. A latência dela não melhora com explicação; melhora com minutos falados e com um número dito em voz alta no fim da aula.

Metodologia — 5 Abordagens

1. Gramática pelo uso, nunca pelo nome

Ela disse com todas as letras: "eu nunca fui boa em saber o que é past perfect, porque nem em português eu sei direito essas coisas". Consequência prática e obrigatória: nenhum slide da tela nomeia um tempo verbal, e o professor também não o nomeia em voz alta. A estrutura aparece em quatro exemplos, ela descobre o padrão, e só então vem a tabela — organizada por FUNÇÃO (o que a frase faz), nunca por rótulo.

2. Um domínio por aula, e o fato biográfico só uma vez

Cada aula tem um mundo próprio, com gênero textual e vocabulário próprios: o palco, o abstract, a escolha de carreira, o protocolo de bancada, a semana impossível. Nenhum detalhe da vida dela sustenta duas aulas. É assim que 48 aulas não viram quarenta e oito variações da mesma conversa — e é por isso que a cachorra que toma remédio às 8h30 não vira refrão.

3. Medir o tempo de fala e dizer o número

A queixa número um dela é latência, não erro: "eu demoro mais para falar uma frase". Latência não melhora com explicação — melhora com minutos falados. Em toda produção livre, cronometre, conte quantas vezes ela usou a estrutura da aula e diga os dois números em voz alta no fim. Medir é o que transforma forma em hábito.

4. O protocolo do estágio é material didático

O pedido mais concreto da consultoria foi este: "várias receitas dos experimentos que eu tenho que fazer são em inglês". Sempre que ela trouxer um protocolo, um abstract ou um e-mail real, largue o slide e trabalhe o documento dela. O material foi desenhado para ser interrompido por isso, não apesar disso.

5. Complementar que não pareça tarefa

Ela não tem o hábito de consumir mídia em inglês e está tentando criar: "eu tô tentando mais agora". Toda indicação precisa caber numa rotina que termina às 23h — no máximo vinte minutos, de preferência encaixada em algo que ela já faz (ela está revendo The Good Place). Complementar que soa como dever de casa não é feito, e minar a única fonte de prazer com o idioma custa caro aqui.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Isabel used to have a rehearsal every Saturday morning. The group would meet at nine, spend the first hour on a warm-up, and only then open the script. She used to be the youngest person in the cast, and the quietest one as well.

She did not use to get stage fright at that age. That came later, in the year she would arrive five minutes before the door closed, with no time to project a single line before the audience walked in. The first time she went blank, the other actor was already waiting for the cue, and there was nothing in her head at all.

She dropped out twice, and neither time was about the acting. Two years later she is standing backstage again, before an audition, and she is honest about it: she is rusty. What she is not is a beginner.

1. "She used to be the youngest person in the cast." Why not "she would be"?
A Because would is only used in questions.
B Because that is how she was, not something she did. Would only works for actions that happened.
C Because would is informal and used to is formal.
2. "The group would meet at nine." How often did this happen?
A Regularly, over a period in the past. It was the normal pattern then.
B Once, on one specific Saturday.
C It has not happened yet, but it is planned.
3. "She did not use to get stage fright at that age." What does the sentence tell you about now?
A That she has never had stage fright in her life.
B That things changed. It was not true then, and the sentence suggests it is true now.
C Nothing at all. It is only about the past.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Saying What Was True Then

GRAMMAR

Two ways to talk about a past that ended, and one of them has a limit.

UseFormExample
Repeated action, finishedused to + base verbI used to rehearse on Saturdays.
Same idea, storytellingwould + base verbWe would run the script twice.
A state, not an actionused to only — never wouldI used to be shy. (not: I would be shy)
Negativedid not use to + base verbI did not use to get stage fright.
QuestionDid + subject + use to + base verbDid you use to perform in English?
Present habitThere is no present form of thisI usually go to the theater in March.

Never: I use to do theater · I would be shy · I didn't used to sing · I used to going there · I use to study at night.

Four Paragraphs, Four Englishes -- The Anatomy of an Abstract

An abstract looks like one block of text and is really four boxes in a fixed order, each written in a different English. Key words: an abstract, a finding, a sample, a baseline, to assess, a trial, significant, to account for, peer-reviewed, a limitation, to rule out, to conclude. Structure: the sentence with nobody in it, which is how every method section is written.

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Three of them mean something different from what they mean in everyday English.

An abstract -- the short paragraph at the top of a paper that tells you the whole study in four moves
"I open about thirty abstracts a week and read two papers properly."
A finding -- something a study discovered, reported as a result rather than an opinion
"The main finding was that the effect disappeared below ten degrees."
A sample -- the group of people, animals or things that a study actually looked at
"The samples were collected in March and stored at minus twenty."
A baseline -- the measurement taken before anything is done, so that later numbers mean something
"Two hundred seedlings were assessed at baseline and again after six weeks."
To assess -- to measure or judge how large, how strong or how good something is
"Growth was assessed every seven days by the same two researchers."
A trial -- a controlled test of a treatment or a method on a group, run under fixed conditions
"The data were analyzed in three independent trials."
Significant -- in a paper, unlikely to be the result of chance. It does not mean large or important
"The difference was significant, although it was only two percent."
To account for -- to be the cause of part of a result. In a paper it is not an excuse, it is a cause
"Temperature accounted for most of the variation between the two groups."
Peer-reviewed -- checked by other specialists before publication. It is a filter, never a guarantee
"Peer-reviewed does not mean correct. It means somebody qualified read it first."
A limitation -- a weakness that the authors admit their own study has
"A paper with no limitations is not stronger. It is quieter."
To rule out -- to show that an explanation is not possible, and remove it from the list
"Contamination was ruled out by running the whole thing again."
To conclude -- to state, at the end, the judgement that the results support
"The authors conclude that the evidence suggests a seasonal effect."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

The abstract of a paper is four boxes in a fixed order, and the second box is the one that frightens most readers. It should not. Look at how it is written: samples were collected at four sites in March. Two hundred seedlings were assessed at baseline and again at six weeks. The data were analyzed in three independent trials.

Nobody appears in any of those sentences. The thing that had something done to it is standing at the front, and the person who did it is gone, because in a method section who did it is not the point. When the person does matter, one small word brings them back: growth was assessed by the same two researchers, and contamination was ruled out by repeating the procedure with fresh material.

The third box is where the numbers arrive, and where two words are easy to misread. The difference was significant, and it was only two percent: significant means unlikely to be chance, not large. Temperature accounted for most of the variation. The fourth box is the most careful English in the paper. The authors conclude that the evidence suggests a seasonal effect, they name one season as a limitation, and they never say that anything has been proved. Note also that the paper is peer-reviewed, which means it was read by specialists first, and nothing more than that.

1. "The samples were collected in March." Who collected them?
A The samples collected themselves.
B The sentence does not say, and in a method section that is on purpose.
C The reader of the paper.
2. "Growth was assessed by the same two researchers." What does the word by add?
A It says where the growth happened.
B It brings the person back, because here who did it is part of the point.
C It makes the sentence more formal, with no change in meaning.
3. The difference was two percent and the paper calls it significant. Why is that not a contradiction?
A Because significant is about chance, not size. A tiny difference can be significant.
B Because two percent is a large number in biology.
C Because the authors made a mistake in the results box.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Sentence With Nobody In It

GRAMMAR

Put the thing first, add was or were, and use the third form of the verb.

UseFormExample
Finished action, one thingwas + third formThe sample was collected.
Finished action, many thingswere + third formThe samples were collected.
Still relevant nowhas / have been + third formThe effect has been observed three times.
When the person matters+ by + the person or the thingGrowth was assessed by two researchers.
Negativewas / were not + third formThe plants were not watered for six days.
QuestionWas / Were + thing + third formWere the samples stored at minus twenty?

Never: The samples was collected · The data were analyze · Two hundred seedlings assessed at baseline · The seedlings were grown by in a greenhouse · The effect has observed three times.

Stage 4: Situational Quiz

Quiz

Choose the answer that a real speaker would give.

You are writing the method section of your own project. You write:
A "The samples was collected in March."
B "The samples were collected in March."
C "The samples collected in March."
A paper reports a difference of two percent and calls it significant. That means:
A The difference is large enough to matter in practice.
B The difference is unlikely to be the result of chance, even though it is small.
C The authors are not sure about the difference.
Somebody says the study is peer-reviewed. The right thing to think is:
A Specialists read it before publication. That is a filter, so I still read the limitations.
B The findings have been proved, so I can quote them without reading further.
C The paper was written by more than one author.
In your journal club you want to say that one explanation is now impossible. You say:
A "Contamination was rule out in the second trial."
B "Contamination was ruled out in the second trial."
C "Contamination has rule out in the second trial."

The Good Place (O Bom Lugar)

You are already rewatching it, so this costs you nothing extra: switch one episode to English audio with English subtitles. The characters spend half of every episode explaining who they used to be before they arrived, which is exactly the structure of this lesson, in short and very clear sentences.
