Linha recorrente: LISTENING COM SOTAQUES

A maior dificuldade declarada por ele não é falar, é ENTENDER quem fala rápido e com sotaque carregado — a cena que ele cita é a imigração americana. Isso não vira uma aula única: vira uma linha de trabalho que atravessa o bloco inteiro. A partir da aula 2, o segundo listening de cada aula usa um sotaque diferente (britânico, indiano, nórdico, francês, italiano, holandês), e o primeiro fica em inglês americano como referência. O controle de velocidade (0.5x a 1.25x) fica em cada player: use 1x primeiro, SEMPRE.

Personagem-Jornada

De: um executivo de 46 anos que dirige quatro áreas na Riachuelo e, em inglês, opera com o que ele mesmo chama de "20% a 30%" do que precisaria. Evita reunião internacional, se apoia na companheira nas viagens e chamou a semana de trabalho em Chicago de "uma tortura". Começa a frase e para na segunda.

Para: "o cara que se vira" — palavras dele. Conduz a própria reunião, se apresenta num congresso sem ensaiar, negocia data com fornecedor, entende o oficial de imigração na primeira vez e sustenta a conversa depois da segunda frase.

Promessa Transformadora

De "tentando me situar com os 20% que tenho" a autonomia. O gargalo dele não é começar — é CONTINUAR: falta repertório ativo para ir da primeira frase à quinta. O motor do programa é vocabulário em contexto, dentro dos temas que ele de fato vive (varejo e suprimentos, viagem, futebol, tecnologia, cultura), com gramática entrando leve e aplicada, do jeito que ele pediu: presente, passado e futuro bem divididos, sem tempos verbais raros. Ele já abandonou aula particular por falta de estrutura — aqui cada aula tem antes, durante e depois, e ele vê a própria evolução marcada.

Metodologia

1. Vocabulário é o motor

Ele não para por insegurança, para por FALTA DE PALAVRA. 8 palavras novas por aula, sempre dentro de uma cena que ele vive, e cobradas de volta em produção na mesma aula.

2. Gramática leve e aplicada

Pedido explícito dele: dividir bem presente, passado e futuro, sem tempos raros. Discovery method — exemplos primeiro, regra depois, nunca ao contrário.

3. Nunca aceitar uma frase só

O hábito a quebrar é parar na segunda frase. Em todo role-play e Quick Fire, exigir resposta longa e devolver a pergunta. "It depends" não é resposta.

4. Sotaque como treino, não como susto

A partir da aula 2, o segundo listening muda de sotaque. Sempre 1x na primeira escuta; 0.75x só se ele pedir. O objetivo é tolerar o desconforto, não eliminar.

5. Estrutura visível

Ele abandonou aula particular porque não havia onde fixar. Antes (Pre-class), durante (IN CLASS) e depois (Complementares) — e o progresso marcado na tela.

Mapa de Personalidade

  • Introversão × Extroversão (7/10) — comunicativo e direto em português, usa gíria e humor seco. Não precisa ser aquecido, precisa ser abastecido de palavras.
  • Seriedade × Ludicidade (4/10) — pragmático. Nada de jogo infantilizado; ele quer ver o retorno prático de cada aula.
  • Reflexivo × Impulsivo (4/10) — pondera antes de se comprometer. Dê dois segundos de silêncio antes de socorrer.
  • Confiança (6/10) — altíssima na vida profissional, localizada no inglês. Trate-o como par intelectual: o inglês é ferramenta, não prova de competência.
  • Tolerância ao erro (média) — aceita correção, mas correção solta não gruda. Corrija por reformulação e registre o erro para a aula seguinte.
  • Estilo de aprendizagem — leitura/escrita com âncora contextual. O Pre-class por escrito ANTES da aula é parte essencial do desenho.
  • Energia preferida — dinâmica e variada. Rotina profissional sem padrão fixo; baixa tolerância ao repetitivo. Pelo menos três atividades distintas por aula.
O que NÃO fazer
  • Nada de inglês jurídico. Ele é formado em Direito e não advoga — o próprio consultor descartou o repertório legal na consultoria.
  • Nada de política partidária ("política é um tema que eu não gosto"). Documentário sobre figura histórica com representatividade, sim; disputa partidária, não.
  • Nada de hipismo, cinema de bastidores ou fofoca de ator — ele disse ter zero interesse.
  • Nada de aula solta de conversação. Foi exatamente isso que o fez desistir antes. Toda aula tem alvo linguístico declarado.
  • Nada de deixar ele levar a conversa para a zona de conforto. Ele avisou que faz isso. Conduza.

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

Marcelo works for a retail group with three hundred stores. He oversees four areas, and each area has its own manager. He reports to the chief financial officer, and four managers report to him.

His week does not follow a fixed shape. Some days he visits a distribution center; other days he stays in the office and meets suppliers. He does not handle prices, and he does not decide the marketing budget. Does he enjoy it? He says the variety is the best part of the job.

1. Three verbs in the first paragraph end in -s: works, oversees, reports. Why does the last one, report, have no -s?
A Because it is a negative sentence.
B Because the subject is "four managers", which is not he, she or it.
C Because the verb "report" never takes an -s.
2. The text says "he does not handle prices", not "he does not handles". Why?
A The -s appears only once. "Does" already carries it, so the verb goes back to its base form.
B "Handle" is an irregular verb and has no -s form.
C Negative sentences in English always use the plural form.
3. Look at the question in the text: "Does he enjoy it?" What would be wrong with "He enjoys it?"
A Nothing. Both are equally correct in a meeting.
B A real question needs "do" or "does" in front. Without it, it is a statement with a question mark.
C The verb should move to the end of the sentence.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Present Simple

GRAMMAR

The tense you use for facts, jobs and routines. One verb, one -s, one place.

SubjectPositiveNegativeQuestion
I / you / we / theyI oversee four areas.I do not oversee sales.Do you oversee sales?
he / she / itShe oversees four areas.She does not oversee sales.Does she oversee sales?
Spelling of the -swork → works · report → reports · watch → watches · go → goes · have → has · do → does
The -s appears onceIf does takes the -s, the verb gives it back: "she does not oversee", never "does not oversees".
No am / is / are"I work in retail", never "I am work in retail". Am, is and are never sit next to another verb here.

Useful with this tense: usually, often, sometimes, never, every month, twice a week. They tell the listener that you are describing a shape, not a single day.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Simple and Present Continuous

GRAMMAR

One tense for your life, one tense for your week. The time word decides, not the verb.

TenseUse it forExample
Present simple
I visit / she visits		Facts, routines and things that do not change: your life.I visit the warehouse twice a month.
Present continuous
I am visiting		Now, this period only, and fixed plans in the near future: your week.This week I am visiting it twice.
Time wordsSimple: always, usually, every month, twice a week, never · Continuous: now, today, this week, at the moment, tonight
Negative and questionSimple: I do not visit · Does she visit?  |  Continuous: I am not visiting · Are you visiting?
Verbs with no -ingSome verbs describe a state, not an action: know, want, need, believe, belong, understand. "I know the supplier", never "I am knowing".

A useful habit: read the time word first, choose the tense, and only then say the verb. That order stops almost every mistake in this pair.

Lesson 03 -- Pre-class

The Supplier Call -- Asking Without Sounding Rude

The words a supply call cannot happen without: lead time, purchase order, batch, shipment, to delay, to confirm, quote, to push back. Structure: direct and indirect questions, and the word order that changes inside them. Mission: get three pieces of information from a supplier without sounding rude.
0%
▼

Stage 1.1: Vocabulary Cards

Vocabulary

Listen to each word and read the example. Tap Listen to hear it.

Lead time -- the number of days between an order and the delivery
"The lead time is forty days, so nothing arrives before March."
Purchase order -- the official document that says what a company buys and when
"The purchase order still shows the old date."
Batch -- a group of products made or delivered together at the same time
"The first batch leaves the factory on the eighteenth."
Shipment -- the goods that travel together in one delivery
"The shipment arrives two weeks after the store opening."
To delay -- to make something happen later than planned
"A busy port delays every container by three days."
To confirm -- to say officially that something is certain
"Please confirm the new date in writing."
Quote -- a written price for an order or a job
"The quote does not include freight to Santos."
To push back -- to ask politely for a later date, or to say no without closing the door
"If the fabric is late, we push back the delivery to April."

Stage 1.3: Grammar in Context

GRAMMAR

Read the text, then answer the questions.

A first call with a supplier is a list of questions, and the list is always the same. Could you tell me when the first batch leaves? Do you know if the quote includes freight? Would you mind telling me who signs the change? Three questions, three openers, and the same information you would get with a direct question.

The difference is what happens after the opener. A direct question turns the sentence around: When does it ship? An indirect question does not. Once you have said Could you tell me, the rest goes back to normal order: when it ships, and the does disappears. For a yes-or-no question you need if or whether: Do you know whether the shipment is on time? The opener is doing all the work, so the rest of the sentence can relax.

1. Why does the text say Could you tell me when it ships and not when does it ship?
A Because ship is an irregular verb after could.
B Because the question is already in the opener. After it, the sentence goes back to normal order and the auxiliary disappears.
C Because the indirect question is always in the past.
2. When do you need if or whether inside the question?
A When the answer is yes or no, and there is no question word like when or who.
B Every time you start with Could you tell me.
C Only when you are writing, never when you are speaking.
3. What is the effect of the opener on the person listening?
A It makes the question longer, so it is less clear.
B It gives them a second to prepare, and it asks permission before it asks for information.
C It shows that you are not sure of your own question.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Direct and Indirect Questions

GRAMMAR

One question, two doors. The direct door is fast and fine with people you know. The indirect door is what you use on a first call.

FormUse it forExample
Could you tell me...The most common opener at work. Works with when, where, who, how much and with if.Could you tell me when the batch ships?
Do you know if / whether...Yes-or-no questions. Whether is slightly more formal.Do you know if the quote is final?
I was wondering...The softest opener. It is a statement, so it ends with a full stop.I was wondering whether you could confirm the date.
Would you mind telling me...Very polite, useful when you are asking for something the person may not want to give.Would you mind telling me who signs this?
The word orderAfter the opener: subject, then verb. No do, no does, no did. Could you tell me when it arrives, never when does it arrive.
Who as subjectWhen the question word is already the subject, nothing moves at all: Who signs this? becomes ...who signs this.

A habit that fixes almost everything: say the opener, take a breath, and then say the rest as if it were a statement. If it still sounds like a question inside, the word order is wrong.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- The Past Simple

GRAMMAR

One tense for everything that is finished. The hard part is not the -ed. It is remembering that the past only shows up once per sentence.

FormUse it forExample
Regular: verb + -edMost verbs. The spelling is regular, the sound is not.I asked · I printed · I walked
Irregular: a new wordA short list you use every day. Learn them in pairs, not alphabetically.go → went · take → took · speak → spoke · fly → flew
Negative: did not + baseSame for regular and irregular. The verb loses its past because did already has it.I did not catch it · I did not go
Question: did + subject + baseNo -ed anywhere in the question.Did you check in online? · Did he take the shuttle?
To be: was / wereThe one verb with no did. I was, you were, he was, we were, they were. Negative: was not, were not.
The sound of -edThree sounds, never a full extra syllable unless the verb ends in t or d: asked = askt, arrived = arrivd, printed = PRIN-tid.

A habit worth building: before you say a past sentence, decide which single word will carry the past. If two words are fighting for it, one of them is wrong.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Past Continuous and Past Simple

GRAMMAR

Two tenses, two jobs. One paints the scene and the other moves the story. A good incident report needs both, in that order.

FormUse it forExample
was / were + -ingThe long action, already in progress when something happened.The team was loading trucks.
Past simpleThe short action that interrupts, and everything that follows it.A forklift hit the arm. The line stopped.
while + continuousMarks the background. While the team was loading, the conveyor stopped.
when + simpleMarks the moment. The supervisor was trying to restart it when I called.
Two continuousTwo long actions at the same time: While I was calling Marco, the team was clearing the aisle.
State verbsknow, want, need, understand, believe never take -ing: I knew the cause, never I was knowing.

Before you speak, ask which action was long. That one takes was doing. Everything else takes the simple past, and the order you say them in does not change that.

Stage 1.4: Grammar Tip -- Present Perfect for Experience

GRAMMAR

One tense for your life and one for your calendar. The hard part is not the form. It is noticing the second a date walks into the sentence.

FormUse it forExample
have / has + participleAn experience, with no date. Your life, not your calendar.I have been to Chicago. She has met him.
everQuestions only. It means at any time in your life.Have you ever worked with a French supplier?
neverNot once. Already negative, so never add not.I have never seen a booth that big.
been or gonebeen = went and came back. gone = went and is still there. Brazilians almost always want been.
The participleNot the past. go → gone, see → seen, meet → met, speak → spoken, be → been.
The date testIf you can answer when?, use the past simple: I saw it yesterday. If you cannot, use the present perfect: I have seen it.

Portuguese lets you say eu fui and eu tenho ido almost anywhere. English does not. Before you speak, ask yourself whether there is a date in the sentence, even a hidden one.
