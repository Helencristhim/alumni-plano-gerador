Aula 03 · Reading / R1 — Lesson 03 — Before you read either version

1 · Two versions, one argument

Before you read

You are going to read the same argument twice. The facts are identical. One of the two was written by someone who told the listener where the argument was going; the other was not.

Mark the three statements that are true of a talk that signals its moves.

The listener knows when the speaker is about to change direction.
It contains more information than the version without signals.
The listener can tell a concession from a conclusion.
It is longer because the speaker repeats the facts.
The listener knows, before the end, that the end is coming.
It uses more difficult vocabulary.
Signalling adds no facts and no vocabulary. It adds a map. The listener spends nothing on working out where you are, and can spend it all on what you are saying.
Why the block opens here
You said, in your very first lesson of this course, that what you needed was presentations in English — and discourse markers in particular. This is that, and it is the axis of the whole block, not a topic at the end of it.

2 · Key language

Vocabulary

Choose the better reading for each expression. You will meet all six in the second text.

Let me start with
Now, here is the problem
What this means is
Having said that
So where does that leave us
My recommendation is
Six expressions, six different jobs
None of them is decoration. Each one tells the listener what kind of sentence is coming — an opening, a turn, a consequence, a concession, a close, an ask. That is why removing them costs the listener so much.

3 · What the speaker is doing

Function

Six sentences from talks. What is the speaker doing in each one?

“Which brings me to what I am asking for.”
“I want to take three minutes on where the model stands.”
“None of that changes the license question.”
“The team is right that the accuracy is there.”
“That is the good news. The bad news is harder.”
“So we would ship a product we cannot explain.”
Concession is the one people skip
Two of the six are concessions, and they are the hardest to hear as a move. Conceding is not losing the argument: it is the sentence that makes the rest of it credible, because it shows you have already considered the objection.

4 · Which one can she follow

Reading
Version A
We tested the model on twelve months of data. Accuracy is ninety-four percent. The team wants to ship in October. The model cannot explain why it declines a case. Regulators in three markets now ask for that. We would have to rebuild the pipeline. Six weeks. I would hold the launch.
Version B
Let me start with where we are. We tested the model on twelve months of data, and accuracy is ninety-four percent — which is why the team wants to ship in October.

Now, here is the problem. The model cannot explain why it declines a case, and regulators in three markets now ask for exactly that. What this means is that shipping in October buys us a launch and costs us the license.

Having said that, the fix is not enormous — rebuilding the pipeline is six weeks. So where does that leave us? My recommendation is that we hold the launch until December.

Mark the three statements that are true.

Version B contains a fact that Version A does not.
In Version B you know, before the recommendation arrives, that a recommendation is coming.
In Version A the six-week fix reads as a new problem rather than as a concession.
Version B is easier because its sentences are shorter.
Both versions end with the same ask.
Version A is more objective because it has no signposting.
Same facts, same ask, same length. What Version B adds is the reader’s certainty about where each sentence sits in the argument — and that certainty is what makes an executive room follow you.
The one that catches people
“Version A is more objective” feels right and is not. Signposting does not add opinion; it marks structure. Removing it does not make you neutral — it makes you hard to follow, and a room that cannot follow you decides without you.

Practice

The opening writes the sentence
Once you say having said that, the room is waiting for a concession — something that works against you and that you are conceding anyway. Following it with your strongest point is the most common way a signal misfires.

Aula 04 · Listening / L1 — Lesson 04 — Before you hear any of it

1 · The three seconds after you stop

Before you listen

You gave your three minutes. You stop. Somebody raises a hand.

Mark the three statements that are true of a good answer under those conditions.

It starts before you have decided the whole answer.
It checks what was asked when the question is not clear.
It never says “I do not have that number”.
It can disagree, as long as it says why.
It is longer than the part of the talk it refers to.
It answers whatever the person seemed to mean.
Two of the wrong ones are the same mistake in different clothes: answering the question you imagined instead of the one that was asked. Checking costs eight seconds and saves the whole answer.
Why this comes right after lesson 3
In lesson 3 you closed by saying what neither version answered — which regulator. This is that meeting, three seconds later, and Diane, who chairs the board, asks exactly that. Two more hands go up after hers: Ray, who runs the program office, and Nadia, who owns the engineering budget.

3 · What the question is really after

Function

Six questions from a real review meeting. What does each one actually want?

“Which market asked first?”
“Is December a hard date?”
“How is that not gold-plating?”
“How many cases were declined last quarter?”
“Are you asking us to approve the delay today?”
“Would you bet the launch on that assumption?”
Each kind wants a different first sentence
A fact wants the number, or an honest “I will send it”. A decision wants a yes or a no before the reasoning. A challenge wants you to name the assumption out loud — and then defend it.

4 · The one that is not clear

Listening

“I want to come back to the December thing. I am not sure I follow. Is that a hard date, or is it more of a, you know, a direction? Because we have said December before.”

Mark the three statements that are true of this question.

It contains a complaint that is not stated as one.
It asks for a fact.
It offers you two answers and expects one of them.
The last sentence is the real question.
It is hostile.
It can be answered without checking anything.
“We have said December before” is where the question actually lives. Answering only the hard-date/direction pair leaves the complaint on the table, and it will come back.
Vague is not the same as hostile
Ray is not aggressive at all — and his is the most dangerous of the three, because you can answer it fully and still not have answered it. Nadia's, which comes last, is the hostile one, and it is easier.

Aula 05 · Grammar / G1 — Lesson 05 — Before you build any of it

1 · Same words, different order

Before you start
Two sentences
1. The explainability gap is the real problem.
2. What the real problem is, is the explainability gap.

Mark the three statements that are true.

They contain the same information.
The second one tells the listener that a conclusion is arriving.
The second one is more formal.
The second one puts the new information at the end.
The first one is wrong.
Only the second one can be used in a presentation.
Neither is wrong. English puts what is new at the END of the sentence, and these three structures exist to move it there — which is why they sound like signalling even though they are only word order.
This is the grammar under lesson 3
What this means is… is not an expression you memorize. It is a structure — and once you have it, you can build it around anything: what we found was…, what worries me is…, what nobody has asked is…

2 · Three structures

Form

Choose the better reading for each structure.

What we found was a gap in the logs.

While the accuracy is there, the explainability is not.
It is the timing that worries me, not the design.
What I am asking for is six weeks.
While I take that point, it does not change the date.
Why these three and not others
They are the three that do the work of a discourse marker with word order alone. You already have the markers from lesson 3; these are what let you build your own instead of reaching for the six you memorized.

3 · Which structure is doing it

Discovery

Six sentences. Which of the three structures is each one using?

“What surprised the team was the false-decline rate.”
“As for October, that date was never mine.”
“While the pilot succeeded, it did not test this.”
“What we cannot do is explain a single decision.”
“While I understand the pressure, six weeks is six weeks.”
“As for the engineering cost, I have the number.”
The concessive is the one that changes the room
A cleft makes you clear. Fronting makes you organized. The concessive makes you credible — it is the sentence that proves you already considered the objection, and it is the one most people leave out.

4 · Move the new information

Practice

Aula 06 · ESP / E1 — Lesson 06 — Before you time any of it

1 · What forty-five seconds is

Before you start

You get fifteen seconds to prepare and forty-five seconds to speak. That is the shape of the independent speaking task in the TOEFL iBT — and it is also the shape of the minute you get in a meeting.

Mark the three statements that are true of a good forty-five-second answer.

It takes a position in the first eight seconds.
It gives the best possible position.
It has two reasons, not four.
It uses the fifteen seconds to decide, not to write.
It ends when the time ends.
It needs an example for every reason.
Two of the wrong ones are the same mistake: treating forty-five seconds as a short essay. It is not. It is a position, two reasons and a close — and the close has to arrive before the clock does.
Why the exam format is in this block
Not as exam practice. You asked for the TOEFL in the first lesson of this course, and this is the most demanding short-presentation genre there is: everything from lessons 3, 4 and 5 is charged at once, under a clock, with no second attempt.

2 · Key language

Vocabulary

Choose the better reading for each expression.

I would say
mainly for two reasons
To take one example
That said
So on balance
which is why
Six words that buy structure
Mainly for two reasons costs you one second and tells the listener the whole shape of the next thirty. In forty-five seconds, that second is the best investment available.

3 · Where the sentence goes

Structure

Six sentences from forty-five-second answers. Where does each one belong?

“No team understands every risk before shipping.”
“So on balance, I would ship early with a plan.”
“To take one example, the false-decline rate only appeared in production.”
“A long delay has a cost nobody counts.”
“I would say launch and correct.”
“In my last role we held a release for four months and shipped into a changed market.”
The close is not a summary
“So on balance…” does not repeat the reasons. It re-states the position after having weighed them — which is why it sounds like a decision and a summary sounds like running out of time.

4 · The model answer

Listening
The prompt
“Some people believe a company should delay a product launch until every risk is understood. Others believe it is better to launch and correct as you go. Which do you agree with? Explain why, using specific reasons and examples.”

You will hear one answer in class. Mark the three things a good answer does here.

Chooses one of the two sides, without arguing for both.
Announces how many reasons are coming.
Mentions the other side, briefly, and keeps its position.
Gives three reasons, to be safe.
Explains the two positions before choosing.
Finishes exactly at forty-five seconds.
Explaining both sides before choosing is the single most common way to run out of time. The prompt already explained them; your forty-five seconds are for the choice.
The concession is worth four seconds
Mentioning the other side and keeping your position is the move from lesson 5, at speed. It costs four seconds and it is what separates an answer that sounds decided from one that sounds like it never heard the objection.

5 · Build the shape

Practice

Complete each move so that the forty-five seconds hold together.

To take one example,

So on balance,
Every rejected option is a way of not answering
“It depends”, “there are arguments both ways”, “it is a difficult question” — all true, all polite, and all of them spend seconds without taking a position. In forty-five seconds, not choosing is the answer, and it is the wrong one.

The shape of a talk

Ler o mesmo argumento executivo em duas versões — uma sinalizada, uma não — e reapresentá-lo em três minutos para um conselho que não leu o memorando, sinalizando cada movimento em voz alta.

O navegador não abriu a janela. O Teacher’s Guide continua disponível dentro da aula.

A · Objetivo e produto

Objetivo comunicativo: Ler o mesmo argumento executivo em duas versões — uma sinalizada, uma não — e reapresentá-lo em três minutos para um conselho que não leu o memorando, sinalizando cada movimento em voz alta.

Produto principal: Apresentação de 3 min a um conselho que não leu nada, seguida do mesmo argumento em 90 segundos para um conselho que já discorda. Você faz o conselho e interrompe uma vez.

B · Percurso da aula

C · Antes de abrir a aula
  • Leia as duas versões antes da aula. Elas têm os mesmos fatos, na mesma ordem: 94% de acurácia, outubro, sem explicabilidade, três mercados, seis semanas, segurar o lançamento.
  • A única diferença é a sinalização — e o ponto da etapa 3 é que ela diga isso, não você.
  • O item que decide a aula é “which regulator”: não está em nenhuma das duas versões, é a primeira coisa que um conselho real pergunta, e é por onde ela deve fechar.
  • As duas versões terminam com o mesmo pedido. Se ela disser que a B é mais longa, conte as palavras com ela.
D · Na condução
  • O turno é dela na etapa 7. Depois de fazer a pergunta, espere. Ela é apresentadora experiente; o que falta não é coragem de falar, é a língua que marca o caminho.
  • Feche os dois textos na tela antes dos três minutos. Com eles à vista, ela lê em vez de apresentar.
  • Interrompa uma vez só, e com pergunta de fundo — não com correção de língua.
  • Se ela quiser usar o próprio caso em vez do inventado, aceite. A atividade 6 do pre-class já abria essa porta, e o argumento real dela vale mais.
  • Ela reclamou de falta de desafio. Se a aula estiver fácil, corte apoio antes de acrescentar conteúdo: tire a lista de frases da tela na etapa 7.
E · O que observar na produção
  • Quais dos seis marcadores saem sem apoio — é o que este ciclo cobra.
  • O que ela CORTA no retask de 90 segundos. Se cortar a sinalização, ela ainda não é automática, e isso se decide na aula 6, que fecha o bloco.
  • Leitura isolada de fala: é a primeira medida de reading do ciclo. Não há registro anterior.
  • O nível em que ela de fato produz — isso está em aberto e o cadastro diz B1.

Aula 04 Bloco 1 · Listening / L1 Não iniciada

The question after the talk

Ouvir três perguntas feitas depois de uma apresentação — uma que pede um fato, uma vaga e uma hostil — e responder cada uma ao vivo, checando antes de responder.

O navegador não abriu a janela. O Teacher’s Guide continua disponível dentro da aula.

A · Objetivo e produto

Objetivo comunicativo: Ouvir três perguntas feitas depois de uma apresentação — uma que pede um fato, uma vaga e uma hostil — e responder cada uma ao vivo, checando antes de responder.

Produto principal: Três respostas ao vivo, uma por pergunta, seguidas da hostil de novo em vinte segundos. Você faz os três interlocutores, em voz, uma pergunta de cada vez.

B · Percurso da aula

C · Antes de abrir a aula
  • Ouça as três antes da aula. Diane pede o fato que a apresentação dela não trouxe — é exatamente o que a aula 3 fechou, e vale dizer isso em voz alta.
  • Ray é o perigoso. Ele não é hostil: a pergunta real está na última frase (“we have said December before”), não no par hard date / direction que ele oferece.
  • Nadia é o desafio. “Gold-plating” carrega uma premissa — que exigência não publicada não é exigência. Nomear essa premissa é a resposta.
  • Nenhuma das três pede que ela confirme a recomendação. Repetir o pedido desperdiça a primeira frase, e é o erro mais provável.
D · Na condução
  • Faça as perguntas EM VOZ, não pela tela. A cena é essa: alguém pergunta e todo mundo olha para ela.
  • O turno é dela na etapa 7. Não complete a frase, não ofereça a palavra. O silêncio é trabalho.
  • Na hostil, mantenha a pressão: se ela recuar, repita a objeção uma vez. Recuar da objeção é o ponto que esta aula existe para trabalhar.
  • Segure a transcrição até a etapa 5. Pergunta em reunião real chega uma vez só.
  • Se a aula estiver fácil, tire a lista de frases da tela antes da terceira pergunta — corte apoio, não acrescente conteúdo.
E · O que observar na produção
  • Se ela CHECA a pergunta vaga ou responde a que imaginou — é o desvio mais provável da aula.
  • O que ela corta no replay de vinte segundos. Se cortar a checagem, ela ainda não é automática, e isso se decide na aula 6, que fecha o bloco.
  • Listening e interação isolados de fala preparada: é a primeira medida dos dois no ciclo.
  • O nível em que ela produz sob pergunta que não previu — isso está em aberto.

Aula 05 Bloco 1 · Grammar / G1 Não iniciada

What this means is

Produzir, sob tempo, as três estruturas que põem a informação nova onde o inglês a espera — no fim da frase: clivada, topicalização e concessiva.

O navegador não abriu a janela. O Teacher’s Guide continua disponível dentro da aula.

A · Objetivo e produto

Objetivo comunicativo: Produzir, sob tempo, as três estruturas que põem a informação nova onde o inglês a espera — no fim da frase: clivada, topicalização e concessiva.

Produto principal: Argumento de 90 segundos sobre uma decisão dela, com as três estruturas e uma discordância sua no meio; depois o mesmo argumento com duas discordâncias.

B · Percurso da aula

C · Antes de abrir a aula
  • Esta aula NÃO é revisão, e isso se diz em voz alta na etapa 1. A professora registrou que ela domina present perfect e mal comete erro gramatical — revisar isso de novo é o que a fez parar de vir.
  • A lacuna defensável é outra: a ordem que põe a informação nova onde o ouvinte espera. É o que faz a fala espontânea voltar à estrutura do português.
  • Anote a frase da etapa 1 exatamente como ela disser. A etapa 2 inteira é a frase dela ao lado da versão marcada, e só funciona com a frase literal.
  • Os dois erros de forma: what the board wants is (concorda com a oração, não com “board”) e it is fixo, que não vira they are.
D · Na condução
  • Na etapa 4 corrija na hora — é a única etapa em que a correção imediata é apropriada, porque o objeto é a forma.
  • Da etapa 6 em diante, anote e devolva na 8. Duas coisas, no máximo.
  • Na etapa 7, discorde de verdade. O movimento certo dela é conceder e segurar; endurecer é o ponto a trabalhar que a aula existe para pegar.
  • Tire as molduras da tela na etapa 7. Se a aula estiver fácil, tire antes — corte apoio, não acrescente conteúdo.
E · O que observar na produção
  • A frase da etapa 1, literal — é a linha de base de produção espontânea do ciclo, e não existe outra.
  • Quais das três estruturas saem sem a moldura na tela.
  • Se ela concede ou endurece sob duas objeções.
  • O nível em que ela de fato produz — H2 continua aberta.

Aula 06 Bloco 1 · ESP / E1 Não iniciada

Forty-five seconds

Responder a um prompt de opinião em 45 segundos cronometrados, com posição, duas razões, um exemplo, uma concessão e um fecho — no formato da tarefa independente de speaking do TOEFL, usado como gênero, não como simulado.

O navegador não abriu a janela. O Teacher’s Guide continua disponível dentro da aula.

A · Objetivo e produto

Objetivo comunicativo: Responder a um prompt de opinião em 45 segundos cronometrados, com posição, duas razões, um exemplo, uma concessão e um fecho — no formato da tarefa independente de speaking do TOEFL, usado como gênero, não como simulado.

Produto principal: Seis respostas cronometradas: quatro na rodada 1, e duas na rodada 2 depois de um único ajuste.

B · Percurso da aula

C · Antes de abrir a aula
  • Esta aula fecha o bloco 1. Reserve os dois minutos finais para o registro pós-aula, sabendo o que ele orienta: se o que trava é sinalização e não forma, em que faixa ela produz, e qual exame Cambridge.
  • O exame se resolve perguntando a ela. Ela trouxe Cambridge em 28/08 e não há exame escolhido. B2 First e C1 Advanced medem coisas diferentes e mudam o bloco 3 em diante. Pergunte hoje e anote.
  • Cronometre de verdade. Os números — em que segundo a posição apareceu, se o fecho chegou — são a evidência; impressão não é.
  • O modelo só toca na etapa 3, DEPOIS da tentativa dela. Tocar antes troca o diagnóstico por imitação.
D · Na condução
  • Não comente entre as quatro respostas da rodada 1. O valor da rodada é ter quatro amostras comparáveis.
  • Na devolutiva, dê NÚMEROS. E escolha UMA coisa para a rodada 2 — uma, não duas.
  • Se ela explicar os dois lados antes de escolher, deixe acontecer na etapa 2. A aula inteira depende de ela ouvir a si mesma fazendo isso.
  • As molduras saem da tela nas duas rodadas. Se ela pedir de volta, diga que voltam na próxima aula.
E · O que observar na produção
  • As seis gravações cronometradas — é o primeiro registro de produção livre dela, e é o que mostra em que faixa ela produz.
  • Se a sinalização sobrevive ao relógio. Se sobreviver, o bloco 2 muda de eixo.
  • Se o único ajuste sobreviveu à rodada 2. Ajuste que só aparece quando ela pensa nele ainda não é ajuste.
  • A resposta dela sobre qual exame Cambridge, perguntada em voz alta e escrita.

Lesson 03

The Shape of
a Talk

Same facts. Same ask.
Only one of them can be followed.

1 · What they asked for

Three minutes, and they have not read it

From: the chair of the review board

Sample message, written for this lesson. No real product, no real board.

“None of us will have read the memo before Thursday. Give us three minutes on it at the top of the meeting — where we are, what the problem is, and what you are asking for. We will decide in that meeting, so whatever is not in your three minutes will not be in the decision.”

What do they need from you, and what will they do with it?

2 · Before you read

Two versions, and the room only follows one

When a room loses the thread of a good argument, what did the speaker fail to do?
Does adding signposting make a talk longer, or only different?
Which is harder to hear without a signal — a fact, or a concession?
3 · Read them once

The same argument, written twice

Version A

Sample text, written for this lesson. Invented product, invented numbers.

We tested the model on twelve months of data. Accuracy is ninety-four percent. The team wants to ship in October. The model cannot explain why it declines a case. Regulators in three markets now ask for that. We would have to rebuild the pipeline. Six weeks. I would hold the launch.

Version B

Sample text, written for this lesson. The same facts, in the same order.

Let me start with where we are. We tested the model on twelve months of data, and accuracy is ninety-four percent — which is why the team wants to ship in October.

Now, here is the problem. The model cannot explain why it declines a case, and regulators in three markets now ask for exactly that. What this means is that shipping in October buys us a launch and costs us the license.

Having said that, the fix is not enormous — rebuilding the pipeline is six weeks. So where does that leave us? My recommendation is that we hold the launch until December.

4 · Where it says so

Six statements, and which version has them

Say which version supports each statement.

Two of them are not in one version alone. Finding those is the point.

The accuracy figure is ninety-four percent.
The listener is told that a difficulty is coming.
The six weeks is presented as a concession, not as a new obstacle.
The recommendation is to hold the launch.
The speaker says which regulator asked for it.
The team’s wish to ship in October is explained by the accuracy.
Two of these are the lesson
The accuracy figure and the recommendation are in both — the facts never changed. And which regulator is in neither: it is the question the room will ask, and neither version answers it. That is what you close on.
5 · Signal or claim

What carries content, and what carries the map

Six sentences from Version B.

Classify each one, then check.

“Accuracy is ninety-four percent.”
“Now, here is the problem.”
“Shipping in October costs us the license.”
“What this means is…”
“Rebuilding the pipeline is six weeks.”
“My recommendation is that we hold.”
The signal is not the claim
A signal carries no content of its own — it tells the room what the next sentence is for. That is exactly why it can be learned separately from the argument, and why it transfers to any argument you ever have to make.
6 · Say the move out loud

Announce it, then say it

Your teacher gives you the bare fact. You give it back with the move in front of it.

Let me start with …
Now, here is the problem. …
What this means is …
Having said that, …
So where does that leave us?  ·  My recommendation is …
7 · Your three minutes

Take the room, and hold it

Your teacher is the review board.
They have not read the memo, and they decide in this meeting.

Where are we, what is the problem, and what are you asking for?

Open by saying where things stand — one sentence, signalled.
Announce the difficulty before you describe it.
Draw the consequence out loud, so nobody has to.
Concede the strongest thing against you, and keep your point.
Close with the ask, and with the question neither version answers.
Let me start with…  ·  Now, here is the problem.  ·  What this means is…
Having said that…  ·  So where does that leave us?  ·  My recommendation is…
8 · Ninety seconds

Again, to a board that disagrees

They have now read the memo — and they want to ship in October.

Same argument. Ninety seconds. What goes first?

What changes when they already disagree
You no longer have to establish the facts. You do have to make the concession early, because a room that thinks you have not heard the objection stops listening for it — and that concession is now the whole ninety seconds.

Neutral order

Lesson 06

Forty-Five
Seconds

Fifteen to decide. Forty-five to say it.
No second attempt.

1 · The task

Fifteen seconds to decide

The independent speaking task

Public format of the TOEFL iBT task. Not a real exam item.

A question with two positions. 15 seconds to prepare, 45 seconds to answer. The clock does not stop, and there is no second attempt.

The fifteen seconds are for choosing, not for writing. Anyone who writes runs out of time reading.

In a meeting, when do you get exactly this?

2 · Try it once

Before any model, just try it

Your teacher reads the prompt, then times you: fifteen, then forty-five.

Speed

Took a position in the first eight seconds.
Announced how many reasons were coming.
Explained both sides before choosing.
Ran out of time before the close.
Used an example from my own work.
This is your own attempt, not a right answer. Mark it honestly — the point is to hear the model against what you actually did, not against what you meant to do.
3 · How it sounds

One answer, forty-five seconds

Speed

4 · The five moves

Position, two reasons, a concession, a close
