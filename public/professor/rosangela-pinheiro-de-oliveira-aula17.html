From Names to People

Say Who They Are

Anybody can say "I'm here with my husband." That sentence ends the conversation. But say who he is — that he is quieter than you, that he is more patient, that he never changes his mind — and the other person starts talking about their own family. That is how a breakfast table becomes a friendship, and you do not need perfect English for it.

Chapter 3: Quieter Than Me

Two People, One Little Word: Than

How to put two people side by side, in one sentence

Grammar Discovery

Listen and Notice

"My husband is quieter than me."

"He is more patient than me."

"My son speaks better English than I do."

"He is not as talkative as his brother."

Every sentence puts two people side by side. Look at the green words: what happened to the end of the short adjective? What word came before the long one? And which little word comes after all of them?

AdjectiveComparativeExample
short (quiet, calm, young)+ -er thanHe is quieter than me.
ending in -y (easy, funny, happy)y → -ier thanShe is happier than her sister.
long (patient, outgoing, generous)more + adjective + thanHe is more patient than me.
good / badbetter / worse thanMy son speaks better English than I do.
the same amount(not) as + adjective + asHe is not as talkative as his brother.

Short adjective takes -er. Long adjective takes more. Never both. And the second person always arrives after than — or between as ... as.

The Anatomy

Four Cards, One Comparison

Four ways to put two people side by side
Short adjective + -er than
one syllable, or two

Quiet, calm, young, old, tall: just add -er, and then than. "He's quieter than me." "My brother is younger than me." If the adjective ends in -y, the y becomes -ier: happy becomes happier, easy becomes easier, funny becomes funnier.

More + long adjective + than
two syllables or more

Patient, outgoing, generous, easygoing, talkative, hardworking: these are too long for -er, so more goes in front. "He's more patient than me." "She's more generous than anybody." Never both together: more quieter and more patienter do not exist.

Good and bad go their own way
irregular, learn them once

Good becomes better, and bad becomes worse. "My son speaks better English than I do." "The second hotel was worse than the first one." Never gooder, never more good, never more better.

(not) as + adjective + as
the same, or less

Use as ... as when two people are equal: "He's as old as my brother." Use not as ... as to say somebody has less, gently: "He isn't as brave as me with English." The word as appears twice, on both sides of the adjective -- never as brave than.

The Toolkit

Put Two People Side by Side

You do not need a long speech to introduce a person. One comparison is enough, and the other person sees them immediately.

Short adjective: + -er than

"He's quieter than me."
"My brother is younger than me."

Long adjective: more + than

"He's more easygoing than me."
"She's more generous than anybody."

And when the two are close, use as ... as: "He's as tall as my son," or "He's not as brave as me with English." One sentence, and the person is in the room.

The Gentle Frame

Not As ... As

Use as ... as when two people are the same, and not as ... as when one has less — it is the kindest way to say it.

He's not as brave as me with English.

as + adjective + as, never "as brave than"

"My husband is as old as my brother."
"I'm not as patient as he is."

Two people, one adjective between two little words. Say it three times, and it stops feeling strange.

Common Mistake

Right vs Wrong

"My husband is more quieter than me."
"My husband is quieter than me." — a short adjective takes "-er", and never "more" too.
"He is more patient that me."
"He is more patient than me." — a comparison always takes "than", never "that".
"My son is more good at English than me."
"My son is better at English than me." — "good" has its own comparative: better.

Three little checks: is it -er OR more (never both)? Is it than and not "that"? And is "good" wearing its own coat, better?

Expression of the Day

I Do the Talking

He doesn't speak English, so I do the talking for both of us.

= he is here, he is with me, and I am the voice of this couple

This sentence does two jobs at once. It explains why he is quiet, and it says, without apologizing, that you are the one who speaks for the two of you.
Then add one comparison — "he's quieter than me, but he's much more easygoing" — and he stops being the silent man at the table. He becomes a person the others have met.

Chapter 4: At the Breakfast Table

Table Nine, Sorrento

Where a polite answer becomes a real conversation

Listening

The Waiter Who Reads Tables

Eighteen years of breakfasts in Sorrento, and the one thing he has learned about travelers. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. Antonio says there are always two kinds of people at the tables. Who are they?
There is always one person who is more talkative than everybody else, who says good morning to the whole room; and there is always somebody quieter, behind the newspaper, who only wants the coffee. He says neither one is better than the other.
2. According to Antonio, which couples enjoy the trip most?
Almost never the ones with the best English — the ones who talk about their people, about their family.
3. What did the woman from Brazil tell him about her husband and her son?
That her husband did not speak one word of English and was much shyer than she was, that he was more easygoing than her and never in a hurry, happy on the balcony in the sun — and that her son was more fluent than the whole family together.
Listening 2

Three Sentences on a Bus

Marina is seventy-one, and for years her husband sat next to her like a piece of luggage. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. For years, what happened when somebody smiled at Marina and her husband in a hotel?
She answered with four words — "I am Marina, from Brazil" — and the conversation stopped, because she had nothing else. She knew the words for the airport and the hotel, but she had no words for the people she loves.
2. What happened on the bus in Portugal, and what did Marina say?
A woman asked her about her husband, and instead of stopping at four words she tried: he is quieter than her, not as brave as her with languages, but more patient, and the most generous man she knows. Three sentences — and the woman laughed, said her husband was exactly the same, and they talked for an hour.
3. What does Marina say you really need in order to make a friend abroad?
Not perfect grammar — the words to say who your people are: who is the talkative one, who is shy, who is easygoing, who never changes his mind. Learn those five or six words and you stop traveling as a tourist.

Comprehension

About Frank

1. Who is Frank traveling with, and where is she during breakfast?
With his wife, Susan. She is still asleep, because she is much slower in the morning than he is.
2. What does Frank say about his younger brother?
That he is more talkative than anybody in their family, and that he gets along with everybody — like the outgoing one at this table.
3. What does Frank say about Susan and new languages?
That she is not as patient as he is with new languages — she just points at the menu and smiles.
4. Frank hears that the son travels with his friends now. What does he say about that?
"And here you are in Italy without him. Good for you." He thinks traveling without the fluent son is something to be proud of.

Sentences needed per personOne
1. Look at the husband line and the son line. Which one uses "-er", which one uses an irregular word, and why?
"Quieter than" uses -er, because "quiet" is a short adjective. "Better than" is irregular: "good" never takes -er or "more" — it becomes "better".
2. Read the friends line out loud. Why "as talkative as" and not "more talkative than"?
Because they are the same, not different. "As ... as" says two people are equal; "more ... than" says one has more.
3. Now add a fifth person to the card — a neighbor, a cousin, a friend from Trancoso. Say two comparisons about them out loud.
For example: "My neighbor is older than me, but she's much more outgoing. She isn't as patient as I am, and she talks to everybody in the street."

The same guest asks what your husband is like. Say one thing, with a comparison.

  • He's quieter than me, but he's much more easygoing.
  • He's more patient than I am. Three hours in an airport, and he never complains.

They ask about your son. Say how old he is and compare his English with yours.

  • He's twenty-four, and he speaks better English than I do.
  • My son is fluent -- his English is much better than mine, and he's very hardworking.

They ask if your son is like you or like his father.

  • He takes after me. He's as talkative as I am.
  • He takes after me -- he talks to everybody, like his mother.

Say something kind about a friend who is very generous.

  • She's more generous than anybody I know.
  • My friend is the most generous person at any table -- she always pays first.

Say, gently, that your husband is less brave than you with English.

  • He isn't as brave as me with English.
  • He's not as confident as I am in English, but he understands more than he says.

The guest tells you their brother never changes his mind. Agree, and say who is like that in your family.

  • My brother is the same -- he's very stubborn!
  • Mine too. My husband is a little stubborn: the map is wrong, never him.

The conversation is ending, and you want it to continue. Ask them about their family.

  • And you? Do you travel with your family?
  • What about you? Who do you usually travel with?

Self-Assessment

What I Can Do Now

I can describe a person with the right word: outgoing, shy, patient, easygoing, generous, stubborn.
I use "-er than" with short adjectives: "He's quieter than me" — never "more quieter".
I use "more ... than" with long adjectives, and "better/worse than" for good and bad.
I can say two people are the same, or not: "He isn't as patient as I am."
I can introduce my husband to a stranger and say who he is — without anybody helping me.
