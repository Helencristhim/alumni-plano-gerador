Chapter 3: The Code

Three Ways to Say Next Year

will · going to · present continuous

Grammar Discovery

Which One Is Already Decided?

"They are applying to six schools next year."

"We are going to visit two campuses in October."

"Look at that number. That is going to be a problem."

"It will be expensive, but do not worry — I will call the aid office tonight."

The first two are already decided. The third is a guess with the evidence in front of you. The blue one was decided one second before it was said.

FormWhen you use itExample
Present continuousAn arrangement already fixed: a date, a booking, a form sent"They are applying next year."
going to + verbA plan you already have in your head"We are going to visit two campuses."
going to + verbA prediction with the evidence right in front of you"That is going to be a problem."
will + verbA prediction, an opinion about the future"It will be expensive."
will + verbA decision you make while you are speaking"I will call the office tonight."

Key idea: the more fixed the future is, the more the sentence sounds like the present. A booked tour is present continuous; a plan is going to; a guess is will. And after when, if or before, English uses the present, never will: "I will send the form when I get her tax number."

Reading

The Education Page

Two Kids, One Envelope -- what college really costs around here

Every fall, families in this area open the same envelope and feel the same thing. The number on the page is called the cost of attendance, and at a private university it now sits close to eighty thousand dollars a year. That figure is not only tuition. It includes room and board, campus fees, books, and an estimate of what a student will spend on a bus. Counselors even have a name for the moment the envelope is opened: sticker shock. What they will tell you, and what is true, is that almost nobody pays the number printed on the page. The problem is that families make big decisions in the ten minutes before they learn that.

The first thing that moves the number is geography. A public university charges one price to students whose families live and pay taxes in the state, and a much higher price to everybody else. In Virginia this year, an in-state student pays about fifteen thousand dollars in tuition; the same seat costs a student from another state more than fifty. Residency is not a matter of opinion. States check driver's licenses, tax returns and how long the family has actually been there. For a household that has just arrived from another country, this single line is the most expensive one in the whole letter, and it is worth asking about two years early.

The second thing that moves the number is the aid office. Financial aid is money the university takes off the price after it looks at what a family earns and owns. A scholarship is money a student is given for grades, for a sport, or for a major the department is trying to fill. The two are counted together, and both start with a form the family files in the fall of the student's senior year. Filing late is the most common and the most expensive mistake in this whole process, because some of the money simply runs out. A student who applies to a school in December and files that form in March is not applying to the same school.

So what are families around here actually doing? Some are taking out a loan, which is still the most common answer and the one that follows a household for twenty years. Some are sending a student to a community college for two years and transferring after that, which saves more than any scholarship most students will ever win. A few are choosing a cheap dorm over a famous name. And a growing number are taking a gap year, working and saving and starting when they are ready. The families who stay on track are not the ones with the most money. They are the ones who started asking these questions long before the first form was due.

Local paper -- education section, Washington, DC

1. What did she and her daughter do in October, and would she do it again?
They drove to four campuses in one week. No — she says she would not do that again.
2. What was their real mistake, and what did it cost them?
They filed the financial aid form in February. By then the money at her first-choice school was already gone.
3. Where is the daughter studying now, and how does she feel about it?
At an in-state school she never even visited — and she is happy there.

Listening 3

Is a Gap Year Worth It?

Both sides of the argument, and where the speaker landed. Sound first — no text.

0:000:00
1. What is the argument against the gap year, in her words?
You lose momentum. A student who stops after high school sometimes never starts again, and a year out is a year of tuition paid later, at a higher price.
2. What does she say about the students who are on track on paper?
That half of them have no idea why they are there, and they are going to spend forty thousand dollars a year finding out.
3. What is her final position, and what is the one condition she adds?
Take the year, but make it a real one — work, save, do something difficult — and apply before you go, so the seat is waiting for you.
