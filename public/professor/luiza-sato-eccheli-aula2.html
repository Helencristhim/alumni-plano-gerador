A Signature Is Not a Promise

A Signature Is Not a Promise

Every year, photographs of heads of state signing a treaty are published around the world, and every year the same mistake is made in the headlines. A signature is treated as the end of the story. In most cases it is only the beginning. When a treaty is signed, a state shows that it agrees with the text, but it is not yet bound by it.

The obligation starts later. In most countries, a treaty has to be approved by the national parliament and then ratified by the government. Only after ratification does a state become a party, and only then is it legally required to comply with every provision it has accepted. Many treaties also say that they will enter into force only when a minimum number of states have ratified them, so a text can be signed by dozens of countries and still have no legal effect for years.

Even then, a state is allowed some room. A reservation can be made at the moment of ratification, stating that one particular article will not be applied. Reservations are not unlimited: one that goes against the object and purpose of the treaty is not accepted. And a state that no longer wants to be bound can withdraw, usually after formal notice has been given.

This is why students of Global Law are often told that the treaty is not the problem. The text is usually clear. The difficult question is what happens after a breach, because in international law there is no police force, and sovereignty is protected by the same system that is supposed to enforce the rules. Most treaties are respected, but they are respected because states choose to, not because anyone can force them.

Written for this lesson, from the general rules of treaty law (1969 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties).

Grammar Discovery

Who Signed, Who Ratified?

"The treaty was signed in March."

"A reservation can be made at the moment of ratification."

"The text has been ratified by forty states."

"Students are often told that the treaty is not the problem."

In each sentence, who did the action? Is that person mentioned? Why would a legal text choose to hide the person, or to mention it only with by?

JobFormExample
The action matters more than who did itbe + past participleThe treaty was signed in March.
Say who did it, when it matterspassive + by + agentIt was ratified by the congress.
A result that is still truehas / have been + past participleIt has been ratified by forty states.
Possibility or obligationmodal + be + past participleA reservation can be made. The text must be approved.
Something in progressis / are being + past participleThe case is being heard this week.
A general beliefit is said / believed that + clauseIt is often said that treaties are weak.
The trapnever was ratify; never was ratified fromNot ratified from the congress. Say ratified by the congress.

In one line: Legal English puts the action first: be + past participle, and by only when the actor matters.

One Question at a Time

1 / 6

A classmate says that a signed treaty is already binding. Correct them in two sentences.

  • Not yet. A treaty is only binding after it has been ratified, and signing only shows agreement with the text.
  • Use the passive: has been ratified, is signed.

Explain what a reservation is, without using the word reservation.

  • It is a statement, made when a country ratifies, saying that one article will not be applied to it.
  • Relative clause plus passive: will not be applied.

Say that the treaty was approved by the congress last year, and that it entered into force in May.

  • The treaty was approved by the congress last year, and it entered into force in May.
  • Passive for approved; active for entered into force.
