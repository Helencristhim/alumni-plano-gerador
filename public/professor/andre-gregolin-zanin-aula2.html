Read for the Main Idea

The Conversation That Ended Four Years

Diego Aragão was fifteen when a scout from a Portuguese club watched him play in a tournament in Recife, and sixteen when he moved to Lisbon on his own. For four years he trained twice a day. He has never described those years as unhappy — he says the opposite, that he would go back tomorrow — but he is also clear about what they cost. “People hear the word sacrifice and they think of something dramatic,” he says. “It is not. It is four hundred small things you do not do.”

The turning point came in his third season, and it was not an injury. It was a conversation. A coach told him, honestly, that he was a very good player who was not going to be a great one, and that the club would keep him another year because he was useful. Diego thanked him, finished the season, and did not renew. “From that point on, I stopped asking whether I could stay,” he says. “I started asking what I actually wanted.”

He came home at nineteen and spent the first year doing almost nothing that looked like progress. The transition was harder than the training had ever been. He had lived alone in another country and could not decide what to study. He has said since then that the demanding part of leaving is not the leaving — it is the eight or ten months afterwards, when the intensity you are used to disappears and nothing has replaced it yet.

Diego is twenty-four now and in his final year of engineering, which he decided to pursue at twenty. He still plays on Thursday nights, badly and happily. He has kept one thing from Lisbon, and it is not the football: it is the commitment to a schedule. He wakes at six. He has not missed a deadline in three years. When younger players ask him whether they should give it up, he refuses to answer. “Nobody can tell you that,” he says. “But I can tell you that it takes a toll either way. Staying takes one and leaving takes another, and you choose which one you are willing to pay.”

Chapter 4: The Code

One Past, Two Shapes

The fence that closes a sentence

Grammar Discovery

Both Are the Past. What Separates Them?

"He moved to Lisbon at sixteen and trained there for four years."

"He has never described those years as unhappy."

"He came home at nineteen, and he has not missed a deadline in three years."

"Have you ever given something up like that?" — "Yes, I did, in 2023."

Both colors are about the past. The red one puts a fence around it — an age, a year, a date — and the story closes. The green one leaves the fence out, because the count is still running.

JobFormExample
Finished, and the time is namedpast simple + a closed timeHe moved to Lisbon at sixteen.
Still counting, no closed timehave / has + past participleHe has never described those years as unhappy.
How long it has lasted, up to nowfor + a length · since + a starting pointHe has lived in Brazil since he was nineteen.
News, with the result still herejust · already · yetHe has already handed it in. / He has not decided yet.
A life experience, with no dateever · neverHave you ever trained twice a day?
The trapa named time kills the present perfectNot Last year I have traveled. Say Last year I traveled.
The other trapsince never takes the present simpleNot I live here since March. Say I have lived here since March.

In one line: name the time and the sentence closes. Leave the time out and the count is still running.

Quick Fire

One Question at a Time

1 / 6

Say when Diego moved to Lisbon, in one sentence.

  • He moved to Lisbon at sixteen, in August 2017.
  • The age is a fence, so the sentence closes: moved, not has moved.

Now say what has been true since he came back.

  • He has not missed a deadline in three years.
  • No date, so the count is still running: has not missed.

Ask a question about his whole life, with ever.

  • Has he ever said he regrets it?
  • Ever means at any point up to now, so it never takes a date.

Correct this out loud: I live in Sao Paulo since last year.

  • I have lived in Sao Paulo since last year.
  • Since always needs the present perfect. This is the number one error from Portuguese.

Say one thing you gave up, and when.

  • I gave up basketball at sixteen, when I moved.
  • A date, so the past simple. Watch the fence.

Now say one thing that has changed since then, with no date at all.

  • I have studied six days a week since March.
  • Since a starting point, so have plus the participle.
