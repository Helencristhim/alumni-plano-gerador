Two Registries, One Ship

Two Registries, One Ship

A convention does not become law because it is adopted. What makes it law in any particular ship is ratification by the state whose flag she flies, followed by transposition into that state's own legislation. It is for that reason that two identical vessels, side by side at the same berth, may be subject to different provisions of the same instrument.

The vessel in this case was registered in a state which had ratified the convention but not the protocol that replaced its survey regime. The classification society to which the owner had contracted the surveys held an authorisation from that flag state and from no other. The port authority in whose waters she lay was acting for a state which had ratified both instruments, and whose inspectors were therefore applying a standard the ship had never been built to meet.

It was not the surveyor who was at fault, and it was not the master. What produced the detention was an amendment that one registry had accepted and the other had not, and a certificate that was valid under the law of the flag and insufficient under the law of the port. Both readings were correct. Only one of them was enforceable in that harbour.

The case is cited whenever the industry discusses jurisdiction, because it shows what compliance actually costs. Liability did not follow the fault; it followed the flag. And enforcement, which is the only part of the system the master can see from the bridge, belonged to the state that had done nothing at all except open its port.

Chapter 4: The Code

What the Writer Put First

It was not the surveyor who... / What produced the detention was...

Grammar Discovery

Two Ways of Pointing

"It was not the surveyor who was at fault, and it was not the master."

"What makes it law in any particular ship is ratification by the state whose flag she flies."

"The classification society to which the owner had contracted the surveys held one authorisation only."

"The port authority in whose waters she lay was acting for a different state."

The first two point at ONE thing and push everything else out of the frame. The last two attach a clause to a noun that is not the nearest one. For each of the four, say what would be lost if you wrote it in ordinary order.

FormWhat it doesExample
It + be + X + who / thatthe it-cleft: it names X and excludes everything elseIt was not the surveyor who was at fault.
What + clause + be + Xthe wh-cleft: it delays X to the end, where the emphasis fallsWhat produced the detention was an amendment.
preposition + which / whom / whoseformal relative clause: the preposition moves to the front with the pronounthe society to which the owner had contracted the surveys
whose + nounpossession inside the clause; it can belong to a thing, not only to a personthe state whose flag she flies
The reading trapthe relative clause does NOT always attach to the nearest noun. The port authority in whose waters she lay was acting for a state which had ratified both -- which points at the state, not at the waters and not at the authority. Every wrong option re-attaches the clause to the wrong noun.

Both structures exist to control where the reader looks. The cleft says look here and nowhere else; the relative clause says this one, not the other one. In a legal text those two instructions are the whole meaning.

Quick Fire

One Question at a Time

1 / 6

It was not the surveyor who was at fault. What does the sentence do that the surveyor was not at fault does not?

  • It names the surveyor in order to exclude him, and it implies somebody else.
  • The it-cleft always points at one item and pushes the rest out of frame.

The port authority in whose waters she lay was acting for a state which had ratified both instruments. Which had ratified: the authority, the waters, or the state?

  • The state. Which attaches to the nearest suitable noun, not always the nearest word.
  • Re-attaching the clause is how the wrong option is built.

Say, in exam register, that a treaty is not law just because it was agreed.

  • Adoption alone does not give a convention legal force.
  • Ratification and transposition are what make it binding.

Turn this into a wh-cleft: an amendment produced the detention.

  • What produced the detention was an amendment.
  • The wh-cleft delays the important item to the end.

Whose flag she flies. Can whose be used for a thing rather than a person?

  • Yes. Whose works for things, states and organisations, not only for people.
  • That is why the state whose flag is correct and the state which flag is not.

In one sentence, say why two identical ships at the same berth can be bound by different rules.

  • Because their flag states ratified different instruments.
  • The ship is bound by what her own registry accepted, not by what the convention says.

Listening 1

The Surveyor Who Was Not at Fault

The man who signed the certificate explains what he was authorised to sign. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. Against which regime did he survey the vessel, and why that one?
Against the regime her flag state had accepted, because that is the only regime his authorisation allows him to survey against. His authorisation comes from one administration and from no other.
2. What does he say people find hard to accept?
That both certificates in the argument were valid: his under the law that appointed him, theirs under the law that controlled the water.
3. What is his conclusion about which one prevails?
The water wins, and it always does -- the law of the place where the ship physically is.

Listening 2

Ratified, and Then Forgotten

A legal adviser on the gap between signing and enforcing. Sound first -- no text.

0:000:00
1. What are the three steps she names?
Adoption at the organisation, ratification by each state, and transposition into that state's national law with a penalty attached.
2. What happens when a state completes ratification but not transposition?
You get a convention that is technically in force and practically invisible.
3. What does she say is the interesting question about a flag?
Not what it has signed, but what it has ever detained a ship for.
